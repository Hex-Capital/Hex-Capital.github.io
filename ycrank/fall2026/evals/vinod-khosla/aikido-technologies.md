# Aikido Technologies -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Aikido Technologies could make power-constrained compute deployable where land, grid access, and cooling water prevent construction today. That is a consequential possibility. But a 15MW+ turbine is a platform specification, not a quantified transformation of compute economics. The dossier’s $450M floating-datacenter market estimate does not establish how much infrastructure spending Aikido can displace or how much cheaper computation becomes. I would pass with my own money today. The consequence remains plausible; its magnitude remains unproven.

The engineering deserves attention. Wind generation, batteries, accessible data halls, and hull-mediated cooling could remove several terrestrial constraints together. They also couple compute availability to marine operations and variable generation. Four-plus hours of batteries cannot establish continuous service through an extended wind lull; subsea connections and diesel backup introduce additional infrastructure and operating costs. Projected PUE below 1.08 addresses cooling overhead, while the investment question includes the entire delivered service. The floating-wind patent establishes a specific engineering asset, but neither it nor the cooling projection establishes a measured 5–10x advantage against terrestrial infrastructure.

The strongest argument against my pass is that these founders have already turned offshore engineering into physical evidence. Kanner led R&D at Principle Power; Yu led hydrodynamics analysis there; Reeves brings helicopter-design experience. ABS witnessed a quarter-scale upending demonstration and issued a statement of maturity in February 2025. That milestone earns credit for removing platform-deployment uncertainty. Aikido fits my recurring preference for difficult physical infrastructure, qualification work, and compatibility with existing suppliers and vessels. Dismissing it because installation is hard would miss the opportunity. Nevertheless, the demonstrated milestone concerns floating wind; the planned Norway compute retrofit must establish a different system’s reliability. The biographies also provide no documented belief revision or difficult recruiting outcome, so I cannot substitute credentials for learning-rate evidence.

I do sometimes invest before a product or a complete business model exists. Those exceptions require an extraordinary, evidenced consequence or a founder commitment strong enough to underwrite a giant existing value pool. Aikido’s domain depth makes that argument credible enough for serious diligence, but does not complete it. Its March 2026 datacenter announcement, customer interest, and UK discussions targeting 2028 operations establish direction rather than customer validation. The $4M floating-wind seed financing and October 2026 offering establish access to capital, not compute economics. There is no evidence here of the red flag of scaling sales before resolving engineering risk; equally, there is no disclosed costed sequence showing which existential uncertainty the next dollar removes.

The decisive uncertainty is whether moving compute offshore lowers its fully delivered cost after redundancy, vessel servicing, downtime, and marine infrastructure. Public pricing and a bottom-up cost model are absent. That earns the missing-evidence default, not an accusation of subsidy dependence. **My verifiable flip condition is an independently audited integrated pilot showing customer-required availability at a fully burdened cost below a named terrestrial alternative, with measured pilot results supporting the scale assumptions and no permanent subsidy.** Until then, Aikido Technologies has stronger evidence for deploying a floating platform than for economically operating a datacenter on it.
