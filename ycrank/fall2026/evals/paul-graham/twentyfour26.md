# twentyfour26 -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Running the machines themselves is the most promising thing about twentyfour26. The founders could have stopped at software that proposes machining plans. Instead, they operate production and inspection, where a plausible plan has to survive contact with metal. That puts them close to the mistakes worth fixing. But I cannot find the founder story I most want: a particular job went wrong, a founder built a workaround, and the customer got a usable part days later. Owning the shop creates opportunities to demonstrate resourcefulness. It does not yet demonstrate it.

There is something real to investigate. The website describes software that checks STEP geometry, selects stock and tooling, and simulates machining for an internally operated CNC. That deserves more credit than diagrams or a plan to hire engineers after fundraising. It remains company-reported technical evidence, however. There is no measured machining result, inspection accuracy, or comparison of programming time. My pattern of backing difficult technical projects after a concrete proof spike fits the ambition here; the proof spike itself is missing. Starting with 6061-T6 aluminum could make automation tractable, but a restricted operating envelope is a hypothesis about how to succeed, not evidence of success.

The October 2 interview supplies a modest demand signal: the company had begun taking manufacturing orders. Hardware teams needing prototypes are a sufficiently specific starting niche. But an order does not tell me whether someone received an acceptable part and immediately ordered another. Nor do individually negotiated quotes establish attractive economics. The important distinction is whether each job teaches the software something reusable or merely creates another job for the founders. CloudNC already supplies overlapping automation to more than 1,000 shops. Twentyfour26 needs its direct production experience to produce an advantage that buying software cannot reproduce.

The strongest case for investing is that this advantage can emerge only by doing the work. Mahendru’s aerospace training and industrial experience give the team relevant context, while Mathew brings software-building experience. Personally inspecting early parts could reveal exactly what a software vendor misses. Their acquaintance goes back eight years, which weakens the concern about founders assembled for an application, though it does not establish shared execution under stress. There is also no disclosed dependence on a major distribution partner or delegated sales organization. Mathew’s leaving school in tenth grade supplies neither proof of exceptionalism nor a reason to reject him.

The pivot from Sutra shows that they can abandon a product. It does not establish my preferred kind of flexibility, because the dossier does not identify the observation that caused the change or the goal that persisted through it. Similarly, industrial employment makes the machining idea plausible without documenting a founder’s personal collision with this particular problem. The operational schlep is concrete; the organic origin remains unproven. I checked the exceptions I make for impressive working prototypes and independently validated technical results. The disclosed system gives this company a claim on attention, but neither exceptional proof nor unmistakable user pull is documented strongly enough to override the missing founder evidence.

I would pass on twentyfour26 today. The decisive gap is evidence of what these founders do when production resists them, rather than the absence of publicity, a long revenue history, or complete autonomy. My flip condition is an auditable four-week production log showing repeat paid orders and founder-built fixes to specific machining failures that measurably reduce intervention on subsequent jobs. That would connect the promising mechanism—owning the entire learning loop—to resourcefulness, technical progress, and customers who come back.
