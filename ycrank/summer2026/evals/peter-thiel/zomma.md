# Zomma -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Jihyun Kim helped launch a $1B multi-strategy hedge fund, yet Zomma discloses no multi-year product milestones. That pairing is Zomma’s signature: unusually credible operators pursuing a domain they understand, but without a definite plan explaining what Zomma must become beyond an approval-gated computer-use agent. Pedigree is Zomma’s strongest dimension; definite optimism is its weakest. Intelligent founders can execute an indefinite idea very efficiently.

Anduril is the high rung for founder lineage: Palmer Luckey had already shipped difficult hardware to a billion-dollar exit before attacking defense. Zomma shares the operator-insight mechanism—Kim worked at Goldman Sachs, Endowus, and Arrowpoint, while Cashel Fitzgerald built systems supporting $300M+ in annual revenue and $20B+ in transaction volume. Zomma diverges because none of its founders has yet shipped a proprietary product to independent scale or produced the kind of prior outcome that justified the Asana and Quora exceptions. The unstated operator-reup pattern helps Zomma, but it does not complete the analogy.

Quora’s low-rung weakness was an indefinite roadmap in a contested consumer category; Adam D’Angelo’s Facebook pedigree supplied the exception. Zomma shows the weakness without an equivalent override: Zomma describes demonstrations, workflow mapping, forward deployment, and human approvals, but names no two-to-five-year technical milestone or architectural commitment. “Agents learn workflows” is a capability, not a plan. No definite-plan evidence appears, so the absent-evidence default applies. Zomma may learn what customers want; the question is whether Zomma already knows what the future must contain.

Zalos has raised $3.6M to make computer agents operate finance systems much as Zomma does, while Eloquent AI has raised $7.4M and reached $0.5M ARR. Over the next 18 months, Zomma’s secret either compounds from Kim’s domain knowledge into a precise claim—perhaps that investment operations require an approval graph general agents cannot safely represent—or dissolves into the red-flag formulation “AI for finance.” Zomma identifies broken workflows across portals, PDFs, and software without APIs, but Zomma never names the incumbent whose architecture prevents a response. Competition is being used as category validation. That is mimetic evidence, not a secret.

Anthropic and OpenAI already expose general computer-use capabilities, so Zomma’s monopoly counterfactual is demanding: Zomma would need to demonstrate that customer-specific workflow maps, correction histories, and audit records produce materially higher reliability and switching costs than both frontier-model APIs and Zalos. Signed multi-year contracts, proprietary exception data, and renewal pricing that rises with workflow coverage would move Zomma into the Invest tier. The dossier instead labels switching costs as inferred, reports no revenue, and finds no structural barrier. Without that evidence, Zomma is an application layer negotiating against commodity intelligence.

Palantir is the relevant single-anchor parallel for Zomma’s regulated-sector dimension. Palantir converted government security requirements, integration depth, and institutional trust into a moat that survived feature competition. Zomma sells into hedge funds, asset managers, wealth advisers, KYC, and investor reporting, where mistakes are expensive and approval trails matter. Yet Zomma has SOC 2 still in progress, unnamed customers, no disclosed certification barrier, and no government contract structure. Regulation gives Zomma a better starting point than ordinary SaaS, but regulation becomes a moat only when Zomma owns the trust boundary rather than merely complying with it.

No public revenue is disclosed for Zomma, despite unnamed financial firms reportedly using the product. By July 2029, I expect Zomma to have narrowed toward wealth-management and fund-administration reconciliation, reached roughly $3M–$7M ARR, and been acquired by SS&C Technologies for under $100M as a workflow feature rather than becoming an independent monopoly. Zomma’s founder-market fit can build a valuable product; Zomma’s absent secret, generic model dependency, and indefinite roadmap make a power-law outcome less likely than vertical consolidation.

Unnamed hedge funds, asset managers, and fintech firms are the strongest case against that forecast: Zomma may already possess customer truth the public dossier cannot reveal, and Kim’s Arrowpoint experience could function like the operator-pedigree override that justified Asana. Forward deployment could turn Zomma’s workflow maps and correction histories into proprietary operational infrastructure, while human approval could solve the reliability barrier before general agents do. The objection stings because pre-seed secrecy hides precisely the evidence contrarian investments often contain. It does not yet win: Zomma discloses no revenue, retention, exclusive data, named incumbent failure mode, or definite roadmap, while three funded substitutes and two frontier-model vendors attack the same layer. I would flip to Invest if Zomma verifies at least $2M ARR from multi-year contracts, net revenue retention above 130%, and a proprietary audit-and-exception architecture that Zalos or OpenAI cannot reproduce through ordinary model access.
