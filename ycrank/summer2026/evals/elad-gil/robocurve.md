# Robocurve -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Robotics capabilities are advancing faster than buyers can verify them, but Robocurve becomes a large company only if independent, cross-embodiment evaluation turns into a recurring purchasing requirement rather than an internal lab function. That is the controlling variable. The dossier does not yet identify a threshold—number of subscribing labs, mandatory procurement standard, or evaluation frequency—at which benchmark authority produces durable economics. One completed pilot shows feasibility, not that laboratories will surrender evaluation ownership or pay an independent operator repeatedly.

The strongest evidence is the product itself. Inspect Robots runs interchangeable policies across simulated or physical embodiments while retaining configurations, grader outputs, transcripts, traces, and visualizations. Roughly 400 commits, 13 public repositories, multiple hardware adapters, and version 0.21.1 indicate substantially more than a demo. Better robot models should increase the need for rigorous evaluation rather than absorb this infrastructure. But only one public pilot has exercised the system, and the API remains in early development, so Robocurve has not yet demonstrated the recurring workflow state, customer integrations, or reference history needed to own the evaluation loop.

Demand is suggestive but weak. The project has 134 GitHub stars, 19 forks, and 33 organization followers, which establishes technical interest. It does not establish paid pull, repeat usage, or benchmark legitimacy. Positronic launched a physical-task leaderboard in March 2026, Instance is automating rollout judging and resets, and ReSim already orchestrates testing across simulation and hardware. This convergence validates the problem while weakening the assumption that Robocurve alone becomes its neutral standard.

The economic shift is less convincing than the technical shift. Robocurve still needs maintained robots, controlled environments, domain experts, and repeated physical resets. No evidence quantifies how its framework lowers evaluation cost, raises throughput, or converts those service-heavy operations into software margins. The MIT license accelerates distribution but lets labs and competitors internalize the framework; the proposed data moat is inferred from evaluations that have barely begun. A reproducible dataset may eventually matter, but its replication cost is currently unspecified.

The bull case is that Jay Chooi’s evaluation background and Aris Zhu’s experience at Amazon Robotics, Amazon AGI Labs, and Yondu give the team unusually complete coverage of both measurement and physical execution. Their open-source framework could become the shared substrate while competing products remain embodiment-specific. Still, founder-market fit is not evidence of desperation or second-time learning, and it does not override a missing market mechanism. Neither the frontier-research exception nor the physical-economics exception applies: Robocurve has not shown a scarce foundational breakthrough, a differentiated capital path, or transformed hardware unit economics.

I pass today. My base case is that within 36 months Robocurve either pivots toward managed robotics test orchestration or remains a useful open-source project without reaching $1 million ARR. The verifiable flip condition is three named frontier robotics labs paying for repeated cross-embodiment evaluations, producing at least $250,000 in contracted ARR and a benchmark used in an external procurement or model-release decision.
