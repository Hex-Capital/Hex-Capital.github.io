# Sidekick -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: a factory worker photographs a failing machine, receives a safe diagnosis, dispatches the repair, and updates the plant’s operating memory without opening an app. Sidekick has one artifact leaking from that world—SMS access deployed across 20-plus plants and 600-plus workers—but the dossier does not map the intervening transitions. Reliable multimodal diagnosis, verified resolutions, PLC/SCADA integrations, and permissioned institutional memory are promises rather than demonstrated steps. I can see the future; I cannot yet see Sidekick’s path through it.

The present-day leak is still meaningful because this is an irritating system with a very ordinary cause. Operational knowledge sits in binders, shared drives, posted documents, and veteran employees’ heads, so the person standing beside a stopped machine cannot act without finding a gatekeeper. SMS removes the account and app-install tax, returns some agency to the frontline worker, and turns each escalation into searchable plant history. But the dossier never quantifies the rent: no downtime reduction, response-time improvement, avoided service call, or evidence that workers resolve problems without waiting for a manager.

Twenty plants and 600 workers is unusually concrete for pre-seed. It proves that factories will admit the product through the front door; it does not yet prove costly or weird behavior after entry. There are no dated cohorts, repeat-question rates, completed work orders, retention figures, or disclosed revenue. The terms mention paid plans, but that is not payment evidence. The strongest bull case is that repeated fixes quietly become the facility’s most useful operating record, creating migration friction before an incumbent recognizes the wedge. I would take that possibility seriously because the customer-owned history is more interesting than the chatbot.

Justin So has legitimate contact with the problem: he comes from a third-generation manufacturing family, spent summers on the EDS Manufacturing floor, earned a computer-science master’s, and worked in finance at Arm. That is context, not yet pre-company obsession. I do not see dated prototypes, years of self-directed industrial work, shipped artifacts, or proof that he recruited complementary technical and commercial talent. The solo-founder structure at least makes accountability unambiguous; there is no 50/50 governance trap hiding in the dossier.

My 36-month base case is that Autodesk acquires Sidekick below $5 million ARR and folds its SMS interface and plant history into MaintainX. The reason is structural: MaintainX already owns maintenance workflows, asset data, AI search, and enterprise integrations, while Sidekick controls no demonstrated lower-stack bottleneck that prevents the interface from being copied. Direct PLC/SCADA synchronization remains unverified, AI answers explicitly require user confirmation, and SOC 2 Type II is still in progress despite the system processing worker phone numbers, company SOPs, and operational messages. The privacy architecture is not fatal yet, but it is a gate—not a future moat.

I am neutral and would not write the personal check today. Sidekick fits my unglamorous-workflow-repair pattern, and the deployments keep this well above a generic AI wrapper. What holds me back is the mismatch between a sharp present-day leak and an undemonstrated independent future: the dossier shows distribution into factories, but not yet compounding behavior or control of the infrastructure underneath it. One condition flips me: three named plants each document six months of weekly repeat usage, paid renewal, and at least a 20% reduction in downtime attributable to resolutions retrieved from Sidekick’s accumulated plant history rather than the original uploaded documents.
