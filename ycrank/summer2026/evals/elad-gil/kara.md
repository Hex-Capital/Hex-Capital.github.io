# Kara -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: AI systems are concentrating more heat—up to 120 kW per Blackwell rack—and semiconductor customers may now qualify diamond components if Kara can manufacture them at acceptable yield, dimensions, cost, and delivery time. Thermal conductivity of 2,400 W/m·K establishes why diamond is technically interesting. It does not establish the controlling belief. The company becomes large only if those properties survive qualification and translate into materially better system economics than copper, SiC, GaN, or integrated cooling alternatives.

The technology shift is real but the value-capture mechanism remains incomplete. Akash’s March 2026 launch of diamond-cooled AMD MI350X servers is useful evidence that diamond has moved beyond laboratory theory. Kara also owns more of the technical stack than a simple materials reseller: it claims in-house growth, processing, and characterization across substrates, membranes, heat spreaders, and engineered layers. But there are no disclosed wafer dimensions, defect densities, yields, throughput figures, or customer-level thermal results. Without those numbers, I cannot tell whether Kara is creating product economics or operating a difficult custom-fabrication service.

The strongest bull case is the team. Aarin Jhaveri is a fifth-generation diamantaire who has sold more than $5 million of diamonds; Anuveer Chadha worked on diamond quantum-sensing systems at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory; and Arjun Lakhanpal adds computing and data experience. That combination is unusually specific for a three-person pre-seed company. In-house reactors further suggest that the founders have crossed from presentation into technical execution. The physical-market exception represented by Anduril is directionally relevant because both cases depend on technology changing incumbent economics, but the material difference is that Kara has not yet documented the margin reconstruction or customer vacuum that made that exception investable.

Demand is therefore the weakest part of the case. Kara has no public customers, pilots, qualification results, shipments, or revenue; 427 LinkedIn followers do not demonstrate product-market pull. I do not penalize a Summer 2026 company for lacking a long traction history, but materials qualification is the mechanism, not a cosmetic metric. A signed design-in with a named GPU, RF, power-electronics, aerospace, or quantum customer would be considerably more informative than broad interest across all five markets.

The competitive structure also argues against underwriting technical performance alone. Diamond Foundry has raised $315 million, Diamfab €8.7 million, and Akash combines $18.28 million of financing with grants and tax credits. More than a dozen firms are pursuing single-crystal diamond wafers, while the identified substrate segment is only $10.2 million today. My pattern of buying coverage across a genuine discontinuity does not rescue Kara: coverage works when multiple layers can become large, not when a supplier lacks a demonstrated wedge inside a crowded, capital-intensive layer. The red flag is not that this is hardware; it is that custom specifications and fabrication could preserve service-heavy economics even if the material performs well.

I would not write a check today, but I would monitor Kara because the team and technical integration are stronger than the current market proof. The verifiable flip condition is one named production qualification showing repeat orders alongside disclosed yield and gross-margin evidence at a commercially relevant form factor. That would validate the single causal proposition: Kara can turn diamond’s laboratory advantage into a scalable semiconductor-material business rather than a capable custom shop.
