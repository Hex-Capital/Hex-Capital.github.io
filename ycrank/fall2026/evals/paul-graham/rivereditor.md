# River -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Two people spent ten months building River in a garage. What happened when they got stuck? The dossier doesn’t tell us. That matters more to me than the garage. Supple’s engineering work at Roboflow and Joseff’s experience at Progressly establish relevant experience. Neither supplies the missing episode: a particular obstacle, something the founders did about it, and a result. I would leave resourcefulness at its absent-evidence default rather than confuse a plausible team with a demonstrated one.

The strongest evidence is that they have shipped something substantial. River offers web access and desktop downloads for a workspace spanning documents, spreadsheets, presentations, files, and a browser. Making those surfaces work together is considerably harder than putting a chat box beside a document. This fits my preference for technical founders who build before asking permission. But the product descriptions do not establish task completion rates or reliable cross-application editing. I give the shipped artifact meaningful credit, with a discount for how little of its performance is demonstrated.

The strongest argument against passing is the amount of apparent activity around that artifact. The founders report 50,000 desktop users, 130,000 agents, and 200,000 files; the current homepage claims 100,000 creators. A two-person team getting even a fraction of those people to do useful work could have discovered something important. Yet these are cumulative, self-reported counts with different definitions. They do not show which researchers, consultants, or authors return because River has become necessary. File creation supports more than a waitlist does, but it cannot establish a narrow well of demand.

River’s origin is more credible than a generic decision to build an AI productivity company. Joseff’s research workflows and Supple’s AI engineering experience connect the founders to the context-transfer problem, although the account is retrospective. Accessing local files and signed-in applications also exposes useful, ugly integration work. The question is whether doing that work produces something users need enough to leave their existing tools. Superhuman Docs already overlaps in collaborative documents and external connections. River’s $14, $36, and $100 plans provide a way to charge for the difference; they do not show that anyone pays for it. Nor does including AI credits establish attractive margins.

I also have no evidence of an observation that caused the founders to reject one route and ship another, so adaptability stays at its default. That is missing history, not demonstrated obstinacy. The ten months of shared work provide some reassurance about the relationship. There is no documented dependence on a large partner, premature delegation, or use of dropout status as proof. My unstated preference for programmable tools is relevant, but River has not yet shown users appropriating its workspace for unexpected workflows. Its breadth alone earns no such credit.

I would pass today. I checked whether the working-product exception should override the missing founder evidence: River has a shipped product and considerable reported activity, but neither independently validated technical proof nor unmistakable recurring pull makes that override convincing yet. The verifiable condition that would make me reconsider is one documented customer episode in which a named researcher’s blocked workflow led to a dated founder-built workaround, followed by four weeks of repeat use. That would connect the software to both the founders’ behavior under resistance and a person who actually needs it.
