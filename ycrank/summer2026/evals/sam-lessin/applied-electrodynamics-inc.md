# Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely the first scarce check. Applied Electrodynamics supplies none of them. For a Summer 2026 YC hardware company, that does not prove an auction, but it prevents me from knowing whether I am buying cheap uncertainty or Demo Day consensus. The company may have built remarkable physics; I still cannot underwrite the security without its price.

The clean venture moment would be easy to state: finance an independently benchmarked field deployment proving that WaveSight can reconstruct pipes, cables, and structural elements through real walls at the claimed 1 mm accuracy, from across a room, well enough to prevent costly construction rework. A positive result would distinguish it sharply from Walabot’s $199.95 contact scanner. But the dossier never says the round will fund that test, names no contractor, metric, budget, or deadline, and instead requests introductions across construction, industrial inspection, and defense. Those are three validation regimes and three sales channels, not one truth-revealing experiment.

The bull case stings because this is an unusually credible technical team. Bilgehan Avser led antenna technology across four iPhone generations; Brian Huppi shipped early iPhone multitouch and the first Nest thermostat; Paul Leutheuser worked on Touch ID haptics and IPX8 sealing; George McLean built semiconductor imagers, retinal implants, and optical sensors. They have worked together for more than eight years and claim working hardware across sheetrock, tile, marble, quartz, brick, and insulation. This is not four tourists wrapping a foundation model. It is a team that knows how to turn difficult sensing physics into manufactured devices.

But they have shipped on only one side of the bridge. The dossier documents deep receipts in antennas, imaging, sensing, and consumer hardware, not construction operations, contractor workflow, industrial inspection procurement, or defense deployment. The hoped-for dark pool—scans paired with verified wall contents—is still hypothetical. Preorder registration, 148 LinkedIn followers, “hundreds of conversations” at a trade show, and conference attendance establish curiosity, not a purchase or retained workflow. The known exception for an expensive or undefined round requires unusually hard external validation; Applied Electrodynamics has no disclosed unit orders, paid pilot, independent benchmark, or acquisition offer that could perform that override.

Deleting the next financing makes the economic hole obvious. There is no unit price, preorder count, gross margin, burn, runway, or route to break-even. The published through-wall radar market estimate is only $134 million in 2025, while Vayyar has raised more than $300 million and already sells an RF wall scanner. A profitable specialist instrument business may exist here, but construction, manufacturing, cargo screening, and defense cannot all be used to manufacture a venture-scale narrative before one customer proves willingness to pay.

I pass at the current evidence level. My verifiable flip condition is a sub-$10 million post-money round explicitly financing a dated, paid contractor deployment in which an independent benchmark confirms the accuracy advantage and documents avoided rework. That would simultaneously show scarce pricing, a finite venture moment, and workflow-driven purchase behavior. Until then, Applied Electrodynamics is an exceptional hardware team attached to an unpriced round and an unvalidated commercial bridge.
