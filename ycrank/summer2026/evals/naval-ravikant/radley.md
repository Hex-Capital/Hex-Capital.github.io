# Radley -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Maryam Jahed and Seak Meng Lay know healthcare operations because they have built inside them. Jahed helped Carbon Health expand from 31 to more than 100 clinics, led products through an insurer acquisition and an FDA breakthrough designation, and built Airo Health to six-figure revenue. Lay was Carbon Health’s founding engineer and VP of Software Engineering. More importantly, their previous Radley product went live and signed $2.2 million in contracts. This is specific knowledge revealed through shipping and selling, not résumé proximity.

The full-stack model is the interesting move. Owning a radiology practice could turn AI from a feature sold into someone else’s workflow into an operating system that controls scheduling, credentialing, reporting, billing, and clinical feedback. The pending acquisition would also bring $4.3 million in annualized revenue. But “pending” is doing enormous work: Radley has not disclosed closing terms, integration milestones, proprietary workflow data, or evidence that its agents improve radiologist throughput or clinical quality.

The counterfactual is simple: if Rad AI or Sirona copied Radley’s software tomorrow, what would remain? Today, not enough. Rad AI already reaches more than 40% of U.S. health systems, Sirona has 12 million annual studies under contract, and Radiology Partners combines practice ownership with AI across more than 25 million examinations. Credentialing infrastructure, tuned agents, and acquisition playbooks could become durable assets, but future nouns are not present moats. Radley must prove that operating the practice creates proprietary learning that a software vendor or scaled incumbent cannot purchase.

The market provides room to maneuver. The immediate wedge sits inside a $5.78 billion U.S. teleradiology segment projected to grow 26.2% annually, with the broader diagnostic-imaging-services market estimated at $149.54 billion. Direct service contracts also offer cleaner value capture than an advertising or attention model. Still, the $4.3 million belongs to an unclosed acquisition, while the prior $2.2 million represents signed contracts rather than disclosed collected revenue, pricing, gross margin, or retention.

The strongest bull case is that these founders have already sold radiology software, understand scaled clinical infrastructure, and can use an acquired practice as the laboratory, customer, and proprietary-data engine simultaneously. This matches my preference for moving fragmented offline supply into an operating network, and I see no disclosed integrity, FOMO, or founder-stability red flag. Exceptional founders can justify patience around an unfinished business model. They cannot permanently substitute for leverage: Radley currently requires owned clinical labor, and no evidence shows that one radiologist, facility, or customer improves acquisition or utility for another.

My base case is that within 36 months Radley reaches $8 million to $15 million in annualized revenue through one or two acquired practices but remains a well-executed, tech-enabled services operator rather than a compounding network. I would not invest my own money yet. The flip condition is concrete: close the first acquisition and demonstrate for two consecutive quarters that the agents increase studies per radiologist by at least 30% without worsening critical-miss rates, then reproduce that result in a second unaffiliated practice.
