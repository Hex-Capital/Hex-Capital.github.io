# Sinter -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Oliver Johnson has operated a print farm. What happened when an order could not be made on time? That is the story I want from Sinter. The dossier gives me relevant experience, but no specific obstacle followed by a founder-built workaround and an observable result. Justin Kwon’s mechatronics and distributed-training work makes this a plausible team to attempt difficult manufacturing. It does not establish how these founders behave when something breaks. I would leave resourcefulness at the missing-evidence default rather than infer it from their résumés.

Sinter has built more than a presentation. It offers CAD-upload quoting and FDM production, with documented REST/MCP ordering interfaces. That earns technical credit. But the important distinction is between the product available today and the manufacturing advance being proposed. Polymer tooling and fill, pack, and warp simulation remain “coming soon”; there are no published mold-life or repeatability results ([Sinter](https://www.sinter.systems/)). My pattern of backing programmable interfaces is relevant, but an ordering interface becomes interesting when people repeatedly use it to accomplish something difficult. There is no evidence of that yet. Nor has Sinter demonstrated the difficult physical substrate that would make the interface unusually valuable.

The May 27 announcement reported onboarding more than 150 printers nationally, including over 20 around NYC. Recruiting that supply took some action. It does not tell us whether a hardware team ordered a part, received it, and came back for another ([company announcement](https://www.reddit.com/r/hwstartups/comments/1tpb0hg/nyc_pilot_on_demand_same_hour_manufacturing/)). Realized revenue is undisclosed. I would therefore keep demand at its default, without treating the missing revenue as rejection. The move from a printer marketplace to in-house manufacturing could be an important discovery: perhaps owning production was necessary to deliver reliable speed. But the dossier never identifies the observation behind the change. A changed website is insufficient evidence of learning, so adaptability also stays at default.

The strongest case for investing is that these founders may already understand the ugly details that an outsider would underestimate. Johnson has operated printing workflows, Kwon has worked on physical automation, and they reportedly have built projects together since age 13. That shared history addresses the concern about cofounders assembled for an application. Their current service could also teach them which parts justify developing better tooling. But prior experience does not establish that Sinter originated in a particular problem they personally encountered; that criterion remains at default. And Xometry already supplies automated pricing and injection-molding procurement. The opportunity would have to come from making a particular class of parts substantially faster or better, rather than merely making those parts easier to order.

I would pass today. This is a team with relevant experience and an available initial service, but my central founder-action gate is unmet. The exceptions I make for an unusually convincing working prototype or validated scientific result do not bridge that gap: ordinary FDM availability does not validate the proposed polymer-tooling advance. **My flip condition is one documented customer episode in which a named tooling obstacle led the founders to ship a workaround within two weeks, deliver parts meeting agreed specifications, and win a paid repeat order.** That would connect founder behavior, technical execution, and real need without asking a quotation page to prove all three.
