# Faraday -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Better models could let manufacturers buy execution across orders, inventory, and production rather than another interface to fragmented systems. For Faraday, the controlling economic variable is the cost of deploying each additional customer workflow: does its shared runtime make implementations progressively reusable, or does every factory require another integration project? The dossier establishes the coordination problem but supplies no deployment-cost curve or customer savings against which to test that belief. I would pass today because this distinction determines whether Faraday becomes a software company with expanding margins or a services business with useful agents.

The strongest evidence is the company’s synthetic benchmark: 96.2 versus 71.7 for the GPT-5.5 baseline, at 2.6 times lower run cost, across 74 episodes and 33 scenario families. That is a measurable technical and economic improvement. It supports the proposition that industrial harnesses add value beyond the underlying model. But these are company-reported results on synthetic enterprise records. Lower inference cost does not establish lower implementation cost, fewer production exceptions, or enough labor savings to support attractive subscription pricing.

Faraday’s architecture is more compelling than a document summarizer. Its platform describes records, dependencies, permissions, approvals, and executable workflows coordinated through a shared runtime. Those are places where stronger models could improve task completion while Faraday retains responsibility for execution. This fits my preference for owning operational work across a technology transition. Customer-specific correction histories could strengthen that position, but their scale and reproduction cost are unestablished. I give credit for the described workflow architecture; I do not yet underwrite a proprietary-data moat.

The strongest objection to passing is that this may be precisely the moment to back the infrastructure before customer demand becomes publicly visible. Oscar Rangel built factory systems at Worthington Steel and shipped medical devices at Stryker; Datta Kaligotla brings enterprise engineering and founding-engineer experience at Handshake AI Enterprise. That combination makes the integration thesis credible. However, these biographies do not establish prior-founder learning or costly personal commitment, and the research-stage exception requires unusually strong frontier research and a credible capital path. A synthetic harness benchmark and reported $500K YC financing do not establish that exception.

Demand remains unknown. Fabtech conversations demonstrate prospecting, while 57 LinkedIn followers and launch coverage establish neither repeat usage nor willingness to pay. I apply the missing-demand default without treating pre-seed silence as failed adoption. Meanwhile, Didero’s $30M Series A in February 2026 establishes a funded entrant already executing procurement workflows inside enterprise systems. Faraday’s broader scope becomes an advantage only if one initial workflow creates reusable distribution into the next; otherwise breadth increases implementation burden.

My flip condition is one verified deployment cohort: three paying manufacturers running the same initial workflow for 90 days, with customer-confirmed labor savings exceeding fees and fewer implementation hours for each successive deployment. That would directly test the market mechanism while supplying the missing product pull. Until then, Faraday has credible workflow technology and relevant industrial experience, but insufficient evidence that the market will amplify either into a repeatable business.
