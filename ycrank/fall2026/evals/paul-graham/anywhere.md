# Anywhere -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most revealing fact about Anywhere is the user who could not receive a verification code. Here is a concrete obstacle between someone wanting the product and using it. What did the founders do next? The dossier does not say. That missing sequence matters more to me than the description of a “living map.” I want to see the founder notice the failure, reach the user, and get them through it. I cannot infer that behavior from a résumé, though I cannot infer passivity from one unresolved public report either.

Charly Delaroche has unusually relevant experience. He led mapping work at Zenly and rebuilt Life360’s map experience. Anywhere also exists as an iPhone app: the review about incomplete customized map coverage provides some independent evidence that users encountered a functioning map. This is considerably better than a diagram. But the technical evidence has limits. The privacy policy describes agent access and map editing; the November 5 Claude connector listing does not establish that those capabilities work reliably in users’ hands. I give credit for shipped software without treating the documented architecture as a measured technical breakthrough.

The strongest case for investing is that Delaroche understands something about maps that a newcomer would miss. A map containing your own history could become useful context for an assistant, rather than another collection of restaurant pins. That fits my unstated preference for programmable tools whose users discover applications the founders did not prescribe. But Anywhere has not yet supplied the decisive part of that pattern: developers or consumers repeatedly using the primitive. Mapstr already saves places; Wanderlog already organizes trips. Agent access becomes a meaningful distinction when someone relies on it to accomplish a specific job.

The demand evidence cannot resolve this. Anywhere launched publicly on November 1, just four days before the research date. Its 2.0/5 rating comes from only four ratings. That is enough to investigate individual failures and far too little to pronounce the product unwanted. Equally, a free download and a connector listing establish availability, not pull. I would leave demand at the untested default. No paid pricing is identified, so the immediate question is whether a small group returns voluntarily; a revenue forecast would be premature.

There are two other tempting inferences I would refuse to make. Fenn’s August group-planning product and Anywhere’s November introduction do not establish an evidence-driven pivot: the dossier supplies neither the rejected hypothesis nor the observation that changed the route. And Delaroche’s mapping career establishes domain familiarity without establishing the particular personal frustration that produced Anywhere. Location permissions and external integrations could contain useful, neglected work, but listing those burdens does not show that the founders have mastered them. Nor does the public launch establish my launch-theater red flag; there is no evidence that the team considers launching its distribution strategy.

I would pass on the evidence available. Anywhere has a working product and a credible builder, but it misses my resourcefulness gate. The known exceptions do not bridge that gap: there is no unmistakable user pull, independently validated technical result, or documented rapid correction strong enough to substitute for the missing founder evidence. My flip condition is one dated learning loop: the founders identify an onboarding or planning obstacle, ship a workaround within two weeks, and show five users in one specific niche returning weekly for four consecutive weeks afterward. That would connect the software to both founder behavior and real need.
