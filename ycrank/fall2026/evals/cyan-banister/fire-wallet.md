# Fire Wallet -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a small merchant letting software collect invoices, convert currencies, and pay suppliers without exposing its entire business to everyone along the payment path. Fire Wallet’s agent sub-wallets point toward that world. But getting there requires private settlement, recoverable self-custody, and delegated spending that preserves the owner’s control. The dossier does not establish those transitions. An Ethereum wallet is a present-day artifact; whether it contains the beginnings of that future depends on what its privacy and recovery actually do.

Why does a merchant’s payment take a particular route? Who gets paid for choosing it? Those are good questions, and Fire asks them concretely. Its terms exclude spreads and payment for order flow, permit disclosed interface fees, and promise direct wallet receipt. That could align the product with the person paying. But there is no numerical fee schedule or completed payment comparison showing how much control or money returns to the merchant. MoonPay and other providers can still freeze, delay, or reject transactions. Fire changes the interface to those gatekeepers; the evidence does not yet show how much of their power it removes.

Joshua Goldbard is the strongest reason to keep looking. Founding MobileCoin establishes experience building inside the exact system Fire wants to change, and his prior working relationship with Manuel gives the team some operating continuity. Michael Divine brings a different design background. This resembles the operator-pain pattern I like, but the dossier does not identify the particular wound that became Fire’s specification. Nor does it document a sequence of independent experiments or unusually strong recruiting. I credit the prior company, without treating Fire’s reported $100M-plus fundraising and unspecified acquisition as proof that this product will work.

The strongest objection to passing is that a merchant may already be pulling the company toward something useful. A prospect handling approximately $1.5M monthly reportedly introduced ten more businesses; Trustboop signed an LOI. Those introductions cost reputation and deserve attention. There is also a downloadable iPhone app. Still, the roughly $100M annual GMV opportunity belongs to prospective customers, and three App Store ratings do not establish repeated financial use. I can back a pre-revenue team when a working technical artifact and a long record of obsession carry the conviction. Here, those exceptions do not supply the missing product evidence.

The privacy promise is where my own money stops. Recovery phrases in the App Store, email/PIN recovery on the homepage, and distributed HSM key management in the terms leave materially different pictures of who can recover or authorize funds. MoonPay/SumSub identity checks and permanently public transaction metadata establish limits that “privacy-first” must explain precisely. These disclosures do not prove a severe privacy violation. They do prevent me from resolving my consent and custody gate. And although the HSM description sounds like concealed infrastructure, the dossier establishes neither a controlled scarce asset nor a dependency that several downstream businesses must use.

I pass at **42/100**. Fire Wallet has relevant builders and a sensible economic grievance, but I cannot yet inhabit the promised product with confidence. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently observed, 90-day paid merchant pilot demonstrating repeated invoice settlement, lower total costs against the merchant’s previous route, and verified privacy and recovery behavior matching Fire’s claims. That would connect the appealing future to something a business actually trusts with its money.
