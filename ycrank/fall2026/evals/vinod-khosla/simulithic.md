# Simulithic -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Simulithic could remove the human verification bottleneck behind AI-generated software. That is a consequential possibility: faster code generation accomplishes little if engineers must still click through every release. But the dossier supplies no baseline for verification labor, no measured fraction eliminated, and no demonstrated expansion in what software teams can safely ship. I can describe the upside mechanism. I cannot yet underwrite its magnitude. Missing revenue is normal at this stage; an unquantified consequence receives my missing-evidence score.

The technical wedge is specific. Discovering application journeys could eliminate test specification itself, whereas Momentic emphasizes plain-English authoring and Playwright requires maintained scripts. Simulithic has documented execution workflows and founder-reported deployments at “several companies” as of October 15, 2026. That earns some technical credit. But QA Wolf also markets autonomous exploration, and BrowserStack already offers testing agents. The decisive question is whether Simulithic finds important failures with substantially less human work at comparable reliability. No comparative benchmark establishes that. The documented exclusion of form submission raises a particular concern: the product may explore the easy surfaces while missing the transactions that make verification expensive.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ ability to turn that wedge into a much larger system. Ryan Ning brings Toronto AI-agent research and application engineering experience; Satyam Singh brings engineering experience at Ramp and Bree. They previously built GitWrap together and report more than 100,000 developer users. That is evidence of building and reaching developers, although those users belong to another product. Combined with early Simulithic deployments, it makes an autonomous release gate a credible ambition. My actual investment pattern includes execution-heavy software; scientific novelty is not compulsory. But my exceptions require unusually persuasive founders coupled with an established enormous value pool, or evidence of a breakthrough whose consequence overwhelms missing milestones. This dossier establishes neither strongly enough. The shift from experiment prediction to verification shows a changed product direction, but without the triggering evidence it does not establish fast belief revision or risk-tuned recruiting.

I would spend the next dollars testing trustworthy coverage. Production safeguards are sensible, but safety restrictions and comprehensive exploration can pull against each other. No disclosed experiment specifies how the team will resolve that tension, what result would kill the approach, or how much the test costs. The economics remain equally open: monthly allowances of 2,000, 8,000, and 25,000 sessions describe packaging; phone sessions consuming triple the allowance suggest different resource requirements. Neither establishes cost per useful defect found, human intervention burden, or savings against existing verification. I give both dimensions their absent-evidence defaults.

I pass at 28/100. Simulithic has a plausible product mechanism, but the evidence does not yet establish a discontinuity or a proprietary learning loop. That leaves the competitive risk unresolved; it does not prove the product is merely a wrapper. My one flip condition is an independently reproducible customer benchmark showing at least 10x lower total verification cost—including compute and human intervention—at matched critical-defect recall against the customer’s existing process, covering transactional flows without unsafe production actions. That result would connect capability, consequence, and economics. Today those connections are hypotheses.
