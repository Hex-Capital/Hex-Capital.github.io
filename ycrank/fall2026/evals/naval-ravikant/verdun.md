# Verdun -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Sonny Tai and Tung Hoang have earned the right to attempt this. Tai built Actuate; Hoang built its first gun-detection model. Actuate’s reported deployment across 110,000 cameras and 15,000-plus sites gives their experience substance beyond job titles. These founders have encountered the gap between recognizing an object and delivering useful security. That is specific knowledge acquired through action. Those cameras belong to their previous company’s record, however; they establish founder competence without establishing Verdun’s traction or ownership of a durable asset.

The investment turns on what survives someone copying Verdun’s implementation. Its cascade of computer vision, multimodal reasoning, temporal analysis, and site context describes a sensible system. Hakimo already offers contextual verification and autonomous responses. The [engineering role](https://verdun.ai/careers/evals-engineer) describes operator feedback and evaluation work, but establishes neither exclusive training rights nor a measured advantage that compounds across deployments. Working with physical cameras does not itself confer a hardware moat. The crucial asset would be learning that competitors cannot purchase from the same model providers.

The strongest objection to passing is that these founders may understand how to create that asset better than an outsider can infer from public materials. Every reviewed incident could improve subsequent decisions, while their operating experience could make customers comfortable entrusting them with footage. This fits my pattern of backing founders who turn specialized knowledge into scalable output. But Verdun has not yet demonstrated the conversion. Site-specific corrections might remain site-specific labor. There is no observed customer-to-customer improvement or distribution loop. My exceptions for exceptional founders, demonstrated prototypes, or emerging networks justify tolerating missing revenue; they do not establish that Verdun already has one of those compounding mechanisms.

Commercial-property monitoring supplies an identifiable buyer and existing expenditure. The cited category estimate grows from $2.382 billion in 2024 to $5.225 billion in 2031, but an industry forecast does not establish Verdun’s route into a larger adjacent market. Direct sales inquiries and Charlie Warren’s referral solicitation provide a route to conversations, not demonstrated proprietary distribution. Pricing, contracts, and revenue remain undisclosed. Tai’s explicit recognition that ingestion and inference costs can produce negative economics is useful judgment; the cheaper-first cascade remains an unverified mitigation.

I am neutral on Verdun as an investment today. The discontinued consumer project alone does not establish repeated abandonment, and the dossier supplies no evidence of integrity problems or artificial financing pressure. My hesitation is structural: accomplished security founders can build a useful monitoring business while each additional site still requires more operational work. One result would change my decision: an independently audited deployment study showing that Verdun-owned learning from earlier customer sites reduces human review at new sites by at least 50% versus the same system without that learning, without increasing missed threats. That would turn the proposed feedback loop into an asset.
