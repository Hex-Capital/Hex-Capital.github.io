# Adside.ai -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, a small business owner might tell a machine what customers are worth, set a spending limit, and stop opening advertising dashboards altogether. Adside.ai points toward that world. But several things must happen between generating a creative and safely delegating a budget: reliable measurement, bounded spending authority, and decisions that survive changes across advertising platforms. The dossier establishes research and campaign tooling; it does not establish those transitions. I can imagine the destination. Imagination alone does not earn the check.

The founders give me a stronger reason to pay attention. Robin Choy and Guillaume Miara spent four years sharing a coworking space, built Botdog together, and sold it in February 2026. They also operated paid ads for their own software business. That is useful operating tuition: they have experienced the customer’s chore and already turned a joint project into an acquisition. Miara’s engineering work at Oscar Health adds substance beyond a credential list. This supports complementary execution and commercial competence, although the dossier does not show a longstanding obsession with advertising or a series of earlier experiments pursuing this particular problem.

Why does the chore exist? Accounts, audiences, tracking, creatives, and campaign decisions live in separate systems. Bringing them together could return meaningful time and control to a founder. Yet Adside.ai has not identified an artificial restriction or quantified incumbent rent that its architecture removes. Its own managed offering charges 10% of spend with a $3,500 monthly minimum. That may be a sensible business, but I need to understand whether software eliminates the coordination burden or whether customers continue buying someone else’s labor to manage it. Omneky’s overlapping creative and publishing capabilities make that distinction commercially consequential.

The strongest argument against my pass is that these founders may understand exactly how to use services to discover what software should automate. Adside reports 30 companies live in October 2026, and Apify’s September case study documents functioning research tooling collecting more than 133,000 ads across 6,055 YC companies. Those records are research inputs, not customers, but the product is more than a rendering. Still, “live” does not tell me whether customers repeatedly entrust budgets, approve campaigns, or return after a disappointing result. My exceptions for companies without revenue depend on unusually strong founder obsession or revealing prototype behavior. Adside’s operating history is credible; the corresponding depth of product behavior remains unestablished.

Adside also fits my pattern of repairing an unglamorous workflow. What it has not demonstrated is the scarce enabling asset underneath that workflow. Public ad libraries and reusable scraping infrastructure make development faster for competitors too. Its promise to keep customer data out of shared-model training is welcome privacy discipline, while leaving a pooled learning advantage unsupported. The named CEO and CTO provide clear operating roles; the dossier establishes neither a governance violation nor a severe privacy problem. I pass because the evidence currently supports a capable automation business more strongly than my six-year bet. One verifiable result would change my mind: a six-month paid cohort of ten unrelated businesses replacing their prior agency with Adside while requiring less than one hour of human campaign management per account each month. That would show the operating burden actually disappearing.
