# ClaimKit -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

ClaimKit could make obtaining R&D tax credits cheaper and less distracting for founders. That is useful. The investment question is how much expensive expertise disappears when the system works. The dossier gives me a $1.45 billion adjacent software market estimate, but no baseline for preparation costs, specialist hours, or businesses excluded by current economics. I cannot turn that market estimate into an orders-of-magnitude consequence. My loss would be one check; the dossier has not established an upside large enough to justify buying the option.

The product attacks a real workflow: connect engineering and accounting systems, identify qualifying work, draft substantiation, then have tax specialists review and lodge it. The critical mechanism is whether automation removes expert work or simply moves it downstream into checking machine-generated material. Boast, Neo.Tax, and Onshore already combine automation with tax expertise. Australian and US coverage offers a commercial distinction, but supplies no measured technical discontinuity. Edexia’s testimonial about lower cost and faster completion is encouraging customer evidence; it does not establish a 5–10x advantage against a named alternative. Nor does the dossier establish that ClaimKit is merely a model wrapper. The technical question remains open.

The strongest argument against my pass is that these founders may have found the right machinery for building an expertise business. Huang performed R&D tax work at KPMG; Mahon brings engineering experience from Google, Akuna, and IMC. Director of Tax Edward See adds former BDO experience. Those are complementary capabilities around the actual delivery risk. Retiring the Grants module on April 22, 2026 demonstrates willingness to narrow the product, although the evidence that prompted that decision is undisclosed. Add the company-reported 80+ customers within six months, and this becomes a credible execution story. My unstated preference for repeatable expert workflows and willingness to back execution-heavy financial businesses both argue against dismissing it because it lacks a scientific invention.

But customer acquisition and risk removal are different achievements. ClaimKit reports a 100% claim success rate without a denominator or measurement period, while its own guidance correctly notes that receiving a refund does not end review exposure. The existential question is whether its evidence remains defensible while specialist effort per claim falls. No disclosed experiment supplies a threshold, cost, timing, and stop-or-change response for that question. Likewise, the upfront software fee and minimum-or-percentage service fee explain how ClaimKit charges, not what delivery costs. The team’s historical $100 million of credits claimed is neither current-product throughput nor ClaimKit revenue. R&D credits being government incentives does not itself imply that ClaimKit’s operating economics require subsidy; those economics are simply undisclosed.

I would pass with my own money. I find no evidence of exit fixation, harmful-category exposure, or refusal to change direction. The relevant red-flag test is whether AI drafting produces proprietary capability or leaves differentiation in implementation and distribution; the dossier does not resolve it. I also checked the exceptions: an exceptional instigator addressing a quantified giant value pool, or demonstrated exponential capability, can outweigh missing early milestones and economics. ClaimKit has credible domain operators, but neither override is established here. One verifiable result would reopen my decision: an independently assessed, representative US/Australia comparison against conventional specialist preparation showing at least 10x lower fully loaded preparation cost, including human review, with equivalent substantiation quality. That would begin to demonstrate expertise becoming radically cheaper.
