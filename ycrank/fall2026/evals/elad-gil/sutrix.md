# Sutrix -- Elad Gil Evaluation

LLMs are lowering the cost of clinical chart abstraction; research teams now buy prepared, auditable datasets; Sutrix becomes a large company if approved studies per reviewer-hour increase as deployments accumulate. That is the controlling variable. Extracting records faster opens the market, but reusable preparation work determines whether the founders build scalable software or a research-services firm. Sutrix’s individually scoped engagements establish a commercial starting point, while leaving that distinction unresolved.

The economic opening is substantial. Sutrix reports an **80% reduction in preparation time**, from months to weeks, and Layer Health separately reports **more than 65% less abstraction time** at Froedtert in March 2025. These are different workflows and cannot be treated as equivalent benchmarks. Together, however, they support a specific technology shift: work previously constrained by manual chart review can become materially cheaper. Sutrix’s figure remains company-reported, and elapsed-time savings do not establish gross margins after reconciliation, validation, and deployment labor.

Customers have already paid for the outcome. The dossier identifies **10+ paying institutions**, **15+ labs**, **50+ studies**, and **$150K+ cumulative revenue** reported before broader software access. Those are unusually informative pre-seed demand signals, even though they come primarily from company and founder accounts. I would count the revenue as payment for research preparation, without relabeling it recurring software revenue. The RRIPG testimonial adds a named customer, but its claimed **28% improvement in follow-up treatment plans** lacks a published methodology and contributes little to my conviction.

The product architecture gives Sutrix a credible route beyond selling labor. Source-linked fields, approved mappings, reusable study definitions, reconciliation histories, and exception routing preserve work around the model. Better extraction models should improve this system while leaving researchers needing provenance and approval controls. That fits my pattern of backing operational infrastructure around a technology discontinuity. The limitation is concrete: FHIR connections currently involve sandboxes, and institutional deployment remains scoped work. Existing workflow state earns credit; production integrations on a roadmap do not.

The strongest objection is that Sutrix has demonstrated demand for expert-delivered projects while software competitors capture the scalable economics. Layer, Dyania, and Carta already address overlapping clinical abstraction workflows. Individually negotiated engagements, customer verification requirements, and PHI onboarding could keep human effort proportional to revenue. That activates my service-heavy economics red flag. Recorded reviewer decisions are useful product assets, but no reproduction-cost evidence establishes a proprietary-data moat. The founders offer a credible response: Ritvik has operated inside clinical research, Supratik previously founded and exited Kairo, and they report four years of building together. The prior exit supports startup learning; neither its size nor financial desperation is established. Their backgrounds also do not qualify Sutrix for a frontier-research or scarce-model-talent exception if the workflow economics fail.

I would make a small personal investment at this stage. Paying demand and retained workflow state outweigh the unresolved delivery model, with conviction limited by the weakest dimension: repeatable market economics. My single verifiable flip condition is a cohort review showing that **human delivery hours per approved study fail to decline between institutions’ first and subsequent paid studies, including amortized onboarding effort**. If reuse does not improve that ratio, I would withdraw the scalable-software thesis. Sutrix has earned investment through delivered customer value; its next proof is that delivering the next study gets easier.
