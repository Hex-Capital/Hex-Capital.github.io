# Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest asset is not the résumé; it is the accumulated ability to make difficult physical systems work. Bilgehan Avser led antenna technology across four iPhone generations. Brian Huppi shipped multitouch hardware and the first Nest thermostat. Paul Leutheuser worked on Touch ID haptics and IPX8 sealing, while George McLean built semiconductor imagers, retinal implants, and optical sensors. The four founders have worked together for more than eight years. They have not publicly proven wall imaging in the field, but their specific knowledge sits unusually close to the problem. This is credible action, not credential theater.

WaveSight also survives the first copy test. A competitor can reproduce an interface more easily than a radio architecture that reconstructs hidden objects from across a room. Applied Electrodynamics says it has built the hardware and reconstruction algorithms across sheetrock, tile, marble, quartz, brick, and insulation. But the claimed 1 mm accuracy has no independent benchmark, specification sheet, or field-validation dataset. The cited 250-plus patents mostly describe team history; the dossier does not establish that the company owns them. Hardware makes the advantage possible. It does not prove the advantage exists.

The opposing case is sharp: this could be an exceptional hardware team standing in front of a merely adequate market. Vayyar already sells a $199.95 wall scanner and has raised more than $300 million. Camero-Tech already serves tactical through-wall imaging. The measured through-wall-radar market was only $134 million in 2025. Construction’s claimed $20 billion of avoidable rework is a problem statistic, not evidence that customers will buy this device. Manufacturing inspection, cargo screening, and defense may expand the option, but they require different validation and sales systems. Adjacent markets are real only when the same capability crosses the boundary without rebuilding the company.

The business currently accumulates expertise, not network value. Every contractor or defense customer appears to require direct selling, manufactured hardware, validation, and support. No user makes WaveSight more useful or easier to distribute to the next user. A proprietary dataset could eventually compound across deployments, but the dossier identifies neither deployments nor verified scan labels. This misses the permissionless pattern while fitting the more recent hard-technology and infrastructure pattern. The latter matters more at pre-seed, but it does not eliminate the scaling burden.

There is also no demonstrated value capture. Preorder registration has no disclosed price, unit count, or paid commitment; hundreds of expo conversations and 148 LinkedIn followers are attention, not transactions. I see none of the stated red flags around integrity, artificial deadlines, founder fragility, or adverse treatment of stakeholders. The relevant known-exception logic would allow exceptional founder-specific knowledge to outweigh early commercial ambiguity, but it cannot also excuse an unvalidated technical claim, uncertain market breadth, and absent pricing evidence simultaneously.

I would remain neutral and wait. The founders make the option credible; the missing field proof prevents conviction. One verifiable flip condition is at least $1 million in annualized revenue from paid construction deployments that independently confirm near-millimeter accuracy across multiple common wall assemblies. That result would prove the technical substrate, the buyer, and direct value capture together. Until then, Applied Electrodynamics is a compelling instrument searching for evidence that it is a company.
