# hardware intelligence -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Rishov Sarkar built AMD AI Engine simulators, Siemens EDA compiler flows, and LightningSim before founding hardware intelligence with his college roommate of nearly a decade. That makes hardware intelligence a company with the strongest possible co-founder configuration and the weakest evidence of animal resourcefulness. The founders look like hackers who found a real schlep. They have not yet shown the cereal-box moment proving they will crawl through broken glass to sell it.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for co-founder configuration: two brothers who had already built together, understood one another’s velocity, and could divide the work without dividing the company. Hardware intelligence has the same load-bearing mechanism in Anand and Sarkar, who met in a Georgia Tech freshman dorm and roomed together throughout college. Hardware intelligence diverges because the Collisons had already shipped and sold Auctomatic before Stripe, while Anand and Sarkar have no comparable shared commercial artifact. But ten years of friendship plus complementary agent and chip-tooling experience is about as good as the configuration gets.

**Kiko** (2005) exposed the low-rung failure mechanism in resourcefulness: shipping software is insufficient if determination eventually points toward an exit instead of users. Hardware intelligence has shipped Wave and is directly soliciting ASIC and FPGA engineers, so hardware intelligence is not a deck-first AI wrapper. But booked demos are intentions, not tracks left by an animal. There is no paid pilot, strange distribution stunt, user rescued at midnight, or customer whose “no” Anand converted into a “yes.” Neither the Airbnb exception nor the Loopt exception applies yet: the dossier contains neither an outrageous hustle story nor evidence that force of personality overrides weak validation.

Synopsys, Cadence, and Siemens make debugging multi-gigabyte waveforms a six-figure-license schlep, and hardware intelligence compounds its advantage only by embracing more of that unpleasantness. Over 18 months, hardware intelligence must enter private simulation environments, handle RTL that customers will not put in a public cloud, reproduce intermittent failures, and prove proposed fixes through hours-long reruns. Each integration can become both switching cost and debugging knowledge. Avoiding that manual work would turn Wave into another terminal interface; doing it founder-mode could make the schlep itself the asset, as compliance did for **Coinbase** (2012).

Athreya Anand worked at AWS Inferentia and Tesla, while Sarkar spent his Ph.D. building chip simulators and compiler flows. That is strong founder-problem proximity, but hardware intelligence has not established that Wave arose from a debugging failure Anand or Sarkar personally suffered and first solved for themselves. The score would flip into the top tier if hardware intelligence showed that LightningSim or an internal AMD, Siemens, Tesla, or Inferentia workflow produced the original tool before a market was chosen. Without that fact, hardware intelligence may be an expert idea rather than an organic one. Those are correlated, but they are not identical.

LightningSim is hardware intelligence’s closest equivalent to the pre-company artifact behind **Dropbox** (2007). Sarkar maintains a non-trivial simulator rather than merely holding an ECE Ph.D., and Anand’s telegram-game-hack supplies a smaller but real hacker trail. Hardware intelligence therefore passes the unstated genealogy test: these are builders, not two managers planning to hire builders after funding. The modest GitHub star counts—nine for Sarkar’s top personal repository and seven for Anand’s—matter less than the technical specificity of simulators, LLVM flows, and terminal-native interaction.

ChipAgents has raised $74 million and already reports NVIDIA and Ambiq collaborations, which makes a narrow wedge more important than a broad story. Within 36 months, hardware intelligence will remain independent and reach $3 million to $7 million in ARR from fewer than 25 semiconductor customers, with waveform debugging still producing most of its revenue. Hardware intelligence can reach that outcome because a technically intimate pair can win a small number of expensive deployments without winning the whole EDA market. The ceiling beyond that depends on whether Wave becomes the place engineers debug chips or merely a feature incumbents copy.

Synopsys introduced an LLM-based Copilot in 2023, and the strongest bear case is that hardware intelligence is a technically elegant feature trapped between incumbent bundling and ChipAgents’ capitalization. Hardware intelligence has no published verification benchmark, no revenue, no named pilot, and no present moat; incorrect root-cause analysis in chip verification is much worse than a bad autocomplete suggestion. The evidence that wins for now is the combination of a shipped Wave product, Sarkar’s simulator genealogy, the founders’ decade-long relationship, and a schlep incumbents have made expensive and unpleasant. I would flip to Pass if hardware intelligence cannot produce three paying chip-design organizations with repeat weekly Wave usage by January 2027.
