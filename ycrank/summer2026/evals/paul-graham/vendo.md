# Vendo -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when Vendo met resistance. Zahzah and Helal have shipped a real system: 3,539 commits, nightly installation tests against 16 open-source applications, and a package receiving 1,220 weekly downloads. But the dossier never names a broken integration, rejected sale, or unsafe agent action and then shows what the founders changed, how quickly they changed it, and whether the result worked. This is evidence of output, not yet of resourcefulness under pressure. I cannot infer the latter from the former.

The technical proof is Vendo’s strongest evidence. The product scans an application’s APIs, components, permissions, and theme, then creates persistent views, actions, and scheduled automations inside a sandbox. The Apache-2.0 repository has 377 stars and 51 forks. This is much more than a diagram for an “AI interface” company. It is also one of the patterns I like: a programmable substrate that lets technical users construct things the founders did not enumerate. The material uncertainty is whether users are actually constructing those things. Downloads and forks prove inspection and experimentation; they do not prove that a product team has embedded Vendo, kept it running, and allowed customers to execute consequential workflows through it.

The product transition is potentially encouraging. The team moved from one-click deployment and customization of open-source business software to letting users build micro-apps inside existing products. The underlying goal—making software adaptable without waiting for its vendor—appears stable while the route changed. That looks like running-back behavior, but only dimly. There is no customer observation, failed hypothesis, or dated before-and-after sequence explaining the change. The continuing Aisle Technologies legal identity and the unfinished governing-law clause make the transition look operationally incomplete as well as strategically unexplained.

The strongest case for investing is that two founders who have known each other since kindergarten, previously built Aisle together, and possess serious systems experience have already assembled a difficult artifact before raising permission. Vendo also offers to perform initial integrations itself, which is the right instinct: integrations, permissions, approvals, audit records, and sandboxing are precisely the schlep that could teach the founders what the eventual platform must become. The $49 Pro and $499 Teams plans give that learning a plausible economic path. If even a few of the 1,220 weekly downloads belong to product teams whose customers repeatedly build useful internal workflows, the current public evidence understates the company.

But there is no named user niche pulling the product back. There is no reported revenue, retained deployment, generated workflow count, or customer willing to tolerate the crude version. This matters especially because CopilotKit already offers authenticated tools, approvals, shared state, generative UI, and self-hosting. Without user behavior, Vendo’s distinction from adjacent stacks remains a description rather than a discovered wedge. The founders’ prior AI-product work does not establish that this problem arose from their own repeated need, and neither founder has documented enterprise-selling experience. No known-exception pattern overrides these gaps: the working product is real, but neither unmistakable demand nor independently validated performance replaces the failed resourcefulness gate.

I would not write the check yet. My one flip condition is three named B2B products still running customer-built Vendo workflows after eight weeks, accompanied by one dated integration or safety blocker that the founders themselves solved within seven days. That single result would show the narrow well, the ugly work, and the founders’ response to resistance. Until then, Vendo is an impressive technical object whose decisive users and decisive founder episode are both missing.
