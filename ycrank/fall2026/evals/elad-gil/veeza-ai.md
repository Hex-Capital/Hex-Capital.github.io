# Veeza AI -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Computer-use models let Veeza AI sell completed visa paperwork to MENA travelers rather than instructions for doing it themselves. The economic variable is contribution profit per completed paid application after human review, failed booking attempts, refunds, and acquisition costs. At EGP 1,500 for Schengen applications and EGP 4,000 for UK/US applications, automation could turn an agent business into a scalable software business. The dossier establishes the prices but does not establish that economic transformation. I would not write a personal check today.

The important constraint is that Veeza automates paperwork while governments control appointment supply. Its October 13 terms explicitly acknowledge unavailable appointments and portal changes, including a refund exception when an appointment cannot be booked. Better form completion therefore does not necessarily produce more billable outcomes. The reported $4.05 billion tourist-visa-services market measures spending across a broader geography; it does not establish how much profitable demand this particular operating model can capture.

There is a credible technology change underneath the product. Computer-use benchmark performance rose from 42.2% to 61.4% between May and September 2025. That supports feasibility, although benchmark gains cannot be translated directly into Veeza’s labor savings or application accuracy. More encouragingly, the product navigates portals, checks documents, fills forms, and books appointments with human review. Those are actions inside a consequential workflow. Stronger models should improve execution, and stored documents provide continuity across applications. Neither fact yet establishes costly-to-reproduce workflow state or a durable advantage over competitors.

The strongest case against waiting is that customers are already finding and paying Veeza. Company-reported users increased from 1,000 to more than 7,000 since joining YC; October reporting describes paying customers, roughly 400 WhatsApp community members, and 10,000 feedback messages. Those signals matter at pre-seed. Aly Moursy also brings personal Egyptian-passport experience and prior company-building experience at Dlvvr, rather than discovering the problem through market research alone. This is meaningful founder evidence, although the dossier does not document a prior exit or specific lessons carried into Veeza. User totals and feedback volume establish interest more clearly than they establish paid completion or repeat demand.

Competition makes the missing economics decisive. Atlys already combines AI assistance with MakeMyTrip distribution, while iVisa/HelloGov combines automation and specialists. Veeza’s Arabic/English service and MENA focus provide a plausible entry market, but travel-agency introductions remain prospective distribution. My pattern of backing several layers of a technology transition supports examining this opportunity; it does not establish which visa provider captures the value. The thin-output-wrapper red flag does not fit the described product. The service-margin concern remains unresolved, and neither the frontier-research exception nor the exception for demonstrated economic reconstruction supplies a reason to override it.

I would keep Veeza AI under observation. My single flip condition is a verifiable, three-month paid-application cohort showing positive contribution profit after acquisition, human handling, retries, and refunds, with human minutes per completed application declining across successive months. That would connect the model improvement to business economics. Until then, the evidence supports a useful regional service more strongly than a compounding software company.
