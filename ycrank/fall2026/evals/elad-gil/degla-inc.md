# Degla Inc -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The March 2026 multi-drone waiver process creates an opening for public-safety agencies to buy autonomous fleet operations. For Degla Inc, the controlling variable is completed missions per paid operator, subject to acceptable safety performance. If every additional aircraft still requires another person supervising exceptions, conversational control improves the interface without changing the business economics. The dossier establishes a plausible product direction, but supplies no measured staffing ratio, intervention rate, or cost per completed mission. I cannot yet underwrite the market’s central proposition.

The institutional change is real enough to deserve credit: Skydio reports approvals for 12 additional public-safety agencies. But that opening also strengthens an established competitor already selling multi-drone operations and reporting annual revenue in the hundreds of millions. Degla enters a market where buyers and suppliers are already forming relationships. The broader $28 billion commercial-drone estimate does little to establish its obtainable market. The sharper question is which operational requirement Degla can satisfy materially better than the existing fleet-control products.

The company-reported San Diego demonstration is the most useful product evidence. Voice-commanded vehicle search and tracking on a VTOL aircraft, alongside reported onboard inference, goes beyond generating a flight-plan document. Better models could improve mission execution while Degla retains responsibility for coordinating the aircraft. That supports some workflow durability. It does not yet establish persistent customer integrations, autonomous recovery, or the complete deployment loop; docking remains scheduled for Q1 2027. FBI, CBP, and DIU representatives observing the demonstration are potential distribution contacts, not demonstrated demand. With no documented customer usage or commercial commitment, product-market pull receives the absent-evidence default.

The strongest bull case is that Aziz Hanafi can turn this technical integration into an operational advantage before fleet architectures settle. His autonomous-systems work at InstaDeep and MIT research provide relevant context. More specifically, building UChallengeMe to a reported 10,000-plus users before closing it and selling its codebase establishes prior startup experience, although the undisclosed terms do not establish a meaningful financial exit. Combined with the flight demonstration, this makes Degla a credible engineering bet. It also fits my pattern of backing software around physical-industry transitions. The unresolved issue is whether that software changes physical economics.

I would pass on Degla Inc today. The physical-business exception requires evidence of changed unit economics; the dossier provides none yet. The research-stage exception requires a rare, documented technical advantage and a credible capital path sufficient to override absent demand; relevant credentials and one demonstration do not establish that combination. Nor would I assume a proprietary-data advantage from future mission logs without knowing their ownership and reproduction cost. My single flip condition is a paid deployment at a named agency documenting, over 90 days, one operator completing missions with at least four aircraft, without additional safety staffing, at a lower fully loaded cost per successful mission than its incumbent alternative. That would test whether autonomy creates economic leverage and give Degla a concrete system worth financing to scale.
