# Nebula Security -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when Nebula Security met resistance. The dossier documents 1,393 validated findings, 98 public CVEs, and eight Chrome zero-days, but it never names a customer objection, technical blockage, or failed deployment followed by a founder-built workaround. Those results prove ability, not yet resourcefulness under startup constraints. I cannot infer the latter from four impressive résumés.

The technical proof is unusually strong. Public CVEs and zero-days are inspectable artifacts, not diagrams or promised milestones. The team’s $400,000-plus in Google bug bounties, kernel and browser research, Black Hat work, and 1.3K-star exploit repository make it credible that Vega’s validation layer is real. These are fierce nerds who began before permission. Their credentials matter mainly because the shipped results confirm them.

What I cannot yet see is who wants the crude product now. An initial release to YC companies followed by an enterprise private beta identifies a plausible niche, but the dossier reports no named customer, repeated scans, payment, retention, or repository expansion. GitHub stars measure interest among security researchers; they do not show that an engineering leader will entrust proprietary code to Vega. Metered pricing and enterprise plans establish a business model, not demand.

The founders did encounter this problem through their work. Microsoft security, kernel fuzzing, exploit development, CTF competition, and vulnerability research are considerably better origins than noticing that application security is a growing market. They have also chosen genuine schlep: reproducing exploits, suppressing false positives, handling sensitive repositories, producing patches, and combining automation with human audits. But the dossier shows only one product route. There is no dated rejected hypothesis or evidence-driven change, so I cannot yet tell whether the team runs like a running back or merely attacks the first route with great technical force.

The strongest bull case resembles **Bindwell**: a deeply technical team supplies numerical, real-world proof before investors can grant permission. The material difference is that Bindwell’s laboratory result tested its central commercial hypothesis, while Nebula’s CVEs establish vulnerability-discovery skill without establishing customer pull for continuous repository scanning. That distinction stings because Codex Security, Microsoft MDASH, XBOW, RunSybil, and Depthfirst can converge on the product. If customers do not pull Vega back, the technically successful outcome may be an excellent audit shop rather than an equity-scale software company.

I would not write the check yet. My August 2029 base case is that Nebula remains technically respected but becomes primarily a security-audit and research business below $3 million ARR, rather than a dominant continuous-scanning platform. One result would flip me: a named customer whose concrete deployment failure prompted a shipped workaround within weeks, followed by at least three months of repeated paid scans and expansion to additional repositories. That would convert the team’s unquestionable technical force into evidence that it can build a startup.
