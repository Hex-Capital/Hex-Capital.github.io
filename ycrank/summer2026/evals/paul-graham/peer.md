# Peer -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The fact that matters is not that Peer changed ideas. It is that the dossier never says what broke. On May 27, 2026, Peer was building screen-recorded workflow automation; by August it was a freight brokerage ([NEIA](https://neiacademy.org/neia-alum-accepted-into-y-combinator/); [YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/peer)). That is a fast route change, but there is no rejected hypothesis, customer observation, or shipped freight workaround connecting the two. A pivot without its cause is not evidence of resourcefulness. It is merely two nouns with dates.

The founders have built real things before. Aaron Zhang worked on trucking voice agents at Waylens, and Felix Peng shipped internal AI tools used by more than 250 LADWP employees ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/peer)). Their shared Harvey Mudd history, accelerator work, and Google DeepMind hackathon also make them more credible than cofounders assembled for an application. But the current product has no comparable proof: no completed quote, booked load, tracking result, exception-resolution rate, broker authority, or bond. Past shipping earns partial credit. It cannot substitute for a working version of Peer.

The best fact is Zhang’s prior collision with trucking workflows. Freight brokerage contains precisely the sort of ugly work that deters programmers: phone calls, carrier selection, tracking, service failures, regulatory authority, and a $75,000 surety bond ([FMCSA](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/registration/insurance-filing-requirements)). This is a plausible founder-originated wedge into a $21.28 billion market. But schlep is valuable only when the founders actually perform it. Peer presently describes the entire quote-through-delivery loop while disclosing none of the licenses, carrier relationships, or human processes required to complete one load.

Who wants the crude product now? The dossier cannot name one shipper. It reports no revenue, shipment volume, repeat use, LOI, carrier count, or distribution method. Meanwhile FleetWorks reports 10,000 carriers and relationships with more than 15 of the top 100 U.S. brokers, while Cartage already combines automation with human escalation. Those competitors do not make Peer impossible. They make a working narrow well essential. “AI-native freight brokerage” is still a category description, not evidence that users pull this particular product back.

The strongest bull case is that this is exactly the sort of young technical team whose credentials understate its speed: prior trucking exposure, prior deployed AI software, an established cofounder relationship, and willingness to abandon a generic product for an operationally unpleasant industry. The relevant exception is Bindwell, where youth ceased to matter after the founders produced numerical technical and laboratory evidence. Peer is missing that decisive half of the mechanism. There is no measured proof strong enough to override the failed resourcefulness and user-pull gates.

I would pass today. My 36-month prediction is that by August 2029 Peer will have left principal freight brokerage for freight workflow or voice-agent software and will remain below $1 million ARR. The verifiable flip condition is 100 paid loads for at least five named shippers within 90 days, with three of those shippers returning and documented broker authority, bond, and exception outcomes. That would show the founders did not merely notice the schlep; they ran through it.
