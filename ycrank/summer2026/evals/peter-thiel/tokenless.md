# Tokenless -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Tokenless’s proposed secret is that apparent progress in reinforcement learning often measures defects in the examiner, and those defects can themselves be discovered systematically. What would prove this wrong? On previously unseen environments, its auditors would fail to find consequential errors beyond what maintainers already catch. The company reports 110 reward hacks and grading bugs across DeepSWE, Terminal-Bench, and BFCL, documented in 66 write-ups. Reproducible findings make this a technical proposition worth investigating. They do not establish how broadly it holds, or identify informed peers who reject it.

Who owns the value after the defect is fixed? Tokenless has a bounded starting customer: teams building RL environments and graders. But its published methodology and MIT-licensed grader establish accessibility rather than an exclusive position. A laboratory might adopt the findings, improve its internal checks, and stop needing the outside auditor. That possibility is unresolved; the dossier does not prove commodity economics. Equally, it supplies no demonstrated proprietary technology, exclusive data, or network effect that would justify assuming monopoly economics.

The strongest objection to passing is that publishing discoveries could be how Tokenless builds its eventual advantage. Public findings attract difficult private environments; private audits could generate trajectories unavailable to competitors; those trajectories could improve a specialized auditor. The unnamed early partners exploring domain-specific reward hacking are a beginning. This fits my interest in adversarial information systems and the infrastructure beneath AI production. But the proprietary red-teaming model remains future work. The exception I make for a verified technical breakthrough cannot rest on a proposed feedback loop: finding defects proves useful capability, while leaving ownership of the resulting market unsettled.

The founders offer more continuity than the recent repositioning suggests. They met as Berkeley undergraduates and shipped Touchy together before moving into infrastructure. That earns affirmative credit for an existing working relationship. Agarwal’s alignment research and Wang’s GPU infrastructure experience also make the technical direction intelligible. However, a Princeton PhD affiliation does not establish part-time involvement, and the dossier establishes neither everyone’s full-time status nor their economic exposure. I see no documented convenience matching, outsourced core engineering, or investor-dominated capitalization. Those red flags remain unsubstantiated.

Research publications, a leaderboard, and direct audit invitations constitute an identifiable route to technically sophisticated buyers. They have not yet demonstrated a repeatable purchasing process. Tokenless discloses no current-product price, contract value, delivery margin, or sales cycle; Touchy’s $14.99 purchase option says nothing about audit economics. A valuable research service could emerge here without producing a business capable of a power-law return. That is a limitation of the investment evidence, rather than a penalty for being pre-seed.

I would pass on writing a check today while keeping the technical work under observation. Tokenless has stronger evidence that evaluators are vulnerable than that an independent evaluator can accumulate durable power. My single flip condition is a documented renewal by a named frontier laboratory for recurring audits of proprietary environments, with that renewal attributable to materially better defect detection from Tokenless’s exclusive audit data. That would connect the demonstrated problem to a business whose advantage grows through use.
