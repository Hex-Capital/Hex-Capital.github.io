# Kiwi -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Kiwi’s possible secret is that reliable GTM agents require reproducible business state: the data, query, and human decision behind every action must survive inspection and replay. That proposition is testable. If comparable agents perform equally well without versioned snapshots, the architecture loses its justification. Melanie Chen’s report of spending 20 hours correcting agent work in one week establishes a problem worth examining. It does not establish that Kiwi has discovered something competitors disbelieve. The dossier supplies neither a named opposing belief nor a comparative result. I would keep the secret unproved.

The more consequential question is who owns the value if Kiwi is right. Versioned records, SQL traces, and historical replay could improve every agent’s reliability while remaining capabilities that several vendors supply. Clay already describes company context and learning from workflow outcomes; Gumloop and Relevance AI occupy adjacent automation workflows. Their existence does not prove Kiwi is interchangeable. But “GTM teams” is still too broad a beachhead, and the dossier identifies no exclusive data, measured switching cost, or technical advantage sufficient to establish a monopoly mechanism. A useful infrastructure layer becomes an exceptional investment only when customers cannot readily substitute another supplier.

The strongest case against passing is that Chen and Bill Qin understand the underlying constraint unusually well. Chen combined data infrastructure work at Plaid with founding engineering and post-sales work at Chalk; Qin built Chalk SQL. Kiwi could become the persistent record of business decisions while individual agents come and go. Historical approvals might then make replacing Kiwi progressively harder. This fits the operator-network pattern I favor: people emerging from a specific operating environment to build infrastructure it needs. Yet shared employment does not establish that they previously shipped together. Qin’s founder status, both participants’ full-time commitment, ownership, and working history remain unresolved. There is also no documented part-time founder, outsourced core engineering, or investor-dominated capitalization. Those are missing answers, not established red flags.

Commercially, Kiwi has a concrete starting route: introductions to GTM teams, founder demos, and invitation-only access. Consulting more than 100 companies gives that route more substance than an unexplained enterprise-sales label, but discovery interviews are not customer conversion. Order-form provisions for paid use and pilots establish contractual machinery, not realized revenue. Without actual contract values, delivery costs, or expansion behavior, I cannot construct the arithmetic for a power-law outcome. I would not penalize a Fall 2026 company for lacking a long revenue history; I also would not turn competitors’ revenue into evidence for Kiwi’s economics.

I would pass on a personal check at present. The exceptions that could override my concern require independent technical proof, persistent customer behavior, or a concrete deployment path that removes the relevant risk. None is documented here. My one verifiable flip condition is a paid customer renewing after a documented attempt to replace Kiwi with Clay fails because Kiwi’s accumulated decision history materially improves production reliability. That would begin to demonstrate that Kiwi can retain the value its architecture creates. Today, the founders have identified credible work to do; ownership of the resulting market remains unproved.
