# Wingman Law -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Wingman Law’s founders have handled legal work and sold legal software. What I cannot find is the incident that tells me how they behave when something breaks. Nicholas Le’s 700-plus prior injury cases establish experience. Sun Woo Lee’s $350,000 of legaltech sales during law school establish that she has persuaded people to buy something ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/wingman-law)). Neither supplies a named obstacle, a workaround, and the time it took to make it work. That leaves my most important founder test unanswered. It does not mean they failed it.

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders know where the bodies are buried. Le has plaintiff and insurance-defense experience; Lee combines litigation with software entrepreneurship ([team](https://www.wingmanlaw.com/our-team)). They could recognize a recurring case-management failure that a software founder interviewing lawyers would miss. Direct representation also gives Wingman control over whether its tools are actually used. Medical records, insurer correspondence, attorney supervision, and confidentiality are substantial work. This is a credible place to discover something through doing it. Their prior work earns meaningful credit for problem proximity, although the dossier does not connect the company’s origin to one specific failure they personally encountered.

But owning the workflow makes the experiment possible; it does not establish the result. Wingman describes attorney-directed agents and internal engineering, yet the dossier contains no measured deployment showing what those agents accomplish. Its public visualization uses licensed litigation data rather than its own client outcomes ([website](https://www.wingmanlaw.com/)). Meanwhile, Supio already markets agents spanning intake through resolution. The interesting question is whether Wingman learns something from representing clients that firms buying those tools cannot readily reproduce. My pattern of backing concrete technical proof or an accumulated proprietary substrate is not established here. Architecture descriptions alone leave technical proof at the absent-evidence default.

Who wants this now, and what happened after they tried it? Accident victims clearly want recoveries. Wingman’s contingency model aligns payment with that outcome, but no client counts, recoveries, or current revenue are disclosed. The founders’ requests for introductions are useful evidence of personal recruitment; branded-car advertising establishes an acquisition attempt, not demand. This does cut against the red flag of waiting for a large partner or ceremonial launch. Their operating backgrounds also argue against a manufactured “AI-for-X” idea. Still, neither those facts nor the prior software businesses establish pull for Wingman. There is likewise no documented rejected hypothesis and subsequent product change, so adaptability remains untested.

I would pass on the evidence supplied. The bull case stings because experienced practitioners may already be building a much better practice behind a thin public record. Missing public metrics are normal at this stage. But the documented exceptions to my usual gates depend on a working artifact, unmistakable user pull, or validated technical results; none is established here strongly enough to replace the missing resourcefulness evidence. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: a dated audit showing that the founders encountered a specific case-processing bottleneck, shipped a fix within weeks, and used it across ten client matters to reduce attorney hours without missed deadlines or increased errors. That would connect their genuine domain knowledge to observable founder behavior and a functioning advantage.
