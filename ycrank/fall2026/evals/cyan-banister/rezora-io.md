# Rezora IO -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can picture a plumber finishing a repair while software answers the next customer, checks availability, and commits to an appointment. Rezora IO already performs pieces of that interaction. My question is where the plumber’s control lives when every phone system and CRM can do the same thing. ServiceTitan’s native booking agent makes this concrete: voice automation can become a feature inside somebody else’s customer relationship. The dossier establishes today’s convenience, but supplies little evidence of the intervening steps that make Rezora indispensable in that future.

The founders give me a stronger reason to pay attention. Matsay built calling automation for his own real estate prospecting; that is an operating irritation turned into software. Richards previously built a watering business serving 80 clients across three towns and brings sales experience from Unit and Gynger. There is practical commercial knowledge here, along with a sensible engineering-and-sales division. I give that substantial credit. The record does not yet establish years of independent technical obsession or exceptional recruitment, but these founders have done more than attach themselves to a category.

Why does the customer need this? Someone working on a roof cannot simultaneously answer a prospective customer. Manual callbacks and answering services consume time, and delayed responses can lose business. Rezora can return control over a working day, which fits my recurring attraction to mundane workflow repair. At $289 monthly plus $0.20 per conversational minute—or the separate $589 home-services offer—the relevant question is how much interruption and paid assistance disappear. The dossier identifies the friction and beneficiary, but does not quantify the existing cost or identify an artificial restriction whose removal changes the customer’s economics.

The strongest argument against passing is that the product may already be earning its place. Rezora reports 1,000 meetings booked, and the Jose Toro testimonial describes two calls costing $0.70 that reportedly preceded $65,000 in closed deals. Prepared conversations and CRM integration could matter much more to a busy operator than access to a configurable voice platform. But the 2,400 adoption figure alternates between users and teams, and the meeting claim lacks cohort detail. I credit reported completed activity within that limitation. The $65,000 belongs to the customer; the $40,000-in-40-days figure has unresolved predecessor-agency attribution. Neither establishes Rezora’s recurring revenue.

The concealed infrastructure pattern I look for is also unproven here. Training on 60 sales books and thousands of conversations does not establish control over a scarce technical asset, particularly when customer recordings cannot be used for training. That prohibition supports data boundaries; I would not trade it away for a more attractive moat story. Documented consent and suppression requirements are similarly constructive, while conflicting AI-disclosure language leaves an important operating question unresolved. There is no evidence of severe privacy abuse or a founder-control shell game. My pre-revenue exceptions remain relevant, but they depended on a distinctive technical bridge and unusually strong founder evidence. Rezora’s missing revenue is not what stops this check.

I pass with my own money today. The team could build a useful business, but the evidence currently favors capable operators selling a workflow increasingly supplied by platforms. My flip condition is a verified twelve-month cohort of paying home-services customers that retains Rezora after gaining access to native CRM voice agents, with documented retention attributable to a permissioned workflow Rezora uniquely controls. That would show where the lasting agency—and the business—actually resides.
