# Locke -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first three numbers I want are absent: post-money valuation, competing term sheets, and the amount of genuinely scarce capital being raised. YC’s standard check is not evidence that this round is cheap, and six weeks of operation do not tell me whether investors are underwriting an unfashionable belief or chasing a $500K revenue run-rate. I am not willing to pretend financing silence is neutrality when price determines whether the upside remains available to me.

The strongest fact is real customer payment. Locke reports an annual revenue run-rate above $500K after six weeks, with clients across defense, healthcare, and GovTech. That is dramatically better than followers, integrations, or an AI demo. It suggests Parth Badhwar’s policy and startup network can convert into purchases. But the accounts are unnamed, the revenue is not separated into recurring retainers versus projects, and there is no evidence that proprietary data or embedded workflow caused the sales. Today the dark pool appears to be founder access. That can be valuable, but it may belong to Badhwar rather than compound inside Locke.

The company is joining two systems that mistrust each other: early-stage technology companies and government institutions. Badhwar has worked inside the White House and Deloitte’s policy practice and on the commercial side at Clipbook. That is credible outsider-insider positioning. The missing receipt is execution: the dossier does not identify a government contract won, a lobbying outcome delivered, a product shipped, or a prior operating result attributable to him. His résumé gets Locke into the room; the first six weeks of revenue show that someone paid. Neither yet proves that software can turn personal access into repeatable institutional throughput.

The bear case for my skepticism stings. A two-person firm reaching a $500K-plus run-rate almost immediately may already have found the base business that most AI startups merely narrate. If those clients are paying for outcomes across legislation, procurement, and proposals, the service layer could generate proprietary stakeholder and contract data while financing its own automation. The problem is that Locke publishes no retention, gross margin, pricing, accuracy, or named-customer evidence. Quorum already serves more than 2,000 organizations with overlapping policy data, AI, stakeholder management, and professional services. Without observed retention or an owned dataset driving conversion, the AI layer is an option attached to a young lobbying practice—not yet the durable asset.

The round also lacks a named venture moment. Two job openings spanning policy and technical staff describe organizational expansion, not a finite experiment. What exact truth does the capital reveal: that one lobbyist can support ten clients, that proposal automation doubles win rates, or that customers renew without Badhwar personally carrying the relationship? There is no metric, deadline, or pass/fail threshold. Delete the next financing and the economic picture is equally incomplete: $500K run-rate is meaningful, but salaries reaching $200K, human delivery capacity, and unknown margins could produce either an excellent cash-generating firm or an expensive services business wearing software language.

My 36-month base case is that Locke reaches $3M–$6M in annual revenue as a technology-enabled government-affairs firm, but remains materially dependent on policy headcount and founder relationships rather than becoming software-like infrastructure. That can be a good business; it is not yet a check I write without knowing the deal. One condition flips me: show that the initial customer cohort renewed for twelve months while Locke sustained at least 60% gross margin and doubled revenue per policy employee. That would verify that paid access is becoming an owned workflow rather than a founder-led service.
