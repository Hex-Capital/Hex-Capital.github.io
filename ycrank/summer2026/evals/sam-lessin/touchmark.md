# Touchmark -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: no proposed post-money valuation, no competing-term-sheet count, and no evidence that this is genuinely the first scarce check. YC participation is not itself auction evidence, but it is not proof of non-consensus pricing either. The capital plan is equally blank. Touchmark has a live product, but the dossier never says what one finite experiment the round funds, what result passes, or when the card flips. “Build liquidity” is not a venture moment; it is several mutually dependent outcomes disguised as one.

The product is an intellectually coherent translation layer between inference infrastructure and financial markets. Buyers prepay for named-model token blocks, receive them through an OpenAI-compatible key, and can resell unused capacity. But a marketplace is real only when somebody crosses the spread. The live GLM 5.2 and MiniMax-M3 books disclose no customer, executed volume, participant count, or completed resale. Listed prices—$3,800 and $870 per billion tokens—prove that contracts can be displayed, not that an owned workflow has caused a purchase. Without trades, the prospective price history and liquidity moat are circular: they arrive only after the market already works.

Bolgov’s mathematics-and-finance training and Revolut Wealth & Trading internship pair sensibly with Yanushevskyi’s Citadel Securities quantitative research, Lovable AI engineering, and IOI gold medal. This is the strongest part of the case. The team can plausibly speak both finance and inference, and it has shipped the current interface. But the dossier shows internships and credentials rather than a prior market, deployment, or operating result on both sides. It also cannot tell me who owns the final product, financing, and marketplace decisions between the two founders. Translation-layer potential is not yet translation-layer receipt.

The sharp bull case is that Touchmark has standardized a genuinely awkward transaction before incumbents recognize the category: named models, delivery months, funded settlement, resale, and low-friction consumption through a familiar API. Inference is shifting toward agents, and OpenRouter’s approximately $50 million annualized revenue proves buyers already accept an aggregation layer. If Touchmark establishes forward liquidity first, executed trades could become proprietary price data. But that case runs directly into $33 million-funded Ornn, $40 million-funded SF Compute, and an OpenRouter with $153 million raised. These firms already own adjacent buyer or supplier relationships and can add contract features; Touchmark has disclosed no dark pool that they cannot reach.

The economics are not underwritten. There is no take rate, revenue, gross margin, burn, runway, or route to break-even without another round. More seriously, inference prices fell roughly 600-fold from 2020 through 2026. A forward market could help customers hedge that volatility, but it can also strand them above spot while leaving Touchmark exposed to thin resale liquidity and operational obligations. My 36-month base case is that the company pivots from a liquid resale exchange into bilateral inference procurement or brokerage and remains below $1 million ARR, because service is easier to bootstrap than simultaneous liquidity across models and delivery months.

I am passing with my own money. There is no exception-grade evidence—no executed volume, repeat buyer behavior, enterprise commitment, or acquisition signal—to override the missing price and capital-plan gates. One result flips me: twelve disclosed months showing at least $1 million of settled notional, repeat transactions from five independent buyers, and positive gross profit after delivery costs. That would demonstrate that the order book is a market rather than a screen and that the team owns a workflow capable of surviving without financing the next round.
