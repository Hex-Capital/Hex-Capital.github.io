# Palisade -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Fnu Prince has worked in offensive and defensive security since age 16, which makes Palisade an organic founder-problem bet trapped inside my least favorite configuration: a solo founder. Palisade begins with a real kernel hacker encountering recurring OS vulnerabilities, not an MBA discovering a $37.2 billion market. But Palisade still asks one person to build a dangerous low-level system, earn enterprise trust, and sell it. The analytical signature is strong organic pain and weak founder configuration: the idea looks earned, but the organization looks unfinished.

**Dropbox** is the high rung for an organic idea: Drew Houston repeatedly lost his USB stick and built what he personally needed. Prince reports encountering OS vulnerabilities through professional red-team and blue-team work, so Palisade shares the load-bearing mechanism—an expert building out of recurring personal frustration. Palisade falls short of Dropbox because the dossier shows three prepared CVE demonstrations, not repeated use of Palisade inside a real fleet Prince operated. I believe the pain is organic. I do not yet know whether the exact product is.

Palisade has one founder, which is the low-rung failure mechanism here: one person eventually becomes the bottleneck at precisely the moment resourcefulness matters most. Palisade needs kernel engineering, adversarial testing, enterprise selling, and incident-level customer support; that is too many different all-night jobs for Prince alone. None of the known exceptions rescues this. **Airbnb** had three founders plus cereal-box proof, **Stripe** had two Collisons with Auctomatic behind them, and **Loopt** was an exception because Sam Altman was an animal—not because solo founding stopped being dangerous. Palisade remains an explicit red flag until Prince recruits someone formidable whom he already knows well.

Three named demonstrations—CVE-2024-1086, CVE-2025-6019, and CVE-2025-32463—put Palisade above the credential-only founder profile. Palisade has shipped an artifact before establishing revenue, and kernel-enforced mitigations are harder to fake than an AI dashboard. Over the next 18 months, however, animal resourcefulness must compound through hostile customer environments: installing agents manually, surviving false positives, writing rollback tooling at 3 a.m., and persuading the first security lead to risk a fleet. No customers, paid deployments, or unscalable acquisition stunt are disclosed. Prince has left tracks, but not yet cereal boxes.

Cisco’s Tetragon already provides eBPF observability and enforcement, while Palisade proposes AI-generated rules across entire fleets. That unpleasant combination of kernel debugging, enterprise onboarding, and liability for breaking production is genuine schlep blindness; Palisade is running toward work ordinary AI founders would avoid. The counterfactual that drops Palisade a tier is simple: if deployment reduces to wrapping Tetragon with generated policies, the schlep belongs to Cisco and customers, not Palisade. Evidence of Palisade maintaining distribution-specific compatibility, safe rollback, and low false-positive rates across two production fleets would instead establish that the schlep is proprietary.

**Stripe** is the single useful hacker parallel: the Collisons had technical genealogy before the company, and their commerce artifact made the genealogy visible. Prince’s Caltech departure at 18, security work from 16, operating-systems background, and competitive-programming education give Palisade unusually strong hacker markers. Palisade nevertheless has no public repositories, Hacker News trail, or independent technical artifact beyond its own launch claims. That contradicts my unstated pattern that hackers leave inspectable trails. Prince sounds like a hacker; Palisade has not yet shown enough code for me to use the word without qualification.

Fnu Prince’s combination of lived security pain and a one-person organization produces my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Palisade will have recruited a technical cofounder and reached $1 million to $3 million in ARR selling Linux runtime remediation to GPU-cloud and server-fleet operators, without becoming a broad cross-platform endpoint suite. Palisade can reach that narrow outcome because kernel remediation is a real schlep, while Palisade’s solo configuration makes simultaneous Windows, macOS, mobile, and enterprise expansion implausible.

Cisco’s Tetragon is also the strongest bear case: Palisade may be an AI wrapper around open-source enforcement, with no disclosed customers, revenue, benchmarks, rollback measurements, or remediation-error rates. One bad kernel rule could interrupt an entire customer fleet, and CrowdStrike, SentinelOne, Sysdig, or Cisco can distribute adjacent functionality through existing relationships. Palisade’s answer is Prince’s domain history plus working demonstrations against three named CVEs; at pre-seed, a difficult artifact can matter more than a distribution chart. I would write a small check, but Palisade flips to a pass if by April 2027 Prince has neither recruited a credible cofounder nor secured two paid production-fleet deployments with documented rollback behavior.
