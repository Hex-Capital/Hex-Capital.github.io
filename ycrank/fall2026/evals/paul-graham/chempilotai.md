# ChemPilot.ai -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Two years in a laboratory can tell me whether founders know chemistry. I want to know what happened when the chemistry refused to cooperate. ChemPilot.ai’s founders researched together at the Technion and worked with defense primes and government R&D agencies. That is useful shared history. But the dossier contains no specific failed experiment, blocked customer request, or unavailable material followed by a founder-built workaround. My most important test remains unanswered. That is missing evidence, rather than evidence that these founders give up.

The strongest reason to pursue ChemPilot is that the idea appears to have grown near actual work. Sam Cogan and Ofir Simhi studied biochemical engineering; Alon Grinberg Dana runs a computational chemistry laboratory. Their preceding defense research gives the materials problem more substance than a fashionable application of generative AI. Sole-source supplies and long development cycles could conceal work that other software founders avoid. Still, I cannot identify the particular shortage they encountered or the neglected operational burden they have already undertaken. Their proximity earns partial credit for origin, without establishing the whole wedge ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/chempilotai)).

There is also something more concrete than credentials: Grinberg Dana co-authored ARC, a public computational chemistry software project. I give that technical artifact some credit. Its 52 repository stars are neither customers nor experimental validation. ChemPilot’s proposed sequence—generation, synthesizability ranking, simulation, then synthesis—needs a result connecting its first step to its last. Which molecule did it propose, what property did the laboratory measure, and how did that compare with the existing method? My pattern of backing scientific teams after a numerical proof spike does not yet apply. The originating laboratory’s software establishes relevant building ability; it does not establish ChemPilot’s claimed discovery acceleration ([ARC](https://github.com/ReactionMechanismGenerator/ARC)).

The best objection to passing is substantial. These founders have spent two years together inside a specialist laboratory with access to defense research problems. A useful result could exist behind confidentiality restrictions. That combination could produce a company before outsiders recognize the opportunity. But the exceptions I allow require a working product, independently validated proof, or unmistakable user pull. None is documented here at that strength. The Fresh Defense-led financing reported in October 2026 establishes investor willingness. The unnamed research collaborations establish access. Neither tells me that a materials engineer returned with a second problem because the first result worked.

I would also distinguish a research relationship from dependence on a partner. The dossier does not establish the latter, so I would not impose that red flag. Nor does it show founders waiting for funding, delegating the difficult work, or rejecting contrary evidence. There is simply no documented product revision to score. The commercial question is whether each discovery teaches ChemPilot something reusable. Paid projects could become a valuable learning engine, but licensing and reusable data rights are inferred or undisclosed. Open-source alternatives make that distinction consequential: a successful engagement must leave behind more than a completed assignment.

I would pass today. ChemPilot.ai has credible problem proximity and some technical substance, but neither replaces evidence of resourcefulness or repeated demand. My single flip condition is a documented customer episode: a named defense-materials user presents a failed specification, the founders ship a workaround within weeks, laboratory testing confirms it meets the specification, and that user purchases follow-on work. That would connect the people, the chemistry, and the need in one inspectable sequence.
