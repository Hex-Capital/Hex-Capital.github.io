# Financial Datasets -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Financial Datasets has found a real bottleneck, but not yet an orders-of-magnitude consequence. Financial institutions and developers already spend inside a $34.8 billion data market, and agents need machine-readable filings rather than human-oriented documents. Yet the dossier does not quantify how faster access changes research cost, analyst capacity, market participation, or decision quality by 10x—much less 100x. One million daily API requests demonstrates use. It does not establish world-changing leverage.

The technical asset is 17,000 manually mapped issuer-specific XBRL tags, built over two years and checked through audits covering 20,000 data points across 1,000 companies and 75 sectors. That is difficult, unglamorous work. It may produce materially better fundamentals than generic SEC ingestion. But Financial Datasets supplies no measured accuracy, latency, coverage, or cost benchmark against Intrinio, Massive/Polygon, Alpha Vantage, or an internal pipeline. MCP access is distribution, not a technical discontinuity. Today this looks like disciplined data operations wrapped for agents, not a demonstrated 5–10x architecture.

The bull case stings. One founder has combined investment-banking context, engineering experience at Airbnb, Acorns, Faire, GoPro, and Sony, a 26,600-star open-source agent, 50,000 social followers, thousands of running agents, and integrations with Julius and OpenBB. A tiny team entering an established financial value pool through a platform-shaped developer wedge fits an important unstated pattern: execution-heavy fintech can become infrastructure even without scientific novelty. The reported one million daily calls would be exceptional pre-seed validation if those calls represent recurring external production workloads rather than free, internal, or concentrated usage.

But the economics are missing where they matter. The company charges $200 monthly for 100,000 standard requests while licensing price data from a third party and maintaining an expanding normalization system. There is no disclosed revenue, customer concentration, gross margin, retention, or cost per audited issuer. At one million daily requests, usage is roughly 30 million monthly calls; the dossier should make it easy to show whether that load creates a compounding data advantage and attractive revenue or merely infrastructure expense. Neither is shown. A $20 credit pack and an agent-friendly interface do not establish defensibility against public SEC data and overlapping APIs.

Capital should attack one kill risk: prove that issuer-specific normalization yields an accuracy or latency advantage customers will pay to retain. The decisive experiment is straightforward—publish a blinded benchmark against three named competitors across restatements, acquisitions, ticker changes, and operating KPIs; then show that production customers renew at healthy gross margins. Instead, the disclosed milestone record emphasizes mapping volume and audit activity without a pass/fail threshold. The unexplained transition from a 2024 synthetic-Q&A library to the current primary-data service might reflect useful belief revision, but without evidence about why the route changed, I will not award adaptability by inference.

My verifiable flip condition is $1 million of annualized recurring revenue from at least 20 unaffiliated production customers, above 70% gross margin, alongside a published benchmark showing at least 5x fewer material normalization errors than the best named API competitor. Until then, I pass. The founder may build a good financial-data company, perhaps even a valuable one. The current evidence buys an option on competent execution inside a known market, not an option on a technical cost collapse with an uncapped societal consequence.
