# Marengo -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: data-center engineers specify power, cooling, redundancy, and physical layout by stating constraints; software generates alternatives, runs simulations, and carries the winning design into construction documents. Marengo could occupy that layer. The present-day leaks are real—data-center electricity use rose 17% in 2025, five technology companies spent more than $400 billion in capex, and Marengo reports working with large builders—but the dossier does not map the transitions between today’s demonstrations and that future. “Twice the speed at half the cost” is a useful claim, not yet a causal roadmap.

The system wound is visible but blurry. Design knowledge is fragmented, reviews are iterative, and coordination is expensive. Yet Marengo has not identified who captures that rent, quantified the actual delay on a project, or shown whose agency improves. Is the customer a developer comparing sites, an engineering firm increasing throughput, or an individual engineer replacing weeks of manual iteration? Those are different businesses. A $21.93 billion services market tells me money is being spent; it does not explain why the workflow has resisted automation.

The founders are the strongest part of this bet. Emil Ares worked in physics and control systems, while Gad Marconi built the HITEN orbital-dynamics library before Marengo existed. Their April 2026 space-mission design product is a genuine first-believer artifact, and their shared Cranfield background suggests they recruited around a technical obsession rather than assembling an “AI for construction” résumé. The sting is the April-to-August switch from aerospace to data centers. That may be intelligent discovery, but it may also mean the team found a fashionable capex wave before it found its enduring problem.

The bull case resembles the pre-revenue industrial exception I am willing to make: a technical team brings simulation software into a difficult physical workflow, then obtains proofs of concept before revenue. The material difference from CosmicBrain AI is evidence quality. Marengo’s builders are unnamed, the claimed speed and cost improvements have no disclosed baseline, and there is no completed design, paid pilot, renewal, or engineer rearranging work around the product. The prior aerospace demonstration proves that the team can build; it does not prove that the current artifact survives data-center reliability requirements.

Marengo also sits beneath today’s AI-infrastructure excitement rather than presenting another visible application wrapper. That activates my concealed-infrastructure pattern. But an enabling layer must control something scarce. No proprietary validation corpus, physics engine, approval data, CAD/BIM integration, or construction-feedback loop is disclosed, while Entangl already addresses data-center errors and SWAPP writes directly into Revit and ArchiCAD. Sensitive facility designs also create a data-custody question, and the dossier supplies no limiting architecture. The CEO and CTO roles are clear, but ownership and a contractual tiebreaker remain unknown; I will not invent a governance problem, nor ignore the missing diligence.

I would not write the check today. This is a technically credible team with an intriguing artifact, but the future thesis currently belongs more to the sector than to Marengo. One condition flips me: a named data-center builder publicly validates a completed Marengo-generated design, including the comparison behind the two-times-faster and half-cost claims, and converts that work into a paid repeat engagement. That would connect founder obsession, product behavior, and a controlled learning loop rather than asking me to infer all three.
