# GodHands -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

GodHands needs to establish that observing work reveals operational judgment competitors cannot recover. Capturing steps is already a business; capturing the reasons behind exceptions might support a different one. The distinction is testable: can its playbooks resolve previously unseen support escalations without returning to the employee who supplied the knowledge? The anonymous testimonial reports undocumented escalations captured within two weeks. That supports usefulness, but does not establish the stronger proposition. The dossier names neither a prevailing belief the founders reject nor an experiment that puts their disagreement at risk.

The decisive problem is ownership of the resulting value. Scribe already markets workflow context and agents; Mimica observes workflows to train agents. GodHands’s emphasis on decisions and exceptions therefore enters territory competitors already recognize. Operations, support, and sales also describe several markets rather than one bounded beachhead. Customer-specific playbooks might eventually create switching costs, but accumulating documents does not itself establish that customers cannot migrate. This is affirmative competitive pressure, beyond the ordinary absence of a pre-seed moat.

The founders provide the strongest counterargument. Sai Vamshi Batchu and Aditya Bommakanti both worked at Graviton, and their account of a disruptive employee departure gives the problem a concrete origin. Bommakanti’s low-latency systems work and NeurIPS research make a technically ambitious implementation credible. Their existing operator relationship fits my preference for companies formed through people who already know one another. It earns modest founding-history credit; the dossier does not establish how closely they worked together, their ownership, or full-time status. Those unknowns are not evidence of misalignment. The bull case is that this team can recover tacit judgment where established vendors recover only visible procedure. But engineering credentials cannot substitute for demonstrating that difference.

Distribution is at least directed toward identifiable buyers: the launch requests introductions to operations leaders, support executives, and private-equity operating partners. Demo solicitation supplies a concrete route to conversations, although no repeatable conversion or delivery economics are documented. Private-equity operators could offer access across portfolio companies, but that remains a hypothesis. Likewise, enterprise subscriptions are inferred; without published pricing or a supported margin structure, I cannot derive a power-law outcome from one revenue engine. Scribe’s company-reported $100 million ARR demonstrates spending in the category, not GodHands’s entitlement to capture it.

I pass. The competitive overlap activates my central red flag, while the exceptional technical proof or persistent transaction demand that could override it is absent. The discrepancy between execution marketing and security text describing read-only agents also leaves the product boundary unresolved; it does not establish deception. I would reopen the decision if a named support customer independently demonstrated that GodHands required one-tenth the human correction time of Scribe and Mimica on the same held-out escalation cases at equal resolution accuracy. That would establish a potentially proprietary capability. Today, the dossier supports capable founders pursuing a useful product, with no demonstrated mechanism for escaping competition.
