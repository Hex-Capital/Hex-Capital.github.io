# Tilion -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Tilion’s strongest candidate for a secret is that reliable agent access depends on coherent browser identities implemented inside Chromium, rather than another layer of JavaScript disguise. What would prove that wrong? Competing browser engines achieving equivalent access and latency under the same network conditions. The dossier supplies working software, but neither a named prevailing belief that Tilion overturns nor that decisive comparison. BrowserArena’s October 22 result—156 ms median lifecycle latency and 99 successful runs out of 100—establishes competent infrastructure. It does not establish a truth competitors have failed to understand.

The founding structure is more encouraging. These four founders met in college, have shared research history, and maintain an actual browser product; CTO Arham’s release activity provides concrete evidence that essential engineering is internal. This is substantially better than a team assembled around a fundraising opportunity. Full-time status and economic ownership remain unspecified, so I cannot establish complete alignment, but the documented relationship deserves affirmative credit. The founders’ shared history is the strongest independent signal here.

The investment problem is ownership of the resulting market. Tilion identifies Camoufox, Multilogin, GoLogin, and Dolphin as other browser-engine patching implementations. Its earlier BSD releases also remain available for forks. Native patches are therefore evidence of engineering work, not an exclusive position. Agent developers facing anti-bot challenges are a recognizable customer group, but the dossier does not identify a bounded segment Tilion can dominate through proprietary technology, network effects, scale economics, or brand. The September licensing restrictions can charge for newer code; they cannot retroactively make the underlying approach scarce. This is affirmative competitive pressure, sufficient to trigger my monopoly gate.

The strongest objection to passing is that distribution may already be outrunning that concern. Company-reported adoption of 100,000-plus installs and engineers at 170-plus companies is meaningful at pre-seed. GitHub, pip, npm, Docker, and MCP give developers practical entry points, rather than a hypothetical partnership strategy. Tilion also fits my unstated interest in adversarial information infrastructure: access becomes valuable precisely because another system resists it. But developer adoption has not yet demonstrated a compounding customer-acquisition advantage. The hosted product’s open-access announcement and current waitlist also leave the delivery route unresolved.

There is one underlying revenue engine—browser infrastructure—with licensing and hosted consumption as alternative packages. At $2,000 per month, roughly 4,167 paying organizations would produce $100 million in annual revenue. At $0.03 per browser-hour, the same revenue requires approximately 3.33 billion annual browser-hours, before infrastructure costs. These are possible arithmetic paths, not demonstrated expansion economics. Missing revenue history is ordinary at this stage; the specific concern is whether Tilion can retain attractive margins while competitors reproduce its capabilities and network egress remains an external dependency.

I pass with my own money. My known exceptions require evidence that directly defeats the failed gate: a genuine technical discontinuity, persistent transaction demand despite regulatory hostility, or a concrete deployment path through a binding physical constraint. Tilion’s benchmark and adoption make the bull case credible, but neither establishes durable ownership. The reported 86.4% protected-site success rate also makes “unblockable” a claim to test, not a property to underwrite. One verifiable result would change my judgment: an independent, longitudinal comparison showing Tilion sustains at least a tenfold advantage in cost per successful protected-site task against named alternatives under matched egress conditions. That would turn browser maintenance into evidence of proprietary technology.
