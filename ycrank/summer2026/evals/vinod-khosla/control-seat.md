# Control Seat -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Control Seat is attacking a real industrial bottleneck, but the dossier describes consolidation, not a discontinuity. Manufacturers spend weeks connecting PLCs, SCADA, HMIs, historians, and databases. A common layer spanning OPC UA, MQTT, control, analytics, and natural-language investigation could remove substantial engineering labor. Yet the only quantified consequence is a $13.87 billion SCADA market growing to $26.59 billion by 2034. There is no baseline for integration cost, downtime, energy waste, or industrial capacity that this product could improve by an order of magnitude. Large TAM is not large consequence.

The technical case is similarly incomplete. Control Seat names Ignition, WinCC, and FactoryTalk and offers a sensible adoption path: operate above installed SCADA rather than forcing replacement. That directly addresses switching friction from years of accumulated configuration. But no evidence shows a 5–10x improvement in commissioning time, investigation speed, reliability, or total cost. FactoryTalk and WinCC support is still “coming soon,” while Seeq, Litmus, and Augury have collectively raised at least $567.6 million to address overlapping analytics, edge-data, anomaly-detection, and optimization workflows. Today the differentiation could be unusually good systems integration rather than proprietary technology.

The Battex Chile deployment matters. Remotely monitoring and controlling a battery through Control Seat’s MQTT infrastructure proves that the two-person team can put software against physical equipment, not merely render a dashboard. The stronger evidence is the founders’ technical proximity: Jack Grodnick worked on controls and SCADA at Tesla and SpaceX, while Warren Shepard worked on Starlink networks, manufacturing, and flight software. They know why these systems resist generic SaaS abstractions. What is missing is evidence of fast belief revision, a complementary recruit for safety or industrial deployment, or repeat usage showing that each installation creates a proprietary learning loop.

The largest kill risk is operational trust. Control Seat can issue commands to industrial equipment, while its terms disclaim uninterrupted or error-free operation and its pilot documentation keeps safety functions independent. Air-gapped deployment is prudent containment, not proof of reliability. The dossier supplies no numerical availability target, fault-injection test, certification milestone, deployment budget, or stop condition. Capital could easily disappear into customer-specific PLC programming and integration work before answering whether one reusable platform can safely control heterogeneous plants.

The bull case stings: industrial infrastructure is fragmented, customers already pay heavily for integration, replacement is unnecessary, and two founders from Tesla, SpaceX, Microsoft, and Amazon have already deployed against a live battery. This could become the control plane that accumulates configuration knowledge across plants. But the current business model mixes software with integration, deployment, support, and custom SLAs—the unstated pattern may therefore be a valuable execution-heavy industrial company, not a technical Black Swan. No known exception overrides the absence of a measured discontinuity because the dossier establishes neither an enormous evolving platform nor an unusually consequential mission.

I pass today. The downside is one check, but the upside presented is still better industrial software rather than a rewritten industrial cost curve. One verifiable result would flip me: demonstrate across three independent plants that a reusable Control Seat deployment cuts commissioning or incident-resolution cost by at least 5x versus a named incumbent, while sustaining a disclosed reliability threshold without proportionally growing integration labor. That would convert the story from services-enabled convenience into scalable technical leverage.
