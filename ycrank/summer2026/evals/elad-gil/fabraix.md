# Fabraix -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI agents now act across websites, files, tools, and permissions; security teams therefore need continuous adversarial regression testing, and the controlling variable is whether Fabraix can convert superior exploit discovery into recurring CI spend before established security platforms bundle the capability.

The market mechanism is unusually legible for pre-seed. Manual red-teaming can take weeks and cost six figures, while Nyx reportedly finds its first exploit in under an hour. Model, prompt, permission, and data changes continually invalidate prior testing, creating a natural recurrence loop rather than a one-off audit market. The missing threshold is commercial: Fabraix has not shown how many agents, scans, or dollars a customer commits once testing moves into CI. The company has identified the right variable, but has not yet demonstrated that independent red-teaming captures meaningful budget.

The technical proof is stronger than the demand evidence. Nyx attacks black-box systems through controlled websites, files, messages, and SaaS replicas, adapts across multiple turns, and reports a 78% AgentHarm success rate versus 67% for GPT-5.6 Sol. Its 10,000-plus strategies and self-learning loop suggest that better models could improve the attacker rather than absorb the product. Continuous regression histories could become valuable workflow state. But Fabraix does not yet own remediation, runtime enforcement, permissions, or audit evidence, so it remains a powerful testing engine rather than an end-to-end security workflow.

The 50,000 Playground attempts demonstrate curiosity and adversarial participation, not product-market pull. Product Hunt rankings, 934 followers, 71 GitHub stars, and vulnerabilities found in agents operated by dozens of Fortune 500 companies help validate technical interest. None establishes that those companies are customers, run repeat scans, or pay materially. This is the lowest-confidence part of the company: the product has proven that it can find failures, but not that enterprise buyers will purchase the finding separately from a broader security platform.

The bear case is sharp. Virtue AI and HiddenLayer already bundle red-teaming with compliance, scanning, and runtime defenses; Zscaler acquired SPLX and Check Point acquired Lakera in 2025. Those platforms possess distribution, procurement credibility, and adjacent workflows Fabraix lacks, while its strategy corpus may be reproducible by a well-funded competitor. This matches the red flag around asserting a data moat without quantifying reproduction cost. Fabraix’s free researcher tier could become a useful corpus and distribution channel, but there is no evidence yet that submissions create a proprietary compounding advantage. Nor does a known-exception override apply: this is not frontier research, scarce model-building talent, or a physical-market reconstruction where absent demand can be ignored.

Ahmed Aly and Ibrahim Abdu are credible builders for this specific problem. Fraud leadership at Monzo, systems protecting $1 billion-plus in annual transactions, production agent work at Meta, and prior experience together reduce technical and execution risk. Ahmed leaving a PhD provides some evidence of commitment, although neither biography documents repeat-founder learning or the desperation signal I weight most heavily. I would make a small pre-seed investment because the technological shift, recurrence mechanism, and working attack system clear the market gates. The verifiable flip condition is commercial: if Fabraix cannot secure at least five enterprises running paid weekly CI scans within twelve months, I would conclude that red-teaming is a bundled feature rather than an independent category.
