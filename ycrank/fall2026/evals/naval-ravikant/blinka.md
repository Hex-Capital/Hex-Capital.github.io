# Blinka -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Blinka’s founders have earned relevant knowledge through action. Juan Pablo experienced an account closure while operating a US LLC abroad; the brothers built business-credit software before moving into cross-border banking. By October 2026, their current product reportedly had 25 pre-launch clients and named customers including Bricksave and Dapta. Those are useful artifacts of learning. The law degree and engineering biography matter less than getting real businesses to entrust them with financial operations.

The question is what that learning accumulates into. Blinka bundles accounts, payments, cards, treasury, and accounting, while Portage Bank, Third National, and Mural Pay supply important underlying capabilities. A competitor copying the interface would still need banking relationships and operational competence. But the dossier establishes neither exclusive access nor an asset that becomes harder to reproduce with each deployment. One customer reportedly managing over 200 entities is the strongest clue: replacing a deeply embedded financial workflow could become painful. Entity count alone does not establish that switching cost, so I give it no premium yet.

There is room to maneuver. The initial buyer is a US company operated from abroad; the same payment, invoicing, and treasury capabilities can address its supplier transactions and broader financial administration. Airwallex’s reported $1.3 billion annualized revenue demonstrates substantial adjacent spending, without establishing how much Blinka can capture. This fits my pattern of bringing fragmented financial activity into one system. It also helps outsiders operate businesses across borders. Yet access remains dependent on institutional approvals, and another customer opening an account does not demonstrably improve the product for existing customers. The LegalOS partnership is a distribution channel; no customer-created distribution loop is established.

The strongest case for investing now is that the founders have found an underserved customer whom larger platforms handle poorly. More than $10 million in reported payments during the first two months is meaningful evidence of use at this stage. A tightly integrated multi-entity product could turn that initial service advantage into durable customer relationships before incumbents respond. I can make exceptions for demonstrated builders before a user loop exists. Blinka deserves that consideration. But the dossier does not yet establish the distinctive prototype, exceptional recruiting evidence, or self-reinforcing behavior that would justify overriding the missing durable asset.

Payment volume also leaves the economics unresolved. The terms authorize service fees, but disclose neither the applicable price nor realized revenue or contribution margins. I cannot turn $10 million moving through the system into $10 million of value captured. Meanwhile, the inconsistency between advertised non-US entity access and US-entity eligibility in the terms requires reconciliation: eligibility is central to the promise these customers are buying. It is not evidence of dishonesty. Likewise, the earlier lending pivots do not establish fickleness, and the dossier supplies no evidence of unfair founder dealings or artificial investment deadlines.

I would keep Blinka under observation and leave my money uncommitted today. Its strongest evidence concerns founders learning and shipping; its weakest concerns value compounding beyond their own effort and their providers’ infrastructure. My flip condition is a verified customer cohort showing that multi-entity workflow adoption produces higher retention and positive contribution profit after partner costs than account-only usage. That would turn the reported operational depth into evidence of an accumulating business advantage.
