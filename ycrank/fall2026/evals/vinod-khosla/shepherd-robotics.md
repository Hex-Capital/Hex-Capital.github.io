# Shepherd Robotics -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Shepherd Robotics could release scarce technicians from wiring, assembly, and testing—the work that turns data-center equipment into usable infrastructure. That consequence interests me. But the dossier does not quantify technician hours consumed, construction capacity delayed, or the fraction of that bottleneck its robots could eliminate. The $41.9 billion industrial-robotics market measures equipment spending, not the value Shepherd could unlock. I can see a consequential problem; I cannot yet price the option on an orders-of-magnitude change.

The decisive variable is human intervention per successfully completed task. Shepherd proposes wire routing and terminal insertion under supervision. If one technician can reliably supervise many robots across changing equipment, scarce expertise becomes scalable. If exceptions repeatedly pull that technician back into the work, Shepherd sells another machine requiring skilled attention. The disclosed performance claims are illustrative and workflow-dependent. There is no measured comparison against manual execution, Micropsi, or Standard Bots establishing a discontinuity in throughput, reliability, or deployment effort. That leaves the technical criterion at its missing-evidence default. It does not establish that the technology fails.

The strongest argument against passing is the founding team. Feifei Duan has deployed factory robots at Chef Robotics and trained models at Waymo; David Fang brings robot-learning experience from Dyna; Kai Ru brings Cruise and Rivian experience. These founders have encountered physical deployment constraints. Their combination fits my preference for teams joining several necessary capabilities around an expensive workflow. The reported work with unnamed hyperscalers and manufacturers could also provide access to real operating conditions. But those relationships have no disclosed deployment counts or paid status, and the biographies document neither material belief revision nor difficult recruiting against a named weakness. Relevant experience earns attention; it cannot substitute for evidence of those particular behaviors.

Shepherd also fits my willingness to fund difficult physical systems before commercial validation. The potentially valuable learning loop is interventions becoming training data that reduce future interventions. Here, that loop remains an inference: no dataset scale or resulting performance improvement is disclosed. Nor does the dossier show the cheapest experiment, budget, or pass/fail threshold addressing the supervision risk. There is no basis to accuse this four-person company of premature scaling, copying a standard model, or depending on permanent subsidy. Equally, there is no disclosed bottom-up cost model covering hardware, integration, maintenance, utilization, and human support. My exceptions for an extraordinary instigator or an independently quantified societal consequence do not supply the missing investment case here.

I pass at 25/100. Missing revenue and a short operating history are normal for Fall 2026 pre-seed; neither drives this decision. The gap is evidence that Shepherd can multiply skilled labor economically rather than absorb it into deployment and exception handling. **My flip condition is one independently auditable customer trial** on a representative wire-routing and terminal-insertion workflow, with thresholds fixed beforehand, demonstrating at least 5x more accepted completions per technician-hour than the manual baseline and a fully loaded cost per accepted completion below that baseline. Count setup, interventions, rework, and amortized equipment. That result would directly change what I am underwriting.
