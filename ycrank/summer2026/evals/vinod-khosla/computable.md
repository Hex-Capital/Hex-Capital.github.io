# Computable -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Computable’s standing buyback quote on every weekly H100 position makes Computable a structurally risky market-infrastructure experiment built by consensus financial experts. That is the signature: highest on capital risk, lowest on founder defiance. Jump Trading and Coinbase experience equips the founders to construct an auction, but it also suggests they are applying the established financialization playbook to compute. One disclosed cluster and overlapping products from SF Compute and Ornn make Computable an optimization of GPU procurement, not yet a new economic regime.

**Commonwealth Fusion** required a $115M Series A because capital-hostile physics was inseparable from the opportunity. Computable shares the capital-hostile mechanism only superficially: maintaining an immediate buyback quote can consume balance sheet precisely when liquidity disappears, while future-dated positions may create commodities or derivatives exposure. That risk could repel generalists. Computable diverges from Commonwealth Fusion because no scientific miracle, proprietary infrastructure, or roadmap requiring more than $50M is identified; SF Compute already operates thousands of chips and supports resale. Computable has financial risk, not deep technical risk.

**Glean’s** domain-expert continuity is the low rung on founder defiance: an ex-Google search founder extending a known discipline, rescued by consequence and enterprise scale. Computable presents an even purer continuity pattern. Yuan Song appears to come from Flow Traders and Jump Trading, while the unnamed co-founder reportedly comes from Coinbase; Computable then turns GPU reservations into tradable positions. Experts extrapolate the past. Glean had a large information-asymmetry consequence to override that concern. Computable has a $4.37B GPUaaS market and no equivalent override, no controversial technical claim, and no documented rejection of trading orthodoxy.

The **$14.46B projected GPUaaS market in 2033** is large enough for a useful business but too small, as framed, for a consequential Khosla outcome. Over 18 months, Computable’s auctions could compound price histories, supplier participation, and liquidity; those mechanisms could lower reservation premiums and unused-capacity losses. Yet Computable does not break the economics of compute. SF Compute can add calendar contracts, Vast.ai already aggregates rentals, and Ornn is pursuing exchange-listed futures. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself” does not mean make a better contract for an existing market. Computable improves allocation; it does not collapse an incumbent regime.

**Yuan Song’s** dossier contains no documented pivot, thesis update, prior exit, or admission that an earlier market design failed—no evidence, applying the absent-evidence default. Computable could flip this dimension into invest territory if the founders demonstrated that sealed auctions failed to create depth, explained the evidence, and rebuilt Computable around a materially different clearing or collateral mechanism that attracted repeat suppliers. A Show HN launch with 46 points and one auction scheduled to close July 31 shows initiative, not learning rate.

Computable’s one disclosed H100 cluster creates a modest parallel with **OpenAI** only on one axis: both may need concentrated capital before conventional revenue math becomes comfortable. The parallel ends quickly. OpenAI justified a $50M first check through model-training scale and a civilizational success-state; Computable discloses neither liquidity reserves nor a capital deployment plan. Computable may need market-making capital, but financing a standing quote without proprietary supply or demonstrated volume is adverse-selection insurance, not the productive capital intensity I seek.

Computable’s first auction and standing buyback mechanism lead to a falsifiable forecast: by July 2029, Computable will have removed the standing buyback obligation and pivoted to software-only GPU procurement and order routing, producing $1M-$5M ARR. Computable’s current mechanism requires simultaneous supplier depth, standardized contracts, regulatory clarity, and funded liquidity—four miracles, none technical and none yet demonstrated. When those miracles fail to arrive together, the rational response is to remove principal risk and sell workflow software.

**SF Compute’s $55M raised and Ornn’s $33M raised** form the strongest bull case: sophisticated capital is validating compute financialization, and Computable’s Jump Trading and Coinbase pedigree could create the liquidity flywheel before larger operators move. DoorDash is the known exception—no scientific miracle, but network effects and ambition produced a $72B outcome. The analogy still fails today because Computable has one disclosed cluster, no transaction volume, no named supplier depth, and functionality incumbents can copy. I would flip to Invest if Computable clears at least $10M of weekly contracts across three independent suppliers while sustaining executable buyback quotes without balance-sheet losses. Until then, consequence does not outrun probability. I pass.
