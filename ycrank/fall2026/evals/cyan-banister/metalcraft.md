# Metalcraft -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a small biology lab sending an agent off to choose its next experiment, with a spending limit and instructions about what uncertainty matters. Metalcraft is building something that world needs: a way to test whether the agent makes sensible decisions before the lab spends real money. Its tasks already include choosing experiments within budgets and predicting unseen measurements. But the journey from passing those tasks to making reliable laboratory decisions remains my extrapolation. The dossier does not establish that bridge, and I cannot award my strongest criterion for a future I supplied myself.

The interesting object here is the grader. Who decides whether an artificial scientist learned something useful? Metalcraft has runnable biological tasks and organism-specific model-curation workflows, including 1,724 metabolic-model cases. That is an actual technical asset beneath the autonomous-science story, and it fits my pattern of looking for concealed infrastructure. Yet an enabling function becomes a business bottleneck only when someone needs this particular supplier. LABBench2 offers a public dataset and harness; laboratories can build internal tests; Tacit and LatchBio pursue overlapping evaluations. Some Metalcraft assessments also depend on a judge’s estimates. If the judge rewards persuasive mistakes, the infrastructure could make research less trustworthy.

Marshall Kiely gives me a reason to keep looking. His YC biography reports a Princeton computational-biology PhD and first-author Nature and Science papers, alongside founding AI engineering work at Notable Health and finance leadership at Perplexity. The papers are more meaningful to me than an impressive employer list: they indicate experience producing scientific work. Still, the dossier supplies no dated history connecting that work to a sustained obsession with evaluating artificial scientists, and no evidence of recruiting complementary believers. October 2026 reporting associated Kiely and Metalcraft Inc. with UFO.ai’s agent platform. I want that history explained, but it does not establish opportunism, deceptive leadership, or a governance problem.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that Metalcraft could become the independent examiner for biological agents before their mistakes become expensive. Its 36 Auto-research tasks and broader task inventory give that argument substance. Missing revenue would not stop me. **CosmicBrain AI** earned a pre-revenue commitment through a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a lifelong robotics record; the shared mechanism is technical work ahead of established purchasing behavior. The material difference is that Metalcraft’s dossier documents inventory, with no completed external user action. A calendar link is not a scientist rearranging a workflow. Nor does the dossier identify a specific institutional rent or restriction that Metalcraft removes; better measurement could help researchers without transferring meaningful control to them.

I pass with my own money today. Metalcraft has credible scientific roots and a plausible infrastructure position, but those are carrying nearly the entire bet. The record does not establish a closed-provider dependency or severe privacy violation, so neither earns a punitive deduction. It also does not establish the user behavior that would justify overriding the missing future-to-deployment bridge. My flip condition is one documented, paid, repeat deployment at a named biological research organization showing that Metalcraft’s scores predict subsequent measured experimental outcomes well enough to change which agent or experiment the organization selects. That would connect the grader to a consequence someone actually cares about.
