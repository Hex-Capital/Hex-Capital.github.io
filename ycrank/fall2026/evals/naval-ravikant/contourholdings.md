# Contour -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Contour proposes to buy the domain knowledge its founders have not yet demonstrated. That can work. But I first want to know what this team has learned through operating an environmental practice. Agrawal built enterprise search at Rezolve AI; Lubar worked in investment banking at Lazard. Those are relevant capabilities for document automation and acquisitions. Neither establishes that they have shipped accurate permitting work, sold it, or integrated a consulting acquisition. The founder score stays at the absent-evidence default. This is uncertainty about specific knowledge, with no evidence of an integrity problem.

The acquisition structure is the most interesting part. Owning a practice could provide established customer relationships, experienced professionals, and project records that remain valuable when competitors reproduce Cortex’s code. Permanent ownership also gives Contour control over implementation and the resulting economics. But the dossier establishes an acquisition strategy, not a completed acquisition or an accumulated asset. Exclusive data rights and demonstrated automation advantages remain unproven. Paces already consolidates permitting data; Transect maintains regulatory logic and curated datasets. Document production alone would leave Contour competing against companies already assembling the underlying knowledge.

There is real expenditure here. Contour names environmental consultants as its initial operating base and reports, studies, and permit applications as related workflows. That provides some grounding for expansion within environmental services. KPMG’s cited estimate puts U.S. environmental consulting at **$27.0 billion in 2024**, although it includes substantial activity outside permitting. I give limited market credit for the identified buyer, related work, and independently documented spending. The parent-market figure cannot establish how much demand the same Cortex capability can actually serve.

The distinction I care about is whether another acquired practice makes the whole system more productive. Embedded engineers could learn reusable workflows, but there is no observed cross-practice improvement, contribution loop, or customer-driven distribution. On-site implementation also leaves the amount of recurring company labor unresolved. Likewise, consulting fees are a plausible monetization mechanism, but the dossier labels them an inference: no acquired revenue, pricing, completed transactions, or contribution economics are established. Both dimensions receive their missing-evidence defaults. Fall 2026 YC participation does not answer those operating questions.

The strongest objection to passing is that Contour could purchase distribution and domain expertise together, then retain the savings from automation. That fits my pattern of moving fragmented offline supply into a more productive system. It could become a valuable business before developing any network effect. But acquisition financing and integration must then produce the advantage that software alone cannot supply. My exceptions for unusually capable founders with a demonstrated prototype, or users already creating a reinforcing network, have no supporting evidence here. Neither do my red flags concerning unfair dealings, artificial deadlines, or founders refusing to update; I would not invent those negatives.

I pass at **27/100**. Contour has an economically coherent ownership model, but my money would currently finance the acquisition of the evidence needed to establish the thesis. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: a six-month operating record from a completed acquisition showing that Cortex increases gross profit per expert hour while maintaining independently reviewed application quality. That would turn an acquisition-and-automation proposition into demonstrated operating knowledge.
