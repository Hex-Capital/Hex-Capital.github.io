# Praxis -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Praxis enters legal administration after incumbents have begun shipping agents; small and midsized firms will buy another platform only if it produces materially greater net labor savings than automation inside their existing software. That is the controlling variable. Clio reported more than $500 million ARR and workflow-executing AI in May 2026. Lawmatics launched native intake qualification in February. The opening is therefore narrower than “law firms need AI”: Praxis must make coordinating several systems sufficiently valuable that firms accept another vendor, integration layer and approval queue.

There is evidence of an expensive problem. One unnamed firm estimated $350,000 in annual losses from slow responses, and a corporate-law-firm partner described spending 1.5 hours daily on setup and engagement letters. Those are company-published accounts of existing pain, not measured improvements delivered by Praxis. They support the causal thesis without establishing its decisive threshold: how much work disappears after supervision and exception handling? The broader $3.34 billion practice-management market establishes an existing software budget. It does not establish how much additional spending this product can capture.

The strongest affirmative evidence is the product’s scope. Praxis describes qualifying inquiries, checking conflicts, preparing engagements, collecting documents and chasing invoices, with lawyers approving actions. Pulling context and triggering actions across systems is a more substantial starting point than generating legal text. Better models could improve completion across that loop. But the dossier does not establish persistent workflow state, named production integrations or accumulated evaluation data. I give credit for the described execution architecture; I cannot yet underwrite the switching costs. Exportable customer records alone would not establish a proprietary data advantage.

The bull case is credible enough to make passing uncomfortable. Praxis reports being live with U.S. firms, and Diana reports taking Gorgias’s AI Agent to 1,000-plus customers and $8 million ARR in under a year. Combined with her law-firm exposure at McKinsey and Maxence’s AI engineering experience, this is a team with relevant operating context. Cross-system administration could also reach work that individual incumbents handle poorly. But the Gorgias figures belong to a prior employer, and “live” establishes deployment rather than repeat use, payment or expansion. Neither establishes founder sacrifice or second-time startup learning, so that dimension remains at its missing-evidence default.

My pattern of buying across a technology discontinuity does not remove the need for a sharper winner case once distribution starts crystallizing. Here, established vendors already own customer relationships and overlapping workflows. The documented red-flag concern is incumbent substitution; the dossier does not establish that Praxis is merely a formatter, a vanity project or making unsupported proprietary-data claims. Nor does it supply the frontier research, scarce model-building capability and credible capital path that would justify an exception for weak commercial evidence. Relevant AI experience improves execution odds, but it does not resolve who captures the economics.

I would pass today. Praxis has a plausible workflow product, while its market mechanism remains below my conviction gate and its economic step change is unmeasured. My single flip condition is a verified cohort of ten paying firms that renews after six months while demonstrating at least a 50% reduction in administrative labor hours, including review and exception handling, against each firm’s existing software workflow. That would connect product scope to customer economics and durable pull.
