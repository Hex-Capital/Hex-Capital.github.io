# Rindler -- Elad Gil Evaluation

MCP’s November 2024 release and adoption across more than 10,000 public servers make Rindler a catalyst-rich, curve-poor infrastructure bet: Rindler is skating toward a real interface transition, but its weakest signal is whether anyone is paying. Rindler’s $100 Starter and $1,000 Teams plans establish pricing, while unnamed “production” ATS, bank, and registry workflows establish only availability—not the revenue velocity that distinguishes something *just working* from a false signal.

**Stripe** is the high rung because APIs turned a recently reopened payments market into infrastructure every internet business could touch. Rindler shares that mechanism: MCP gives Rindler a standard distribution interface, while agents increasingly need authenticated actions rather than another public-web scraper. Rindler diverges from Stripe where the abstraction meets reality—each site still requires a typed mapping, some mappings require credential or cookie capture, and well-funded Browserbase, Browser Use, Kernel, and Firecrawl can move upward into the same layer.

**Lyft** exposed the low-rung failure mechanism: growth evidence that does not answer the decisive structural question cannot overcome an unfavorable market position. Rindler provides even less curve evidence—306 LinkedIn followers, undisclosed customer counts, no MRR, and company-reported benchmarks of 3x fewer failures, 4x faster completion, and 6x lower cost without independent validation. Rindler may have real usage, but “production workflows” without paid-session growth or named buyers is the AI-infrastructure version of pilot tourism.

**Browserbase’s $67.5 million** creates the central 18-month index-position test. Each new Rindler mapping could make the next customer easier to serve, and accumulated failure-resolution data could turn Rindler into an index on authenticated agent activity. The compounding breaks if Rindler must continually remap sites by hand while Browserbase, Kernel, or Browser Use adds typed schemas, verification, and MCP endpoints to infrastructure customers already consume.

**Michael Serrano’s** Roblox ML engineering and MIT CSAIL LLM research, combined with Arthur De Los Santos’s vision-language navigation work, are directly relevant artifacts rather than generic pedigree. Rindler has also shipped hosted MCP access, encrypted reusable sessions, and production-described workflows, but Rindler shows no Perplexity-style record of building from one user conversation to the next. The founder score flips upward if Serrano and De Los Santos can show a release history tied to repeat customer requests—especially paid users expanding from one mapped workflow to several.

**Color Genomics** is the useful single-anchor parallel: workflow infrastructure can capture value on top of a hard technical layer without owning the underlying science. Rindler similarly relies on Kernel and Browserbase for managed browsers and tries to own mappings, authentication, recovery, and structured records. Rindler is not a science project, but the current architecture still needs several independent things to work—mapping economics must scale, encrypted credentials must earn enterprise trust without SOC 2, and third-party-site restrictions must not invalidate the highest-value workflows—so the single miracle has not been cleanly isolated.

**Browserbase’s adjacency to Rindler’s core execution layer** drives my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Rindler will have abandoned the horizontal “any website into an API” position and will sell an ATS-specific authenticated automation product at $1 million–$3 million ARR rather than remain a general MCP infrastructure layer. Rindler’s strongest disclosed production category is multi-tenant ATS workflows, and vertical focus would convert expensive per-site mapping from a scaling defect into reusable domain infrastructure.

**Rindler’s $1-per-successful-session model** supports the strongest opposing case: a usage-based mapping flywheel could make Rindler the Stripe-like index on agent-to-web transactions, while its ATS, major-bank, and registry workflows suggest horizontal breadth already exists. The **Color Genomics** exception strengthens that bull case because Rindler is explicitly building workflow infrastructure above commodity browsers. The evidence that wins today is the absence of mapping counts, paying-customer counts, reliability history, or revenue growth despite four capitalized adjacent vendors; one audited cohort showing more than 20% month-over-month paid-session growth for four consecutive months across at least 25 customers would flip my verdict to Invest.
