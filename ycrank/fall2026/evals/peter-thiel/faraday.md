# Faraday -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Faraday’s proposed secret is that foundation models are already capable enough to operate manufacturers; the remaining bottleneck is the company-specific harness that makes their actions reliable. That is a proposition worth testing. It would be falsified if the harness’s advantage disappeared on unfamiliar factory records, permissions, and exceptions. Faraday reports a score of 96.2 against GPT-5.5’s baseline of 71.7, at 2.6 times lower run cost. But its 74 episodes across 33 scenario families use synthetic records on its own benchmark. This earns credit for a concrete experiment. It does not establish that named peers reject the proposition, or that the result survives production.

The investment question is who keeps the value if Faraday is right. Its product maps dependencies, permissions, and approvals into executable workflows, potentially placing it at an important control point. Yet “back office to shop floor” describes considerable territory without identifying the first market Faraday can dominate. Didero already executes procurement workflows; Tulip supplies configurable frontline software. These are overlapping alternatives, not proof that Faraday is interchangeable with them. Equally, customer-specific mappings are not yet evidence of exclusive data, accumulated switching costs, or a monopoly. The benchmark supports the technical hypothesis; it cannot also establish market ownership.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ combination of relevant experience. Oscar Rangel built factory systems at Worthington Steel and shipped medical devices at Stryker; Datta Kaligotla brings enterprise engineering and agent experience from Microsoft and Handshake AI. They could recognize industrial exceptions that a generic software team would miss. Faraday also fits my observed interest in extending AI into physical production. But that pattern does not substitute for founding structure: the dossier establishes neither prior work together nor ownership and full-time status. I use the prescribed prior for those unknowns. There is no documented part-time founder, outsourced core engineering, or misaligned capitalization to justify an adverse judgment.

Distribution is slightly more concrete than the economics. Rangel is meeting manufacturers at Fabtech, and Faraday offers walkthroughs using prospects’ records—a credible way to expose a painful workflow. There is no demonstrated conversion rate, sales cycle, or implementation cost, however. Subscription and agreed-pilot contracts identify a business model without supplying prices or margins from which a power-law outcome can be calculated. The concern is whether each new manufacturer requires another custom integration project. Approval gates and replayable environments show attention to deployment control; they do not yet show that delivery becomes repeatable.

I would pass today. The exceptions that could overturn this judgment require evidence Faraday has not supplied: an independently established technical breakthrough, persistent customer transactions, or validated physical deployment. Its synthetic result is the strongest opposing evidence, but it does not remove the central value-capture uncertainty. Nor does the dossier establish the red flag of several indispensable breakthroughs having to synchronize; optional robot controllers need not define the initial product. My one flip condition is an independently audited, paid deployment cohort showing the same bounded workflow operating across three unrelated manufacturers with positive contribution margins after integration and support. That would turn an interesting harness into evidence of a repeatable business—and justify reopening the investment.
