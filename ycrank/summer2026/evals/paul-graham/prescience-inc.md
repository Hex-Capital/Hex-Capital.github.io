# Prescience, Inc. -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Rishab and Aditya Jain are brothers who have built together for ten years, while Prescience, Inc. gives no evidence that either founder personally experienced employer health insurance as a buyer or patient. That pairing is Prescience, Inc.’s signature: unusually strong founder configuration attached to an idea whose organic origin remains unproven. The brothers look more convincing than the story of why they began. I would bet on them finding the right version, but not assume Time Machine is already it.

**Stripe** is the high rung for co-founder configuration: two brothers with complementary abilities, years of shared context, and commerce software they had already built together. Prescience, Inc. has the same potentially load-bearing mechanism. Rishab brings ML, health research, and a prior exit; Aditya brings an MD, biomedical engineering, and Broad Institute research. Prescience, Inc. diverges because “building together for ten years” is asserted, while the dossier names no substantial artifact the brothers jointly shipped before Prescience. The configuration is nearly ideal; its joint output is less documented than the Collisons’ was.

**Loopt** is the low-rung warning on organic ideas: a forceful founder can outrun weak product timing for a while, but personal determination does not create user pull. Prescience, Inc. describes Harvard medicine, computational neuroscience, health-policy work, and an enormous cost problem, but it never says Rishab bought benefits, Aditya administered a plan, or either founder built Time Machine to solve his own recurring pain. Prescience, Inc. has excellent founder-market adjacency. Adjacency is not the Dropbox forgotten-USB-stick test. No direct evidence shows the founders were users first, so the absent-evidence penalty applies.

Prescience, Inc. reports employer customers, roughly 20% lower premiums, a California administrator license, and direct physician contracting. Those tracks raise the animal score above credentials alone. Over eighteen months, each unpleasant employer implementation can compound resourcefulness: claims ingestion, physician negotiation, PHI controls, plan renewal, and savings reconciliation create repeated chances for Prescience, Inc. to do things that do not scale. But the dossier contains no cereal-box moment, rejection-rescuing prototype, named employer, or specific manual stunt. I see an animal’s footprints, not yet the animal itself.

**Coinbase** turned compliance from an obstacle into the product, and Prescience, Inc. is walking into an even denser thicket: PHI, claims, medical records, optional Gmail data, underwriting, provider contracting, and savings-dependent compensation. Prescience, Inc. also promises $0 deductibles while collecting only when employers save money. Most hackers unconsciously route around precisely this combination of regulation, bodily consequences, and awkward reconciliation. The missing point is proof that the claimed 20% premium reduction survives risk adjustment and twelve months of realized claims, but the schlep is unquestionably real.

Rishab Jain has authored more than ten ML and health papers, exited Qurios, led acquired ICOR Therapeutics, and maintains a codon-optimization repository with 70 GitHub stars. Prescience, Inc. therefore has more hacker genealogy than a merely credentialed medical-AI startup, while Aditya’s Broad Institute research gives Prescience, Inc. a second technical line rather than a salesperson bolted onto one programmer. The score would flip into the very top rung if Prescience published prospective evidence that Time Machine’s trajectory search—not ordinary underwriting or care navigation—caused better clinical or claims outcomes. Without that artifact, I cannot tell whether the impressive phrase “millions of possible patient trajectories” describes working code or ambitious packaging.

Angle Health already serves more than 2,600 employers across 44 states, so within 36 months I expect Prescience, Inc. to reach $8–15 million in annualized savings-share revenue as a specialized level-funded administrator, while Prescience, Inc. quietly subordinates the “superintelligence” story to physician contracting, navigation, and claims operations. The strong sibling configuration makes survival through the schlep plausible. The weak organic-origin evidence makes a practical narrowing more likely than the original integrated payer-provider vision arriving intact.

Angle Health’s 2,600 employers, Collective Health’s 800,000 members, and Prescience, Inc.’s unpublished clinical validation form the strongest bear case: incumbents can copy the visible software while Prescience, Inc. retains the underwriting risk and must somehow finance $0 deductibles from uncertain savings. That objection stings because the unstated hacker pattern cuts both ways—ten papers and a 70-star repository are not proof of a production health plan—and “superintelligence” can become deck-first language. Prescience, Inc. nevertheless has already crossed several boundaries an AI wrapper would avoid: an administrator license, design-partner plans, direct physician contracts, and claimed employer savings near 20%. I would invest, but an independently audited twelve-month cohort showing less than 10% risk-adjusted savings would flip the verdict to Pass.
