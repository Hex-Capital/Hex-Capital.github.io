# OpEra -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

A factory loses a machine halfway through a shift. In the future I want to inhabit, the planner describes what happened, checks the proposed tradeoffs, and keeps production moving. OpEra’s connection to WMS, ERP, and Excel points toward that world. But getting there requires two substantial transitions: turning messy operating knowledge into trustworthy constraints, then letting ordinary planners change those constraints without breaking the model. The dossier establishes the proposed interface and integration approach; it does not yet establish those transitions working together. I can imagine the six-year destination. I cannot count my imagination as the company’s evidence.

The founders give me something firmer. Hindy Rossignol and Bernardo Chalita met at MIT’s Operations Research Center and worked together on retailer routing before OpEra. Chalita’s public implementation includes carrier tariffs, heuristic assignment, and mathematical routing optimization using JuMP/Gurobi. That is actual contact with the machinery of the problem. Their McKinsey, BMW, and Sanofi experience adds relevant context, although employers alone would not excite me. The shared project does. This is credible pre-company building, with less evidence of longstanding independent obsession, recruiting magnetism, or command of the commercial business.

Why does the planner still rebuild the spreadsheet? Because the software needs an explicit model of the business, and the business keeps changing. OpEra could return control to the person who knows which machine is unreliable and which delivery promise really matters. That fits my pattern of backing unglamorous workflow repair. But the dossier identifies repetitive labor rather than a quantified institutional toll: no measured planning delay, specialist fee, or gatekeeper whose power the product removes. And bespoke modeling may simply transfer that work from the customer’s spreadsheet to OpEra’s implementation team. The interesting economic question is whether each deployment makes the next one easier.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that these founders already understand operational complexity and have produced unusually concrete early results: reported delivery costs down 38%, sugar sales premiums up 10.4%, and unfulfilled marketplace orders down 54%. My pre-revenue investment in **CosmicBrain AI** provides a relevant exception: technical founder depth and industrial proof can justify a commitment before revenue. The material difference is that its prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept established engagement beyond an analytical result. OpEra’s retailer figure comes from a historical backtest against nearest-store, single-order dispatch. That can establish useful optimization work; it does not show planners repeatedly trusting a live product. The other unnamed results do not supply that behavioral evidence either. Missing revenue is not what holds me back.

There may be an enabling business here, underneath the language interface. Translating tariffs, capacity, staffing, and operating rules into executable models is a real technical task, and the public routing code gives it substance. But OpEra has not demonstrated control of a scarce dependency across factories, warehouses, and fleets. OR-Tools supplies solver components; Nextmv, acquired by FICO in May 2026, supplies overlapping infrastructure and potential distribution. This is not enough evidence to dismiss the team as a thin wrapper, nor does the dossier show dependence on one closed model supplier. It also shows no affirmative governance deception or severe privacy violation. CEO and CTO titles clarify responsibilities, although they do not establish control arrangements or data safeguards. Those red flags remain unproven, not reasons to subtract points.

I pass with my own money today. OpEra fits my workflow-repair pattern more clearly than my pattern of concealed infrastructure becoming indispensable. The founders have earned attention; the product has not yet earned that larger conclusion. My one flip condition is a referenceable customer site where ordinary planners have independently used OpEra for live constraint changes every week for eight weeks, with logs showing feasible plans reaching execution without recurring founder model repairs. That would show the difficult transition happening: operating knowledge becoming something its owner can actually control.
