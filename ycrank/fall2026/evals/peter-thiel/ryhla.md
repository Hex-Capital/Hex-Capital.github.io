# Ryhla -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Ryhla’s proposed secret is that utility approval decisions are predictable enough to reject bad projects before developers pay to enter the queue. What would prove it wrong? A prospective test showing that its screening adds little beyond ordinary engineering diligence. The reported 91% approval rate among model-approved 2025 applications is encouraging, but the denominator, baseline approval rate, and rejected-site outcomes are undisclosed. A model can achieve impressive precision by approving only obvious cases. Ryhla has a testable technical proposition; the dossier does not establish which prevailing belief it overturns.

The founding relationship is stronger evidence. Sara El Baghdadi and Yassine Guennoun have known each other since middle school, attended Polytechnique and Stanford together, and reportedly researched grid models before deploying them with utilities. This is a company with a shared history behind it. Their statistical and uncertainty-modeling backgrounds also make the product technically credible. Full-time status, ownership, and economic exposure remain unspecified; pursuing a master’s degree does not itself establish divided commitment. I credit the demonstrated alignment without assuming the rest.

The decisive question is who owns the knowledge produced by each application. Ryhla claims proprietary distribution-grid data and models, but neither exclusive rights nor a measurable learning advantage is documented. Distribution-level projects provide a recognizable starting market, yet Paces already offers overlapping diligence and power-flow studies, while Enverus owns interconnection simulation capabilities. These competitors do not prove Ryhla is a commodity. They do make exclusive information or demonstrably superior predictions essential. Calling a model proprietary does not establish a monopoly mechanism.

The approximately $160,000 in avoided fees across eight eliminated battery projects gives Ryhla a concrete customer-value argument. It supplies no corresponding evidence about what Ryhla collects. Project fees are inferred, pricing and margins are unavailable, and there is no arithmetic connecting one revenue engine to an exceptional outcome. Distribution has slightly more substance: an unnamed Midwest solar developer has used the product, establishing that the team can reach at least one relevant buyer. The public request for introductions identifies a prospecting route, but reveals nothing about repeatability, sales cycles, or implementation cost.

The strongest objection to passing is that Ryhla may already possess the information advantage the public dossier cannot reveal. Actual utility outcomes and consequential project screening are substantially more informative than a presentation about grid congestion. The company also fits my allocation pattern toward constraints on physical infrastructure and AI production. It avoids the familiar cleantech coordination trap: its initial service does not require financing a factory or building a power plant. Those points deserve weight. But my exceptions for competitive markets require an independently demonstrated technical discontinuity or customer behavior that directly answers the ownership problem. The disclosed results establish neither. No documented founder-alignment red flag overrides the team’s strengths, and no exception removes the unresolved monopoly question.

I would pass on a check today. Ryhla has credible founders and evidence of useful work; the investment case still depends on whether that work produces information competitors cannot obtain. One verifiable flip condition would be an independent, prospective comparison across multiple utilities showing that Ryhla’s exclusively controlled data reduces costly application errors by an order of magnitude versus Paces at comparable approval recall. That would justify serious investment diligence because it would connect technical usefulness to durable value capture.
