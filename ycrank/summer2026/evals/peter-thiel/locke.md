# Locke -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Locke’s contested truth is that government affairs can become a software-leveraged operating function for startups rather than an expensive relationship business reserved for incumbents. What would prove this wrong? If the first $500,000 of annualized revenue requires lobbyist hours to grow proportionally with revenue, Locke has created a modern consultancy, not a zero-to-one company. Six weeks of customer spending makes the claim more than a slogan, but no published accuracy, productivity, retention, or contract-win result yet isolates the AI agents as the cause.

The strongest fact is the speed of monetization: more than $500,000 in annual revenue run-rate, with clients across defense, healthcare, and GovTech. At pre-seed, customers paying this quickly reveal a real institutional pain that market surveys would obscure. The bull case is that Badhwar’s White House, Deloitte policy, and government-facing GTM experience supplied a distribution channel, while each engagement creates proprietary stakeholder, legislative, and procurement data. If software makes every lobbyist substantially more productive, services are the wedge and the accumulated workflow becomes the product.

But the beachhead is not yet a monopoly. “Early-stage companies needing government help” is a customer description, not a bounded market Locke demonstrably owns. Quorum already serves more than 2,000 organizations with policy data, AI assistance, stakeholder management, and professional services; Plural, Legible, and NationGraph cover adjacent workflows. Locke reports no exclusive data, technical benchmark, network retention, dominant share, or tenfold result. The hybrid execution layer is differentiated, but differentiation by doing more work can increase labor intensity rather than value capture. Competition is not defeated by bundling the competitors’ features with consultants.

The founding structure is also unresolved at precisely the point where it should be definite. YC reports a two-person team but lists only Parth Badhwar as an active founder, and the dossier supplies no prior working history, ownership, salary, or confirmation that core engineering is controlled by full-time founders. This is missing evidence rather than proof of a damaged foundation, so it earns the stated prior. Yet hiring both founding policy and technical staff immediately makes the ambiguity consequential: is Locke a founder-built technical system, or a policy practice recruiting the people who will eventually build it?

The exception case is real but insufficient. Extraordinary early revenue can sometimes override an unproved moat because customers disclose urgency with money. Here, however, “annual revenue run-rate” does not disclose recurring revenue, pricing, gross margin, implementation burden, renewal, or whether one or two retainers generated the number. The adjacent advocacy-software market is estimated at only $1.10 billion, while the larger government-contracting opportunity remains unquantified. My 36-month base case is therefore a $3–8 million revenue, tech-enabled government-affairs firm—potentially excellent, but not yet a power-law monopoly.

Locke fits the pattern of making contested institutional information legible, but it does not yet possess the proprietary information system that makes that pattern investable. It also carries a coordination stack: AI accuracy, lobbying compliance, relationship-driven delivery, proposal execution, and policy hiring must work together. None is fatal alone, and the reported revenue proves some demand; collectively they make scale economics the decisive unknown. I pass today. One verifiable condition flips the decision: twelve months of cohort data showing at least $2 million in recurring revenue, above 70% gross margin, and expansion without proportional growth in policy headcount. That would show the agents are converting relationships into software economics rather than merely decorating services with AI.
