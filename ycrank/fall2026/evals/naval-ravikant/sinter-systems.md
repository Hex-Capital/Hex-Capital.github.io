# Sinter -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Oliver Johnson has operated a print farm and a makerspace. Justin Kwon has worked on mechatronics at Shinkei Systems. By May 2026, the founders had launched a manufacturing marketplace and reported onboarding more than 150 printers nationally. That is knowledge acquired through action. It earns Sinter considerably more founder credit than an attractive manufacturing thesis would. But operating printers and recruiting supply do not yet demonstrate mastery of injection-mold tooling. The most valuable part of their proposed business remains ahead of the evidence.

My decisive question is what survives when somebody copies the quoting interface. Sinter currently offers FDM production; polymer tooling and mold-flow simulation are labeled “coming soon.” Owning machines gives the company production capacity. It does not automatically give it a scarce process. The dossier establishes no proprietary material, demonstrated mold life, exclusive dataset, or repeatability advantage. Xometry already offers automated manufacturing procurement and reported $229 million in Q2 2026 revenue. The interface is therefore an entry point into competition, while the proposed tooling process is still an unproven escape from it.

The strongest case for investing is that the founders can learn something valuable by doing the work themselves. Hardware teams need prototypes, then production parts. Serving those stages could let Sinter carry an existing customer relationship into larger orders, with operational experience informing better tooling. This fits my pattern of backing physical bottlenecks and founders who understand fragmented supply. The $362.5 billion plastics market supplies context, however, rather than evidence of Sinter’s reachable opportunity. The bridge from today’s printing orders to economical molded production depends on the very capability still under development.

There is also a consequential change in how value could compound. The earlier marketplace assembled outside printer supply; today’s presentation emphasizes in-house manufacturing. Neither the 150-printer count nor REST/MCP ordering establishes that one customer improves another customer’s experience. There is no demonstrated matching, referral, or shared-learning loop. Order-specific quotes suggest a sensible direct-payment model, but disclosed prices, completed transactions, and contribution margins are absent. I assign the missing-evidence defaults to both dimensions. I do not turn supplier onboarding into demand or a quote form into revenue.

I would pass with my own money today. I find no documented integrity problem or artificial financing pressure, and one product transition does not establish habitual abandonment. My exceptions for exceptional founder execution, an unusually capital-efficient prototype, or an already-compounding network deserve consideration; this dossier does not establish evidence strong enough for an override. The bull case remains credible: these operators could discover a valuable manufacturing process through repeated production. One customer-verified repeat deployment in which Sinter’s proprietary polymer tooling meets agreed tolerances and beats conventional tooling on delivered cost at a positive contribution margin would flip my decision. That would demonstrate the missing mechanism: specific knowledge becoming a productive asset.
