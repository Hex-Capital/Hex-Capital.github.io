# Hilstart -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first numbers I want are missing. Hilstart has sold $1.08 million of a $1.5 million SAFE to three investors, but the dossier gives no valuation cap, competing-term-sheet count, or evidence that this is a genuinely scarce first check. Three investors do not prove an auction; they also do not prove non-consensus pricing. Without the strike price, I cannot tell whether I am buying an unfashionable hardware insight or paying YC prices for the newly fashionable idea that agents should touch physical equipment.

The more serious omission is what the $1.5 million flips. Hilstart has a technically coherent product: ingest schematics, netlists, BoMs, requirements, and datasheets; produce an engineer-approved specification; generate tests; then execute them through Tracer with read-only defaults and approval gates. But the financing is not tied to a dated experiment—ten boards brought up, a measured reduction in validation time, an acceptable physical-actuation error rate, or conversion of early-access teams into paid deployments. “Onboarding a small group” is activity, not yet a venture moment. Hiring and continued product development would merely extend the company; one measured test could reveal whether autonomous hardware validation works.

The founders are the reason not to dismiss this. All four worked together at Aquila, and their receipts span firmware, mechatronics, propulsion, test rigs, electronics debugging, electric racecars, and a 500W mid-flight drone power transfer. This is an unusually credible outsider-insider translation layer between software agents and physical engineering. They know why a plausible firmware change can still destroy a board. Tracer’s voltage constraints, customer-controlled API access, and human approval gates reflect that lived understanding rather than a generic AI wrapper applied to manufacturing.

The bull case is that this team’s workflow becomes the dark pool: every schematic, test, firmware build, failure, and physical-run result could improve the system while embedding Hilstart in board bring-up. Early access is already online, and the physical interface gives the company more control than competitors that merely orchestrate existing instruments. That case stings because the data flywheel could begin before revenue is publicly visible. But it remains a forecast, not evidence. Hilstart discloses no completed deployment, repeat usage, benchmark, purchase, or exclusive failure corpus, while Assemble Labs, BootLoop, Liquid Instruments, and Nominal are simultaneously attacking overlapping layers.

Nor can I delete the next financing from the model. There is no pricing, revenue, gross margin, burn, or route to break-even for a business that may require both proprietary hardware and recurring cloud infrastructure. Four co-founders also create an unresolved control question: CEO and CTO titles exist, but the dossier does not identify who has final authority over product, hiring, and financing. Physical complexity is acceptable; ambiguous accountability and unmeasured economics are not.

I pass today. My one verifiable flip condition is a priced or capped round below a $10 million post-money tied to a six-month test in which at least five early-access teams complete repeated board-validation runs, three become paying customers, and Hilstart reports both validation-time reduction and actuation-error rates. That would simultaneously establish cheap convexity, a finite truth-revealing experiment, and evidence that the workflow causes purchases rather than merely generating demos.
