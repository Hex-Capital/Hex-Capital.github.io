# Pennant -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first underwriting fields are blank: no post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or evidence this is genuinely a scarce first check. That does not prove Pennant is being auctioned, but I will not invent cheapness because it is a two-person YC company. Price tells me whether I am financing unresolved belief or purchasing a consensus narrative about AI, governance, and regulatory change. On the available record, the deal gate receives its missing-evidence default.

The company itself is considerably better than the financing record. Ryan Nowicki Stewart has operated inside State Street proxy voting, BlackRock Aladdin, and PJT’s board-advisory work. Tomas Taylor connects institutional product experience with shipped AI work, including AutoGPT contributions and Mindware. Together they form the outsider-insider translation layer I care about: governance professionals understand the policy problem but rarely build modern software, while AI builders rarely understand why an auditable override can matter more than a fluent answer. Pennant has crossed that bridge far enough to sell the product to a large U.S. public pension plan, a Top AmLaw 100 firm, and governance advisors.

Those purchases are the strongest signal. Pennant is not asking me to underwrite 326 LinkedIn followers, a fellowship badge, or generic “engagement.” Enterprise customers are paying for a workflow that applies their policies, flags exceptions, and preserves sources, approvals, overrides, and rationales. That begins to create a proprietary dark pool of institutional decisions. But “begins” matters: the dossier does not establish retained usage, conversion rates, exclusivity, or whether those records create switching costs. Public SEC filings and general-purpose models remain available to ISS, Glass Lewis, Tumelo, Minerva, and internal teams.

The hard bear case is JPMorgan building this function itself. If the most valuable customers own the workflows and data, Pennant may be a prototype for software they eventually internalize rather than a durable vendor. Model errors also land in a high-consequence process, while the proposed safeguards—citations, human review, approvals, and audit trails—are good product design but not a moat. Pennant resembles the enterprise-AI exception represented by Ando only in selling a measurable workflow; the material difference is that Pennant has disclosed neither forecast-level performance nor complete pilot conversion and retention. The exception therefore does not override the missing underwriting evidence.

The second blank gate is what the money proves. “Hire, integrate, and grow” would be a capital-factory plan, not a venture moment. Pennant could instead define a finite test—convert the global asset-manager design partnership into a paid deployment covering a specified number of meetings, with a measured reduction in analyst review time and no material policy errors by a fixed date—but the dossier never says that is the round’s use. Paying customers prove demand; they do not tell me what new truth this check buys. Likewise, custom pricing establishes revenue but no ARR, gross margin, burn, runway, or seed-funded path to break-even. Deleting the Series A from the model leaves an unanswered question.

I am passing today despite a strong team and unusually credible pre-seed customers. The named exception evidence is real, but not yet strong enough to waive both price and capital-purpose gates. One verifiable flip condition would change the decision: a signed financing package below a $10 million post-money valuation that commits the proceeds to converting the asset-manager design partner into a paid, retained deployment by a dated operational milestone. That would show scarce pricing, define the card being flipped, and test whether Pennant’s emerging workflow data becomes owned infrastructure rather than another replaceable AI research layer.
