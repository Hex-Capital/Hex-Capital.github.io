# Ooak Data -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the founders’ résumés. It is that this five-person team turned licensed company workflows into seven-figure contracts with the two largest AI labs within its first year ([YC job posting, Jul 2026](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/ooak-data/jobs/TqbBKm2-technical-ops-manager)). Thomas Aubry brought ML, financial-data, and autonomous-systems experience; Grégoire Lamy had operated product and operations at Epsor; Pierre-Louis Vouteau knew distribution. More importantly, they combined those fragments, survived a pivot, launched APEX Explorer, and sold into the exact problem. Specific knowledge is demonstrated by compressed execution.

If a competitor copies Ooak’s anonymization pipeline tomorrow, the code is not the asset. The surviving asset is the contractual right to use operational data spanning communications, documents, financial figures, and software tools. That can compound into a scarce library of real workflows. But the dossier proves contracts, not yet compounding: the target of 300 company ecosystems remained forward-looking in July 2026, and Mercor already has hundreds of reconstructed enterprise applications. Ooak has found a possible substrate beyond software; it has not yet shown that one acquired workflow can be reused across customers without proportional sourcing and customization.

The economics provide unusually strong pre-seed validation. Two seven-figure lab contracts show a payer, a meaningful price floor, and direct value capture. They do not reveal recognized revenue, renewal behavior, gross margin, or whether Ooak pays up to $300,000 for data that serves only one bespoke engagement. The same evidence therefore supports both sides: extraordinary willingness to pay, but a possible high-end services business whose costs scale with every dataset.

The market is broad enough to maneuver. Tool-using agents need training across documents, communications, and enterprise applications, and the overall training-dataset market is estimated at $4.44 billion in 2026, growing toward $23.18 billion by 2034 ([Fortune Business Insights, 2026](https://www.fortunebusinessinsights.com/ai-training-dataset-market-109241)). Ooak can expand from frontier labs to smaller labs, enterprise AI teams, and agent developers through the same digital-twin capability. Yet this is still a concentrated buyer market: Mercor, Scale AI, and AfterQuery can bundle adjacent environments, while either major customer could internalize production.

The company fits my recurring interest in converting fragmented offline supply into a price-discoverable network, but it is not permissionless yet. Source companies enter through negotiated contracts; no cohort, referral, liquidity, or contribution evidence shows that one participant attracts or improves another participant’s experience. The known exceptions do not need to excuse missing monetization because monetization already exists, and they do not justify inventing a network effect. I see no disclosed integrity breach, artificial financing deadline, or reassurance-seeking red flag; the first-year pivot is updating, not fickleness. The real bear case is structural: privacy constraints, bespoke labor, customer concentration, and better-capitalized competitors could prevent the library from becoming leverage.

I would invest, but as an option on the data rights becoming reusable infrastructure—not on the anonymization software. My 36-month base case is $20 million to $40 million in ARR from three to five major AI customers, still concentrated but supported by a workflow library reused across contracts. One condition flips the decision: if neither current seven-figure lab contract renews by August 2027, I pass, because the supposed durable asset would have behaved like a project.
