# Vestris -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Aahil Valliani and Joshua Tang spent only six weeks processing settlement files before founding Vestris, while Vestris now offers custom order-volume pricing across Washington, D.C. and Montgomery County. That gives Vestris a high-capital-structure, low-round-mispricing signature: a potentially default-alive workflow business wearing the batch’s most consensus costume—AI-native vertical automation. “SaaS is worthless” is too strong; the operational point is that generic SaaS financing can destroy an otherwise sound regional services business, and Vestris has disclosed neither the price nor the financing shape that would prevent that outcome.

**Zapier** is the high rung on capital structure: recurring workflow revenue, extraordinary capital efficiency, and a business that did not need five financing tacks to become durable. Vestris shares the useful mechanism—two founders, enterprise pricing tied to order volume, and a narrow workflow where eliminating 8–10 processor hours can create measurable customer economics. Vestris diverges from Zapier where the analogy matters most: Vestris has disclosed no revenue, repeat order volume, or downside cushion, while expansion potentially requires integrations across more than 3,700 county systems. Vestris looks bootstrappable but not yet proven default-alive, which puts the structure in the middle of the ladder rather than near the high rung.

**Robinhood’s** later consensus rounds illustrate the low-rung failure mechanism on price: a great asset can still become a terrible new check once the markup has already resolved the ambiguity. On Vestris’s round terms there is **no evidence — applying absent-evidence default**. Vestris also calls itself an “AI native platform” in a market where Qualia already has built-in AI, Dono has raised $10.2 million, and RealPact is building real-estate paperwork agents. Vestris therefore sits between a discounted pre-consensus entry and Robinhood-style consensus exposure, but much closer to the latter until actual SAFE terms prove otherwise.

Aahil Valliani’s software patents and the founders’ six-week settlement-company stint give Vestris more pre-pitch conviction than a purely synthetic YC idea. Over the next 18 months, Vestris can compound that experience if every rejected Schedule B clause and underwriter correction becomes operating knowledge competitors cannot cheaply reproduce. The mechanism is fragile, however: six weeks is not the six-month Wizard-of-Oz signal, and Vestris has no founder essays, public repositories, or named category thesis demonstrating missionary commitment before the opportunity appeared. Vestris earns credit above the absent-evidence prior, but not enough to call the conviction asymmetric.

Aahil Valliani’s approximately 2,000 LinkedIn followers and Joshua Tang’s 745 followers are professional reach, not owned distribution. Vestris’s founder-brand score would flip upward if those audiences became a measurable community of title processors producing referrals, product corrections, and repeat orders without paid acquisition. Today Vestris relies on a waitlist, demo-led enterprise sales, and direct outreach; none demonstrates that customers attach to Valliani or Tang as people. Vestris is a sales-led software asset, not a creator or community asset hiding inside a startup cap table.

**Fin** is the single useful category parallel: a manual service exposed the repetitive work before automation became the product. Vestris has the same germ because Valliani and Tang personally processed settlement files before Vestris began automating record retrieval, prorations, title schedules, and lender packages. The parallel breaks after six weeks: Fin embraced prolonged Wizard-of-Oz learning, while Vestris already presents an end-to-end autonomous platform despite leaving commitments subject to underwriter sign-off. Vestris has a Fin-shaped origin but a consensus vertical-AI destination, placing it only slightly above my generic AI-agent exclusion.

Qualia’s $160 million funding base makes the 36-month outcome unusually legible: by July 2029, I expect Vestris to reach roughly $1–3 million ARR and be acquired by Qualia for less than $40 million rather than raise an independent Series A. Vestris can win several regional firms because saving 8–10 hours per file is concrete, but Vestris’s county-by-county integrations are more likely to become a useful incumbent feature bundle than a standalone network moat. That is a respectable software outcome; at an ordinary hot-YC SAFE price, it is not a venture outcome for my own money.

**Ro** is the Known Exception that makes the opposing case sting: fragmented regulatory rails can turn apparent capital inefficiency into the moat itself. Vestris could become the Ro of title infrastructure if its claimed decades of approved and rejected titles, underwriter corrections, and 3,700-county integration burden create a data advantage that Qualia cannot reproduce. Vestris has not earned that exception yet—the dataset’s ownership and exclusivity are undisclosed, coverage spans only two jurisdictions, no customers or revenue are named, and no security certification is public despite Vestris handling contracts and lender packages. I would flip from Pass to Invest if Vestris reaches $1 million ARR across at least 10 jurisdictions before raising more than $2 million; that would prove the county regatta is capital-efficient rather than founder-draining.
