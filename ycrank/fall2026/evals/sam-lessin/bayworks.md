# Bayworks -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Eighty dealerships in 110 days is enough to make me take Bayworks seriously. It is not enough to price a check. The dossier gives me neither the proposed post-money valuation nor competing offers, and it does not say what specific uncertainty my money resolves. I will not infer an auction from YC membership. Equally, I cannot infer scarce capital from financing silence. Bayworks has evidence of customer access; the deal and the venture moment remain undocumented.

The interesting mechanism is capturing information where it originates. A technician dictates against a repair-order line, the product asks for missing findings, and a human reviews the resulting narrative before it returns to the DMS. That connects shop-floor knowledge with the administrative requirements of reimbursement—a translation layer inside an operationally messy business. The reported 80-plus dealership deployments suggest access beyond a demo funnel. But the 4,000 daily repairs describe those dealerships’ activity, not verified Bayworks usage. Neither that number nor the unnamed customer’s $3 billion revenue establishes purchases, retention, or cash generation for this company.

The strongest case against passing is that the founders have already crossed the hardest practical barrier: getting dealerships to put a young company into their repair workflow. Employer-provisioned accounts and deployment within an unnamed top-50 dealer group could make a narrow documentation product valuable before it becomes technologically exceptional. Goyal has also shipped an actual application, rather than merely supplying a backend-engineering résumé. That earns some founder credit, although neither his Navi, Scapia, and Cisco experience nor Bansal’s disclosed background establishes operating experience on the dealership side. The speed of deployment is meaningful; it does not establish that the team understands warranty economics.

Who owns the advantage if this works? Bayworks says operational data belongs to the dealership, and cross-customer learning rights remain unverified. Meanwhile, Tekion already offers native technician voice documentation. That activates my concern about application companies whose customers and incumbent platforms control the durable assets. Technician review is a sensible response to model errors, so I would not accuse Bayworks of assuming perfect automation. But the dossier does not measure review labor, reimbursement outcomes, or an advantage that survives native bundling. Its workflow access is promising; a proprietary dark pool is not yet demonstrated.

I pass at **37/100**. There is no disclosed pricing, margin, burn, or runway from which to establish a business that survives without another financing. Those omissions receive the specified defaults, not invented evidence of bad economics. I checked the exception case: unusually strong paid conversion, retention, cash generation, or contractual access could outweigh missing financing evidence, but deployment count alone does not establish those receipts. My one verifiable flip condition is a documented financing below $10 million post-money that funds a six-month test of whether the existing dealership base can generate recurring customer gross profit sufficient to cover operating burn. That would connect the promising workflow to a price and a truth-revealing use of capital.
