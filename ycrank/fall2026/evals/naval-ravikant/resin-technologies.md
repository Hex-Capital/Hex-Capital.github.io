# Resin Technologies -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Resin Technologies has built something that makes its founders’ experience relevant. Koosha’s electronics work at Bosch and Kodak, followed by robotics engineering, puts him near the consequences of a bad board. Anmol brings enterprise AI deployment experience from You.com. More persuasive than either résumé is their October 2026 launch: an editor connecting requirements, schematics, component footprints, and routing ([YC launch](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/VG7-resin-codex-for-electronics)). That is evidence of action. It earns meaningful founder credit, although the dossier does not identify previously fabricated boards, engineering customers, or measured outcomes establishing exceptional knowledge of this exact workflow.

The decisive question is what Resin owns after another team reproduces the editor. Its proprietary routing engine is a concrete candidate, but “proprietary” establishes ownership, not superiority. There is no comparative evidence that it produces better manufacturable boards or improves with deployment. Customer design history might eventually make switching inconvenient, but that remains an inference. Resin prohibits training on customer designs; I cannot assume installations feed a proprietary learning system ([Security](https://resineda.com/security)). Helping customers build hardware does not automatically give the software company a hardware moat.

The strongest argument against passing is that Resin could own the feedback between schematic intent and physical layout. Combining those stages could expose mistakes that separate tools miss. The founders’ complementary experience makes that possibility credible, and Resin’s broader workflow distinguishes it from Quilter’s layout focus and CELUS’s component-and-schematic emphasis. I can accept an absent network when a prototype demonstrates exceptional capability. Here, however, routing remains in beta, comparative validation is unavailable, and Flux already competes with an integrated AI editor. The exception would require demonstrated product insight; integration alone does not establish it.

Resin does fit my preference for tools that turn individual knowledge into scalable output. Self-service access lets an engineer attempt work without buying an enterprise implementation. But the dossier shows no mechanism through which one engineer makes the product more useful for another; founder onboarding and promotional credits are acquisition efforts, not a demonstrated user loop. The economics are more legible: $199 monthly for Pro and $149 per user for Team identify a payer and a direct charging mechanism ([Pricing](https://resineda.com/)). Those are offered prices, with no evidence yet of completed payments or contribution margins. The cited $6.1 billion PCB design software market establishes substantial existing spend, but Resin has not shown how its initial buyer leads into additional buyers or transactions.

I would pass with my own money today. Neither the short operating history nor the absence of revenue creates that decision; the unresolved issue is whether engineering effort becomes an asset competitors cannot readily reproduce. I see no documented integrity or financing-pressure red flag, and the explicit warnings about incorrect wiring are not evidence of dishonesty. My one verifiable flip condition is an independent, reproducible fabrication benchmark showing Resin’s proprietary router delivers materially fewer board respins than competing workflows across multiple customers. That would turn the strongest bull case into evidence and make this a different investment.
