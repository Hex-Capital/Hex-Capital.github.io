# Exosat -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Edward Ge has already done the expensive learning. He built CubeSats and rockets, co-founded two space companies, launched two Aethero satellites, and reportedly sold millions of dollars to Blue Origin, Booz Allen Hamilton, and AFRL. The $109,740 Air Force SBIR adds an externally verifiable transaction in satellite edge computing. This is specific knowledge acquired through hardware, launches, and customers—not credentials projected onto a fashionable market.

The product also survives the copy-the-code test better than most pre-seed companies. Exosat has published ITU filings for an 11,000-satellite constellation and proposes an ITAR/EAR-free hardware stack operating across S-, L-, C-, and Ka-band links. Spectrum coordination, flight heritage, manufacturing relationships, and network operations can become durable assets. But potential durability is not present durability: the $300,000 satellite cost and days-long production claim remain unvalidated until the first launch, currently booked for April 2027. A filing is an option on infrastructure, not infrastructure itself.

The sovereign wedge is large and coherent. Governments and operators outside the United States and China have a principal-agent problem: connectivity controlled by a foreign constellation can disappear when political incentives change. Exosat can enter through satellite IoT, then sell capacity, broadband, direct-to-device links, or complete national constellations using the same underlying capability. The independently forecast $25 billion LEO communications market and $6.51 billion satellite-IoT market provide room to maneuver. Starlink’s $11.4 billion of 2025 revenue also proves demand while demonstrating how punishing the required deployment scale can become.

The strongest bear case is arithmetic. One founder is proposing an 11,000-satellite system against Starlink, 13 deployed AST SpaceMobile satellites, Lynk’s operating fleet, and Sateliot’s nearly €100 million financing. Even at the claimed $300,000 manufacturing cost, the filed constellation implies billions in satellite hardware before launches, gateways, operations, replacements, or regulatory coordination. ITU milestones turn the spectrum position into an execution liability if capital and deployment do not arrive on schedule. The company could possess the right geopolitical insight and still die between a filing and a functioning network.

The $400 million-plus of reported LOIs across six nations makes that bear case less decisive, but not yet false. LOIs show that sovereignty is a purchasing concern; they do not prove price, procurement authority, gross margin, or completed transactions. Early pilots help, yet no named payer, binding capacity contract, or revenue is public. The usual exception for missing monetization requires either a demonstrated product or a self-reinforcing user network. Exosat has neither on orbit today.

This matches my recent preference for infrastructure bottlenecks, but not the permissionless pattern in which each participant creates value for the next. Dedicated national constellations require capital, integration, and continuing company labor; six prospective customers do not constitute a network effect. I find no evidence of unfair dealing, artificial round pressure, or the other founder red flags that would veto the investment, but integrity remains untested rather than proven. I would write a small option-sized check because founder-specific knowledge, scarce spectrum positioning, and a real sovereign market produce nonlinear upside. The verifiable flip condition is technical: if Exosat has not completed an NTN communications link through its first satellite by April 2028, I pass on further capital.
