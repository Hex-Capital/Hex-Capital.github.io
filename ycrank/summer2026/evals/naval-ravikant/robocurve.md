# Robocurve -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Jay Chooi’s AI-evaluation research and Aris Zhu’s robotics deployment experience give Robocurve unusually deep specific knowledge, while one completed pilot and 96 GitHub stars reveal no compounding user network. That high-knowledge, low-network pairing defines Robocurve: a builder-tool created by genuine builders, but still an evaluation instrument rather than a platform. Robocurve fits my unstated builder-product pattern; the question is whether expert code can escape the gravity of physical, labor-bound testing.

**Stack Overflow** is the high rung for specific knowledge: developers with years of lived frustration encoded their knowledge into infrastructure for other developers. Robocurve shares that mechanism. Chooi contributed to Inspect Evals and researched at the UK AI Security Institute; Zhu built navigation and fleet-management systems at Amazon Robotics and Yondu Robotics. Robocurve diverges because Stack Overflow’s knowledge immediately became user-generated knowledge, while Inspect Robots’ 40-plus VLA and 260-model integrations remain founder-produced infrastructure. The specific knowledge is real. Its compounding vessel is unfinished.

**Perplexity** lacked a marketplace or direct user-added network, surviving that low-rung mechanism only because Aravind Srinivas brought extreme retrieval expertise, permissionless product distribution, and pre-consensus AI timing. Robocurve also lacks evidence that its Nth user improves the product for the first: one physical-robot pilot, 13 forks, and requests for benchmark contributions do not constitute a network. The Perplexity Known Exception partly applies because Robocurve combines open-source distribution with rare founder knowledge, but Robocurve’s physical operations make the exception weaker. A benchmark becomes a network only when researchers, vendors, and buyers depend on everyone else publishing through it.

Inspect Robots v1 distributes through an MIT-licensed GitHub repository, so Robocurve begins with code leverage: 96 stars, 13 forks, reusable tooling, and integrations spanning more than 260 language models. Over 18 months, Robocurve can compound if outside laboratories contribute adapters and results faster than Robocurve adds physical labor. If every credible evaluation instead requires Robocurve to acquire hardware, reproduce kitchens, and sell bespoke engagements, Robocurve becomes a consulting laboratory wearing an open-source interface. Code is permissionless. Robots, facilities, and customer-funded evaluations are not.

No public TAM exists for Robocurve, but the counterfactual for a higher market score is clear: Robocurve must show that one evaluation substrate expands from model benchmarking into procurement assurance, regulatory certification, fleet monitoring, and training-data feedback without rebuilding the physical stack for every robot. General-purpose robotics targeting 2028 provides room, while KitchenBench and DataCenterBench already imply adjacent task domains. Robocurve falls a tier if those environments remain disconnected bespoke projects; a collection of narrow benchmarks is not automatically a large market.

**Twitter** passed the 10-year-partner test because Jack Dorsey shipped and Ev Williams carried a clean operating history; credentials mattered less than demonstrated behavior over time. Robocurve shows intelligence and energy through Chooi’s ACM EC, ICML, ACL, and EMNLP work and Zhu’s Amazon Robotics and IEEE record. Robocurve provides no evidence about prior partnerships, treatment of colleagues, or long-duration integrity, so I will not manufacture it from Harvard affiliations. The Buffett trio is three traits, not two logos and an inference.

One completed physical-robot pilot points to my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Robocurve will have pivoted from an independent public benchmark publisher into a paid robotics evaluation-and-assurance platform producing $1 million to $3 million in ARR, without becoming a meaningful marketplace. Robocurve’s founder knowledge should win technically demanding evaluations; Robocurve’s weak network mechanics should push monetization toward recurring vendor or buyer assurance. Specific knowledge creates the wedge. The absence of user compounding determines the shape.

**METR** supplies the strongest bear case: independent evaluation can be institutionally valuable without becoming venture-scale, while robot vendors already own the hardware, telemetry, and incentive to internalize testing. Robocurve’s MIT license permits replication, physical evaluation consumes capital and labor, and accepting vendor funding could compromise the independence Robocurve sells. The counterweight is the working v1, one real pilot, and a founder pairing that spans evaluation science and deployed robotics—more substance than a generic standards proposal. I would write the wide-funnel angel check, but zero repeat or paid evaluations by July 2027 would verify that Robocurve built a respected research artifact rather than a business and flip my verdict to Pass.
