# Runtime.si -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Runtime.si could make robot reliability cheaper to establish. That matters: if developers can discover deployment failures in simulation, physical testing stops being the bottleneck for every policy revision. But the dossier does not quantify today’s testing cost, the fraction Runtime can eliminate, or the resulting increase in deployable robot capability. The forecast $2.25 billion simulation-software market is a supplier revenue pool, not a calculation of societal consequence. I can imagine the enormous outcome. I cannot credit my imagination as company evidence.

The strongest evidence is that the founders have built something technically inspectable. Nomad validates proposed actions against robot limits, executes them in simulation, and records task outcomes. Their October 15 research reports 152/525 successes without skills versus 283/525 for selected skill cohorts. That is approximately 1.86x, with retrospective, in-sample selection—not an established performance improvement. More fundamentally, better policy performance inside a harness does not establish that the harness predicts physical failures. There is no measured advantage against SignalFlag, Duality, or an internally assembled NVIDIA workflow. The implementation earns some technical credit; the claimed discontinuity remains unproven.

The strongest argument for writing a check is the team’s relevant combination of skills: manipulation and manufacturing experience, control and reinforcement learning, and GRASP research. Runtime also explicitly discloses limitations in its experiments. These are useful foundations for turning a scarce robotics workflow into a repeatable technical platform, an investment pattern I favor. But the proprietary learning loop is still hypothetical: no physical deployment outcomes are shown feeding back into better test selection. Shared Penn affiliations do not establish insular recruiting, and conflicting Lambda Robotics job descriptions do not establish productive belief revision. Neither deserves a fabricated conclusion. The dossier supplies no observed revision or difficult complementary recruit, so that dimension stays at its missing-evidence default.

My first dollar would attack simulation-to-reality validity. Ten thousand announced simulations could multiply useful coverage—or multiply false confidence. The published work establishes neither a prospective physical-transfer threshold nor a costed experiment with a stop condition. Four advertised openings do not prove premature scaling, but they do not resolve that uncertainty either. Similarly, absent revenue is ordinary at this stage; absent evaluation economics leaves a different question unanswered. Runtime has not disclosed simulation compute, environment maintenance, physical validation, or engineering costs per useful failure discovered. Software can still be expensive when every customer needs custom robotics work.

I pass at 27/100. My exceptions permit an incomplete product when a documented instigator and an enormous, concrete consequence justify buying the option. Runtime’s relevant founders and working harness make that case worth investigating, but do not yet establish it. There is no evidence here of an avoided business category or a generic AI wrapper; the decision turns on unproven leverage. **My flip condition is one independently reproducible, prospective customer trial showing that Runtime’s predeclared tests reduce fully loaded physical-validation cost by at least 5x at an equal or lower rate of missed deployment failures versus that customer’s existing workflow.** That would connect the technical artifact to the economic bottleneck. Then a small check could purchase a consequential option.
