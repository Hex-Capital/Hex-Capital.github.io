# Wingman Law -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Wingman Law’s potentially important secret is that owning the legal practice lets AI improve case execution enough to capture economics unavailable to firms buying software. That proposition is testable: compare attorney hours, net recoveries, and contribution profit across comparable claims. But it is my reconstruction. The dossier supplies neither a named prevailing belief the founders reject nor results that test it. Direct representation changes where Wingman collects revenue; whether it creates a fundamentally better business remains open.

The strongest objection to passing is the founders’ unusually relevant operating experience. Nicholas Le has handled 700+ injury cases; Sun Woo Lee combines litigation experience with legaltech development and $350K of prior legaltech sales. Their combined plaintiff and insurance-defense experience could help identify where insurers exploit procedural friction. Wingman also fits my less-public interest in adversarial information systems: medical evidence, policy language, and insurer behavior determine who gets paid. These credentials make the operating hypothesis credible enough to investigate. They do not establish its truth, and neither the $350K nor StrongSuit’s $4M ARR belongs to Wingman.

The ownership question is decisive. Wingman develops technology internally, but its public visualization uses licensed litigation data, and the dossier identifies no exclusive dataset or measured technical advantage. EvenUp and Supio sell tools to incumbent firms; they are not interchangeable with Wingman. Their existence nevertheless means competitors can purchase overlapping automation. California accident victims provide an initial service territory, but there is no evidence that serving this territory creates a monopoly mechanism. Accumulating case files would become meaningful only if those files produced an advantage that competitors could not readily reproduce. That remains an inferred possibility.

The founding structure earns limited affirmative credit. Lee hired Le before he became a co-founder several months later, giving them an actual working relationship. Essential technology is described as internally developed. Ownership, economic exposure, and each founder’s full-time status remain undisclosed, so the evidence cannot support a high alignment score. The dossier establishes none of my specific founding red flags—part-time work, outsourced core engineering, or investor-dominated ownership. California’s AI guidance introduces supervision and confidentiality obligations, but it supplies no evidence that Wingman faces an enforcement order or lacks a viable operating arrangement.

Contingency fees give Wingman one coherent revenue engine. They also make acquisition cost, case duration, expenses, and recovery probability central to the economics. The fee percentage and margins are undisclosed; the $57.8B sector estimate cannot fill those blanks. Founder introductions and Bay Area branded-car advertising are concrete acquisition routes, earning modest credit, but their conversion and cost are unknown. No public client or recovery figures establish a repeatable channel. Those absences are normal for Fall 2026 pre-seed; they leave the investment thesis unverified rather than demonstrating failure.

I would pass on a check today. The bull case is substantial: experienced legal operators could capture the full value of automation by delivering the service themselves. My known exceptions would matter if Wingman showed an independently verified technical discontinuity, persistent customer behavior overcoming an apparent obstacle, or a concrete deployment result resolving the central risk. None is documented here. My one flip condition is an independently audited cohort of comparable closed claims showing that Wingman’s proprietary system produces at least twice the contribution profit per case of conventional practice, after acquisition and attorney-supervision costs. That would turn the proposed secret into evidence worth underwriting.
