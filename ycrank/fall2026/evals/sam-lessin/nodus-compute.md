# Nodus Compute -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Nodus Compute reports roughly $500,000 of YC funding, but that tells me neither the price of my check nor what it buys. Proposed post-money, competing offers, and capital scarcity are undocumented. I will not infer an auction from YC affiliation. I also cannot infer a bargain. More consequentially, the dossier never connects the round to one finite experiment with a deadline and a result that changes the company’s value. Saving customers money is a product proposition; proving that recoverable workloads make otherwise unusable GPU capacity economically reliable could be a venture moment.

The ten founder-reported startup pilots and claimed savings above 30% are the strongest evidence here. Those claims deserve modest credit, with their provenance kept intact: an October 2026 social post available through a mirrored search snippet, without payment status or measurement methodology. The compelling possibility is that Nodus becomes the execution layer customers entrust with recurring jobs. But the dossier does not establish that its owned workflow caused repeat purchases, that it controls exclusive GPU supply, or that accumulated workload data improves results across customers. LinkedIn followers and SDK stars add nothing to that case.

The product does fit my pattern of backing bridges between systems: holders of idle GPU commitments on one side, teams needing dependable execution on the other. Checkpoint integrity checks and generation fencing address a real obstacle to making that bridge usable. The strongest bull case is that routing plus recovery produces savings customers cannot obtain simply by shopping hourly prices. The material qualification is that recovery restores saved files so customer code can reload checkpoints; ephemeral workloads are excluded, and the pilot has no standard SLA. Whether the bridge works economically depends on completed jobs, recovery overhead, and customer intervention—not the cheapest available GPU.

That distinction matters against Modal’s multi-cloud execution platform and SF Compute’s capacity resale market. Both already occupy parts of the proposed bridge. Nodus needs a reason customers keep their workloads with it after competing vendors offer similar routing. This activates my concern about advantages that disappear as infrastructure becomes broadly available, although the dossier does not establish that Nodus depends on model scarcity or assumes errors vanish. Published H100 supply pricing of $2.600 an hour, before Nodus fees, gives me no contribution margin. Delete the next financing: burn, runway, support costs, and the route to break-even remain unknown. These are underwriting gaps, not evidence of an uneconomic business.

Kotra and Varshney have relevant infrastructure exposure, met at Scale AI, and have put a product and public SDK into the world. Varshney’s documented Google cost-optimization work is particularly relevant. Their internships do not, however, establish that either has operated GPU supply or delivered reliable customer workloads at scale. I see a plausible technical team, without demonstrated operating receipts on both sides of this market. Nor does having two co-founders establish the equal-control committee I worry about; decision authority is simply undisclosed.

I pass on the evidence available. My exceptions require unusually strong conversion, cash economics, proprietary access, or an explicit financing structure; Nodus’s reported pilots have not yet supplied that override. The single result that would move me into active underwriting is invoice-reconciled evidence that five existing pilots remain paying customers after 90 days at positive contribution margin, including recovery and support costs. That would demonstrate a functioning base business worth pricing. Today, the savings claim is promising, but the durable economic asset remains unproven.
