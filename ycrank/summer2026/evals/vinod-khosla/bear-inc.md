# Bear -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence worth buying here is radically cheaper accounting expertise for small businesses. Bear addresses an existing spending pool: the dossier cites $740.1 billion in global accounting services in 2026. But that number includes work Bear has not demonstrated it can replace. There is no quantified reduction in accounting labor, customer cost, or businesses left unserved. I can see the expensive workflow. I cannot yet calculate the consequence of this particular technology succeeding.

The strongest bull case is that Bear owns both the ledger and the service. Replacing Procountor, Fennoa, or Netvisor gives its agents control over the underlying accounting system, while accountant-reviewed outputs could supply corrective feedback. The founders report more than 10 paying customers; the company reports over $80 million processed for dozens of Finnish businesses. Those are meaningful pre-seed signals of actual deployment. The $80 million is transaction volume, however, and establishes neither revenue nor automation efficiency. My preference for scarce expert workflows becoming repeatable platforms gives Bear a reason to investigate.

The decisive uncertainty sits inside the human review step. If accountants must reconstruct the work to trust it, agent activity may leave the expensive bottleneck intact. Bear supplies no measured comparison of review time, error rates, or cost against an incumbent accounting workflow. Balance already combines agents with accountant signoff, and Skalar combines proprietary technology with tax professionals. Owning the ledger creates an engineering opportunity; the dossier does not establish a 5–10x technical advantage. Nor does the fixed-price bundle disclose whether customers receive lower costs or whether Bear absorbs more labor.

The disclosed evaluation process deserves some credit for attacking correctness. Recurring comparisons against validated outputs, consenting design partners, and audit trails address a real failure mode rather than merely adding features. But the dossier supplies no acceptance threshold, experiment budget, or decision rule for what happens when automation fails. I want dollars spent against errors and reviewer minutes removed. Bear has described a relevant feedback process without yet showing a decisive risk-removal milestone.

Sami and Riki’s financial-data infrastructure experience, including Riki’s petabyte-scale work at Token Terminal, makes this a credible engineering team. It does not document rapid belief revision or difficult complementary recruiting. The original founders’ finance and law backgrounds could matter, but their current operating roles are unconfirmed. I would not turn that ambiguity into a team red flag. My exceptions for execution-led financial infrastructure and unusually committed founders prevent an automatic rejection of a service business; Bear’s paying customers strengthen that opposing case. They still do not establish the exceptional mechanism needed to override the missing technical and economic evidence. There is also no basis to accuse this team of exit-seeking, permanent subsidy dependence, or resisting contrary evidence.

I pass with my own money today. Bear could build a useful accounting firm, but the evidence does not yet price an option on accounting abundance. **My flip condition is one independently checked, matched-workload production study showing at least 5x lower fully loaded delivery cost than a conventional outsourced accountant, at equal or better filing accuracy, including all human review and exception handling.** That would directly test whether the proprietary ledger and feedback loop remove the expert bottleneck.
