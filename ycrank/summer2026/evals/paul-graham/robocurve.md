# Robocurve -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Inspect Robots v1 already connects 40-plus VLAs and more than 260 language models, but Robocurve has completed only one physical-robot pilot. That makes Robocurve a schlep-rich, animal-evidence-light startup. The founders have built unusually broad machinery for doing unpleasant work, yet the dossier contains no cereal-box moment: no customer wrestled into paying, no rejection overturned, no manual stunt that makes me say Jay Chooi and Aris Zhu simply will not stop. Robocurve has found a promising schlep before proving that its founders are animals.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness because compliance was not incidental to the product; enduring it became the advantage. Robocurve shares that mechanism. Standardizing kitchens and data centers, operating physical robots, preserving trace logs, and reproducing results across different form factors are precisely the jobs software-only founders avoid. Robocurve diverges from Coinbase where the schlep meets demand: Coinbase users urgently needed a compliant way to hold bitcoin, while Robocurve has one pilot, no published comparative results, and no revenue signal establishing that laboratories will pay an outsider to grade them. This is still one of the strongest schlep scores I would give at pre-seed.

**Kiko** (2005) exposed the low-rung failure mechanism: shipping software is insufficient if the founders eventually optimize for an exit from the problem rather than relentlessly finding a way through it. Robocurve is above that rung because Robocurve shipped Inspect Robots v1, integrated hundreds of models, attracted 96 GitHub stars and 13 forks, and ran a physical pilot. But breadth is not yet animal resourcefulness. Nothing in Robocurve’s dossier resembles Airbnb selling 1,000 cereal boxes at $40 each or Armstrong building a wallet to reverse a rejection. The artifact is real; the tracks of the animal are faint.

Jay Chooi worked on Inspect Evals at the UK AI Security Institute, while Aris Zhu built navigation and fleet-management systems at Amazon Robotics and Yondu Robotics. That makes Robocurve’s idea substantially organic: evaluation software and deployed robots are the founders’ prior worlds joined together. Over the next 18 months, this compounds if Robocurve’s own frustration with inconsistent testing lets Robocurve design tasks faster than outsiders can specify them. It fails to compound if the founders merely observed an institutional gap, because then every new robot category becomes consulting work rather than accumulated product knowledge. The missing fact is whether Chooi or Zhu built the first benchmark to solve a problem they personally could not escape.

Inspect Robots is MIT-licensed and has 96 GitHub stars, which puts Robocurve above technical-by-credential-only founders. Chooi’s evaluation contributions and Zhu’s robotics systems show that Robocurve can build across software and machines, but Zhu has no public repositories and Chooi’s named personal repository has 9 stars. The hacker-genealogy score would jump a tier if the repository history showed that the 40-plus VLA integrations were predominantly built by the founders before fundraising, or if outside roboticists were already contributing difficult hardware adapters. Without that, Robocurve looks like two capable researchers who shipped, not yet like hackers whose trail existed before the startup.

**Stripe** (2010) is the useful single-anchor parallel for founder configuration: two founders with complementary abilities working on a problem spanning code and an obstinate external system. Robocurve also has two founders, with Chooi covering evaluation and Zhu covering physical robotics. Robocurve does not yet earn the Collison-level score because “both studied at Harvard” does not establish that Chooi and Zhu built together for years, survived failure together, or even worked closely before Robocurve. The pair is correctly shaped, but chemistry is asserted by the org chart rather than demonstrated by history.

One completed physical-robot pilot leads me to predict that by July 2029 Robocurve will have narrowed from general independent robot benchmarking to paid pre-deployment certification for warehouse and data-center robots, reaching $1 million to $3 million in ARR. Robocurve’s physical evaluation schlep should produce customer value sooner than its open benchmark produces a standard, while the weak animal evidence makes a broad category-defining platform less likely. Robocurve can become a good business by productizing the manual work before it becomes the measurement layer for all robotics.

MIT licensing, 13 forks, one unpublished pilot, and no conflict-management policy form the strongest bear case: Robocurve could become a respected open-source project while robot vendors keep evaluations in-house and paid independence proves structurally awkward. Robocurve’s answer is that vendors can copy Inspect Robots but cannot cheaply copy continuously operated physical environments, comparable trace data, and the accumulated knowledge of making tests reproducible. The schlep is the potential moat, not the repository. I would invest, narrowly, because Robocurve has already crossed from benchmark prose into a physical pilot. I would flip to Pass if Robocurve cannot secure two robot vendors paying for repeat evaluations within 12 months; that would show the schlep is merely expensive rather than valuable.
