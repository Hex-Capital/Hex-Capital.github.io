# ObsessionDB -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Agent workloads may turn warehouse query frequency into a much larger expense; ObsessionDB sells infrastructure to the teams absorbing that expense, and its controlling variable is the cost of serving a production workload at a fixed latency and reliability target. The investment works if its storage and cache architecture sustains a material advantage after competitors respond. Numia’s migration establishes an economic opening against BigQuery. It does not yet establish the advantage against ClickHouse Cloud that determines whether this becomes a large independent company.

The June 2026 Numia case study is substantive: 200 TB migrated, with reported monthly costs falling from approximately $8,000 to $2,000. A 75% reduction can fund migration effort and create a purchasing decision. Shared object storage, stateless compute, and locality-aware NVMe caching provide a plausible technical explanation. However, this is a company-published customer result, and customer savings do not establish ObsessionDB’s gross margins. MotherDuck’s September observation of 29 times more MCP queries than human UI queries supports a possible demand acceleration; it does not establish that those queries require ObsessionDB’s architecture.

There is already more product pull than I would ordinarily require at pre-seed. The October launch describes millions of customer API requests and a customer serving APIs over 50 TB while ingesting hundreds of thousands of events per minute. The CEO reports paying production customers and monthly petabyte processing. These remain company-reported figures, but production API dependence is substantially more informative than registrations or a demo. The launch quota bottleneck adds modest corroboration, although a low initial cloud quota can also produce that symptom. I would give this meaningful demand credit without inventing retention or revenue from throughput.

The strongest objection is that ObsessionDB creates savings customers want while the upstream supplier captures the category. ClickHouse reportedly reached $250 million in annualized revenue and raised $400 million in January 2026. This market is already crystallizing around a well-funded operator that controls the underlying engine. ObsessionDB’s proprietary SharedMergeTree and cache implementation constitute real engineering, but the dossier does not establish how expensive they are to reproduce. Better models could increase demand for this database without improving its competitive position. Owning storage and serving production queries gives the company operational depth; it still needs a durable reason for customers to choose its implementation.

The founders make that competitive bet more credible. They previously operated Numia together and are productizing infrastructure developed around workloads they understood firsthand. Höffl and Leuthardt also have documented prior company-building and acquisition experience. This is strong evidence of accumulated startup learning, without needing to infer motivation from credentials. Their first warehouse deployment could become distribution for additional data services, but the dossier earns no credit for that expansion yet. Capital should first prove repeatable migrations and production operations. Conflicting encryption disclosures and unsupported native backup operations create specific obstacles to that repeatability.

I lean toward investing my own money at this stage. The combination of demonstrated customer savings, production use, and repeat operators supports an infrastructure bet across the AI demand shift. The upstream competitor limits conviction, rather than erasing the evidence already present. **I would reverse that lean if an independently reproduced comparison on representative customer workloads put ClickHouse Cloud within 20% of ObsessionDB’s total operating cost at equivalent latency and reliability.** That result would undermine the economic reason to accept migration and supplier risk.
