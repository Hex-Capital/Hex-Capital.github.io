# Datoric -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that Datoric sells training data. It is that Nikhil Reddy learned the weakness of annotation platforms from inside them. In 2023 he helped automate more than 15,000 hours of paid work by bypassing their QA systems. The founders then built the inverse: approved contributors, private capture apps, isolated customer projects, specification-based review, and sample-level rights records. This is a named obstacle followed by a shipped workaround. The missing piece is a dated account of how quickly a particular failure produced each system, so I would not give full resourcefulness credit.

The technical proof is unusually large for pre-seed. Datoric has assembled more than 300,000 active contributors and a commercial computer-use dataset containing 250,000 traces and roughly 15,000 hours of screen activity. Those are inspectable artifacts, not a diagram of what the founders intend to build after funding. Jeffrey Lin’s procedural-video benchmark and robotics background add credibility, but the deployed collection machinery matters more than his résumé. This matches the pattern I care about most in recent investments: difficult infrastructure already operating before the round.

Nearly seven figures of revenue in 30 days proves that someone wants the product badly enough to pay. It does not yet prove a narrow well of durable users. Datoric names voice-model, robotics, and world-model teams as the likely niche, but discloses neither customers nor customer count, retention, repeat purchases, or revenue concentration. One large custom collection could produce the reported month and then disappear. I therefore count the revenue as strong demand evidence without pretending it is recurring revenue.

The rapid move from partnership-intelligence product Arzule in Winter 2026 to Datoric in Summer 2026 looks like running-back behavior, and the resulting revenue makes mere flailing less likely. But the dossier supplies no stable mission, rejected hypothesis, customer observation, or explanation of the transition. I can see that the route changed quickly; I cannot see why. The founders’ prior shared work is nevertheless useful evidence against the application-assembled-cofounder red flag.

The schlep is real. Every sample needs a contributor, valid consent, a commercial license, a collection record, QA, and isolation from other customers. A single broken link can contaminate a buyer’s rights to train on the data. Larger competitors have vastly more resources—Scale AI reported a $1.5 billion annualized run rate, Turing $300 million ARR, and Mercor $2 billion annualized revenue—but their scale may make private, project-specific provenance harder rather than easier. Datoric’s founder-originated knowledge of how contributor systems are attacked is consequently more valuable than the market-growth estimate.

The strongest bear case is that Datoric is a services company temporarily enjoying one concentrated AI-lab contract. Its 300,000 contributors could become a fraud surface rather than a moat, the Hugging Face repository has no visible engagement, and incumbents can copy provenance workflows while subsidizing sales from much larger businesses. No known exception needs to rescue this company, and no partnership or ceremonial-launch red flag presently contradicts the investment; the working system, paid demand, and founder-led operating posture clear the ordinary gate. But uncertainty about repeat demand keeps this below Strong Invest. I would write the check. I would reverse that decision if audited invoices showed that more than 80% of the reported 30-day revenue came from one nonrenewing project.
