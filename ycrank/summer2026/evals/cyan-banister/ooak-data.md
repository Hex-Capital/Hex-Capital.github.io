# Ooak Data -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: software agents are doing real work across inboxes, spreadsheets, financial systems, and internal tools, but they cannot learn that work from sanitized chat transcripts. Ooak Data’s chain is unusually legible—license actual company workflows, anonymize them without destroying their structure, construct digital twins, then train agents inside them. The present-day leak is not the $4.44 billion dataset TAM; it is that two leading AI labs have reportedly signed seven-figure contracts while frontier agents still complete fewer than half of realistic tasks. That is a credible glimpse of what is coming, though the dossier does not establish renewals or how much of each contract has converted into deployed training environments.

The costly behavior is promising but not yet cleanly observable. Seven-figure commitments from two sophisticated buyers would normally be spectacular pre-seed evidence, yet the figures come from Ooak’s own recruiting material, with no dates, recognized revenue, repeat usage, or customer confirmation. APEX Explorer is a working public artifact, but it explores Mercor’s dataset rather than proving Ooak’s core workflow-twin pipeline. The 13,990-row LSAT dataset and its 300 monthly downloads show that the team can publish data products; they do not verify that companies are repeatedly surrendering operational records or that labs are training on the resulting twins.

What pulls me closer is the layer beneath the fashionable agent demo. Licensed cross-tool workflows could become scarce infrastructure shared by many model developers, and Ooak controls more of that input than a thin model wrapper would. What pushes me away is that Mercor already has hundreds of reconstructed applications, AfterQuery operates real APIs, and Scale AI can bundle environments with a much broader data platform. Acquiring 300 company ecosystems in six months is not yet a moat; it is an operating promise. If anonymization preserves too little structure, the data loses value. If it preserves too much, people and companies can be re-identified. That tension is the product, not a compliance chore.

The founders have relevant pieces without yet showing pre-company obsession. Thomas Aubry brings robotics, Samsung ML, PayLead data leadership, and an earlier autonomous-vehicle company; Grégoire Lamy spent more than five years moving through product and operations at Epsor; Pierre-Louis Vouteau brings distribution and quick-commerce experience. Together they cover technology, production, and enterprise sourcing, and the CEO, COO/CPO, and CTO roles are at least explicit. But the company pivoted during its first year, Thomas joined later, and the dossier provides no dated sequence of experiments showing that this particular problem pulled them in before agent training became competitive. Six open roles demonstrate ambition, not yet magnetic recruiting or commercial command.

The weakest part of the case is agency. Static benchmarks are inadequate, but the dossier does not identify a rule extracting rent from workers or customers, nor an institution whose control Ooak returns to an individual. Source companies may receive up to $300,000, yet the people whose communications, documents, and financial activity constitute the data are not clearly the people granting permission or receiving value. Ooak offers NDAs, regional storage, written usage terms, automated anonymization, and deletion rights, which is more serious than hand-waving. Still, GDPR compliance is not the same as explicit participant consent or demonstrated resistance to re-identification. I have overridden a privacy concern for **Flock Safety** when an accidental technical artifact revealed unmistakable product utility; here the purported utility remains company-reported, while the custody architecture itself is the unresolved risk.

The bull case stings: if the lab contracts renew, Ooak may already possess the acquisition-grade workflow library that larger vendors cannot quickly negotiate into existence. My 36-month prediction is that Mercor acquires Ooak primarily for its licensed company-workflow supply rather than Ooak becoming an independent general data platform. The bear case is that the two labs internalize environment production, competitors substitute synthetic applications, and privacy restrictions prevent the library from compounding; customer concentration then turns apparent traction into two expiring projects. I would not invest my own money today. One verifiable flip condition would change that: an independent diligence package confirming renewal or expansion of at least one seven-figure lab contract while auditing employee-level provenance, consent, and re-identification controls.
