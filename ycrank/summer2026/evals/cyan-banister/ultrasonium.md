# Ultrasonium -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A machinist should be able to feed ordinary metal into a compact machine and produce geometries that currently require powder handling, high-powered lasers, expensive post-processing, or cannot be manufactured at all. Ultrasonium offers a credible route toward that world: acoustically confine molten metal, control it with a phased array and feedback models, qualify progressively harder alloys, then move the capability from laboratories into small shops. The leak from that future is real but early—a January 2025 patent and a $305,000 NSF award, not yet a production result.

I like the technical shape of this bet. The company is not decorating today’s AI wave; it is trying to remove a physical bottleneck beneath aerospace, defense, energy, medical devices, and compute. Its patent covers integrated acoustic and thermal modulation rather than a thin interface, and accumulated metallurgical data and feedback parameters could compound into serious process knowledge. But “could” matters. Submillimeter accuracy, density above 99%, and aluminum and copper processing remain stated research objectives. The scarce enabling layer is visible; control of a production-ready version is not.

The founders are the strongest evidence. Chris Carter worked on additively manufactured steel at Argonne and NASA-funded melt-pool simulation. Hunter Brown entered national-laboratory work at fifteen and contributed to ITER and CERN. Alexander Urbanski previously invented a nuclear thermoelectric generator and became the first employee at manufacturing-monitoring company Phase3D. Jack Qiu brings MIT and LBNL control-hardware depth. That is not four résumés assembled around a fashionable category. It looks like independently accumulated technical tuition, and growing to 17 people with 14 specified openings demonstrates unusual recruiting pull. The team clearly has Tizz and Rizz; commercial Biz remains unproven.

The irritating system is also concrete: metal additive manufacturing frequently requires costly machinery, powders, lasers, and secondary processing. Ultrasonium claims 15 times the speed at 75% lower cost, which could return advanced fabrication capability to small machinist shops instead of concentrating it inside enormous factories. Yet the dossier provides no machine price, operating-cost comparison, qualification timeline, or customer workflow demonstrating where that rent is captured. If the actual system still requires specialist operators, long validation cycles, and expensive finishing, the agency story collapses even if the acoustic physics works.

Here is the objection that stings: Ultrasonium may be staffing a capital-intensive science project ahead of both the physics and the customer. Its headline performance claims disclose no test methods, geometries, volumes, or comparison systems. Meanwhile, Seurat has raised roughly $178 million and VulcanForms $575 million, and the proposed go-to-market path wanders from small shops to research, dental, medical, aerospace, defense, energy, and compute—markets with very different qualification burdens. Pre-revenue hard tech can clear my bar when a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept substitute for revenue. Neither is documented here. My 36-month base case is therefore specific: by August 2029, Ultrasonium has three to eight paid pilot systems and $500,000–$2 million in ARR, but has not yet reached broad production deployment.

The concealed-infrastructure pattern fits me unusually well, and there is no obvious privacy problem, closed-model dependency, or claim that incumbent operations are trivial. Governance is the unresolved red flag. Four co-founders have differentiated titles and Jack is explicitly CEO, which is better than a leadership shell game, but the dossier does not reveal voting control or a contractual tiebreaker. I will not invent a problem, but I will inspect it before signing.

I land at Invest, narrowly rather than romantically. The team and stack position justify taking the technical risk; the absence of demonstrated user behavior prevents a stronger commitment. My one verifiable flip condition before wiring is an independently witnessed run producing 100 consecutive aluminum parts at greater than 99% density and submillimeter accuracy. Pass that test and the patent becomes a working artifact. Miss it, and this remains beautiful research rather than a company I fund with my own money.
