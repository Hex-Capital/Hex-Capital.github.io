# Arbital -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the founders’ résumés; it is the work they already performed inside the exact machine they are rebuilding. Karon Pangestu helped grow Aevo from roughly $1 billion to $100 billion in cumulative volume. Steven Mulyawan onboarded more than 10,000 traders. Vincent Owen Toniwan built backend and market-making infrastructure operating below two milliseconds at 100,000 events per second. Growth, distribution, and execution knowledge sit inside one three-person team. This is specific knowledge acquired through action.

The product has also crossed beyond demo evidence. Arbital served 2,600 traders and routed $1.3 billion in its first seven months, including $377 million in current monthly volume. Those figures prove that the founders can attract order flow and operate real trading infrastructure. They do not prove a business. No pricing, realized revenue, take rate, or unit economics are public, and incentive-driven volume can disappear when exchange points and rebates disappear. Volume is activity; retained net revenue is value capture.

The central problem is that the founders possess more durable knowledge than the company presently possesses durable assets. Multi-venue integrations, inventory limits, circuit breakers, and maker-only execution are useful engineering, but a well-capitalized competitor can reproduce them. Arbital depends on third-party DEX liquidity, fee schedules, and incentives, while Axiom, Fomo, Backpack, and Hyperliquid already overlap substantial parts of the product surface. The dossier does not show exclusive order flow, proprietary models, scarce data, or execution quality improving with every deployment. If the code is copied, the advantage currently retreats into the team.

The market has room to maneuver. Perpetual-DEX volume grew from $1.5 trillion in 2024 to $6.7 trillion in 2025, and Arbital can plausibly move from cross-venue perps into memecoins, tokenized equities, and broader trading infrastructure. But the shared capability is still underspecified. The public website sells a decentralized market-making engine while the YC page points toward a social, mobile-first multi-asset application, and the reported volume is not separated between them. A broad menu is not yet a causal expansion path, particularly when tokenized equities and derivatives introduce new jurisdictional constraints.

The bull case stings: a team that has already scaled an exchange, recruited thousands of traders, and routed $377 million in one month may be discovering the business model from behavior rather than from a deck. The known exception for an unmonetized product applies only when a self-reinforcing network is already visible. Here, 2,600 traders create volume for Arbital, but the dossier does not show that one trader improves acquisition, liquidity, data, or execution for another. Arbital resembles a high-throughput service more than a permissionless network. Its prior KOL and high-net-worth relationships are proprietary distribution only while those relationships keep producing customers without proportional labor.

I see no evidence of the integrity, artificial-scarcity, reassurance, or founder-conduct red flags that would veto the option. I also do not yet see the unstated pattern that would make the system compound while the founders sleep. My 36-month base case is that Arbital remains an active cross-venue crypto terminal or pivots toward institutional execution, but does not become a defining consumer trading network. I would not invest today. One condition flips the decision: a six-month cohort showing that, after incentives end, retained order flow produces a measurable proprietary routing advantage—better fills for later users rather than merely more volume.
