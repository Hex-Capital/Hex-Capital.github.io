# Caladh -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Kristen Kita has done something more interesting than identify a defense market. In February 2022, she co-authored research demonstrating passive tracking of an underwater vehicle amid passing boats. That is a real technical problem, with an experimental result. But the founder question remains unanswered: when something prevented Caladh from working, what did she build next, and how quickly did it change the situation? The dossier documents scientific ability. It does not yet document that sequence of resourcefulness.

The distinction matters because Caladh’s advertised products go beyond the demonstrated research. Eclipse promises to deny navigation, communications, and sensing; Cloak promises to degrade and deceive underwater sensing. Tracking a vehicle establishes relevant competence, but it does not establish the ability to defeat it. I give the published experiment meaningful technical credit. Product descriptions supply no additional measured proof: the dossier reports no operating ranges, defeat rates, or independent trials for either system.

The strongest argument for investing is that Kita encountered this problem through years of actual work. Her 2022 MIT–WHOI doctorate and subsequent engineering background make the connection substantive. Underwater acoustics amid vessel noise is precisely the sort of difficult, unglamorous problem that can conceal an opportunity. Caladh fits my unstated willingness to back scientists outside familiar software markets when they have experimental evidence. The relevant exception is Bindwell: laboratory validation made an unusually young team investable. The material difference is that Caladh’s published result validates adjacent sensing work, while its commercial proposition depends on denial. That gap prevents the exception from carrying the investment.

Who needs the crude version badly enough to keep using it? Ports and naval bases describe potential buyers, but Caladh identifies no particular operator returning for another deployment. Briefing and pilot request forms are invitations to discover demand. YC participation supplies no evidence of it. Nor does the adjacent market estimate of $1.98 billion establish a purchasing wedge. Sonardyne already advertises underwater detection at 1,200 metres. A buyer needs a concrete reason to add Caladh to that installed capability, and demonstrated denial could provide one.

I see no affirmative evidence of the founder red flags: waiting for a partner, delegating the hard work, or persisting after repeated disconfirmation. Those behaviors should not be inferred from a sparse dossier. Equally, there is no documented product revision following customer or technical feedback. Resourcefulness, returning users, and evidence-driven route changes therefore receive their specified missing-evidence defaults. The founder’s research history earns credit for technical proof and problem origin; it cannot also stand in for those separate observations.

I would pass on the evidence here. Caladh has a credible scientist working near a problem she knows, but it does not clear my founder-action gate. One verifiable event could change that: a dated, buyer-witnessed pilot documenting a deployment failure, Kita’s shipped correction within weeks, measured denial after the correction, and the buyer requesting another deployment. That would connect the scientist to the founder, and the experiment to something someone wants.
