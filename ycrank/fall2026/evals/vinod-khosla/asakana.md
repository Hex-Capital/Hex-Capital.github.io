# Asakana -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Asakana has identified wasted labor inside food distribution. It has not yet shown how eliminating that labor changes the economics of food distribution by an order of magnitude. One customer processes roughly 60 orders daily, some exceeding 75 lines, with processing consuming approximately 30% of representative time. That is a concrete problem. The company-reported 40%+ order-desk time savings is useful evidence of customer benefit. Neither figure establishes the industry-wide cost pool or the mechanism by which success makes food substantially cheaper. I will not mistake an early productivity result for a ceiling on ambition, but I cannot underwrite an undisclosed consequence.

The founders are the strongest reason to take Asakana seriously. Yudai Matsuoka has actually worked in seafood distribution and fishing. Benjamin Solis-Cohen spent eight years at Google, including Gemini and Project Mariner. Bringing an agent builder into a distributor’s manual order desk connects two relevant disciplines around an observed problem. This is more informative than assembling generic AI credentials. It supports complementary team construction; the dossier does not yet document rapid belief revision or recruiting additional people to own specific weaknesses.

That combination creates a credible bull case: seafood shorthand, variable catalogs, customer preferences, and awkward ERPs could make this an unusually demanding entry point into automating distributor operations. Live US deployments and reported broadline customers suggest the opportunity extends beyond a laboratory demonstration or a single seafood niche. My investment patterns include execution-heavy businesses; a scientific breakthrough is not mandatory. Asakana could earn the right to automate much more of the back office by first mastering these messy orders. That is the strongest objection to passing.

But customer usefulness and technical discontinuity remain different claims. Choco already offers message-to-ERP automation and claims more than 200 ERP connections; Pepper combines order automation with a broader commercial suite. Asakana’s customer context and human escalation may produce superior results, but there is no comparative accuracy, exception-rate, throughput, or deployment-cost benchmark. Nor is there evidence that accumulated corrections create a proprietary learning advantage. This activates my concern about AI businesses competing through implementation rather than differentiated capability. The dossier does not establish that Asakana is such a business, but it does not resolve the concern either.

The next dollar should test whether reliable automation survives the cost of exceptions and integrations. Routing ambiguity to humans is sensible product design; it does not establish how much human work remains. Live deployment removes some feasibility uncertainty, but the dossier supplies no ranked kill risk, decisive experiment, numerical threshold, or experiment budget. Transaction-volume commissions also reveal little about economic leverage without rates, support costs, and integration maintenance. I assume neither subsidy dependence nor attractive margins. The economics are undisclosed.

I pass with my own money at **29/100**. The known exceptions do not rescue the case: Asakana has a credible founding pair and existing demand, but neither a demonstrated giant value pool accessible through its mechanism nor a quantified consequence sufficient to override the missing discontinuity. **One verifiable flip condition:** an independently auditable, multi-distributor deployment study showing at least a 10x reduction in fully loaded cost per correctly entered order against both manual processing and an overlapping vendor, including human review and amortized integration maintenance. That would turn the central question from whether this team can build useful software into how broadly its economics can propagate.
