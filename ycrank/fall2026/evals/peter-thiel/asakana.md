# Asakana -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Asakana’s possible secret is that seafood orders contain enough customer-specific knowledge to make a specialist substantially better than general distributor software. That proposition is testable: run the same ambiguous orders through Asakana and Choco, then measure correct entries, human intervention, and implementation cost. The dossier supplies no such comparison and identifies no prevailing belief the founders have disproved. Live deployments establish that the workflow can be automated. They leave the important investment question open: what does Asakana understand that its competitors do not?

The initial market is sensible. Seafood distributors have shorthand, changing catalogs, and established ordering habits that could reward specialization. But a narrow market becomes attractive when the company can own it. Choco already offers message-to-ERP automation and claims more than 200 ERP connections; Pepper combines overlapping automation with a broader commercial suite. Customer histories and catalog matching are useful capabilities, yet the dossier establishes no exclusive access, superior performance, or demonstrated replacement cost. This is affirmative competitive overlap, sufficient to fail my monopoly gate on the present evidence.

The strongest argument against passing is unusually concrete. Yudai Matsuoka has worked in seafood distribution and yellowtail fishing; Benjamin Solis-Cohen spent eight years at Google, including work on Gemini and Project Mariner. They are addressing a task one customer reportedly spends roughly 30% of representative time performing, and Asakana reports returning more than 40% of order-desk time to selling. Their domain knowledge could expose exceptions that larger vendors handle poorly. That is a credible route to discovering a secret. My exception for a verified technical breakthrough would matter here, but the reported labor savings do not establish a breakthrough relative to available substitutes. Human escalation protects customers while leaving the underlying performance advantage unmeasured.

Founding structure remains a separate uncertainty. The dossier does not establish the founders’ prior working history, full-time status, ownership, or economic exposure. I assign the stated default rather than infer either commitment or a founding defect from their credentials. My unstated preference for companies assembled through an established operator network also remains unconfirmed: showing a prospective co-founder a manual order desk establishes problem discovery, without establishing durable prior alignment. There is no documented part-time founder, outsourced engineering arrangement, or investor-dominated capitalization to trigger those red flags.

Distribution has more substance than the financial model. Columbia’s Japanese business network produced introductions to chefs and grocers, giving Matsuoka a concrete starting channel, although its conversion into repeatable distributor acquisition is undocumented. Transaction-volume commissions provide one coherent revenue engine. Negotiated rates, customer counts, support costs, and margins remain undisclosed, so fund-scale outcome arithmetic receives the default score. Custom ERP connections could consume the economics of that channel; the dossier does not establish whether they do. I would pass with my own money. One verifiable flip condition is a customer-run comparison showing that Asakana’s accumulated seafood context produces a sustained, substantial accuracy advantage over Choco at comparable implementation cost. That result would directly address the competitive gate currently deciding the investment.
