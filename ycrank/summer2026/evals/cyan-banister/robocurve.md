# Robocurve -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Inspect Robots v1 has already crossed from code into one physical-robot pilot, making Robocurve a tactile truth machine with an unfinished founder constitution. Robocurve’s highest signal is physical-world verifiability: 40-plus VLAs, more than 260 language models, and traceable tasks performed by actual robots. Robocurve’s lowest signal is governance: Jay Chooi is CEO and Aris Zhu is CTO, but titles do not tell me who wins the argument that cannot be compromised. I can touch the work; I cannot yet see the tiebreaker.

**Flock Safety** is the high rung because its truth was observable in the environment—a camera, a Wi-Fi network, a physical artifact working outside a slide deck. Robocurve shares that mechanism: KitchenBench, DataCenterBench, trace logs, and one completed frontier-model pilot turn AI claims into events a person can watch and reproduce. Robocurve diverges because Flock controlled a deployed product and accumulated proprietary field data, while Robocurve has published its core framework under an MIT license, reported only one pilot, and has not yet released the full comparative results. Robocurve is physically real, but the physical reality has not yet become a proprietary habit.

**HQ Trivia** is the low rung because intoxicating product evidence temporarily concealed an unresolved founder tiebreaker, and the fracture eventually ate the artifact. Robocurve shows a healthier CEO/CTO division—Chooi leads after working at the UK AI Security Institute, while Zhu brings Amazon Robotics and Yondu deployment experience—but Robocurve provides no founder-vesting, deadlock, equity, or exit-conflict evidence. Two Harvard co-founders can be beautifully complementary until independence, publication timing, and vendor revenue pull in different directions. For Robocurve, “CEO” is a rebuttal to my concern, not yet a hard answer.

Jay Chooi contributed to Inspect Evals before Robocurve, and Aris Zhu built navigation and fleet-management systems before Robocurve; that is substantially better than two tourists discovering robotics evaluation in a market memo. Over the next 18 months, Robocurve’s founder experience can compound through better task design: Chooi knows how evaluators make claims legible, Zhu knows how physical robots fail between the lab and the floor, and every repeated run can improve both the benchmark and the operating judgment behind it. The mechanism fails if Robocurve remains an academic integration project collecting GitHub approval—currently 96 stars and 13 forks—rather than learning from recurring physical deployments. Biz and Tiz are visible; I have no environmental evidence yet for the Rizz.

METR, Epoch AI, and Artificial Analysis are reference models rather than direct physical-robot competitors, which makes Robocurve weird enough to hold my attention but not yet “burn gas flares to mine Bitcoin” weird. Robocurve would move into my top non-consensus tier if two sophisticated robotics investors passed because independent evaluation looked unmonetizable, while named robot vendors nevertheless paid Robocurve for repeat evaluations. Robocurve would fall toward the consensus AI-tooling pile if frontier laboratories simply adopted Inspect Robots internally and the YC round became crowded because “robotics plus benchmarks” is fashionable. The dossier establishes an uncrowded category, not an underpriced round.

**SpaceX** is the useful single-anchor parallel on frontier ambition: hardware makes truth expensive, slow, and impossible to manufacture with a dashboard. Robocurve operates on that same side of reality because reproducible kitchens, data-center construction tasks, robot form factors, and telemetry cannot be conjured from an API response. Robocurve is not yet a SpaceX-class capital-intensive artifact, however; Robocurve’s owned asset appears to be an open-source evaluation interface rather than a robot, factory, or regulated testing facility. I give credit for standing where software meets metal, but not full frontier-hardware credit for measuring someone else’s machine.

DataCenterBench points to my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Robocurve will have pivoted from broad open-source robot benchmarking into paid acceptance testing for data-center robotics, reached $1 million–$3 million in ARR, and completed a founder-role reset that leaves Jay Chooi as the unambiguous operating CEO. Robocurve’s physical-evaluation strength naturally concentrates where failed robot deployments are expensive, while Robocurve’s soft governance signal makes a role clarification more likely as vendor payments, benchmark publication, and evaluator independence begin colliding.

Inspect Robots’ MIT license is the bear case that stings: Amazon Robotics, a frontier lab, or a standards nonprofit can fork Robocurve’s 40-VLA interface, run evaluations on hardware already inside its facilities, and leave Robocurve holding an admired repository rather than a business. Robocurve also invites funding from parties it may evaluate, without a published conflict-management policy; paid independence can become performance theater. The counterweight is that Robocurve has already operated one physical pilot, and vendor-run tests cannot supply trusted cross-vendor comparability merely by copying code. I invest because operational credibility and longitudinal field data can outrun the fork, but I flip to Pass if Robocurve cannot show two repeat paying evaluation customers and a published conflict policy within 12 months.
