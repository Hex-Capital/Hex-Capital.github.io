# Evardi -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Evardi’s market opens if utilities can turn existing meter and network records into reliable engineering decisions without installing more hardware. The buyer is the distribution utility; the controlling variable is engineering labor saved per safely completed task after accounting for data cleanup and operator review. Virtual transformer meters could make that equation attractive. The dossier establishes a plausible mechanism, but it does not establish the threshold at which deployment becomes repeatable software rather than another utility integration project.

The switching results bear directly on that distinction. Evardi reports catching 60% of switching errors and reducing unsafe storm-drill actions by 42%, while explicitly identifying these as simulations on public data. That is useful preliminary evidence, with a substantial discount from live operational results. The inspection study—61% hit rate versus 6% from billing flags—also suggests a potentially large improvement in field-resource allocation. Neither study measures implementation cost, engineering hours saved, or realized customer savings. Rising electricity demand increases urgency, but the projected data-centre load does not itself establish why Evardi’s outage-focused workflow has become economically feasible now.

The strongest part of Evardi is its product architecture. Combining GIS, SCADA, billing and meter records, checking network models against feeder-head measurements, and retaining operator approval puts the product inside an engineering workflow. Better models could improve recommendations while those integrations and utility-specific models remain valuable. This fits my pattern of backing software that captures a physical-industry transition. However, documented functionality does not establish a defensible data advantage: the dossier gives no reproduction cost for a competitor, and Neara and Bidgely/Grid4C already operate in adjacent modelling and analytics markets.

The company-reported footprint—utilities serving more than 10 million customers across 180,000 miles of line—is the most consequential unresolved demand signal. Even a small number of genuine production deployments across that footprint would matter considerably at pre-seed. But those figures describe the utilities’ networks; they do not establish paid contracts, recurring operator usage, or expansion. I give the footprint some credit for reported access, without treating it as demonstrated product-market fit. The absence of public revenue is not evidence that customers rejected the product.

The strongest argument for investing now is that the founders may already possess the access and operational understanding needed to cross this gap. Shah’s Kenya Power experience and Autodesk utility integrations are relevant, and her reported prior business sales, alongside Borele’s previous energy venture, provide some evidence of startup learning. Those facts make the workflow credible without proving its economics. The research-stage exception does not apply: there is no documented frontier breakthrough and associated capital path sufficient to override weak commercial evidence. Nor does the dossier establish the physical-economics exception through measured delivery costs or margins. Conversely, I see no evidence of vanity fundraising or premature liquidity; those red flags should not be invented.

I would pass on writing a personal check today while keeping Evardi under observation. Workflow ownership is ahead of commercial proof, and the market-plus-economic-change evidence does not clear my investment gate. My flip condition is one independently referenceable, paid utility deployment showing at least a 30% reduction in engineering hours per completed switching plan, including data preparation and review, with no deterioration in safety outcomes. That would test the central belief directly: whether Evardi sells repeatable engineering capacity at software economics.
