# Definite -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: stronger reasoning models now let regulated finance teams automate reconciliations and controls, but Definite becomes large only if it can convert messy customer systems into trusted, audit-ready workflows faster and more reliably than heavily funded competitors. That is the controlling variable. The dossier explains the mechanism—source mapping, deterministic calculations, exception rules, and evidence-linked workpapers—but supplies neither a deployment-time threshold nor customer evidence showing that the mechanism works in production. Definite therefore does not clear my primary market gate yet.

The strongest part of the company is architectural. This is not merely a model reformatting financial documents. Definite retains mappings among entities, accounts, transactions, policies, and approvals; models interpret inputs while a deterministic engine performs the financial math; humans receive evidence-linked workpapers. Better models should improve document interpretation without absorbing the rules, workflow state, permissions, and verification layer. That places the product on the favorable side of the GPT Ladder, although there is no deployment or retention evidence proving that these customer-specific structures create switching costs.

There is also a plausible economic step change. Definite reports that its verification engine using DeepSeek V3 surpassed GPT-5 on FinBalance reconciliation accuracy at roughly 50% lower cost. If replicated in live close and regulatory-reporting work, that combination could turn weeks of review into hours. But the claim is company-reported, the broader why-now is inferred from Basis rather than measured inside Definite, and missing or stale customer data stops dependent lines. The product may accelerate computation while implementation, data cleanup, assurance, and procurement remain the real cost centers.

The bull case is stronger than the public traction makes it look. Gurshabd encountered manual attestations at BitGo, Mazin saw regulatory rechecking at Optiver and built finance agents at Boosted.ai, and Farhan worked on machine learning at Meta. Numeric, Stacks, and Basis have raised $38 million, $35 million, and $137.6 million respectively, which validates buyer interest in institutional finance agents. A small technical team with firsthand workflow knowledge could still find the narrow control surface those broader competitors neglect.

The opposing evidence changes the decision. Definite has moved from Ledgix and Bylaw compliance infrastructure to bank reporting and then broader reconciliation and controls. That may be rapid discovery, but it also means the first repeatable product and buyer are unsettled. The disclosed traction—170 LinkedIn followers, 11 Launch YC votes, and custom-order-form language—does not establish usage, revenue, renewal, or even a named pilot. Meanwhile, the market is already crystallizing around well-capitalized competitors, and the inferred moat of customer mappings is reproducible unless Definite can quantify the time, money, and access required to recreate it. None of the known exceptions applies: there is no frontier research breakthrough, rare model-building team, or reconstructed physical-market economics sufficient to override absent pull.

I would not write the check today. My base case is that over 36 months Definite narrows back toward regulated bank reporting and reaches roughly $1 million to $3 million ARR, rather than becoming a horizontal operating layer for finance. The verifiable flip condition is three regulated financial institutions each paying at least $100,000 annually, completing a second recurring reporting or close cycle, with initial deployment under eight weeks. That would simultaneously identify the controlling variable, demonstrate product-market pull, and show that the owned workflow—not the model benchmark—is the business.
