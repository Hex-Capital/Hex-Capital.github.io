# Liquid Energy -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Liquid Energy promises first-token delivery within eight weeks of signing a contract. That is potentially valuable against grid-connection waits averaging more than four years. But what does my check buy? Proposed post-money, competing term sheets, and whether this is the first scarce check are all undisclosed. YC participation does not establish an auction; it also does not establish cheap entry. I cannot underwrite the deal from the urgency of the customer’s problem.

The strongest case is that factory integration changes what customers can buy. Combining compute, cooling, networking, and an unfolding solar array could turn a delayed construction project into a delivered product. Liquid Energy fits my pattern of taking physical complexity seriously when reorganizing the operating system creates value. Carrying its own generation also distinguishes NanoDC from Rune’s use of existing solar facilities. If that distinction removes a binding deployment constraint, it could matter more than another improvement in inference software. This is the argument that makes passing uncomfortable.

The unresolved mechanism is whether faster deployment produces economically useful capacity. Independent benchmarks, storage sizing, and measured uptime are absent; the company’s terms say advertised capabilities remain under development and may never be offered. That disclaimer does not establish technical failure. It does mean the eight-week promise cannot carry the underwriting. Nor is it a capital plan: the dossier names no round-funded experiment with a measured result and deadline. A contract-to-delivery claim tells me what the customer is promised, not which uncertainty my money resolves.

Who owns the customer access once the engineering works? Liquid Energy lists an inquiry email, 247 LinkedIn followers, and affiliations with YC, Velocity, and NVIDIA Inception. None documents an observed purchase, pilot, or retained deployment. The founders have some operating substance: Sajan Khosa’s AJMER signed three brokerages, while Sid Ravikumar held software engineering roles at Jelly.fm and Lotus Laboratories. Those brokerage agreements belong to a prior business, and the software titles do not establish deployed power infrastructure. I see neither demonstrated proprietary distribution nor documented execution across both energy and compute. That is unproven fit, not evidence that the founders cannot build it.

Deleting the next financing exposes the other unanswered question. There are no disclosed prices, margins, burn figures, or customer payment terms with which to model a self-funding fleet. Solar variability could reduce utilization or require more storage; the dossier supplies no measurements to settle that tradeoff. I therefore use the missing-economics default rather than assume hardware makes the business uneconomic. The generic AI-application red flag is not the decisive objection here—the proposed advantage is physical deployment—but Liquid Energy still needs customer cash to cover equipment, operation, and replacement. My known exceptions require documented conversion, operating economics, proprietary access, or unusually strong external validation. This dossier supplies none sufficient to override the missing price and capital-purpose gates.

I pass with my own money. The market bottleneck is credible; the investable transaction remains undefined. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: **a proposed sub-$10 million post-money round whose budget funds a paid NanoDC deployment by a specified date, with customer-agreed availability and a measured cash payback threshold that includes compute, power, storage, cooling, and maintenance.** That would connect the price, the experiment, and the route to a business. Today those connections are hypotheses.
