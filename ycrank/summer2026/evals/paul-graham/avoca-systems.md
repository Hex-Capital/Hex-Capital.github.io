# Avoca Systems -- Paul Graham Evaluation

More than 250 live clinics make Avoca Systems a schlep-heavy operator whose founders have not shown that radiology was their own pain. That high-schlep, low-organic pairing is Avoca Systems’s signature. Campbell Mercer-Butcher and Max Shand have already forced clinical rules, faxes, rosters, protected health information, and six healthcare systems to cooperate. But neither founder’s biography contains the radiology equivalent of Drew Houston forgetting his USB stick. Avoca Systems has built something difficult that customers evidently want; the unresolved question is why these particular founders started building it.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness: compliance was not incidental to the product but the unpleasant work that made the product possible. Avoca Systems has the same useful ugliness. Avoca Systems writes appointments into RIS products, interprets referrals, encodes modality and contrast constraints, and has obtained SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001, and HIPAA compliance. The divergence matters: Armstrong was running into a grind most founders had not recognized, while AbbaDox and OpenDoctor already advertise radiology-specific voice, fax, and scheduling automation. Avoca Systems is deep in a schlep zone, but it did not discover an empty one.

**Loopt** (2005) exposed the failure mode at the low end of organic founder pain: a plausible future can arrive before the product is rooted in something the founders urgently need themselves. Sam Altman’s animal quality overrode that weakness. Avoca Systems has a similar missing link. Mercer-Butcher encoded complex workflows at Honey Insurance and Ailo, and Shand was Afterpay’s first employee, but neither biography shows either founder working inside a radiology network or trying to book an imaging patient. Avoca Systems may have learned the problem unusually quickly from customers, yet that is still different from building the tool you were already desperate to use. No direct evidence connects Avoca Systems to founder pain, so I apply the absent-evidence default.

Max Shand’s prior Serenade acquisition and Avoca Systems’s 250-clinic deployment are stronger evidence of resourcefulness than polished credentials alone. Over the next 18 months, this dimension compounds through deployment: every strange booking rule, reluctant RIS vendor, and clinical escalation gives Avoca Systems another opportunity either to behave like an animal or retreat into manager mode. Reducing call abandonment from roughly 30% to 2% suggests Avoca Systems has already survived real contact with users. But the dossier contains no cereal-box moment, rescued rejection, or other named pre-funding stunt. Animals leave tracks, and Avoca Systems has operational tracks without the decisive founder story.

Campbell Mercer-Butcher was a principal software engineer, a tech lead, and Epex’s engineering founder, but Avoca Systems’s public hacker genealogy is thinner than those titles imply. Mercer-Butcher’s GitHub profile has eight repositories, three followers, and a top repository with one star; Shand has no public repositories. Avoca Systems would move up a full tier on this dimension if Mercer-Butcher had built the booking engine before fundraising, published a technically admired healthcare integration, or shown an old artifact proving he had been hacking on workflow systems for years. Without that, Avoca Systems has credible builders, not yet the unmistakable pre-startup trail that Patrick Collison or Aaron Swartz left.

**Stripe** (2010) is the useful single-anchor comparison for cofounder configuration: two founders with complementary abilities can be extraordinarily strong when the relationship predates the startup and the hacking history is shared. Avoca Systems has the right numerical shape in Mercer-Butcher and Shand, with technical depth on one side and prior operating and founder experience on the other. But Avoca Systems provides no public evidence that the pair worked, lived, or built together before Avoca, and its headcount of eight means the original pair’s direct contribution is already less visible than at the ordinary pre-seed stage. I give Avoca Systems modest credit for complementarity, not Stripe credit for founder chemistry.

More than 250 clinics lead me to predict that Avoca Systems will reach $8 million to $12 million in annual recurring revenue within 36 months, remain focused on radiology networks, and stay independent rather than being acquired. Avoca Systems can get there because the integrations and customer-specific booking rules make each successful deployment expandable across locations, while the reported 86% call-resolution rate gives an executive a concrete reason to add sites. The mechanism is not magical AI. It is accumulated schlep: Avoca Systems turns hundreds of local exceptions into an operating system that becomes harder to remove.

AbbaDox is the strongest argument that Avoca Systems will instead become a feature. AbbaDox already combines RIS, PACS, VoiceAI, FaxAI, scheduling, results, and referrer communications, while OpenDoctor already offers exam-mapping and 24/7 AI self-scheduling. Add Avoca Systems’s missing radiology origin story and weak public hacker trail, and the bear case stings: capable enterprise operators may have entered a recognized category where incumbents own the system of record. The evidence that presently wins is Avoca Systems’s 250-plus live clinics and the reported fall in abandonment from 30% to 2%; those are shipped-work signals, not deck claims. I would flip to Pass if customer references showed that fewer than 50 of those clinics were paying production deployments rather than pilots, or that network-level renewals were below 80%.
