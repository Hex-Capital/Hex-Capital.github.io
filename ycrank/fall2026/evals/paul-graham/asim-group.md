# Asim -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Asim’s most useful fact is the external robot-safety watchdog that won the June 2026 YC Paris–Unaite hackathon. The founders built something before forming the company. That matters more to me than their affiliations. But I cannot find the episode I most want to see: a robot developer encountered a specific obstacle, these founders worked around it, and something changed within days. The prototype establishes initiative and technical execution. It does not establish that whole sequence ([Lecourtois’s portfolio](https://tristanlecourtois.github.io/index.html)).

The strongest argument for investing is that this team could turn an awkward research problem into a useful product unusually quickly. Tilley researches human–robot interaction, Lafargue worked as a senior research scientist at Sony, and Neau has computer-vision and social-robotics experience. The hackathon watchdog gives those credentials an artifact to attach to. This partially matches the pattern I like in technical companies: build the proof before asking for permission. Yet the prize measures success in a competition, not intervention latency, missed hazards, or performance on an unfamiliar robot. Asim’s own terms describe its demonstrations as illustrative. I give the prototype meaningful credit, but cannot treat it as independently validated safety performance ([Asim](https://www.asim-group.com/); [terms](https://www.asim-group.com/terms)).

Who needs the crude version badly enough to keep using it? The dossier identifies robotics teams as prospects, but no particular team returning for another assessment or installing the watchdog repeatedly. YC backing, A3 membership, and NVIDIA Inception membership do not answer that question. Nor do the roughly 1,200 stars on Neau’s separate RelateAnything project establish demand for Asim. The missing revenue is ordinary at this stage. The missing connection between a particular user’s problem and the product is what leaves demand untested.

There is potentially a good schlep here: integrating with different robot stacks, reproducing failures, and finding dangerous behavior that developers missed. Robocurve’s MIT-licensed evaluation harness makes basic testing infrastructure easier to obtain, so Asim would need to discover which difficult part customers still cannot do themselves. Paid assessments could teach the founders that. They could also become a succession of bespoke research assignments; negotiated engagements are only an inferred business model today. The founders’ research backgrounds establish proximity to the field, but the dossier supplies neither a specific prior collision that produced this idea nor a customer observation that caused them to change the product route.

I would pass on the present evidence. The founders met through a hackathon and have at least some shared execution; I would not turn their short documented history into an automatic rejection. Likewise, there is no evidence of dependence on a large partner or premature delegation. But the working-product exception does not carry the decision: the watchdog has neither unmistakable user pull nor a measured result strong enough to replace the missing resourcefulness evidence. My flip condition is one documented customer deployment showing a named blocker, a founder-built workaround shipped within weeks, and repeated use afterward. That single episode would tell me considerably more about Asim than another research credential.
