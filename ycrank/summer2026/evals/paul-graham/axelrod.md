# Axelrod -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when Axelrod broke. The founders have built agents that operate hotel systems through browser control, where changed interfaces, ambiguous requests, and execution errors are inevitable. Yet the dossier contains no named failure, dated workaround, or evidence of how quickly Saman Sayahpour and Adrian Stoica restored a live workflow. This matters more than their credentials. A company automating hotels will encounter resistance continuously; I need evidence that the founders accelerate when it appears.

The product itself is considerably stronger than that omission suggests. Axelrod is live across four continents, operates in Michelin Key-awarded and Marriott Luxury Collection properties, and reportedly lets one butler cover four times as many rooms. That is a functioning technical system deployed inside real operations, not a diagram awaiting funding. But the numerical result is company-reported, and there is no customer count, independently measured error rate, retention figure, or revenue. It is a good proof spike, not yet a conclusive one.

The narrow well is also visible. Boutique and luxury hotels have expensive service standards and fragmented PMS, POS, RMS, maintenance, and communications systems. These customers appear willing to let Axelrod access mailboxes and operational software, which is a stronger signal than joining a waitlist. Still, “live across four continents” conceals the variables that matter: how often staff delegate work to the agents, whether hotels renew, and whether the same property expands usage. The absence of public revenue is not a pre-seed defect, but it prevents deployment from becoming evidence of pull.

The founders encountered the problem before inventing the company. They report growing up cleaning guest apartments in Berlin and behind a Vienna hotel reception. Their solution also embraces the ugly part: mapping property procedures, supervising agents until they earn autonomy, and controlling legacy interfaces without waiting for APIs. This is genuine schlep blindness. It is Axelrod’s strongest signal. The property-specific memory layer may create switching costs, but the decision not to pool customer data limits cross-property learning, while Lance, Zaplar, and the heavily financed Mews can attack overlapping workflows.

The bull case is that deployment itself proves unusual execution. Two founders have crossed enterprise trust boundaries on four continents without hiring, and the absence of active job postings suggests they remain close to the product. This partially matches the pattern of a concrete proof arriving before the financing story. It also weakens the red flag around young founders selling to experienced operators. But it does not erase the missing causal sequence. No known-exception override applies: the product works, but the dossier supplies neither independently validated technical performance nor unmistakable repeated usage strong enough to substitute for evidence of founder behavior under resistance. The unknown cofounder history and lack of any documented evidence-driven route change remain unresolved.

I would not write the check yet. The single flip condition is a dated incident report showing that a third-party hotel interface broke an active workflow and the founders restored it within 48 hours across multiple properties without losing continued customer usage. That would turn the most important uncertainty into evidence. Until then, Axelrod looks like an authentic, technically capable company whose founders may be relentlessly resourceful, but the dossier has not shown it.
