# Jinko -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: my agent handles a canceled flight while I sleep, changes the hotel, and gets the refund right. That future needs more than a better itinerary generator. It needs permission to transact, reliable execution, and someone accountable when a supplier fails. Jinko’s [documented search, payment, booking, and servicing interfaces](https://docs.gojinko.com/introduction) make part of that path concrete. But the sleeping traveler is my extrapolation. The dossier establishes an emerging transactional channel, not yet evidence that travelers routinely delegate this much authority.

The founders are the strongest reason to keep looking. Gauthier, Texier, and Zhang previously built travel businesses together, including Mowgli, acquired by Hopper; their experience spans airline operations, distribution, and booking engineering. This is substantially better evidence than three impressive résumés assembled around agents. They have worked together inside the machinery they now propose to expose. The August 2026 consumer-to-infrastructure pivot could reflect an operating wound becoming a product specification, which attracts me. The dossier does not establish that origin, however, or document the independent experiments that would turn domain experience into unmistakable obsession.

Why does an agent need this intermediary? Browser blocking and conventional search-to-book restrictions are specific obstacles, and removing them could give small developers access to transactions previously requiring substantial travel integration work. That returns some control to builders. But Jinko has not identified a quantified incumbent rent that disappears. Its own [pricing](https://gojinko.com/pricing/)—1% per confirmed flight booking and $0.005 per search above a 1,500:1 ratio—also exposes the underlying constraint: tireless agents can generate expensive exploration without buying anything. Charging for that is sensible. It does not yet demonstrate that Jinko changes the economics rather than repackages them.

The concealed operating layer is more interesting than the MCP connection. Jinko identifies itself as the [contracting travel operator](https://gojinko.com/terms/) and carries professional liability coverage. That means the product reaches into responsibility, not merely information retrieval. This fits my preference for infrastructure beneath a fashionable interface, serving personal agents, banks, and other platforms. The strongest objection to my hesitation is that a team already fluent in travel could absorb the unpleasant servicing work everyone else avoids and become indispensable. Yet Etraveli’s October 28, 2026 launch includes payment, ticketing, and servicing too. Responsibility is valuable; the dossier has not shown that Jinko controls a scarce capability competitors cannot supply.

The Exa Connect listing is a real distribution artifact. The advertised 800 airlines and 2.6 million hotels describe available supply. Neither establishes that a developer has repeatedly entrusted Jinko with customers’ money and disrupted trips. I can invest before revenue when a working artifact and exceptional founder evidence make the destination tangible; that exception does not require pretending documentation is costly user behavior. Likewise, the CEO and Co-CEO titles deserve scrutiny under my accountability rule, but they do not establish equal voting control or a leadership shell game. The pivot itself is not evidence of trend chasing.

I am **Neutral** with my own money. Jinko has founders who understand the system and a plausible place beneath the agent wave, but insufficient evidence that its particular layer becomes necessary. My verifiable flip condition is one independently referenceable platform customer showing 90 days of repeat paid bookings through Jinko, including successfully handled cancellations or refunds, with positive contribution after search and servicing costs. That would reveal whether developers are buying an interface or entrusting this team with the hard part.
