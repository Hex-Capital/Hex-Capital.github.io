# Avoca Systems -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The contested truth is precise: radiology scheduling is not a generic call-center problem but a repository of clinic-specific rules that an integrated machine can operate more reliably than centralized staff. The claim would be disproved if horizontal healthcare agents achieved comparable resolution and booking accuracy without Avoca’s specialized knowledge bases and RIS integrations. Avoca has run a meaningful experiment: several live radiology networks, including groups exceeding 150 clinics, report a 90% reduction in call abandonment; Jobfit reports 86% call resolution and 4.6/5 satisfaction. This is more than an AI slogan, although the results remain company-published rather than independently verified.

The narrow market is real enough to dominate. Epic, Oracle Health, MEDITECH, Karisma, Aura, and Visage integrations, combined with protocol knowledge and real-time write-back, embed Avoca more deeply than a voice interface. But embedded is not yet proprietary. openDoctor and AbbaDox already cover overlapping radiology workflows, while Assort Health and Prosper AI can approach from the horizontal patient-access layer. There is no disclosed exclusive data, measured retention, network effect, or 10-times technical advantage. Avoca has found a beachhead; it has not shown that the beachhead belongs to it.

The hidden positive pattern is operator-led formation. Max Shand was Afterpay’s first employee and previously sold Serenade, while Campbell Mercer-Butcher has led engineering teams and is building the core product in-house. That is materially stronger than two novices assembling around the current AI fashion. Yet the dossier does not establish how long they have worked together, whether both are unambiguously full-time, or whether founders and employees retain meaningful ownership. These are missing facts, not adverse facts, but founding structure cannot be inferred from résumé quality.

The strongest bull case is that I am demanding monopoly evidence too early. A repeat founder has already reached named customers, crossed healthcare-security barriers with SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001, and HIPAA compliance, and produced measurable operational improvement before YC Demo Day. A network-level deployment can spread across many clinics, accumulating exceptions and integration knowledge until replacement becomes painful. If those deployments expand organically, today’s implementation burden becomes tomorrow’s distribution advantage.

The difficulty is that a successful product can still be an inadequate investment. The published radiology-scheduling market is only $780 million, Avoca discloses neither revenue nor pricing, and the route from Australian reference customers to U.S. radiology executives remains unmeasured. Australia-only hosting, unfinished athenahealth and eClinicalWorks integrations, protocol-safety exposure, and enterprise selling must all be resolved during that expansion. Existing Australian deployments show that this coordination stack is possible, so this is not the classic unvalidated hard-technology red flag; they do not show that it scales economically in America.

None of the known-exception mechanisms overrides these concerns. The 90% abandonment reduction is useful technical proof, but not a zero-to-one event that neutralizes commodity competition; there is no persistent transaction network, regulatory arbitrage, or validated physical frontier. My likely outcome is a valuable radiology-software company reaching roughly $6–12 million ARR within 36 months, not yet a last mover capable of a power-law return. I would investigate rather than invest today. One event would flip the decision: a U.S. radiology network expanding Avoca from a pilot to at least 100 clinics under a seven-figure annual contract.
