# Nori -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Nori’s $1,288 L2 is a schlep-rich robot attached to a founder-thin configuration. Nori scores highest where manufacturing, teleoperation, safety, and household mess create work mainstream hackers avoid; Nori scores lowest because Antonio Sitong Li is the only active founder YC identifies despite a listed team size of four. It is an ambitious artifact with an unresolved human architecture.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness: compliance was not incidental to the product but the unpleasant work that made the product possible. Nori shares that mechanism through manufacturing, in-home teleoperation, privacy controls, task training, and physical support at a $1,288 price. Nori diverges because Coinbase embraced a named grind, while Nori advertises dish loading, bed-making, food preparation, and multi-robot coordination without publishing delivered-unit counts, task-success rates, runtime, or safety tests. Nori has found a schlep zone; it has not proved it likes schlepping.

**Coinbase’s** initially rejected, lone-founder application illustrates the low rung’s failure mechanism: a solo configuration leaves too much dependent on one person until an extraordinary artifact overrides the concern. Nori currently lists Antonio Sitong Li as its only active founder, and Nori discloses neither a cofounder nor a prior working relationship among the four people associated with it. That is a direct red flag. Armstrong supplied a working wallet strong enough to become a known exception; Nori has pre-orders and promises, but no disclosed household deliveries strong enough to earn the same exception.

Antonio Sitong Li previously built anti-interview-cheating software that reached 2,000 users and 1.5 million impressions, and Nori’s launch reached about 41,000 views before its first production batch filled. That is evidence of motion, not yet animal resourcefulness. Over the next 18 months, Nori’s resourcefulness will compound only if Antonio repeatedly turns manufacturing failures, remote rescues, and awkward customer installations into a better robot. If Nori merely converts social attention into a waiting list while delivery slips, the apparent hustle decays into marketing.

Antonio Sitong Li researched VR-demonstrated goals for robot teams at Columbia, which puts Nori closer to an organic idea than a market study would. But Nori’s dossier never says Antonio needed a household robot himself or built Nori to solve a chore he personally could not tolerate. The score would flip upward if Nori showed that its first machine began as Antonio’s own continuously used tool and that downloadable skills emerged from real household repetition. Until then, Nori has organic technical lineage but incomplete organic pain.

**Dropbox** (2007) converted hacker genealogy into proof through Drew Houston’s working demo; credentials mattered less than the artifact people could see. Nori has Antonio’s computer-science, architecture, robotics, and prior-product record, which is substantially better than technical credentials alone. But Nori has no public repositories, published task benchmarks, or inspectable demonstration trail comparable to Dropbox’s Hacker News video. The unstated hacker pattern is present in Nori’s biography and weak in Nori’s public artifacts.

Sunday Robotics has announced a $165 million Series B while Nori is attempting a sub-$1,400 household robot with one disclosed founder. By July 2029, I expect Nori to have pivoted from general household chores to a narrower light-commercial job such as restaurant prep or hotel-room turnover, with less than $3 million in annualized revenue. Nori’s appetite for physical-world schleps should keep it alive, but Nori’s founder configuration and unsupported task breadth make narrowing more likely than winning the home.

Nori’s first production batch reaching capacity is the strongest case against that forecast. Nori may be an **Airbnb** (2009)-style known exception: an implausibly cheap product whose founders reveal their quality by delivering what sensible investors thought could not be delivered. The $1,288 price, Antonio’s 2,000-user prior product, and transferable-skills design make that bull case sting. But undisclosed order volume can mean ten robots, and Nori supplies no delivered-unit count or reliability evidence. I remain neutral rather than writing a check; I would flip to Invest if Nori verifies a cohort of at least 100 paid household deliveries with 60% still running tasks weekly six months later.
