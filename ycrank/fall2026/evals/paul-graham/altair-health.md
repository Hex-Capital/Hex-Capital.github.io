# Altair Health -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most useful fact about Altair Health is that Aouss visited clinics in Texas and got a design partner. Someone went out and persuaded a practice to entrust the company with an unpleasant, important job. But the founder test remains incomplete: what broke during installation, what did the brothers build to get around it, and how quickly did that work? The reported year between first conversation and full deployment shows persistence. Without the intervening decisions, it tells me little about resourcefulness.

The strongest argument for investing is the substance-use-treatment practice. Jad reports that Altair collects several hundred thousand dollars monthly from insurers for this customer. Those are the practice’s receipts, not Altair’s revenue, but they still matter. A practice allowing a startup to operate its billing is taking a consequential risk. This is a much stronger demand signal than the behavioral-health EHR waitlist. Aouss’s enterprise-sales experience and Jad’s healthcare-ML work also make the division of labor plausible. The bull case is that these brothers have found a narrow group of users who will let them learn inside a real billing operation.

The technical question is whether that learning produces software that can take over progressively more work. Altair says agents propose actions, a theorem prover checks them against insurer policies, and human billers handle exceptions. That is a sensible architecture. A proof can nevertheless be perfectly correct about an incorrectly translated policy. The dossier supplies no measured verification coverage, exception rate, or comparison with human billing. I give the architecture provisional credit, but the client’s collections cannot establish how much the software accomplished. The pattern I find compelling in technical founders is a concrete proof spike; Altair has an architectural claim awaiting that proof.

There may be a useful change of direction here. Aouss announced a billing co-pilot at HealthCon 2026; the current website offers managed billing. Taking responsibility for the whole job could expose problems a co-pilot vendor never sees. Yet the dossier does not identify the observation that caused this change, so I cannot score it as demonstrated learning. Nor does healthcare research establish that the founders encountered this particular problem themselves. The integration and service burden is real. The founder-originated insight remains undocumented.

Altair clears several red flags: the CEO is recruiting customers personally, distribution does not depend on an unspecified major partner, and the founders are brothers rather than strangers assembled for an application. Their relationship alone does not prove shared execution under stress. Competition from Adonis, Candid Health, and Anomaly does not settle the decision either; a small practice may prefer someone who simply gets its bills paid. The economic uncertainty is more immediate. Managed-billing fees are undisclosed, and the separate $395/year authorization listing tells me nothing about the cost of serving this practice. Human escalation could be productive early learning or a permanent labor requirement.

I would pass today. The framework’s exceptions allow unmistakable user pull or independently validated proof to overcome missing founder evidence. One founder-reported deployment is meaningful, but does not yet justify that override. My flip condition is one documented second-practice deployment showing a specific blocker, the founder-built workaround shipped within weeks, and eight weeks of recurring claims processing with measured human intervention. That would show whether Altair can turn what it learned at the first practice into a repeatable product.
