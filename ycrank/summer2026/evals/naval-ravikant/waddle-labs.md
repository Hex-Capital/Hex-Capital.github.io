# Waddle Labs -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the founders’ Harvard affiliation. It is six months of operating robots, roughly 1,000 overnight LEGO pick-and-place repetitions, Yiding Song’s program-induction research, and Hanming Ye’s work on robot policies. They have acted inside the exact problem. The unresolved discontinuity—from a 2025 teleoperation-data product with Oscar Melendez to today’s two-founder control-agent company—reduces confidence in persistence and team history, but the technical learning is real and transferable.

The central question is what remains after a competitor copies the agent loop. Today, not enough. The architecture—inspect video, generate control code, execute, observe failure, and re-plan—is reproducible from public research such as CaP-X and ASPIRE. Performance rises with third-party model capability and reasoning spend, so some of Waddle’s progress accrues to its suppliers. The shared skill library and six months of intervention traces are the beginning of a proprietary asset, but internal repetitions are not yet a compounding deployment network. A moat described in the future tense is still a hypothesis.

The bull case stings because physical execution is harder than software imitation. Reliable control requires accumulated failure traces, hardware-specific knowledge, and thousands of ugly interactions that a copied repository does not contain. The preview API could turn each deployed robot into a contributor of reusable skills for every subsequent robot. The Notion exception is the closest precedent: a working, capital-efficient prototype can justify investing before distribution is visible. The material difference is that Waddle’s underlying models and execution architecture are available to well-funded competitors, while no external deployment has yet shown that its product language or data advantage compounds faster than theirs.

The wedge is legitimate but not yet expansive. Industrial robot-programming software was estimated at $1.671 billion in 2025, growing 11.9% annually. That supports a business, not automatically an outlier. The dossier does not show the causal path from developer tooling into a larger transaction layer, robot fleet, proprietary model, or physical automation market. Skild AI and Physical Intelligence have raised more than $2 billion and $1.07 billion respectively; Waddle should not fight them by financing a smaller version of their model strategy. It must become the permissionless coordination layer above heterogeneous robots and models.

There is no direct value-capture evidence: no price, paid deployment, recurring revenue, or named payer. There is also no observed user-to-user loop; 971 X followers and preview registration measure attention, not contribution. Pre-seed companies are allowed to lack revenue, but investors are not allowed to convert missing evidence into imagined economics. The public safety gap around autonomously generated physical-control code could also lengthen enterprise adoption precisely where the largest budgets sit.

I see no documented integrity violation, artificial financing pressure, or reassurance-seeking behavior. I do see two diligence questions that cannot be waved away: what happened to the prior co-founder, and what safety boundary prevents generated code from producing physical harm? My base case is promising technical founders building a useful robotics tool, but not yet a company whose leverage survives copied code. I remain Neutral. One verifiable result would flip me to Invest: a published external-deployment cohort across at least three robot platforms showing that previously accumulated Waddle skills materially improve later customers’ task success without proportional engineering work.
