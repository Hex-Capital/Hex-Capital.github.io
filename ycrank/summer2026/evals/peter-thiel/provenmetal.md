# ProvenMetal -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The contested truth would be: hardware teams will pay ProvenMetal for certainty inside the design workflow, even when the underlying factory is neither exclusive nor always faster. What would prove this wrong? Engineers bypass the KiCad plugin and order directly from Network PCB, which advertises three-day turnkey service against ProvenMetal’s five-day fastest turn. The dossier provides no installation, retention, reorder, or price-premium evidence to resolve that test. “American made” is also less definite than it sounds because fabrication may remain overseas unless the customer selects a fully domestic route. This is presently a policy-aligned slogan, not a demonstrated secret.

The strongest fact is the founding structure. Johnny Doyle and Will Carkner studied at Trinity College Dublin, built Syncra together, and carried a physical product through a 17-day office pilot that collected more than 20 million datapoints. Carkner has PCB-testing experience; Doyle has encountered hardware sourcing and deployment firsthand. They appear to be operating the company together rather than having met opportunistically for YC. But founder ownership, compensation, secondary liquidity, and explicit full-time status are undisclosed, so alignment is promising rather than fully proved.

The monopoly test is much worse. ProvenMetal owns a KiCad 9+ plugin, one-minute distributor checks, quoting, procurement coordination, and inspection records. Network PCB owns the San Jose assembly capability—and sells substantially the same sourcing, fabrication, assembly, and testing directly. MacroFab has a network of more than 100 North American factories, CircuitHub automates production from one to 10,000 units, and Sierra Circuits advertises faster fabrication. The proposed future moat—BOM histories, revision data, and supplier-performance records—is inferred. Without repeat orders or exclusive data, the interface can be copied while the supplier can disintermediate it. This is competition around a commodity, not yet a narrow monopoly.

The bull case is that ProvenMetal has found a useful beachhead in drone and defense teams, embeds sourcing at the moment engineers make component decisions, and obtained five LOIs after more than 30 conversations. A workflow wedge can become a data advantage if every revision makes quoting and procurement better. But those LOIs may concern the earlier benchtop X-ray inspection concept rather than the current managed-PCBA service, and plugin availability is not plugin use. None of the known-exception mechanisms applies: there is no independently validated zero-to-one result, persistent transaction demand, or deployed pilot that overrides the failed monopoly gate.

The economics remain indefinite. The $16.12 billion North American PCB market demonstrates that boards matter, not that ProvenMetal can capture the value. Quotes are custom; prices, gross margins, sales cycles, implementation costs, and revenue are absent. A direct-order route and KiCad distribution are at least concrete, but their economics are unknown. The broader pattern toward owning complete production stacks cuts against this structure: ProvenMetal owns the customer interface while a direct competitor owns the operational bottleneck.

I pass. The company’s signature is a credible, long-aligned hardware team attached to an unproved intermediary position. One verifiable condition would reverse the judgment: a disclosed cohort of at least 20 drone or defense customers showing greater than 50% twelve-month reorder rates and gross margins above 35% through the KiCad-originated channel. That would begin to show that ProvenMetal owns a compounding workflow rather than merely reselling factory capacity.
