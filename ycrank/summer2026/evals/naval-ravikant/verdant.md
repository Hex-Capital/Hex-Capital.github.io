# Verdant -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Jason Yi built local-government HR software at Holly, while Aidan Ng worked around DOJ, USDA, Congress, Deloitte, and commercial real estate. Verdant is a specific-knowledge-heavy, permissionless-leverage-light government SaaS company. Verdant’s 200-plus municipal interviews suggest real curiosity and energy, but founder-led demos and jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction configuration turn code into labor-assisted distribution. The founders understand the buyer better than they have designed around the gatekeeper.

Stack Overflow is the high rung because developers built a developer product that spread among developers before an enterprise buyer granted permission. Verdant shares part of that founder-product-market-fit mechanism: Yi researched public-benefits automation and shipped government software, while Ng knows government institutions and land-use economics. Verdant diverges because neither founder is a practicing planner or building official, no permit-review artifact predates the financing story, and Yi’s most visible GitHub repositories have two stars each. Two hundred interviews create learned knowledge. Years spent living the problem create specific knowledge. Verdant is between those states.

Anchorage Digital’s low-rung architecture required regulatory permission, but an OCC charter became the moat and pre-consensus crypto timing justified the exception. Verdant also depends on permission—municipal procurement, private order forms, founder-led demos, and configurations for each jurisdiction—but Verdant has no charter-like barrier or category-timing advantage. This activates my red flag against enterprise selling before unprompted organic adoption. Code and media are leverage because they replicate without asking. Verdant’s software replicates cheaply; Verdant’s distribution does not.

GovWell already serves more than 150 agencies, while Verdant has no explicit mechanism through which municipality 151 makes the product more valuable to municipalities 1 through 150. Over 18 months, ordinance mappings and workflow configurations could deepen switching costs, but switching costs are captivity, not a network. Verdant mentions no marketplace, public corpus, contributor ecosystem, or shared data layer; even the anonymized-usage advantage is inferred rather than deployed. Each new Verdant jurisdiction presently creates another implementation obligation, not another compounding node.

The $307 million global permit-management market—forecast to reach only $567 million by 2032—is below the market size that gives mistakes room to become pivots. Verdant could flip this score if permitting is merely the wedge into a horizontal rules-as-code operating system spanning licensing, inspections, grants, zoning, and benefits administration. Verdant’s March 2026 grant-management positioning shows adjacent pivot capacity, but it also shows that the broad platform remains an aspiration rather than a demonstrated architecture. A niche is dangerous because the first idea rarely survives.

Notion survived multiple rebuilds because Ivan Zhao’s product obsession made him legible as a long-term builder before the correct product existed. Verdant’s closest corresponding evidence is the founders’ Georgetown relationship, Yi’s prior government-software work, and the discipline required to conduct more than 200 municipal interviews. Verdant shows intelligence and energy; no contested history or self-advertised integrity creates concern. Yet Verdant provides no prior venture completed together, no record of treating a previous organization through adversity, and no decade-long shipped artifact. The ten-year-partner read is promising but unproven.

GovWell’s 150-agency lead and Clariti’s acquisition of CivCheck constrain the likely path. By July 2029, Verdant will have pivoted from replacing end-to-end permit systems to an ordinance-review copilot layered over incumbents such as Accela, and Verdant will remain below $3 million ARR. Verdant’s founders can learn their way toward a useful wedge, but founder-led procurement and a non-networked product make a respectable vertical tool more probable than a 10,000x outcome.

Anchorage Digital supplies the strongest opposing case: permission can become a moat when regulation, trust, and accumulated configuration make replication difficult. Verdant has interviewed 200-plus officials, admits AI accuracy may remain at 80–90%, keeps decisions with government personnel, and could turn jurisdiction-specific ordinance mappings into the trusted infrastructure layer competitors cannot safely commoditize. That bull case stings because GovWell’s 150 agencies prove municipal software can scale. It still loses today: Verdant has no named deployment, no structural barrier, and faces GovWell, Clariti, Govstream.ai, and Accela with overlapping AI review. I would flip from Pass if Verdant reached 25 named live jurisdictions through inbound or municipal-referral adoption rather than founder-led outbound.
