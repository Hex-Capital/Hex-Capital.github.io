# Plek AI -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Pierre Bouquet has done the work that Plek AI requires. His published 2026 research concerns task-level workforce estimation and AI exposure—the mechanism inside the product. That is specific knowledge exposed through action. The MIT affiliation adds little by itself; the research artifact adds considerably. His reported 2022 Biot exit provides operating context, but it does not establish expertise in selling to PE operating partners. I give the founder substantial credit without treating his knowledge as an asset the company exclusively owns.

The decisive question is what Plek keeps when a competitor reproduces its analysis. Related research publishes its code and data, and the dossier establishes no exclusive license, proprietary model, or accumulated customer dataset. Employee-level reconciliation across compensation, location, organization, and AI opportunities is useful: double-counting savings can make an investment case fictitious. But useful implementation remains reproducible. Orgvue already has organizational-design buyers and automated role classification. Plek must accumulate something that an incumbent cannot obtain simply by assigning engineers to the problem.

The strongest argument against my caution is the company-reported assessment of a 3,600-person business: $30 million in annual EBITDA opportunities, matching an advisory firm’s six-week study. If Plek can repeatedly deliver comparable analysis with little human intervention, Bouquet could turn specialized research into considerable economic output with a two-person team. I can back a prototype before network effects appear when the founder and product evidence justify the exception. Here, however, agreement with another estimate does not establish realized savings, repeat usage, or delivery cost. The benchmark earns product interest; it cannot also become revenue, defensibility, and customer love.

Plek’s initial buyer has room to expand the relationship. A PE operating partner assessing AI exposure can also use employee-level modeling for compensation, location, and organizational scenarios across portfolio companies. The adjacent workforce-analytics market’s estimated $2.92 billion in 2026 supports the existence of broader demand, although it is not Plek’s addressable revenue. Negotiated order forms establish a route to charging for that work. They establish neither completed payments nor attractive margins. The $30 million belongs to a modeled customer opportunity, not Plek’s income.

Against my unstated patterns, Plek currently resembles an expert tool more than a permissionless system. Customers contribute sensitive workforce information, but there is no observed mechanism through which one customer improves another’s utility or acquisition. Permission to aggregate de-identified information is only permission. It becomes economically interesting when deployment produces reusable, differentiated benchmarks. Nothing in the dossier establishes founder misconduct or artificial fundraising pressure. The disclosed handling of unredacted survey text nevertheless creates a concrete obstacle to earning the customer trust that such a benchmark system would require.

At 49/100, I would keep the relationship open and hold my money. Plek has a founder with relevant knowledge and a plausible commercial wedge; it has not yet shown that deployment compounds an advantage. My one flip condition is a documented cross-customer learning result: benchmarks accumulated from earlier deployments measurably improve a new customer’s assessment against a public-data-only baseline, under enforceable reuse rights. That would turn today’s research-led tool into evidence of an asset that grows with use.
