# Assemble -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important question is what broke. Assemble recognized that an agent allowed to mutate Salesforce could make an enterprise system worse faster than a person could. The founders responded by building API version control, scoped branches, recorded mutations, verification, and deterministic rollback. That is a shipped workaround to a real technical obstacle. But the dossier does not show the sharper resourcefulness sequence: a customer encountered a specific failure, the founders changed the product within days, and the customer’s result improved. The workaround is credible; the founder behavior behind it remains partly hidden.

The strongest evidence is the artifact itself. Assemble’s agent completed 30 Salesforce Superbadges, earned 629 badges and 371,300 Trailhead points in about 6.4 hours, and passed 93% of challenges on its first attempt. That is unusually measured proof for a pre-seed company. Aliyan Ishfaq’s work at LangChain and on a 10,400-star repository, combined with the team’s Stanford AI research, makes the result more believable than a diagram accompanied by the word “agent.” Still, Trailhead is a clean obstacle course. A real Salesforce instance contains years of accidental business logic, frightened administrators, and changes whose cost is political as well as technical.

Who wants the crude product now? The dossier cannot name one IT team repeatedly using it, paying for it, or trusting it with production changes. The 107 LinkedIn followers, 40 X followers, and demo form do not answer that question. Nor does a $491.5 billion services market. Large markets are often where startups go to hide from the absence of a small group of desperate users. Assemble’s two open go-to-market roles are therefore slightly worrying: the founders should learn personally why the first CIO says no before attempting to turn that learning into a department.

There is also no documented running-back test. The product route has not yet changed in response to usage, so flexibility receives the missing-evidence score rather than a penalty. The problem is certainly schlep-heavy: privileged access, undocumented dependencies, cross-system testing, and rollback are precisely the work intelligent competitors prefer to avoid. But the founders’ connection to that schlep is less clear. Their backgrounds establish strong agent-building ability; only Shrish Janarthanan’s KPMG cybersecurity internship provides even a partial documented collision with enterprise operations. This looks more like gifted builders discovering an ugly market than insiders building the tool they previously needed.

The best case is substantial. Assemble matches an important unstated pattern: a young technical team produced a numerical proof spike before raising or obtaining institutional permission. Aliyan’s previous ParaAI project reportedly reached more than 100,000 users and $50,000 in ARR, so current-product demand is not the only evidence that this team can attract users. The rollback substrate could also become a proprietary record of enterprise configuration changes. But none of the known exceptions supplies the missing bridge here. A working benchmark can override doubts about technical ability; it cannot prove that enterprises will grant access or keep using the product. Meanwhile Salesforce, Workato, SAP, Oracle, and Workday control the surfaces Assemble needs, and may absorb the easier parts of the product.

I would not write the check yet. My 36-month base case is that Assemble narrows into Salesforce-specific configuration testing and rollback and remains below $3 million ARR, rather than becoming a cross-platform autonomous IT engineer. The one condition that flips me is verifiable: three named enterprise IT teams each complete at least ten production or production-shadow changes with Assemble, and a documented failed change causes the founders to ship a corrective product revision within two weeks. That single test would establish both the narrow well and the founders’ response when reality resists them.
