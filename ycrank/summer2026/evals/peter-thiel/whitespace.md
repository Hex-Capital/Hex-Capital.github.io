# Whitespace -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Whitespace’s contested truth is that a distributor’s accumulated operating knowledge can be encoded into agents that execute back-office work more effectively than either legacy ERP software or horizontal AI tools. What would prove this wrong? If deployments remain bespoke integrations whose accuracy depends on continuing human supervision, then the knowledge base is not proprietary technology; it is consulting expressed through software. The dossier does not identify a belief rejected by peers. Plato, Proton.ai, Canals, and Conexiom are already making overlapping claims, so the apparent secret is presently consensus.

The strongest evidence is the founding structure. Alex Tung and Leon Yao have been best friends for 12 years, have lived and worked together, and both grew up in wholesale-distribution families. Tung advised manufacturers and distributors at BCG; Yao built optimization products for logistics-distribution companies at InstaDeep. This company was not assembled at a networking event around a fashionable category. Yet founder ownership, salaries, outside employment, and whether all core engineering remains in-house are undisclosed. The foundation appears unusually strong, but economic exposure is not proved.

Two named customers—Kingfisher Direct and Warwick—make Whitespace more than a presentation. Live deployments span order intake, customer service, and inventory planning; the company reports two-to-four-week implementation, three-month payback, and productivity gains above 80% in selected workflows. The bull case is sharp: a long-aligned team with lived domain knowledge has already crossed the integration barrier that supposedly protects legacy systems. If correction histories measurably improve subsequent deployments, the current service burden could become proprietary infrastructure.

But the product is attempting six departments at once: inventory, procurement, sales, customer service, finance, and planning. Breadth is not a beachhead. Whitespace has not identified a bounded distributor segment it can dominate, while funded competitors already overlap across ERP workflows, inventory, pricing, order entry, and customer service. Customer-specific learning may create switching costs inside each account, but the policy against cross-customer training also limits any demonstrated data-network effect. At present, each deployment may become more useful without the company itself becoming structurally harder to compete with.

Distribution is more credible than the moat. The founders are pursuing direct introductions to wholesalers and have relationships around Affiliated Distributors, NAED, ASA, Distribution Strategy Group, and FWD. A two-to-four-week implementation and three-month payback could support founder-led enterprise sales. Still, there is no disclosed contract value, sales-cycle length, conversion rate, renewal evidence, or channel that becomes cheaper with scale. Usage- and workflow-based pricing describes how Whitespace charges, not whether one revenue engine can produce a power-law outcome.

The strongest objection to passing is that pre-seed monopoly evidence often appears first as customer behavior, and Whitespace already has two deployments with purportedly dramatic economics. I cannot use that as an exception: unlike a verified zero-to-one technical event, the 80% productivity figure is company-reported, selected by workflow, and not connected to retention or competitive wins. I pass today. One result would flip the decision: a cohort of at least 20 paying distributors in one narrowly defined vertical showing over 120% net revenue retention after 12 months, with deployment time and human-review rates falling across successive customers because of reusable proprietary infrastructure. That would convert the story from founder-quality in a crowded market into an observable monopoly mechanism.
