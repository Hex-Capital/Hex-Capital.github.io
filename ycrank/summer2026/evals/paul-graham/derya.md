# Derya -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Mert Turna built ship-operations software used on more than 30 vessels, but Derya has no public GitHub trail or similarly legible hacker artifact. Derya’s signature is a unusually strong founder pair wrapped around an unusually grubby problem, with less evidence of hacker genealogy than I would expect from the best version of the bet. Oguzhan Karaca and Turna have known each other for eight years, earned their first money together, and already converted Derya into $80,000 of contracted first-month revenue. These look like animals who may build a great freight office, though it is not yet clear they are building the Stripe of freight.

Stripe is the high rung for co-founder configuration: two brothers with years of shared context, complementary abilities, and commerce software already in their hands. Derya has the same load-bearing mechanism in Karaca and Turna’s eight-year friendship, debate partnership, and prior student work together. Derya diverges because the Collisons had jointly accumulated hacker evidence through Auctomatic and commerce experiments, while Derya’s dossier names no technical artifact the founders built together before starting. The relationship is as strong as one can verify from a dossier; the shared building history is not.

The polished AI-wrapper pair in my pass example is the low rung for hacker genealogy: managers proposing to hire the people who will make the product. Derya does not fit that failure mechanism, because Turna was CTO of Hemut and shipped operational software across a fleet worth $200 million. But Derya also has no public repositories, programming prizes, open-source work, Hacker News presence, or technical measurements showing what its agents automate. Karaca’s Oxford law background is useful in freight, but Derya’s hacker case currently rests on one prior production system rather than a trail of compulsive building.

Derya produced $80,000 of contracted revenue and sourced $3.2 million of freight opportunities in its first month. Resourcefulness compounds here because every manually qualified carrier, customs exception, and awkward booking can become both revenue and training data over the next 18 months. Karaca also previously built Ivy Consultants to six-figure ARR, which means Derya’s selling is not merely a plan. The missing Airbnb-like evidence is an improbable stunt: Derya has shown strong execution, but not yet the act of desperation that makes me think the founders will crawl through broken glass.

Karaca worked in legal at CEVA/CMA CGM, while Turna built truck and ship operations systems before Derya. For the organic-idea score to move into Dropbox territory, Derya would need evidence that Karaca or Turna first built these agents to solve a repeated workflow they personally suffered, then kept using them before customers asked. Derya’s biographies place the founders directly inside freight, but the dossier does not say the product began as their own indispensable tool. That distinction separates an organic idea from an intelligently selected opportunity.

Coinbase is the clean parallel for Derya’s schlep. Coinbase’s compliance grind was the asset; Derya voluntarily owns quoting, bookings, customs, carrier qualification, tracking, customer updates, and financing above $20,000. Derya could have sold an AI copilot and avoided the liability, but instead it directly moves freight. That is the right instinct. The danger is equally specific: without disclosed underwriting criteria, a funding facility, or risk transfer, Derya’s financing schlep can produce defaults faster than its shipment data produces intelligence.

Derya will reach $8 million to $12 million in annualized net revenue by July 2029, but Derya will still look primarily like a fast, technology-enabled freight forwarder rather than the software default for freight offices. The strong founder configuration and appetite for operational grime should win bookings; the weaker public evidence of technical compounding makes margin growth more likely than Stripe-like software leverage. Same-day quotes are valuable, but Flexport, Forto, and Nuvocargo prove that software attached to freight does not automatically escape freight economics.

Flexport and Forto are the strongest bear case against Derya: well-funded digital forwarders already learned that logistics complexity, working capital, and human exceptions can swallow the software story. Derya reports no automation rate, proprietary IP, exclusive carrier relationships, financing facility, or measured model advantage, and $3.2 million of opportunities is not revenue. But Derya’s $80,000 contracted in month one, TVO’s signed 20-container commitment, and the founders’ direct freight history beat the generic AI-wrapper pattern today. I would reverse the check if Derya cannot show repeat contracted revenue plus improving gross margin and automation after its first $1 million of completed bookings.
