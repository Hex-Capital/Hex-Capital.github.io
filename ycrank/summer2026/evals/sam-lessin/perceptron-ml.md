# Perceptron ML -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: no post-money valuation, no competing-term-sheet count, and no evidence this is genuinely the first scarce check. That does not mean Perceptron ML is auctioned; it means I cannot tell whether I am buying a non-consensus belief or paying YC pricing for a consensus category. Legal AI is already emphatically consensus: Harvey has raised more than $1 billion and reached an estimated $195 million ARR, while Legora has raised more than $850 million and exceeded $100 million ARR. The round terms therefore receive the explicit missing-evidence default, not credit inferred from the company’s small team.

The potentially interesting asset is not another legal copilot. It is the workflow Perceptron installs inside each firm: matter-specific training, customer-controlled deployment, no shared training, auditable output, and a grounding engine that blocks claims until checked against primary sources. Three anonymized systems—timekeeping, monitoring, and research—are reportedly deployed inside firms. That is real pre-seed product evidence and suggests the team can cross the procurement boundary that has kept firm-level legal-AI adoption at 34% even as personal usage reached 69%. But “deployed” does not establish paid conversion, repeat use, renewal, or that Perceptron owns a proprietary data advantage rather than performing bespoke integration work on customer-owned data.

The bull case is that the custom work is the wedge. Michael Marcotte has built production agents for hardware debugging and formal verification, which is relevant technical shipping experience for source-gated legal systems. Peyton Marcotte has delivered an engineering project supported by NASA, Brown, and the 1517 Fund. A two-person team getting three systems into conservative law firms may be more informative than a polished self-service funnel. The translation-layer pattern is plausible: rigorous agent engineering on one side, messy firm workflows on the other. The problem is that neither founder has documented legal-practice experience, legal buyer access, or a shipped result on that second side. Today they are technical outsiders selling implementation, not demonstrated outsider-insiders.

The strongest exception precedent would be Ando: an AI application can survive model commoditization when the base business already proves accuracy, pilot-to-paid conversion, retention, and customer economics. Perceptron has the shared mechanism—AI embedded in an enterprise workflow—but not the evidence that made the exception investable. Its grounding claims have no independent benchmark, its case studies disclose no conversion or retention, and Harvey already supports more than 25,000 customer-created agents. If the next model release improves grounded legal retrieval, Perceptron’s defensibility may collapse toward deployment labor rather than compound into software.

Deleting the next financing makes the weakness sharper. Pricing is undisclosed; revenue, gross margin, burn, runway, and implementation capacity are undisclosed; and the inferred model combines custom fees with recurring support. There is no named use of proceeds, dated experiment, or pass/fail metric. The obvious venture moment would be proving that a firm-specific deployment can convert into repeatable, high-margin software without adding implementation headcount linearly, but the dossier never says the round funds that test. A possible July-to-August pivot from a cross-industry event engine into legal workflows further increases the need for one accountable product thesis, especially because the two brothers are both identified simply as founders and no final decision owner is named.

I pass at the current evidence level. Perceptron ML has built something and apparently crossed three customer thresholds, which keeps this from being a generic AI-wrapper dismissal. But I would be financing an unspecified round, an unspecified experiment, and an unspecified economic model in one of the most heavily capitalized AI categories. The single flip condition is five law firms converting paid pilots into 12-month renewals at greater than 70% gross margin without proportional implementation hiring; that would show the deployments are a proprietary workflow engine rather than consulting revenue wearing software clothes.
