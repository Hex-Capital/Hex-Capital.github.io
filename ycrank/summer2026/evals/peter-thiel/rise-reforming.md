# Rise Reforming -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Rise Reforming’s contested truth is precise enough to matter: small wastewater plants possess stranded biogas that can be converted economically on-site into saleable chemicals without pipeline access. The obvious falsifier is equally precise: its container must process contaminated live biogas continuously and produce specification-grade DME or methanol at a fully loaded cost below the contracted sale price. The company has not yet run that experiment. Its 1,800 hours of stable syngas production establish more than a climate slogan, but the expected 2027 wastewater pilot must still survive moisture, hydrogen sulfide, VOCs, siloxanes, and downstream synthesis.

The founders have built together since Spring Finals Week 2023, not assembled themselves for YC. Lucas Zubillaga led construction and operation of the bench reformer; all three studied molecular engineering at Chicago, and their experience spans catalysis, waste management, offshore wind, and carbon capture. A delivered pilot container and an advertised $125,000 process-engineering role indicate an operating company rather than a research project. Yet the dossier does not establish founder ownership, salaries, or whether every founder is now unambiguously full-time. The foundation appears serious, but economic exposure remains unproved.

The proposed beachhead—small and midsize wastewater plants excluded from pipeline-dependent RNG—is genuinely bounded. Rise also has one binding gas-supply agreement, multiple producer MOUs, and a conditional DME offtake. But a narrow market is not automatically a monopoly. M2X has already completed multiple modular deployments and raised $40 million; CRecTech also sells containerized biogas-to-methanol systems. DME-first sequencing and filed process IP are differentiation, not yet a demonstrated proprietary advantage. Unless live-feedstock performance is radically better, Rise is entering competition rather than escaping it.

The business model compounds the technical risk. Rise intends to finance the equipment, maintain it, purchase feedstock, and sell commodity chemicals. This can align hosts and capture more value than equipment sales, but it also puts utilization, plant financing, maintenance, chemical pricing, and offtake risk on one pre-seed balance sheet. The $41.6 billion methanol market does not answer the power-law question: no module output, installed cost, gross margin, payback period, or portfolio-level capital requirement is public. One revenue engine exists conceptually, but its arithmetic does not yet exist evidentially.

The strongest bull case is that the pieces are unusually advanced for pre-seed: 1,800 operating hours, a physical unit at a wastewater host, binding feedstock supply, conditional downstream demand, and $1.7 million raised. These facts could make Rise an exception to the usual hard-technology coordination failure. They do not yet provide the exception’s necessary proof, because the prior hours were not on live biogas and no commercial chemical volumes have been delivered. I expect that within 36 months Rise will either pivot toward licensing its reforming subsystem or remain below $2 million in annual chemical revenue because field reliability and project financing constrain owned-module deployment.

I would not invest today, but this is an investigation rather than a categorical rejection. The single flip condition is verifiable: operate the Chicagoland unit on untreated host biogas for six continuous months, deliver contracted specification-grade product, and disclose module economics showing a sub-four-year unlevered payback after cleanup, maintenance, and downtime. That result would transform the company from coordinated optimism into a technical secret with the beginnings of a monopoly mechanism.
