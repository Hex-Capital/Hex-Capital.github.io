# Peer -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Peer’s strongest asset is Aaron Zhang’s specific knowledge. He built voice agents for trucking fleets at Waylens; Felix Peng shipped internal AI tools used by more than 250 LADWP employees. The first is direct exposure to freight communication, while the second proves applied execution. But neither founder has publicly brokered loads, managed carrier failures, or sold this service to shippers. They know the automation layer better than the transaction layer.

The failure mode is structural. If a competitor copied Peer’s software tomorrow, the dossier identifies no asset that would remain: no proprietary model, exclusive data, carrier relationships, shipper distribution, or accumulated operating graph. FleetWorks already reports 10,000 carriers and more than 15 top-100 broker relationships; Cartage already automates phone, email, tracking, and escalation. Code is leverage only until everyone has it. Peer currently owns the code, while established competitors own the learning loops.

The market is real but the wedge is not yet sharp. U.S. freight brokerage is estimated at $21.28 billion in 2026, growing 7.23% annually, and digitizing fragmented offline supply matches a pattern I like. Yet a large parent market is not a path through it. Peer names neither its first shipper segment nor a capability that opens adjacent transactions. Its proposed spread per load could provide direct monetization, but no payer, price, completed load, revenue, or unit economics has been disclosed.

The strongest bull case is that the founders discovered a better problem while building their earlier screen-recording automation product. Zhang’s trucking-agent work could let two capable engineers automate enough coordination to enter a large market before incumbents adapt. Pre-seed companies often begin without metrics, and there is no evidence of an integrity, morale, or artificial-deadline red flag. A rapid pivot is not abandonment if costly customer contact produced it.

But the chronology presently shows a new category, not that learning: Peer was still described as workflow automation on May 27, 2026 and had become a freight brokerage by August. There is no disclosed shipment, shipper, carrier, brokerage authority, or $75,000 bond. The known founder-first exception does not apply merely because the founders are talented; it requires unusually strong action evidence that compensates for an illegible wedge. Nor does Peer yet instantiate the permissionless-network pattern: hypothetical lane data is not proprietary data, and hypothetical carrier liquidity is not a network effect.

I would pass today. The likely path is that Peer discovers brokerage operations are dominated by relationships and human exception handling, then pivots within 36 months into agent software sold to existing brokers. The single flip condition is verifiable: show 100 completed loads for repeat shippers, with positive gross profit, while demonstrating that proprietary lane and carrier-performance data measurably improves pricing or exception resolution. Then the brokerage would be producing an asset rather than merely consuming software.
