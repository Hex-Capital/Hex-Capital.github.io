# Induction Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The strongest fact is not that Photon-1 is a 106-billion-parameter model. It is the obstacle the founders chose to remove. Computer-use models usually need application-specific action labels, so Jonathan and David Li built a route from unlabeled screen recordings to predicted future states and then actions. They assembled roughly two million recordings, compressed frames by more than 100 times, wrote fused training kernels, and trained on 575 million frames. This is a shipped workaround with an observable result. But the dossier does not say what failed during the build, how the brothers reacted, or how quickly they recovered. I see formidable output, but only indirect evidence of the behavior that produced it.

The technical proof is unusually large for pre-seed. Photon-1 reportedly beats Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite while using 30 times less pretraining compute and serving at one-third the cost. The proprietary filtering system and two-billion-video index also look like a difficult substrate assembled before permission. This matches my actual pattern of backing young technical teams that arrive with a numerical proof spike. The discount is important: every decisive benchmark is internal, the Gemini comparison partly uses company estimates, and there is no public leaderboard or independent reproduction. It is a fierce-nerd artifact, but not yet a settled result.

The missing half of the proof is a user. Eighty thousand announcement views, 515 LinkedIn followers, and press coverage show that people noticed the research. They do not show that anyone needs the product. There is no public API, price, revenue, named design partner, repeated prototype use, or developer who has bent Photon-1 into an unexpected workflow. Induction Labs may have built an excellent engine without yet discovering the vehicle. At this stage that is fixable, but it prevents me from treating technical attention as a narrow well of demand.

The bear case is sharper because H Company already offers APIs and open weights and reports a 78.85% OSWorld-Verified score. A customer can use that substitute today without accepting Induction Labs’s internal benchmarks or uncertain rights to two million public screen recordings. The inferred distribution plan also ends with “partnerships,” one of the least useful words in an early startup plan. Three-times-lower serving cost could become excellent API economics, but with no access terms or usage, it is still an engineering ratio rather than a business.

There is no evidence that the brothers discovered this need by suffering it themselves, or that contrary user evidence has forced them to change routes. Jonathan’s Cohere work gives them genuine domain depth, and being brothers who have built together before reduces cofounder risk. But none of the known exceptions supplies the missing override: there is no independently validated result, unmistakable user pull, or personal problem history comparable in causal force to the technical artifact. The data-rights burden could become schlep blindness if they build licensing, provenance, and removal machinery; today it is merely unresolved exposure.

I would not write the check yet. The company is a strong research result attached to an unproven startup, and it narrowly fails my resourcefulness gate because the dossier shows the finished machine rather than the founders changing the situation under resistance. The bull case could still win spectacularly: the corpus, compression, and kernels may let a two-person team outrun competitors with hundreds of millions in funding. My flip condition is verifiable: five external developer teams using Photon-1 weekly for eight consecutive weeks, with at least one paying and independently confirming a material cost or task-success advantage. That would turn the model from an impressive claim into something users pull back.
