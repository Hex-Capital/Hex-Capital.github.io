# Micora -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Remote electricity is consequential. Micora could eliminate repeated diesel deliveries and make power available where construction labor is scarce. But how much delivered electricity cost disappears? How many sites become economically viable? The dossier does not answer either question. A $43.47 billion microgrid market establishes a spending category; it does not establish an orders-of-magnitude consequence from Micora succeeding. I can see the useful product. The extraordinary outcome still needs a mechanism and quantities.

The claimed **100 kWp/day autonomous installation rate** is the mechanism worth investigating. Installation automation could remove a constraint that cheaper panels and batteries leave untouched. But an absolute throughput number is not a technical discontinuity: against what crew size, terrain, preparation time, and incumbent deployment rate? BoxPower already supplies prefabricated microgrids. Micora’s forklift-placeable modules and planned LAKI robotics collaboration leave unresolved which installation steps are autonomous today. Those descriptions could be compatible, but they do not yet establish a measured advantage. I give limited credit for a specific technical claim, not credit for having proven it.

The strongest argument against passing is the team’s unusually relevant combination of skills. Akemoto shipped heat pumps and worked at NASA JPL and Astrobotic; Choo combines Serve Robotics drivetrain and manufacturing experience with Tesla solar/BESS deployment; Mareels adds finance and startup operations. That is a sensible assembly around the actual risks. The October 21 LAKI collaboration also suggests access to complementary robotics capability. This fits my preference for teams combining disciplines around physical infrastructure, and company-reported deployments at Pacific islands and manufacturing sites suggest contact with real operating conditions. However, neither complementary biographies nor a collaboration demonstrates rapid belief revision or exceptional recruiting. The founder score receives modest credit, not a presumption of exceptional execution.

Micora also fits the quieter pattern of climate businesses using standard industrial components rather than requiring customers to replace their entire operating environment. Standard LFP batteries can be an advantage if deployment automation creates the value. They also make integration easier for competitors. The decisive economic question is whether automation reduces **total delivered cost per reliable kWh**, including freight, site preparation, storage replacement, maintenance, and backup power. Purchase and 12- or 24-month leases reveal how customers can transact, not whether the economics work. The industry’s reported $70/kWh battery-pack price is not Micora’s procurement cost or a bottom-up parity model. There is no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, but there is no demonstrated unsubsidized advantage either.

I would pass with my own money today. My exceptions for an unproven product require an extraordinary quantified consequence or compelling evidence that the founders can overturn an enormous existing constraint; this dossier establishes neither strongly enough. Nor would I label four vacancies evidence of premature scaling—the spending sequence is undisclosed. What is missing is the experiment that makes the investment decision change: the largest kill risk, its test, its cost, and the response to failure. **My flip condition is one independently documented, representative customer deployment showing at least 5x lower installation labor than a comparable conventional microgrid, while delivering equivalent reliable power below the site’s diesel cost without subsidy.** That single comparison would connect the robotics claim to a purchasing advantage. Until then, Micora may become a capable microgrid supplier; I cannot yet underwrite the discontinuity.
