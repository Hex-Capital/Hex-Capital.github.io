# Perit.AI -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Perit.AI has delivered more than 4,000 hours of audio, according to its website. What I cannot find is the episode that tells me how these founders behave when something breaks. Which lab rejected a delivery? What did Kshitij and Poorva change, and how quickly? Building a contributor operation is substantial work. But its existence does not establish the particular quality I am buying: founders who find another route when the obvious one closes.

The strongest argument for investing is that customers may already have answered the important question. Kshitij reports six major AI labs served and multiple millions in ARR. For a Fall 2026 company, that is meaningful commercial evidence, even with unnamed customers and an unspecified revenue methodology. The niche is intelligible: labs whose voice models misunderstand transaction details or interruptions need practitioners who can recognize consequential mistakes. I give that demand substantial provisional credit. I cannot yet tell whether the reported ARR represents repeat purchasing or annualized project deliveries. Non-exclusive licensing could let Perit sell an asset repeatedly; contributor compensation itemized separately means the revenue figure alone does not reveal the economics.

There is also something inspectable. Perit has released 45 conversations totaling 5.10 hours on Hugging Face. That earns technical credit beyond a description of Foundry or the founders’ engineering credentials. But a downloadable corpus proves that data was produced, not that the data fixes the customer’s model. The scripted, studio-clean English sample leaves the central technical claim untested in public: whether practitioner-authored examples improve performance on spontaneous conversations across locales. Its 350 monthly downloads establish distribution, not retained lab usage.

Perit fits one of my less explicit preferences: assembling an awkward operational substrate before declaring oneself a platform. Recruiting, calibrating, obtaining consent, and aligning speech are work that someone must actually do. The counterargument to passing is therefore serious: perhaps a functioning production operation and substantial reported sales reveal resourcefulness more reliably than a tidy founder anecdote would. I checked the exception that would justify that inference—unmistakable customer pull or independently validated technical proof. The dossier does not establish either strongly enough to replace the missing founder evidence. Mercor and Turing can recruit and calibrate experts too; Perit’s defense must emerge from better results and customers returning for them.

Kshitij’s previous company reportedly reached $2M ARR profitably, and Poorva has worked at Google, Atlassian, and Uber. Those are useful reasons to meet them. They do not document Perit’s origin in a problem the founders personally encountered, or a product change caused by contrary evidence. I use the missing-evidence defaults for those dimensions. Nor do I infer premature delegation from 47 reported team members or eleven open roles. There is no demonstrated dependence on a major distribution partner, ceremonial launch, or inexperienced enterprise selling. This is an unproven founder case with encouraging commercial evidence, rather than an affirmative founder red flag.

I would pass on writing a personal check today. The founder-resourcefulness gate remains unmet, despite the promising demand signal. One verifiable condition would change that decision: a lab-confirmed customer history showing a rejected initial delivery, a founder-led revision shipped within weeks in response to the rejection, and repeat paid deployment afterward. That would connect the operation we can see to the learning behavior I need to back.
