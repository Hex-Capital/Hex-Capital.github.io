# Confetti -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine someone describing a peculiar object they need, receiving compatible parts, and spending Saturday assembling it instead of learning three engineering disciplines. Confetti’s promise—conversational design, component selection, printed-part ordering, and community remixing—points toward that world. But getting there requires reliable mechanical and electrical validation, dependable fulfillment, and designs that survive assembly by beginners. The dossier establishes the advertised workflow; it does not establish a completed object that proves those transitions have begun. I like the destination. The present-day leak is still missing.

Why must a person learn CAD and firmware to make one useful thing? Confetti identifies a real burden: the beginner must coordinate design, compatibility, sourcing, and programming before getting any satisfaction. Removing that coordination could return creative control to people who currently abandon projects. Yet the dossier identifies neither a specific gatekeeper collecting rent nor a measured cost Confetti eliminates. Printed-part transaction fees are explicitly an inference, and published pricing was unavailable. I can credit the concrete workflow problem without pretending the economics have been demonstrated.

The founders give me more substance than the product evidence does. Tianyi Evans Gu’s Wolfram and Stanford research supports technical capability; Lucas Yuan’s Penn Electric Racing participation adds relevant hardware exposure. More useful than the university names is their shared EngineLab work, accepted at the 2026 ICML NExT-Game workshop: they have produced a research artifact together. That earns some first-believer credit. It does not establish a longstanding obsession with helping people build physical objects or command of manufacturing operations. The apparent Asteria transition is insufficiently documented to call opportunistic. Gu is identified as CEO and Yuan as CPO; equal control, leadership misrepresentation, and severe privacy problems are not established.

The strongest case for writing a check is that the consumer project becomes the entrance to something much harder: a library recording which geometries, components, and assembly instructions actually work together. That would fit my pattern of finding concealed infrastructure beneath an approachable product. But Confetti’s library and its learning effects are hypotheses, not controlled assets shown in the dossier. Zoo already advertises geometry inspection and debugging; Flux combines electronics design with sourcing and manufacturing outputs. Confetti must make the journey across those boundaries work for an ordinary person. Its 58 LinkedIn followers tell me nothing about that journey, and the website’s “Opening your workspace…” response proves neither functionality nor failure. The working-artifact and enabling-layer dimensions therefore stay at their missing-evidence defaults.

I pass on this dossier. My willingness to fund pre-revenue physical technology does apply here, but the exception needs affirmative substitutes for revenue: a working prototype, credible commitments, or unusually deep founder obsession. None is established strongly enough to carry this check. My expectation is that, by October 2029, Confetti will have narrowed into design-to-order tools for experienced makers rather than delivered its broad beginner workflow. One verifiable result would change my decision: an independently observed cohort of ten first-time builders each completing, using, and then paying for a second Confetti-designed project, with component substitutions and assembly failures recorded. That would show people crossing the difficult boundary from a sentence to an object—and choosing to cross it again.
