# Executor -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Rhys Sullivan is a solo founder who previously built Answer Overflow to 1.5 million monthly users, making Executor a governance-clean infrastructure bet with authentic technical lineage—and almost the inverse of my capital-intensive frontier swing. Executor has one person holding the tiebreaker, but Executor produces schemas, policies, and credential routing rather than a rocket, drone, camera, or other artifact that escapes the screen. The signature is a credible builder with unusually clear authority operating in a crowded, software-only layer.

SpaceX is the high rung for unambiguous authority: Elon could make the irreversible call when the rocket and the money were both burning. Executor sits just below that rung because a team size of one gives Rhys Sullivan uncontested operating authority without negotiation. Executor diverges because the dossier names Sullivan only as “Founder”; it provides no founder-vesting terms, board structure, or conflict protocol. One captain is better than two people reaching for the wheel, but I still want to see who owns the boat after financing.

DeepMind failed my capital-intensive, tactile-frontier test and needed the polymath exception to overcome it. Executor shows the same axis-level weakness without equivalent evidence of a Hassabis-shaped exception: Executor is an MIT-licensed integration runtime competing with Composio, Arcade, Pipedream, Nango, Zapier, and Workato. Credential concentration creates genuine security stakes, but stakes are not frontier technology. Sandboxing and host-side secret injection make Executor useful infrastructure; they do not make Executor ugly, regulated, or physically difficult enough to repel capital.

Answer Overflow serving 1.5 million monthly users is the mechanism that could compound Executor’s founder-market fit over the next 18 months. Sullivan has already worked on Vercel Domains and Vercel for Platforms, then built a cross-community integration product before Executor accumulated roughly 2,800 GitHub stars and 199 forks. Executor did not emerge from a TAM memo; Executor emerged from repeated contact with developer platforms, fragmented services, and open-source distribution. The missing piece is autobiographical specificity—the moment Sullivan personally felt agent integrations breaking—so this is adjacent lived experience, not the full Niantic decade-of-obsession pattern.

Composio’s $29 million and Arcade’s $12 million show that Executor is entering a watched category, not discovering a category everyone else finds ridiculous. Executor becomes magically weird enough to move up a tier only if arbitrary imported MCP, OpenAPI, and GraphQL sources prove fundamentally incompatible with managed catalogs—or if sophisticated investors reject Executor because self-hosted credential execution looks too dangerous while users adopt it anyway. Today, 2,800 stars against only 22 trailing-30-day Homebrew installs looks more like developer attention than secret pull. Executor may be underbuilt, but the evidence does not yet show that Executor is underpriced.

Niantic let me inhabit the product before the investment thesis arrived; Ingress put the signal directly in my hand. Executor offers desktop, CLI, Docker, Cloudflare, and hosted runtimes, so I can install Executor and watch a 10-of-10 benchmark result within 48 hours. Yet Executor remains a developer abstraction whose value depends on tool discovery, token consumption, and credential handling rather than something a normal person can ride, scan, or photograph. The installability keeps Executor above the bottom rung; the absence of an observable physical-world consequence keeps it far below Niantic.

Executor’s $150-per-organization Team plan gives me a falsifiable forecast: by July 2029, Executor reaches $3 million to $7 million in ARR and is acquired by Cloudflare for its open-source integration runtime and host-side credential architecture. Executor’s likely path is not monopoly through proprietary connectors; Executor becomes the neutral normalization layer, converts organizational policies and execution histories into switching costs, and fits naturally beside the Cloudflare runtime already supported. I am arguing with my future self: the founder quality can produce a good infrastructure outcome even though the artifact never leaves the screen.

Caplets completing the same 10 tasks with 236,803 tokens versus Executor’s 675,842 is the bear case that stings. Executor could be a beautifully distributed feature: MIT-licensed, expensive in tokens, surrounded by Composio, Workato, Pipedream, and platform vendors capable of bundling equivalent routing into existing products. Executor’s 57,245-follower founder account and 2,800 stars could amplify curiosity while 22 monthly Homebrew installs reveal weak durable usage. Sullivan’s prior 1.5-million-user product and Executor’s 199 forks keep that from being my base case, but they do not earn my own-money check yet. More than 200 paying Team organizations with at least 70% six-month retention would verifiably flip Executor from Neutral to Invest.
