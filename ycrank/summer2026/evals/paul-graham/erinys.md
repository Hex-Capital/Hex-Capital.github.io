# Erinys -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The first question is what broke. Erinys has chosen a business full of things that should break: nonlawyer ownership restrictions, fee-sharing rules, client confidentiality, working-capital demands, and the daily mess of operating plaintiff firms. But the dossier does not name one of these obstacles actually encountered, the workaround the founders shipped, or the result. Saying you provide compliance and capital is a description of the intended machine. It is not yet evidence that the machine survived contact with reality.

The strongest evidence is the April 2026 investigation prototype Sasha Zyuzin and Jason Guo built to query 22 government databases. That is a real technical artifact produced before the broader company was established, and their security, OSINT, and electrical-engineering backgrounds make it more credible than a mockup. But it proves they can build an investigation tool, not that they can operate a law firm. Erinys moved from a False Claims Act prototype in April to a network of plaintiff firms by August. The speed is good. Without the customer or technical observation that caused the change, however, I cannot distinguish a running back changing routes from founders choosing a larger-sounding noun.

The missing fact is a lawyer pulling the product back. Erinys has a precise audience and a “Start a Firm” funnel, but its public launch asks for lawyers and referrals rather than showing one using the system. There is no named member firm, paid case, repeat workflow, signed commitment, or revenue. Eve’s 1,000 firms and 200,000 cases prove that plaintiff lawyers will use AI; they do not prove that one will hand Erinys operations, billing, staffing, and economics. Competitor traction may validate the market while invalidating the more demanding network structure.

The bull case is that the demanding structure is the point. Staffing, intake, collections, compliance, capital, and internal software create an unusually schlep-heavy wedge, and deploying technology inside operated firms could produce a better learning loop than selling software from outside. The $396.8 billion legal-services market leaves room for a large outcome. But Erinys has not disclosed its economics or a lawful ownership structure, and none of the three founders is publicly shown to have practiced law. This is therefore an ugly problem they have selected, not yet an ugly problem they encountered organically. Existing firms can also buy overlapping software from Eve or Supio without surrendering control.

The prior prototype is the best argument for an exception: two founders have built together, shipped something concrete, and changed direction quickly. It still does not replace the failed priors. The technical proof belongs to the former product; the present product lacks both current-system proof and unmistakable user pull. The full trio’s history is also thinner than the Zyuzin–Guo relationship. YC backing and a public launch are not substitutes for seeing what these founders do when the first lawyer, bar regulator, or financing source says no.

I would pass. My 36-month base case is that by August 2029 Erinys has abandoned the operated-firm network and become an investigation or intake-software vendor to existing plaintiff firms, with less than $2 million ARR. One verifiable fact would flip me: a named lawyer launches or converts a firm through Erinys under a disclosed state-compliant structure and processes at least 25 paid matters through the system. That would simultaneously reveal the workaround, the crude product, and the user who needs it now.
