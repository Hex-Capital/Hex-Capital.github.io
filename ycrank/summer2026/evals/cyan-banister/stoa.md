# Stoa -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. AI hardware changes hands continuously among model companies, neoclouds, lenders, and data-center operators; used accelerators have observable curves for price, condition, location, and remaining economic life. Financing can clear against those curves instead of a forwarded spreadsheet. Stoa has found a plausible leak from that future: more than $300 million of RFQs entered its marketplace during its first month. But the dossier maps only one transition—Blackwell upgrades pushing H100s into secondary supply. It does not yet show the sequence from episodic RFQs to trusted settlement data, credible benchmarks, and financeable collateral.

The broken system is concrete even if its rent is not. Buyers receive private quotes, dealers circulate partial inventory lists, and neither side sees a shared history of firm bids and completed transactions. A 48-hour blind-RFQ process could return negotiating control to buyers while giving dealers cleaner access to institutional demand. Yet Stoa never quantifies the broker spread, delay, or failed-trade cost being removed. More importantly, its terms disclaim responsibility for hardware existence, authenticity, condition, delivery, and payment. Those are not incidental details around the market; they are much of the hidden operating complexity that makes this market broken.

The strongest bull fact is also the fact I trust least without another layer of detail. Routing $300 million of first-month RFQs means serious organizations were willing to describe expensive purchasing intent through a functioning product. That is much more interesting than a waitlist. But an RFQ is not a trade, Stoa discloses neither executed volume nor revenue, and its H100 index is unavailable. The 12-month non-circumvention clause is another mundane artifact worth staring at: Stoa already knows that introductions can leak off-platform. If counterparties exchange contact information but not money through the system, the supposed data flywheel records curiosity rather than price discovery.

The founders are unusually well composed for this exact mechanism. Eren Berke Saglam traded interest-rate derivatives at Citi; Kaan Yigit built production trading, pricing, and data systems at Uniper; Berat Celik is a repeat founder who left an xAI opportunity, and the three have known one another for more than ten years. That covers technical construction, market mechanics, and an identifiable CEO. It does not establish the deeper first-believer pattern I look for: no dated pre-Stoa GPU-market experiments, prior hardware trades, or operating wound explains why this team could not leave the problem alone. Their résumés fit the market beautifully; the dossier does not yet prove obsession preceded the fashionable AI-hardware opportunity.

Stoa does sit beneath today’s AI application frenzy. Contractual ownership of aggregated bids, offers, volumes, and transaction data could become infrastructure for dealers, operators, and GPU-backed lenders rather than another thin interface to a model provider. The bear case stings because that controlled asset exists only if trades settle on-platform. Compute Exchange already spans physical GPUs, reserved capacity, and forwards across more than 100 providers, while Procurri owns inspection, processing, logistics, and IT-asset-disposition operations that Stoa currently disclaims. Nvidia upgrade cycles create supply, but they do not grant Stoa control of it.

I am not writing the check yet. Pre-revenue is not the issue; a working artifact plus institutional proof can override missing revenue. The unresolved question is whether Stoa is becoming an exchange or merely a broker-shaped lead generator in a hot market. My flip condition is one independently verifiable quarter with at least $50 million of completed on-platform trades, repeat participation from both named buyers and dealers, and a published index calculated from settlements rather than RFQ notional. That would turn the $300 million leak into evidence that the future has actually begun.
