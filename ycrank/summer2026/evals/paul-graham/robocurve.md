# Robocurve -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important fact about Robocurve is missing: what broke, and what did the founders do next? Jay Chooi and Aris Zhu have built a great deal—approximately 400 commits, 13 public repositories, a versioned PyPI package, and a framework spanning simulated and physical robots. But the dossier names no customer rejection, hardware failure, or evaluation bottleneck followed by a founder-built workaround. This is evidence of industriousness, not yet of resourcefulness under resistance.

The technical proof is much stronger. Robocurve has completed a pilot scoring a frontier model on a real robot. Inspect Robots produces grader scores, configurations, transcripts, traces, and visualizations across embodiments. This is functioning infrastructure built before the market granted permission, not a diagram of what the founders intend to build. Chooi’s evaluation work at the UK AI Security Institute and Zhu’s experience at Amazon Robotics and Amazon AGI Labs make the artifact unusually credible for a two-person pre-seed team.

The missing half of the proof is a narrow well of users. The 134 GitHub stars and 19 forks show that technical people notice the project, but stars are applause and forks are ambiguous. The single pilot is assigned to the technical proof because the dossier does not identify the laboratory, whether it paid, whether it returned, or what decision the evaluation changed. Robocurve may become a useful standard, but today there is no named user niche pulling the crude product back.

The bull case is sharp. Robotics evaluation is genuinely unpleasant work: maintained hardware, controlled setups, resets, domain experts, and reproducibility across embodiments. Those schleps could produce a valuable proprietary history even when the framework is MIT-licensed. The founders also match an unstated pattern I like: technical builders releasing a programmable interface before its category has settled. But the more important part of that pattern is repeated developer use. Public repositories and one pilot do not yet establish it.

There are also two structural traps. Positronic, Instance, and ReSim are already converging on overlapping evaluation systems, while laboratories can reuse Robocurve’s open-source code internally. The inferred commercial route therefore depends on paid evaluation engagements or benchmark-operation partnerships, yet no buyer, price, or recurring workflow is disclosed. That approaches the “partner” red flag: another organization controls both the timetable and the conversion to revenue. The known-exception logic does not rescue this. A working product can override some missing history, but only when unusually strong user pull, independent validation, or rapid correction replaces the missing evidence. Robocurve has technical proof, not that replacement.

I would not write the check yet. My forecast is that within 36 months Robocurve either becomes a small operated-testing business with under $2 million ARR or pivots toward internal robotics QA infrastructure, because the framework is ahead of demonstrated external demand. One result would flip me: a dated case study showing the founders overcoming a named deployment blocker within two weeks and the same three identified robotics teams each paying for at least two evaluation cycles. That would prove both the runners and the route.
