# SubVysion -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first facts I need are missing. SubVysion sold $355,000 of SAFEs to six investors beginning February 27, 2026, but the dossier gives no valuation cap, competing-term-sheet count, or evidence that this remains a scarce first check. Six investors do not prove an auction, yet they do not establish cheap, non-consensus pricing either. I cannot underwrite the deal merely because the company is young.

The capital also lacks a named venture moment. The real technical uncertainty is whether SubVysion can maintain useful detection accuracy across clay, moisture, and small-diameter utilities—the exact conditions FHWA identifies as GPR limitations. “100% visibility” and centimeter accuracy are marketing claims until an independent field trial supplies detection and false-negative rates. The dossier names neither that experiment nor a deadline or use-of-funds plan. Hiring more people to deploy more rovers would expand an operation; it would not reveal a discrete new truth.

There is nevertheless a real company underneath the claims. The founders cold-called their way to a first customer and investor, secured job-site contracts, and put the product onto construction sites across the United States. That is materially better than followers, demonstrations, or unsigned interest. But it remains early distribution rather than a proprietary dark pool: there is no paid revenue figure, repeat-project rate, retained map usage, or evidence that accumulated corrections make the next site cheaper or more accurate. Cold calling can start a service business; it does not by itself create owned access.

The team is the strongest part. Maxwell Stone built on-device inference software deployed in more than 10,000 pharmacies and worked on railyard mapping and defect detection. Wenson Tang shipped emergency-dispatch software and built a LiDAR search-and-rescue drone. Combined with their direct work alongside contractors and crews, that gives SubVysion credible translation ability between field sensing and safety-critical infrastructure operations. They are not merely two computer-science graduates applying generic AI to construction.

The bull case stings: technically capable founders formed in January 2026, reached live sites through cold outreach, and may use project work to accumulate a proprietary subsurface dataset before larger competitors can serve smaller contractors economically. Physical deployment could be the moat because it is unpleasant and operationally difficult. But today that dataset is inferred, while Exodigo already reports 86.1 million square feet mapped, more than 100 customers, and over $200 million raised. SubVysion has disclosed neither independent accuracy evidence nor a structural advantage in collection cost, throughput, or customer access.

Delete the next financing and the dossier stops answering questions. There is no pricing, gross margin, burn, rover throughput, or controllable cash metric from which to derive a profitable path. Field collection, alignment, validation, and quality control could become a good project-based mapping business, but company equity only becomes attractive when each deployment compounds rather than merely generates another services invoice. I am passing. The single condition that flips me is a dated independent field trial showing commercially acceptable detection and false-negative rates across wet, clay, and small-utility conditions, with three customers subsequently purchasing a second project at positive gross margin.
