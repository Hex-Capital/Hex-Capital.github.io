# Contour -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Contour’s potentially important secret is that permitting automation must be built inside an owned consulting practice because the knowledge required to automate the work cannot be purchased through a software contract. That would explain acquiring firms and embedding engineers on-site. It is my reconstruction of the opportunity, however, not an established finding in the dossier. No named practitioner rejects this proposition, and no operating comparison tests it. If Cortex produces no durable productivity advantage over the same professionals using commercially available tools, the secret disappears.

Ownership could nevertheless change the economics substantially. A software vendor must persuade consultants to adopt a tool that may reduce their billable hours; an owner could retain the savings and sell additional capacity. This is the strongest objection to passing: Contour might acquire both distribution and the environment needed to develop its technology, bypassing the adoption problem faced by external vendors. Paces and Transect demonstrate that environmental workflows already attract software investment, but their presence does not establish that Contour’s ownership model is interchangeable with theirs. Equally, acquiring client relationships does not establish a monopoly. The dossier documents no completed acquisition, exclusive data rights, dominant local position, or measured Cortex advantage.

The four founders have capabilities relevant to assembling this business. Agrawal built enterprise search at Rezolve AI; Lubar worked in investment banking at Lazard. Those backgrounds make the division between engineering and acquisitions intelligible. They do not establish founding alignment. Shared attendance at Jumeirah College does not prove that Simpson, Agrawal, and Miller previously worked together; full-time status, ownership, and core engineering arrangements remain undocumented. I therefore use the founding-structure default. There is no evidence of the part-time-founder or investor-dominated-capitalization red flags, but there is also no demonstrated operator network of the kind that makes internally assembled companies especially interesting.

Contour has one coherent prospective revenue engine: service fees from acquired environmental consulting practices. Its scale economics remain unmeasured. The broader market’s $27.0 billion of 2024 revenue includes work outside permitting, and 3.0% annual growth does little to establish a power-law outcome. Purchase prices, financing terms, service pricing, and post-automation margins would determine whether expansion creates exceptional equity value or merely purchases more labor revenue. The website names a route to practice sellers considering succession. It supplies no evidence about acquiring infrastructure-developer customers or delivering their projects economically. Fall 2026 YC participation establishes neither customer demand nor distribution performance; zero job postings carries no negative weight.

I would pass on a check today while leaving the operating thesis open. Contour fits the unstated preference for enabling physical buildout, but that exposure does not establish proprietary technology or value capture. Its acquisition-and-integration sequence raises a coordination question without yet proving the severe failure stack that would justify an adverse gate score. I also find no exception supported by independent technical proof, persistent customer transactions, or validated production economics that resolves the missing primary evidence. One verifiable condition would reopen my decision: an independently audited six-month operating record from an acquired practice showing that Cortex doubles gross profit per professional while maintaining regulatory acceptance and repeat customer business. That would begin to distinguish an automation business from a succession buyer.
