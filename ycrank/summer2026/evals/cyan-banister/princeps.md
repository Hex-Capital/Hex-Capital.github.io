# PRINCEPS -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. GPU fleets are financed like power plants, availability is priced into contracts, and lenders can insure interruption and residual value instead of hiding those risks inside covenants. Getting there requires carriers to accept new loss models, operators to expose telemetry, and claims experience to turn bespoke policies into repeatable products. The present-day leak is real: more than $20 billion of GPU-backed debt, $822 billion in projected 2026 data-center spending, and SLA credits standing in for insurance. PRINCEPS sees the future, but the dossier does not yet show that it owns any part of the bridge.

The irritating system is also identifiable. Standard policies can exclude GPU outages and SLA breaches, leaving operators, customers, and infrastructure lenders holding exposures that service credits may not cover. But why does that failure persist? Who captures the rent? How large is the uncovered loss? PRINCEPS has not quantified those answers or shown how much additional financing its coverage unlocks. It describes a missing product more clearly than the economics forcing someone to buy it.

The team has useful adjacent pieces. Anya Trofimova worked in data-center project finance and infrastructure security; Wilhem Hector modeled uncertainty in energy systems; Conrad Frøyland Moe has operated an events business reporting more than €15,000 per event. That is a credible finance-technical-commercial combination. It is not yet first-believer proof: there is no dated underwriting experiment, shipped insurance artifact, actuarial history, or evidence that this obsession preceded the current compute boom. Moe is identified as CEO, so I do not see a leadership shell game, although decision rights among three co-founders remain unreported.

The workbench supposedly combines SLA terms, market prices, grid data, failure modes, and contract structures, and Ornn is a potentially useful distribution and transparency relationship. Yet no carrier has bound a policy, no operator has signed an LOI, and no user is shown paying, sharing sensitive telemetry, or changing a financing decision. YC, NVIDIA Inception, four job listings, and a London Tech Week finalist slot are institutional signals, not costly user behavior. I have tolerated missing revenue when a pre-seed company supplied a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a founder’s lifelong technical record, as with CosmicBrain AI; PRINCEPS currently supplies none of those substitutes.

The bull case stings because insurance may be the concealed infrastructure underneath the visible compute wave. Sparse loss histories could become an advantage if PRINCEPS binds the first policies and compounds proprietary claims data. But today the dependency runs the other way: third-party carriers control capacity, Forward Compute already markets the same three principal protections, Parametrix monitors more than 7,000 cloud services and 750 data centers, and American Compute reports more than 600,000 hardware-pricing datapoints. PRINCEPS does not yet control the scarce input. It is assembling an interface around other parties’ balance sheets and data.

My 36-month expectation is that PRINCEPS narrows into GPU-finance brokerage or risk analytics, remains below $3 million ARR, and does not establish a proprietary underwriting franchise. The verifiable flip condition is one named carrier-bound policy for a named neocloud or infrastructure lender that renews after twelve months while supplying PRINCEPS exclusive operating and claims telemetry. Until then, this is a clever map of a real future without the mundane artifact proving that PRINCEPS gets to own it. I would not write the check today.
