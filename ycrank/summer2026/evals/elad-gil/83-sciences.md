# 83 Sciences -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: stronger materials models and new substitution mandates let industrial labs exploit experiments previously trapped in notebooks and instruments; the controlling variable is whether those histories produce reproducible discoveries quickly enough that partners pay, renew, and contribute usable data rights. The reported battery result—revised synthesis conditions exceeding a prior capacity plateau and a manuscript completed within two months—is evidence for that mechanism. It is not yet evidence of repeatability: the customer is anonymous, the result is company-reported, and no commercial renewal or validation threshold is disclosed.

The technology shift is real but its economics remain undefined. MatterGen’s January 2025 publication expanded what generative materials systems can attempt, while France’s February 2025 PFAS law created a concrete replacement-material need. Yet 83 Sciences has not quantified how Dalton changes experiment count, discovery cost, laboratory throughput, or partner margins. A $170.4 million materials-informatics market growing to $410.4 million by 2030 is too small to carry the venture case by itself; the larger outcome requires capturing discovery economics, not merely selling research software into that category.

The product is more substantial than a model wrapper. It ingests voice notes, notebooks, instrument exports, and failed runs, structures experimental state, proposes process conditions, and keeps discovery contracts inside Dalton. Better models should improve this workflow. The unresolved issue is value capture: there are no public terms establishing exclusive access, cross-partner learning rights, retention, or ownership of foreground IP. Calling unpublished experiments proprietary data is premature until the company can state what a competitor would pay, how long reproduction would take, and which contractual rights prevent customers from taking the structured corpus elsewhere.

Demand is promising but still soft. One novel material, one draft manuscript, and dozens of partnerships “in progress” are meaningful at pre-seed; 781 LinkedIn followers and grants of up to $50,000 are not product-market pull. The strongest bull case is that industrial laboratories normally move slowly, so producing a superior result and manuscript in under two months may be unusually informative—and the founders combine chemical engineering commercialization, an MIT chemistry PhD, materials ML, and enterprise AI deployment. That could justify a research-stage override. It does not yet: the dossier shows neither independently validated frontier technology nor a named partner repeatedly paying for the outcome.

The red-flag path is a services-heavy discovery business whose grants subsidize onboarding while open models and well-funded competitors commoditize the model layer. Three simultaneous hires—including a Head of Sales and two engineers—also precede evidence that the company is scaling a repeatable system rather than broadening around one successful engagement. My 36-month base case is therefore a $2 million–$5 million ARR, platform-enabled materials consultancy rather than a category-defining software company.

I would monitor rather than invest my own money today. The verifiable flip condition is two named industrial customers each paying at least $250,000 annually and renewing after independently validated discoveries, under contracts that permit cross-partner model learning. That would prove both the controlling market variable and the compounding data mechanism; without it, 83 Sciences has an interesting workflow but not yet a large-company market structure.
