# Verno -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Verno’s opportunity depends on whether AI can make an approved engineering change substantially cheaper across the entire document set. Process EPCs and industrial operators already buy CAD software; the opening is automating the reconciliation work that remains after a design changes. The controlling variable is total engineer time per correctly completed change, including review and correction. The dossier establishes the problem but provides no measurement of that variable. I would pass on investing today because the economic mechanism remains unproven.

The March 2026 ChatP&ID research supplies a concrete technical catalyst: structured representations can improve diagram reasoning and reduce processing costs relative to direct ingestion. That supports some credit for timing. It does not establish that Verno reduces customer engineering costs. Cheaper inference could be economically irrelevant if checking proposed edits consumes the saved drafting time. Autodesk already supplies P&ID authoring and configurable validation, so Verno needs an advantage large enough to justify another vendor and approval process.

The strongest case against passing is that Verno already acts on the underlying engineering files. Its described workflow edits native CAD, checks related project documents, and incorporates changes after engineer acceptance. Better models could improve task completion within that workflow. This fits my pattern of backing software around physical-industry transitions. However, connectors being built with design partners receive less credit than established integrations, and the dossier does not document persistent project state, accumulated correction data, or ownership of the complete change process. There is meaningful workflow substance here, but durable control remains only partly demonstrated.

Customer access makes this more interesting than a technical demonstration. The July 2, 2026 recruiting post describes an unnamed North American EPC partnership processing actual drawings; the November announcement reports live customer deployments. Those are useful pre-seed signals, discounted because they are company-reported and do not establish repeat usage or expansion. The 618 LinkedIn followers and pitch competition win add no evidence of product-market pull. Demonstrations using customer drawings could become an effective sales channel, but there is no evidence yet that the first deployment creates reusable distribution into additional projects.

The team can plausibly build this product: Wesche brings engineering-document automation experience, Madan brings document-extraction experience, and Shinde adds more than a decade in petrochemicals and EPC consulting. Those credentials support execution credibility without establishing the costly commitment or prior startup learning my founder criterion measures. My research-stage exception also does not apply: the dossier contains no comparable frontier research record or documented capital path. Nor would I invoke the physical-business exception merely because customers operate plants; Verno must demonstrate its own software delivery economics. The relevant red-flag test is whether deployments require substantial recurring engineering services. That remains unknown, rather than an established negative.

My flip condition is one documented, paid, 90-day EPC deployment showing at least a 50% reduction in total engineer time per approved cross-document change, including review and correction. That would directly test whether native editing creates enough economic value to support an independent product despite Autodesk’s distribution. Today, Verno has a credible workflow and early customer access, but insufficient evidence that its market will amplify them into a large business.
