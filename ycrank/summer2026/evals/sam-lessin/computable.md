# Computable -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first three numbers I need are missing: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely the first scarce check. That is not ordinary pre-seed missingness for me; price is evidence. Without it, I cannot tell whether Computable is a cheap wager on a strange market structure or another AI-infrastructure thesis already auctioned into consensus.

The product does identify a clean hypothesis: GPU capacity becomes more valuable when eight-H100 nodes can be bought by calendar week, redeemed for SSH access, or resold through resting bids and offers. The founders’ reported years building trading infrastructure at Jump Trading and Coinbase make the exchange machinery credible. But the dossier does not say what this round funds, when the experiment ends, or what measurable result flips the card. A potentially good venture moment is not the same thing as a financed, dated venture moment.

The bull case is the translation layer. Computable is importing price discovery and secondary liquidity from electronic markets into private, inflexible GPU leases reportedly carrying 2x price dispersion and 6–24-month commitments. Its terms give it ownership of bid, order, trade, volume, and market-pattern data, which could become a proprietary forward curve. Live H100 listings from August 2026 through January 2027 at $2.56–$2.69 per GPU-hour show a working market surface, not merely a slide.

But listings are supply, not liquidity. The dossier reports no matched trade, buyer, seller, repeat redemption, trading volume, or fee revenue. The Show HN launch produced 45 points and 26 comments; those are attention metrics with no demonstrated causal path to a purchase. More damagingly, the website promises every position a standing buyback quote while the terms disclaim any repurchase obligation or guaranteed price. Instant liquidity is the product’s central claim, so this discrepancy sits inside the mechanism rather than at its edge.

Delete the next financing and the economics are equally unresolved. A 2% fee charged to each side can support a compact marketplace if volume and servicing costs cooperate, but Computable discloses no revenue, gross margin, burn, runway, or route to break-even. SF Compute already offers resale and reports more than $100 million of third-party hardware under management; Runpod and Vast.ai have substantial operating scale. Those facts do not kill a focused weekly contract, but they mean market data becomes defensible only after Computable owns transactions competitors cannot cheaply reproduce.

My strongest opposing view is that the founders already know the difficult half of the problem: designing credible markets, pricing risk, and operating transaction infrastructure. A thin initial market can become nonlinear once buyers trust redemption and suppliers trust settlement. Yet there is no documented operating receipt from the compute-supply side, and none of the hard external evidence that would justify overriding missing terms and economics. I pass. The single flip condition is $1 million of independently verifiable matched reservation volume, including repeat trades by both buyers and sellers, completed without company-funded buybacks; that would prove the claimed liquidity is customer-created rather than staged inventory.
