# Standard Machines -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Chip-design agents generate architectures, test alternatives, and learn from failed layouts inside simulated environments before scarce engineers or fabs touch them. Standard Machines could own the training ground beneath that behavior. The present-day leak is real but noisy: chip design still takes two years within a three-to-five-year production cycle, while ChipAgents, Cognichip, and Ricursive have raised a combined $502 million around adjacent automation. What is missing is the causal bridge—what Standard Machines uniquely simulates, which task improves first, and why its environment compounds faster than the tools already inside Cadence and Synopsys.

Why does chip design take two years? Which delay comes from human search, which from verification, which from proprietary IP, and which from physics? The dossier identifies the wait but not its rent. It does not quantify engineering cost, name the party benefiting from the delay, or show whether the product returns control to chip architects rather than inserting another vendor between them and their designs. A shorter design loop could create enormous agency, but “could” is carrying too much weight.

Jacob Peake is credible at the point of attack. He graduated from Imperial College London in 2025, worked on GPU architecture at Apple, and has published material spanning computer architecture, hardware/software co-design, and AI-chip architectures. That earns real founder-market-fit credit. It does not yet show the pre-company trail I want: dated independent builds, an environment shipped before the category became fashionable, recruited complementary talent, or a customer who pulled him into the problem. This is strong technical proximity, not yet first-believer proof.

The most attractive reading is that reinforcement-learning environments are the concealed infrastructure beneath the crowded chip-agent wave. The painful counterargument is stronger than a generic competition objection: Cadence Cerebrus and Synopsys DSO.ai already apply reinforcement learning, while chip-design data remains private and difficult to acquire. Standard Machines has disclosed no controlled dataset, proprietary reward system, benchmark, deployment, or structural access advantage. An enabling layer without control of its scarce input is a position in a diagram, not a moat.

There is also no product behavior to inhabit. No public benchmark, customer deployment, taped-out chip, repeat training run, or production-performance result appears in the dossier; even the website was inaccessible during research. The pre-revenue exception represented by CosmicBrain AI does not rescue this evidence gap: both companies attack difficult technical infrastructure, but CosmicBrain had a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept. Standard Machines currently offers neither publicly.

I would not write the check from my own money today, but I would keep the conversation alive. The one condition that flips me is a named semiconductor team completing at least 100 training runs in Standard Machines’ environment and reporting a reproducible reduction of 20% or more in time to solve one defined design task. That single artifact would connect the founder, the future, the technical layer, and costly user behavior. Until then, this is a compelling place in the stack with insufficient proof that Standard Machines owns it.
