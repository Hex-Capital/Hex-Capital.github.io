# Hemlock -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

A robot that sees a lightbulb but cannot control its grip is missing something fundamental. Hemlock proposes recovering that missing force signal from workers’ muscles, without covering their hands. If this works across ordinary workplaces, it could make human demonstrations substantially more useful for teaching physical work. That is a consequential mechanism. But the dossier does not quantify how much labor, collection expense, or training data it could eliminate. The cited $1.05 billion robot-training-data market measures an adjacent spending pool; it does not establish an orders-of-magnitude societal consequence.

The strongest evidence is the company-reported 10× improvement in egg handling and 2.7× improvement in bulb installation when Bracelet data supplements demonstrations. These are useful comparisons against training without the signal. They directly test whether force information changes robot behavior. But ten times a tiny success rate can still be commercially useless. Hemlock supplies no absolute success rates, trial counts, or sufficiently detailed protocol, and no measured comparison with MANUS or StretchSense collection. I give the prototype meaningful technical credit, while keeping the claimed discontinuity provisional.

Julian Olschwang has worked on the relevant problems: wearable hand sensing through Talk To The Hand and robotic training-data generation at UCLA’s Biomechatronics Lab. That combination matters more than a generic AI résumé. It makes the strongest bull case credible: a founder who understands both signal acquisition and training data may have found a scalable way to capture information that video misses. His record does not, however, document a belief revised after disconfirming evidence or complementary recruits brought in to own specific risks. Those dimensions remain unknown; a one-person team is ordinary at this stage.

The dangerous counterargument to my pass is that Hemlock could turn an obscure sensing workflow into a repeatable data-production platform. Physical-AI labs need manipulation data, the Bracelet has reported task-level results, and collection across workers could eventually produce a difficult-to-recreate corpus. That matches my preference for scientific workflows that become platforms. Yet the proprietary learning loop remains hypothetical. FACT’s EgoForce research shows that force-supervised data is also being developed elsewhere. Hemlock must demonstrate that its particular signal generalizes and that customers cannot obtain equivalent useful data more cheaply through another pipeline.

The next dollar should attack transfer across workers, objects, and tasks. The dossier names that uncertainty, but provides no company-owned experiment with a threshold, budget, timing, and stop decision. Likewise, pricing, calibration labor, usable-data yield, and collection agreements are undisclosed, so I cannot infer unsubsidized cost advantage from a wrist-worn form factor. **My flip condition is one independently run, preregistered comparison showing at least 5× higher robot task success on held-out workers and tasks, at equal training budgets and lower fully loaded collection cost than glove capture.** That would connect the technical claim to a scalable economic mechanism.

I pass on the evidence supplied. Hemlock has genuine hardware work and relevant founder experience; the dossier does not establish the standard-model-wrapper red flag. I also checked the exception cases: there is no quantified giant consequence, documented exponential capability trajectory, or demonstrated founder-led platform expansion sufficient to override the missing gates. The absence of revenue does not drive this decision. The promising force signal does. Today it supports a technical experiment more strongly than it supports my investment thesis.
