# DeepReach Inc. -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Several million dollars of signed contracts prove that robot developers will buy real-world demonstrations. They do not yet prove that DeepReach changes the economics of physical intelligence by orders of magnitude. The dossier quantifies supply—475 devices, more than 1,000 experts, and 150,000 professional-skill demonstrations—but not the cost of obtaining useful training data today, the share of robotics development constrained by that cost, or the improvement its network could deliver. This is a real demand pool without a quantified consequence-of-success case.

The technical claim is weaker. Scale AI already collects more than 1,000 demonstration hours daily, while Asimov and others offer overlapping egocentric workplace data. DeepReach’s distinction is organizational: entrepreneur-operated local businesses rather than centralized factories or directly managed collectors. Its stereo hardware may matter, but the dossier supplies no comparison of usable-data yield, environment diversity, policy performance, collection cost, or rejection rate against Scale AI. A different labor topology is not a 5–10x technical discontinuity. Synthetic trajectory generation could also compress the value of undifferentiated clips before this network compounds into a proprietary asset.

The strongest bull case is execution. In three months, six people reportedly established operations across seven countries, collected 500,000 operation clips, and signed contracts worth several million dollars. Tim Li previously built a $15 million-revenue staffing company and a 70,000-person annotation network; Chris Liu adds computer-vision research and production ML experience. This resembles the known exception represented by Block: an execution-heavy wedge into an enormous existing value pool can become much more than its initial product. The material difference is that DeepReach has not shown the proprietary product expansion, exclusive data rights, or compounding customer loop needed to escape services economics.

Its completed deployment proves that distributed collection can operate, but it does not attack the largest kill risks decisively. DeepReach publishes no acceptance-rate cohort, consent and retention framework, partner-retention data, customer concentration, or experiment separating human demonstrations from synthetic substitutes. The company has also moved among evaluation platform, robot deployment, data refinery, and collection-network descriptions. That may be fast belief revision; without the evidence that triggered each change, it may equally be positioning in search of a durable layer. Capital currently appears to be scaling collection before proving which collected data remains uniquely valuable.

My verifiable flip condition is one independently validated customer cohort showing at least five times Scale AI’s accepted, policy-improving demonstrations per dollar across customer-required environment diversity, with repeat orders at positive contribution margin. That single result would connect technical advantage, customer value, and unsubsidized economics. Without it, my 36-month base case is a $10–25 million ARR data-services network concentrated among a few frontier laboratories—not a foundational world-model platform. That could be a good company. It is not yet the asymmetric technical option for which I invest my own money.
