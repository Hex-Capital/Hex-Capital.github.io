# Risklytics -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is: 2026 AI-liability exclusions create policy variation for frontier-technology companies, but Risklytics becomes large only if that variation produces enough costly coverage failures to move customers from established brokers to a specialist. That is the governing variable. The dossier establishes the regulatory change, but not its commercial threshold: there is no disclosed premium volume, uncovered claim, customer switching evidence, or frontier-tech SAM. Carrier access also precedes useful brokerage data, while carriers reportedly want placement history before granting access.

The bull case is real. Risklytics is building around structured intake, policy-form and endorsement analysis, regulatory filings, customer contracts, AI-governance records, carrier routing, and exclusion review before binding. If those components become a continuously updated system of record, improved models should make policy comparison and submission preparation better rather than erase the product. Wholesale brokers and MGAs could also provide reusable distribution. But today the claimed database has no disclosed size, the company is not licensed to place coverage, and there are no placement outcomes feeding the loop. This is an architectural hypothesis, not yet an owned workflow.

The technology shift earns partial credit because ISO introduced optional AI-liability exclusions in 2026 and carriers are treating them differently. That can increase search costs and make specialist brokerage valuable. It does not yet demonstrate a step change in margins or adoption. Risklytics has not quantified how frequently exclusions create material gaps, what customers will pay to resolve them, or whether specialist analysis changes loss ratios or placement speed. Alton Risk already markets substantially the same policy-gap analysis, carrier matching, submissions, and physical-AI specialization; Corgi approaches the category with more than $268 million and control of underwriting as a full-stack carrier.

There is no product-market pull yet. YC acceptance, 123 LinkedIn followers, and a Center for Data Innovation feature are attention signals, not demand. Risklytics has no disclosed revenue, policies, named customers, repeat usage, or signed placements. Pre-seed status explains that absence, but it cannot satisfy a market-first gate. The research-stage exception does not apply: the dossier documents neither frontier technical work nor a rare team whose capabilities independently justify underwriting ahead of demand.

The founders are the strongest part of the evidence. Samuel Gold and Alexander Risio left Harvard to work full-time, Risio has previous startup experience and passed an insurance licensing examination, and the team abandoned Ember after concluding its catastrophe-model product was a non-starter. That shows commitment and willingness to revise. It does not substitute for insurance-brokerage experience, carrier leverage, or customer pull. My base case is that within 36 months the team has pivoted toward policy-analysis and compliance software for wholesale brokers or MGAs rather than operating a standalone brokerage above $1 million in annualized commission revenue.

I pass today because both market gates remain unresolved. The verifiable flip condition is $250,000 in annualized brokerage revenue from at least 20 bound frontier-technology policies, with documented renewals or expanded coverage and more than half placed without founder-specific wholesale intervention. That would show that exclusion complexity changes buying behavior, that carrier access is repeatable, and that the workflow is accumulating proprietary placement state rather than remaining an analysis feature.
