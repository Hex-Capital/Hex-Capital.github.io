# Vestris -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Vestris’s live coverage in Washington, D.C. and Montgomery County makes the analytical signature clear: Vestris is strongest as a single-miracle workflow architecture and weakest on evidence that buyers are already pulling it into the market. Browser agents, document generation, and underwriter sign-off all use capabilities that now exist; Vestris does not need new science. The missing piece is the “just working” signal: a waitlist and custom enterprise pricing are distribution scaffolding, not a revenue or usage curve.

**Color Genomics** is the high rung on single-miracle architecture because Color placed workflow and data infrastructure on top of commodity sequencing rather than betting on new chemistry. Vestris shares that load-bearing mechanism: Vestris places orchestration on top of existing county records, title rules, and human underwriting rather than trying to replace the legal substrate. Vestris diverges because more than 3,700 county systems, browser-agent reliability, Schedule B accuracy, and security-sensitive lender packages may turn one product miracle into several operational miracles. The architecture passes the science-project test, but not yet cleanly.

**Lyft’s** failure mechanism in my Series C pass was that usage did not demonstrate the share inversion required for attractive winner-take-all economics. Vestris has an even earlier version of that evidentiary problem: Vestris discloses a preview waitlist, two supported jurisdictions, and no paid customers, repeat order volume, revenue, or month-over-month growth. Custom pricing based on order volume is not order volume. In an AI workflow market, silence on the curve receives my absent-evidence default because real buying behavior is easy to disclose once it exists.

Browser-operating agents are the inferred catalyst for Vestris, and the 18-month mechanism could compound: every completed closing can improve county navigation, firm-specific corrections, and title-document generation, making Vestris faster to deploy in the next office. Vestris has not tied that mechanism to a named model release, regulatory change, or incumbent failure in the last 24 months, while Qualia already offers built-in AI and Dono raised $6.5 million in February 2026 around adjacent automation. Vestris is skating for the puck, but the dossier does not establish that the rink itself just changed.

Aahil Valliani’s supervised-content patents and Joshua Tang’s six weeks processing settlement files are better than credential-only founder biographies, but Vestris lacks the Perplexity-style record of artifacts appearing between conversations. The counterfactual that flips Vestris into the Invest tier is specific: Valliani and Tang repeatedly expand Vestris into new jurisdictions in response to paid processor feedback while preserving closing accuracy. A live product across two jurisdictions proves Vestris can ship once; it does not yet prove the iterative execution rhythm that predicts shipping at scale.

**Stripe** became an index on e-commerce because every new internet merchant could enter through the same developer rail. Vestris could become an index on automated closings if title firms route every order through Vestris and accumulated corrections make replacement increasingly painful. Today Vestris instead sells jurisdiction by jurisdiction into a workflow already occupied by Qualia, Snapdocs, Endpoint, and Dono. The unlimited-order enterprise plan creates the right economic shape, but Vestris must win share against funded incumbents before it can ride category growth automatically.

Washington, D.C. and Montgomery County are enough to produce a narrow regional product but not yet a national closing rail. My 36-month forecast is that by July 2029 Vestris reaches $1 million to $3 million in ARR from Mid-Atlantic title firms and is acquired by Qualia for under $30 million as an agentic pre-closing module. Vestris’s technically plausible architecture should produce usable automation; Vestris’s absent demand curve and county-by-county deployment burden should prevent independent breakout velocity.

The $18.5 billion in 2025 title-insurance premiums supplies the strongest bull case: Vestris’s founders lived inside an 8–10-hour manual workflow, Vestris already spans contract ingestion through lender packages, and decades of approved and rejected titles may hide a proprietary dataset comparable to the workflow advantage behind Decagon. That case stings because a few reference customers could turn a regional wedge into a national system of record. The evidence that wins today is that Vestris discloses neither dataset exclusivity nor a single paid customer while Qualia already owns workflow distribution. Documented revenue growth above 20% month over month for six consecutive months from paid firms operating across at least ten jurisdictions would flip my verdict.
