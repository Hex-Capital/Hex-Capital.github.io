# OpenHack -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Ananay Arora has found vulnerabilities, collected bug bounties, and shipped security software. That is specific knowledge acquired through action. OpenHack’s published account of reproducing Papermark’s authentication bypass—reported March 1 and patched March 2, 2026—is more informative than his Apple title. The founder understands the work he wants to automate. I give substantial credit for that under founder quality; discovering a vulnerability does not also establish ownership of a durable advantage.

The decisive question is what OpenHack keeps when a competitor copies the scanner. Its public implementation is MIT-licensed, and the dossier identifies no exclusive model, dataset, or established workflow lock-in. Exploit verification can make a finding more valuable without making the business harder to reproduce. Browser and sandbox verification remain beta, with Docker required for sandbox execution. Those are engineering problems a capable founder may solve, but solving them does not automatically create an asset that compounds with deployments. This is the active mismatch with my current software thesis.

OpenHack does fit my pattern of giving individuals tools they can use without institutional permission. Its open-source CLI has 430 stars and 65 forks. But permissionless access is only the entrance; I want to understand what accumulates afterward. There is no demonstrated contribution, referral, or shared-learning loop through which another user improves everyone else’s experience. Likewise, the displayed customer logos establish neither payment nor retention. A $0 entry plan and negotiated Enterprise usage rates describe a route to monetization, while leaving realized value capture unproven.

The strongest argument against withholding a check is that Arora can turn demonstrated security judgment into a product that customers trust more than broad platforms. Automated exploit reproduction addresses a concrete problem: engineering teams cannot investigate every scanner alert. The same application understanding could extend from code review into continuous testing for security teams. That is a credible expansion path, supported by existing spending: Terra reported a historical $1 million ARR milestone, while Aikido reportedly exceeds $40 million ARR across a broader offering. These figures establish demand, not OpenHack’s revenue. They also identify competitors capable of absorbing the feature. The exception I make for an unusually capable founder with a working prototype deserves consideration here, but the dossier does not establish unusual capital efficiency or persistent user behavior strong enough to override the structural concern.

I am neutral on OpenHack and would keep my money uncommitted today. The missing revenue history is acceptable at this stage; the affirmative evidence of reproducibility matters more. There is no documented founder-integrity or financing-pressure red flag. My concern is that an excellent security researcher builds useful software whose economic advantage accrues to customers and competing platforms. One verifiable flip condition would change my decision: a controlled benchmark showing that an exclusive corpus accumulated through customer deployments materially improves exploit verification over the same system without that corpus, with contractual rights to retain and use it. That would demonstrate that serving customers creates something competitors cannot simply download.
