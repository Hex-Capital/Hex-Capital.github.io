# Sona8 -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Sona8’s founders encountered this problem while doing the work. Hantel and Tamme interviewed employees at BCG and saw how much an organization’s account of itself depends on whom the consultants happen to ask. That is a credible origin for a startup. Employee trust, confidentiality, and works-council approval also create the sort of unpleasant work that can conceal an opportunity. But I’m missing the founder story that matters most: what blocked a deployment, what they built to get around it, and how quickly it worked. Prior employment establishes exposure to the problem. It doesn’t establish resourcefulness under resistance.

There is substantially more here than a consulting presentation. Sona8 offers adaptive multilingual interviews, screen sharing, and analysis traceable to employee excerpts. Malmsten’s engineering background is relevant because the team has shipped a product requiring those capabilities. The reported 10,000 interviews support actual deployment, and Pacific Southwest Container’s strategy director describes finding previously unseen efficiency and safety opportunities. This is a specific user job with evidence of use. Yet 10,000 interviews could represent a few large assignments. The number doesn’t tell me whether customers return after the first report, which is the difference between an interesting project and a product they need.

The most revealing technical fact is the founders’ September 2026 research finding that singleton concerns disappeared in 86% of 45 LLM summaries. An unusual workaround known to one employee could be precisely what a transformation team needs to discover. Sona8 describes a fixed codebook, ten coding agents, and six-agent agreement, but agreement alone doesn’t demonstrate preservation of rare observations. The audit shows that the founders can identify a serious failure mode; it doesn’t establish their production error rate or document a shipped correction. Nor does the dossier show an evidence-driven change of product route. I leave adaptability at its missing-evidence default.

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders may already have crossed the difficult part: getting enterprises to let software interview their employees. Founder networks produced customers, an unnamed top-three consulting firm has signed, and a named industrial customer reports useful discoveries. My pattern of backing working products with concrete proof argues for taking this seriously. But Sona8’s proposed workflow graph is still a development goal, rather than an accumulated proprietary substrate. Its platform fee and interview/query charges could support recurring software economics; they could also remain attached to episodic consulting assignments. Celonis already supplies enterprise process context. Sona8 must earn continued use through knowledge those systems miss.

I would hold off on writing a personal check. The score reflects a credible organic idea and a functioning product, with founder behavior under constraint still unestablished. The working-product exceptions deserve consideration, but the disclosed customer evidence isn’t yet unmistakable enough to override that missing founder test. I see no evidence of waiting for permission, premature delegation, or dependence on a promised partnership; the consulting firm is a signed customer. One verifiable event would change my decision: an existing customer documents a concrete deployment blocker that the founders personally solved within weeks, then purchases and completes a second interview cycle because the fix made the product useful. That would connect founder action to returning demand.
