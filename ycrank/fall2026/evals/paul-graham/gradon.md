# Gradon -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Between Vendo’s July–August launch material and Gradon’s October 13 announcement, the founders changed direction. What did they learn? The dossier doesn’t say. That missing link matters more to me than the new product category. Nour Zahzah and Yousef Helal have known each other since kindergarten and have substantial engineering experience. Those are reasons to meet them. But I cannot yet identify an obstacle, the workaround they built, and how quickly it changed the situation. Resourcefulness gets the absent-evidence default. So does evidence-driven persistence: an unexplained pivot proves neither flexibility nor obstinacy.

The strongest evidence is the civil-engineering project reportedly completed in 180 hours against a 750-hour manual estimate. This suggests Gradon has progressed beyond a diagram. It also identifies a plausible first user: a small engineering firm with more work than engineering capacity. But the comparison is company-reported, the customer is unnamed, and the baseline is an estimate. I give the technical proof partial credit. Customer use earns separate demand credit, but the dossier doesn’t establish repeat projects, payment, or referrals. “Doubled revenue per engineer” describes a customer outcome claim; it tells me nothing about Gradon’s own revenue.

On-site implementation could be where the founders discover the real product. Historical drawings, firm standards, and licensed review are exactly the sort of tedious details that can conceal an opportunity. Zahzah’s mechanical-design work gives him relevant context. It does not establish that he personally encountered this particular problem before founding Gradon, so the origin criterion stays at its default. The important tradeoff is whether each installation teaches the software something reusable. Unlimited-usage seats could make buying easy, but private prices and undisclosed implementation costs leave that tradeoff unresolved. SWAPP already advertises overlapping workflow memory and firm-standard validation; those features alone don’t establish an advantage.

The strongest argument against passing is that these founders may already be doing the difficult work while the public record lags behind. A longstanding team with engineering depth has apparently put software into a real civil workflow and produced a substantial time saving. That fits my preference for concrete proof before fundraising rhetoric. But Gradon hasn’t yet demonstrated the stronger version of that pattern: independently inspectable proof or unmistakable user pull. Neither is strong enough here to justify an exception to the founder-evidence gate. I also see no affirmative evidence of the red flags about waiting for partners or delegating early sales. Compensated introductions are an acquisition tactic; their existence doesn’t prove dependency.

I would pass on the evidence available. This is a promising reason to investigate, but insufficient evidence to invest my own money. My flip condition is one documented customer sequence: an engineering firm identifies a blocking failure, the founders ship a workaround within two weeks, and that same firm pays to use it on a second project. That would connect founder behavior, working software, and returning demand without asking the marketing claims to stand in for all three.
