# Keres -- Exit Evaluation

**Action:** Watch  
**Evidence confidence:** Low

### Company case

A strategic acquisition by a defense prime is Keres’s strongest private-company exit path. The memo records a defense accelerator award and procurement of competing systems. Keres claims Garmur can recover for reuse after an intercept. A prime could have reason to buy Keres if repeat use materially lowers the cost per intercept and ownership gives the prime more control over integration and supply than a partnership would. The memo supplies no documented intercept-and-recovery result, measured cost advantage, or evidence that a prime values that control. (*The Idea*, *Market & Traction*, *Key Risks*)

A documented demonstration of repeated post-intercept reuse, with measured costs to return the aircraft to service, is the operating milestone. The founders’ military aviation and jet flight-test experience supports the ability to run a test. The accelerator award amount is undisclosed, the reported $125,000 raise is unconfirmed, and the memo gives no test budget or runway. IP ownership and transfer terms remain unverified. The memo supplies no independently supported fallback that could pay this investor. (*Founders & Team*, *Market & Traction*, *Defensibility*, *Key Facts*)

**Proceeds bridge.** Actual investment terms are unavailable. The illustrative seed case assumes a $500,000 check at a $10 million post-money valuation, 5% initial ownership diluted to 3%, and no follow-on checks. A 5× gross return within eight years requires $2.5 million in holder cash, or about $83.3 million in distributable equity proceeds. The hurdle is $125 million at 2% ownership and $62.5 million at 4%.

For both modeled sales, headline enterprise value is before adjustments. Assume $0 net debt, $0 fees, and $0 in senior waterfall claims, so enterprise value equals distributable equity proceeds. Consideration is all cash at closing, with $0 stock, $0 escrow, $0 earnouts, $0 rollover, and $0 compensation. The prices and dates are scenario assumptions. The memo has no usable evidence to establish a sale-price range. (*Market & Traction*, *Key Facts*, *Key Risks*)

| Scenario | Timing | Enterprise value → distributable equity proceeds | Investor cash at 3% | Gross multiple |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Modest sale | Year 6 | $50 million → $50 million | $1.5 million | 3× |
| Strong sale | Year 8 | $100 million → $100 million | $3 million | 6× |
| Prolonged private | Through year 8 | No sale or distribution | $0, shares retained | 0× realized |

In the prolonged-private scenario, growth plateaus, buyers stay away, public scale remains out of reach, and the company reinvests available cash.

### Countercase

A prime can already source competing systems or partner with Keres if Garmur proves useful. Fortem’s and Cambridge Aerospace’s procurement wins show buyers have alternatives, and the memo identifies no current moat. Even successful recovery could leave too little cost advantage to justify buying the company at the required price. The memo establishes substitution risk while leaving comparative costs and buyer willingness to pay unresolved. That gap supports Watch. The evidence does not establish a binding price ceiling or a probable prolonged-private outcome, so neither Pass override applies. (*Defensibility*, *Market & Traction*, *Key Risks*)

### Decisive diligence question

Would a defense prime’s assessment of a witnessed repeat-use demonstration show an economic advantage to owning Garmur that could support an acquisition near or above the modeled $83.3 million hurdle? A documented assessment linking measured cost per intercept to the value of ownership over a supply agreement would move the action to Reach out.
