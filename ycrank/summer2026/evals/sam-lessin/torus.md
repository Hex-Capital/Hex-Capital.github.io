# Torus -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is the first scarce check. YC participation does not prove an auction, but neither do “multiple Fortune 500 companies” and three open engineering roles prove that capital is scarce. With no terms or financing-process evidence, I assign the low prior required by an undisclosed seed round. I cannot tell whether I am buying unresolved risk cheaply or paying consensus pricing for “AI for physical engineering.”

The product contains a potentially excellent venture moment, but the financing plan does not name it. Torus could test whether project-wide parameter mapping catches a material inconsistency that existing review misses, prevents a documented rework order, and converts the responsible EPC into a paid deployment. That is finite and truth-revealing. The dossier instead says the system runs more than 50 check categories and drafts RFIs, approvals, and follow-ups; it gives no funded milestone, pass/fail metric, or deadline. A plausible experiment is not the same as management committing the round to one.

The strongest evidence is the team. Marcus Lima has operated inside energy and capital infrastructure through Heimdal after studying energy-systems engineering; Rahul Thayil previously built and financed an AI risk-intelligence company. That is a credible translation layer between physical-project complexity and machine-assisted risk detection, and Torus is already serving and deploying with major EPCs and Fortune 500 companies. The CEO/CTO split also gives more accountability than an undifferentiated founder committee, although the dossier does not establish their working history or document scaled operating outcomes from either prior company.

Those customer deployments are meaningful pre-seed evidence, but they do not yet establish a proprietary dark pool. Torus receives five or six live project documents, extracts relationships, and embeds human-reviewed outputs into an engineering workflow. That access can cause a pilot and may accumulate valuable parameter maps and approval histories. Yet no customer is named, and there is no contract value, pilot-to-paid conversion, repeat usage, or retention. The distinction matters: access to confidential documents is promising; evidence that the workflow causes a purchase is investable.

The bear case is not that physical engineering lacks pain. It is that Torus becomes a feature before it becomes a business. Trunk Tools, Transcend, Augmenta, and SWAPP have raised more than $147 million across adjacent document analysis and design automation, while Torus discloses no moat, revenue, gross margin, burn, or route to break-even. Fifty checks and human approval reduce model-risk rhetoric, but incorrect outputs remain safety- and cost-sensitive, SOC 2 remains in progress, and sovereign deployments can turn software margins into implementation economics. Ando is the relevant exception mechanism: enterprise AI can override my skepticism when accuracy, pilot conversion, retention, and customer economics are measured. Torus has disclosed none of those receipts.

My base case is that by August 2029 Torus has narrowed into document-consistency and RFI automation for data-center EPCs and remains below $5 million ARR, rather than becoming the project-wide engineering system it describes today. I am passing at the current evidence level, despite a team capable of proving me wrong. The verifiable flip condition is a disclosed cohort of at least three Fortune 500 or major-EPC pilots converting to annual paid deployments and renewing after twelve months at positive gross margin. That would show the confidential workflow causes purchases and can support a business without another financing narrative.
