# Trope -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what broke. Matthew Chow and Victor Vannara have shipped an ERP product, but the dossier never names an obstacle they encountered, the workaround they built, or how quickly it changed a customer outcome. Their backgrounds at Tesla, Zipline, Amazon, and Schneider Electric show that they can program. They do not yet show the more important thing: what they do when the obvious route stops working. That leaves my main founder test at its missing-evidence default.

There is real technical evidence beneath the marketing. Trope reports an operating agent harness, field-level Business Central APIs, native side panels, release-wave tests, human approval gates, and run logs. The preceding workflow product also had paid pilots and tests reporting two-to-three-times faster completion with 90% fewer errors. But that proof belongs to the May 2026 product. The ERP offering launched in July, and no current deployment has produced a disclosed migration result, go-live time, or independently measured error rate. This is a functioning artifact, but not yet the unusually concrete proof spike I look for before a round.

The same discontinuity weakens the demand evidence. A contract covering an organization with more than 400 staff is meaningful, as are paid pilots, but neither validates the present fixed-fee Business Central implementation service. The current product has no disclosed customer, revenue, repeat usage, or renewal. LinkedIn followers and 28 GitHub stars do not answer the simple question: who needs this crude product now badly enough to let two young founders touch their financial records?

The pivot could be encouraging. In two months the founders moved from general workflow guidance to ERP implementation, which is a much uglier and potentially better wedge. But the dossier does not say what customer observation falsified the earlier route. Without that causal link, I cannot distinguish running-back behavior from attaching a new noun to the same technology. The founders have found real schlep—migration mappings, reconciliation, release regressions, approvals, and master-data errors—but there is also no documented founder-originated collision with ERP implementation. The 200-plus implementations belong to unnamed principal consultants, not demonstrably to the founders.

The strongest bull case is that fixed-fee delivery converts precisely the work conventional integrators prefer to bill by the hour into software. Trope’s technical team could use early services to accumulate exception histories, tests, and migration mappings that become a proprietary substrate. That matches my pattern of programmable infrastructure assembled before category certainty. Yet today the substrate is asserted rather than demonstrated at deployment scale, and the distribution plan partly depends on systems integrators, VARs, and Microsoft partners whose incentives and timetables Trope does not control. Microsoft can also alter the APIs or absorb overlapping functionality into Copilot. No known-exception pattern overrides the missing founder-action and current-user evidence: there is neither unmistakable present-product pull nor an independently validated technical threshold strong enough to substitute for them.

I would not write the check yet. This is a neutral rather than a categorical pass because the product is real, the schlep is valuable, and the prior pilots suggest the founders may be close to stronger evidence. One result would flip me: a named Business Central customer that pays Trope, goes live within eight weeks, runs the agents repeatedly after launch, and documents the specific implementation blocker the founders personally worked around.
