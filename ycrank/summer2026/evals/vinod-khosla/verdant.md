# Verdant -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Verdant’s March 2026 shift from grant management to permitting is the strongest signal in the dossier, while Verdant’s modular SaaS model is the weakest fit with my capital. The analytical signature is a learning-oriented government-software founder pair pursuing a problem too small and too capital-light for my consequence-first portfolio. Verdant may learn quickly enough to find a good business. “Good business” is not the standard. What economic mechanism breaks if Verdant wins? Today, none.

**Slash** (2024) is the high rung for founder learning rate: a documented pivot became a durable business and helped justify a $100M Series C. Verdant shares the pivot mechanism—more than 200 local-government interviews preceded the move from grant management to permitting—but Verdant has no named customer, repeat usage, or revenue proving that the new thesis is better than the old one. Verdant therefore sits below Slash: the founders have demonstrated willingness to update, but not yet the speed or accuracy of learning that earns the exception.

**DoorDash** (2013) failed my required-capital screen because its early roadmap did not require a scientific or capital miracle; extraordinary ambition eventually overrode that weakness. Verdant has the same capital-profile failure without the override. Verdant sells jurisdiction-configured SaaS into a global permit-software market measured at only $307 million, with no disclosed infrastructure, model-training, deployment, or acquisition plan requiring a concentrated $50M check. Any competent generalist can finance Verdant. I am over-resourced for it.

The $307 million permit-management market compounds through slow municipal procurement, ordinance configuration, and implementation services—not through a mechanism capable of redirecting civilization. Over 18 months, Verdant could shorten application cycles and reduce incomplete submissions, but Verdant’s own design leaves decisions with government personnel and describes AI accuracy of 80–90%. That preserves the incumbent labor and administrative regime rather than collapsing it. Replacing Accela or Clariti could create a respectable vertical-software outcome; it does not resemble OpenAI replacing broad classes of work or Impossible Foods attacking animal-protein production.

Verdant’s 80–90% accuracy claim would become interesting if Verdant had built a proprietary ordinance compiler and plan-understanding system that improved across jurisdictions faster than incumbents could copy it. Verdant instead processes data through OpenAI, Anthropic, Reducto, Supabase, AWS, and Google Cloud, while GovWell already offers AutoCheck and Clariti owns CivCheck. The technical-risk score flips upward only if Verdant can name two falsifiable miracles—cross-jurisdiction ordinance reasoning and reliable drawing interpretation—and demonstrate that funded incumbents cannot reproduce either. Today the risk is procurement execution around commodity models, not science that repels consensus capital.

**Glean** overcame founder domain continuity because enterprise information asymmetry offered sufficient consequence and capital scale. Verdant presents the continuity without that offset: Aidan Ng worked across DOJ, USDA, Congress, Deloitte Government and Public Services, and commercial-real-estate underwriting; Jason Yi built local-government HR software at Holly and researched public-benefits automation. Those experiences give Verdant buyer fluency, but Verdant has not articulated a position that contradicts planning experts, municipal vendors, or established permitting doctrine. Two informed founders following 200 interviews is evidence of diligence. It is not evidence of defying expert consensus.

Verdant’s March-to-July product change predicts another adaptation more strongly than it predicts dominance in permitting. By July 2029, Verdant will have pivoted from standalone land-use permitting into an AI intake-and-review layer spanning local-government licensing and grants, while remaining below $3 million ARR. Verdant’s high learning orientation should keep the founders moving; Verdant’s low capital intensity, absent moat, and three better-funded competitors will prevent the permitting product from becoming a defining standalone platform.

GovWell’s 150-agency footprint is the strongest bull case because it proves municipalities will buy modern permitting systems, while Verdant’s 200 interviews and prior pivot resemble the learning-rate exception that mattered in Slash. Verdant could also become a DoorDash-style exception: YC founders enter an unglamorous operational market, learn faster than credentialed incumbents, and expand far beyond the initial category. The case fails today because Verdant has no named deployment, no defensibility signal, and a core mechanism already offered by GovWell, Clariti, CivCheck, and Accela. One verifiable condition flips my verdict: Verdant reaching $1 million ARR across at least five named jurisdictions whose applicants and reviewers use the product end to end.
