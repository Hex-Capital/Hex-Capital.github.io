# Baud -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first numbers I want—post-money valuation, competing term sheets, and check size—are missing. That matters more here because AI chips are already a consensus capital market: Cerebras, Groq, Etched, and d-Matrix have collectively raised billions. Baud may hold a non-consensus technical belief, but I cannot tell whether I am buying that belief cheaply or purchasing the same fashionable category through a smaller company. Financing silence gets the low prior, not the benefit of the doubt.

There is a legitimate venture moment hiding inside the plan. Baud has scheduled a 12nm chip tape-out by year-end 2026, and physical silicon can falsify whether its multiplier-free representation survives the transition from FPGA emulation to an ASIC. The current FPGA reaches more than 1,000 tokens per second, the compiler handles PyTorch-exportable models, and a training-and-inference cluster is live. But the dossier does not say the round is sized specifically to reach tape-out, identify the silicon benchmark that constitutes success, or explain what runway remains if conversion is not bit-exact. It is a discrete experiment, but not yet a properly underwritten capital experiment.

The team is the best evidence. Sarang Zambare shipped ML through more than 100,000 Peloton Guide devices and worked as Caper’s founding ML engineer before its acquisition; Eric Taylor has completed four tape-outs across more than a decade of ASIC work at companies including NVIDIA and Enfabrica. That is a real software-to-silicon translation layer, not résumé adjacency. The multiplier-free representation, ASIC architecture, compiler, distributed-training stack, and GlobalFoundries validation show that Baud has already built across both sides of the bridge.

The missing asset is a dark pool that changes customer behavior. Baud reports active design partners, so this is better than a slide-deck semiconductor company, but it discloses neither their number nor a named workload, repeat usage, prepayment, or capacity commitment. The reserved-capacity program is an offer, not evidence that anyone has purchased. Model migration is especially important: customers may need to retrain in Baud’s representation, while bit-exact conversion works only “in most cases.” Without observed switching behavior, the proprietary stack is technical complexity rather than owned distribution.

The strongest bull case is that this is precisely when such a company must be financed: the rare team has built an end-to-end prototype, demonstrated 1,000-plus tokens per second before tape-out, and validated the process while incumbents spend extraordinary sums preserving multiplication-heavy architectures. If the arithmetic is right, physical silicon could create an enormous discontinuity. The problem is that Baud has disclosed no pricing, revenue, gross margin, burn, fabrication budget, or route to break-even. Delete the next financing and the current plan stops at an unspecified first cluster. My 36-month base case is therefore a pivot toward licensing the multiplier-free compiler and architecture to an established accelerator vendor, rather than Baud becoming a self-financed training-and-inference capacity provider.

I am passing today. The product and founders are unusually credible; the deal and customer economics are not yet legible. One verifiable flip condition would be a named design partner making a non-refundable $1 million capacity prepayment contingent on first silicon meeting a disclosed performance-and-accuracy benchmark. That single contract would connect the technical experiment to a purchase, define the truth the capital must reveal, and provide the first evidence that Baud can finance progress through customers rather than a capital factory.
