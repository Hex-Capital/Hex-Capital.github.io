# Hickory & Company -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Deployable agents give sponsor banks a new way to supervise fintech partners, but Hickory & Company becomes a large business only if it reduces the human work required to produce defensible compliance evidence. My controlling variable is analyst time per examiner-acceptable workpaper, including review and corrections. Automating document collection matters less if checking the resulting work consumes the savings. The dossier establishes the problem and buyer; it does not establish that this economic threshold has been crossed.

Hickory’s product gives me a reason to investigate that possibility. Source-linked retrieval, institution-specific regulatory knowledge, acceptance criteria, and employee approvals describe a substantive workflow. Stronger models could improve task completion while Hickory retains institutional context and the approval process. That is affirmative architectural evidence. However, retained examination records and migration costs remain proposed advantages, and the unnamed live-bank integration is a company claim rather than independently demonstrated workflow ownership.

The strongest bull case combines that product with a team that has operated inside the customer’s environment. Joshua Howland previously co-founded Seis, which he reports reached 500,000 accounts and $10 million in revenue before closing. Those are prior-company results, but building and closing a banking business supplies relevant startup experience. Scott Grow brings more than 20 years of compliance experience and previously worked with Howland; Kevin Anderson adds banking-systems engineering. This team could identify the operational details that generic agent vendors miss. I give credit for prior startup experience without assuming that a closure proves desperation or specific lessons learned.

Current demand remains unestablished. The October 2026 claim of a live-bank integration supports a possible deployment foothold, but says nothing about repeat usage, payment, or expansion. The reported $500,000 financing and Humanfold’s 22 launch videos do not answer those questions. I use the absent-evidence default for product-market pull; I am not treating missing public revenue as evidence of customer rejection.

Market structure makes the missing economic proof consequential. Parcha already offers configurable agents and audit logs; Bretton disclosed a $75 million Series B in February 2026; Hummingbird spans investigation and reporting workflows. My pattern of backing several companies across a technology shift leaves room for Hickory, but does not establish its advantage after competitors emerge. Parcha’s July 2025 launch supports the timing of deployable agents, while Hickory supplies no measured cost or throughput improvement. The service-margin red flag therefore remains an underwriting question: how much institution-specific implementation and review must accompany each contract? There is no evidence here of a thin output formatter, but neither is there evidence of software economics at scale.

I would pass on a personal check today. The founder and workflow evidence deserve attention, but they do not clear my market gates. The exceptions for frontier research or documented reconstruction of incumbent economics do not apply to the supplied evidence. One verifiable result would change my decision: a named sponsor bank completes a paid, 90-day deployment showing at least a 50% reduction in total analyst time per accepted workpaper, including corrections, without a lower acceptance rate. That would test the mechanism that matters rather than reward another agent demonstration.
