# Control Seat -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Battex Chile makes **Control Seat** a high-schlep, low-animal-evidence company: two founders are unifying MQTT brokers, databases, dashboards, APIs, PLCs, and old SCADA, but the dossier contains no cereal-box moment showing what Warren Shepard or Jack Grodnick will do when ordinary methods fail. **Control Seat** has chosen work most hackers instinctively avoid. The remaining question is whether the founders are merely qualified to do it or relentlessly resourceful enough to win.

**Coinbase** is the high rung for schlep blindness because compliance was not incidental to the product; enduring it became the advantage. **Control Seat** has the same load-bearing mechanism in brownfield factories: every plant contains years of configuration data, incompatible controllers, commissioning work, and operators who cannot tolerate downtime. The Battex Chile deployment proves **Control Seat** has entered that machinery rather than merely drawing dashboards for it. But **Control Seat** stops short of the Coinbase rung because its documentation keeps safety functions independent, while CODESYS already markets a certified SIL3 controller. The ugliest and most defensible part of the schlep is precisely where Control Seat currently draws the boundary.

**Kiko** exposed the low-rung failure mechanism for animal resourcefulness: shipping code is not enough if the response to adversity becomes a tidy exit rather than a new attack. **Control Seat** has built proprietary SCADA, historian, analytics, and Virtual PLC software and deployed it at Battex Chile, so **Control Seat** is not a deck-first startup. But there is no named paying amount, manual installation stunt, rescued rejection, or sale won after everyone said no. Even the Loopt exception does not help: Sam Altman supplied overwhelming force of personality when product timing was weak, while the dossier supplies no equivalent anecdote for Shepard or Grodnick. Animals leave tracks. These tracks are technical, not yet commercial.

SpaceX and Tesla put the organic-idea dimension close to the **Stripe** pattern. The Collisons had wrestled with commerce before building the API; Shepard worked across Starlink networking, manufacturing, and flight software, while Grodnick worked directly on SCADA at both Tesla and SpaceX. **Control Seat** therefore looks like software its founders wished had existed inside their own prior workflows, not an industrial market selected from a spreadsheet. **Control Seat** scores below Stripe because the dossier does not identify a particular incident—an integration that consumed three weeks, a failed controller migration, an internal tool they first built for themselves—that crystallized the idea.

**Control Seat’s** self-reported victory over CODESYS on nine of ten IEC execution workloads shows real hacker behavior: build the controller, run the benchmark, publish the result. The counterfactual that would move **Control Seat** into the top hacker-genealogy tier is visible public evidence predating the startup—an important open-source system, competitive-programming result, technical community reputation, or nontrivial independent artifact. Shepard has no public repository, and Grodnick’s visible GitHub trail is a zero-star project. That absence contradicts my unstated hacker-genealogy pattern even though their SpaceX, Tesla, Amazon, and Microsoft work makes technical-by-credential-only an unfair description.

Shepard and Grodnick met during their first year at Dartmouth and later compared their industrial internships, giving **Control Seat** the right two-founder geometry. Over the next 18 months, that relationship can compound: Grodnick can translate plant-level SCADA problems into software while Shepard handles the broader networking, security, and manufacturing surface. **Control Seat** benefits because industrial integrations repeatedly force product and field decisions into the same room. The risk is not cofounder formation—the pair was not assembled weeks before applying—but whether both founders stay in founder mode when bespoke commissioning begins to resemble a systems-integration consultancy.

Battex Chile is enough evidence to predict that by July 2029 **Control Seat** will reach roughly $2 million to $5 million in annual recurring revenue across 15 to 30 production sites, with substantial integration work attached, while remaining an overlay on Ignition, FactoryTalk, WinCC, and similar installed systems rather than replacing them. **Control Seat** can grow because the schlep supplies distribution: each integration project creates the opening to install the proprietary tag layer, historian, and monitoring stack. The low animal evidence keeps the forecast below breakout scale; selling and commissioning 30 plants requires more than writing fast software.

CODESYS already has containerized control and SIL3 safety certification, while Rockwell, Siemens, AVEVA, and Ignition possess installed bases **Control Seat** cannot reproduce. The strongest bear mechanism is services gravity: **Control Seat** could become a clever two-person integrator whose supposedly proprietary platform never escapes custom projects, especially when its benchmark excludes I/O latency, scheduler jitter, motion, and safety certification. The Battex Chile deployment and overlay strategy blunt that objection because **Control Seat** can enter without forcing a rip-and-replace decision, and owning the software lets repeated integrations become product rather than pure labor. I would invest, but I would reverse the verdict if **Control Seat** cannot show at least three paying production sites with repeat usage—and a majority of revenue attributable to recurring software rather than one-off engineering—by July 2027.
