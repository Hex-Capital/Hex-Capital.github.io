# Instaplay -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the founders’ résumés. It is that three people shipped a product used by 20,000 creators and 100,000 monthly users before demo day. Gary Wu brings LLM-evaluation experience from Verita, Frank Liu built consumer software at Instagram and reached the top 1,000 in Teamfight Tactics, and the team has turned those fragments of specific knowledge into an operating game-creation system. The action is real. The deeper question is whether they have discovered a durable game network or merely arrived early at a rapidly commoditizing feature.

Instaplay removes the labor between an idea and a playable browser link. That is useful leverage, but code generation itself is not the asset: Astrocade, Verse8, Pops, and general-purpose agents already produce overlapping experiences. The company records prompts, edits, model outputs, player preferences, and engagement from meaningful usage. This could become proprietary evaluation data, yet FunBench has not shown that the data makes Instaplay’s models uniquely better. If a competitor copied the interface and used the same frontier models tomorrow, the dossier does not establish why creators or players would remain.

The bull case is the emerging permissionless loop. Creators publish games into group chats and Discord; players generate engagement and preference signals; more than 3,000 blinded FunBench votes help evaluate future games. This resembles a network more than a conventional software tool, and 100,000 monthly users is unusually strong evidence at pre-seed. But the loop is not yet self-proving. Median engaged play of 3.0–7.9 seconds, inverted controls, broken interfaces, and incomplete progression suggest that supply is being created faster than lasting utility. Participation compounds only when each contribution makes the next user’s experience better. More content alone can compound noise.

The market is broad enough, but the expansion mechanism is underspecified. Games generated $201.6 billion in 2025, yet a large parent market does not automatically give a browser-game generator room to maneuver. Instaplay calls itself an AI-native social platform, but the dossier does not show how its present creation engine expands into a larger transaction, identity, or distribution layer. Astrocade’s 20 million users and Verse8’s 3.5 million monthly users also show that prompt-to-game supply is abundant. The scarce asset will be retained attention, creator identity, or a discovery graph—not generation.

Economics are the immediate constraint. Each delivered game costs a measured $0.86–$7.11 in model spend, while the product is free to start and has no disclosed subscription, transaction fee, advertising model, or creator revenue share. A social network can defer monetization when user behavior is becoming self-reinforcing, but Instaplay’s seconds-long engagement does not yet justify treating monetization as merely postponed. Free creation paired with costly generation can manufacture activity while concealing negative value capture.

I see no disclosed integrity problem, artificial financing deadline, founder instability, or other personal red flag. The known-exception case is therefore credible: strong organic adoption can override an unfinished business model. It does not override weak retention and a copyable substrate simultaneously. I would pass today, but remain close. One verifiable condition flips the decision: a published six-month cohort showing that retained creators generate games whose players return at least weekly, while paid revenue covers generation cost for that cohort. That would prove the telemetry is becoming a network and a business rather than an expensive stream of disposable demos.
