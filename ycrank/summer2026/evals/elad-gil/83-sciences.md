# 83 Sciences -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Eric Riesel’s materials-ML research and Yankang Yang’s AI deployments to more than 30,000 users make **83 Sciences** a founder-credible science workflow whose strongest dimension is adjacent shipping experience and whose weakest is multi-miracle architecture. **83 Sciences** is not merely asking whether an LLM can interpret experiments; 83 Sciences must obtain proprietary failed-experiment data, integrate heterogeneous instruments, earn scientific trust, resolve derivative-IP ownership, and turn recommendations into reproducible discoveries. That dependency chain is the analytical signature: capable builders attempting a structure where too many independent things must work.

**Perplexity** is the high rung for founder iteration because Aravind Srinivas repeatedly built the discussed product within a week. **83 Sciences** has credible but weaker evidence: Riesel developed ML systems and a generative method for chemical experimental data, while Yang deployed AI programs to 30,000-plus users. Those are real shipping artifacts, not résumé decoration, but **83 Sciences** provides no evidence that Ian Naccarella, Riesel, or Yang has iterated the current product with partner laboratories between conversations. The founders sit between Perplexity’s observable cadence and a merely credentialed founding group, closer to the middle because prior deployment does not establish present velocity.

**Lyft** exposed the low-rung failure mechanism: success required both overturning an incumbent’s network advantage and preserving attractive economics as number two. **83 Sciences** has an analogous probability stack rather than a single miracle. Laboratories must release proprietary unpublished data; recommendations must survive the documented generalizability and robustness problems in materials ML; and papers, patents, and model rights must be allocated without blocking adoption. **83 Sciences** could deliver each component competently and still fail because data access, scientific validation, and IP cooperation are independent miracles. That is the “science project” red flag I normally avoid.

**Citrine’s** July 2026 natural-language Catalyst release weakens the catalyst case for **83 Sciences**: agents can now translate research goals into datasets and suggested experiments, but the same release shows that incumbents received the capability simultaneously. Over the next 18 months, interface costs will fall for every materials platform, while **83 Sciences** still must change laboratory data-sharing behavior. A technological step-function is present; a specific market re-opening uniquely favorable to **83 Sciences** is not. This looks more like a new feature layer in a market growing 19.2% annually than Stripe APIs reopening payments.

**$50,000 grants** to early partners are acquisition spending, not evidence that **83 Sciences** is “just working.” The under-two-month manuscript claim could be informative, but **83 Sciences** names no partner, repeat engagement, revenue, or usage curve, so I apply the absent-evidence default rather than treating funded collaboration as demand. The score flips into Invest territory if three named industrial laboratories pay after their grants expire and partner-derived revenue compounds above 20% month over month for six months. Until then, the distinction is enterprise-pilot tourism versus actual buying behavior.

**Stripe** became an index on e-commerce because every new internet merchant strengthened demand for the same payments rail. **83 Sciences** has the beginnings of an index position—voice notes, instrument outputs, failed experiments, and subsequent outcomes could form the record layer every physical-science laboratory touches—but **83 Sciences** currently distributes through individually negotiated partnerships and joint publications. Citrine already reports 550 deployed models, 380 projects, and 70,000 monthly experiment suggestions. Without a standardized ingestion layer or cross-lab learning rights, 83 Sciences accumulates bespoke projects rather than becoming an index on materials R&D.

**$410.4 million of projected materials-informatics revenue by 2030** is too small to rescue a discovery model with slow, negotiated data access. By July 2029, I expect **83 Sciences** to have pivoted toward AI laboratory knowledge management—an ELN-adjacent search and experiment-record product—with less than $2 million ARR rather than operating a validated autonomous materials-discovery platform. **83 Sciences** has stronger odds of selling organization and retrieval than discovery because the founders’ software experience compounds while scientific validation and invention-rights dependencies are removed.

**Color Genomics** is the strongest objection to that forecast: **83 Sciences** could become workflow infrastructure on top of commodity foundation models, just as Color placed workflow and data above commodity sequencing. Riesel’s chemistry PhD, Naccarella’s Sila experience, Yang’s 30,000-user deployment history, and proprietary failed-experiment records make that bull case sting. **83 Sciences** still lacks the evidence that justified the exception: no signed partner, accessible dataset volume, benchmark, retained customer, patent output, or disclosed IP framework. I would flip to Invest if 83 Sciences produces one independently validated discovery from a named industrial partner, retains commercial learning rights, and converts that partner into paid recurring usage.
