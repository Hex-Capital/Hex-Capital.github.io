# Touchmark -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequential pool is real; the consequential mechanism is not. AI inference represented an estimated $106.15 billion market in 2025, but Touchmark has not shown that forward token trading makes inference 10x cheaper, expands access by 100x, or removes a physical resource bottleneck. It has shown a financial instrument: prepaid blocks for named models and months, an OpenAI-compatible delivery key, and resale before consumption. That may improve procurement. It does not yet rewrite the cost curve.

The product is clever market design, not a demonstrated technical discontinuity. A buyer can list $3,800 for one billion GLM 5.2 tokens or $870 for one billion MiniMax-M3 tokens, but there is no measured saving against on-demand inference, no execution data, and no proprietary delivery technology. OpenRouter already has approximately $50 million in annualized revenue, while Ornn and SF Compute have raised $33 million and $40 million respectively. Any of them can add reservations or resale. Touchmark currently has a feature distinction against named incumbents, not a 5–10x constraint-breaking advantage.

The largest kill risk is fragmented liquidity across model, delivery month, and price. Touchmark’s live books are the correct place to test that risk, but “live” is not evidence of a market: the company discloses no counterparties, executed volume, repeat purchases, spreads, or completed resales. The cheapest decisive experiment is an end-to-end cycle in which independent providers sell, buyers consume, and other buyers purchase the remaining position. Until that occurs at repeatable volume, spending on broader model coverage would add empty shelves rather than remove risk.

The strongest evidence is the team. Ilia Bolgov combines mathematics, finance, and Revolut trading-product exposure; Roman Yanushevskyi combines Citadel Securities quantitative research, Lovable AI engineering, and an IOI gold medal. Those are unusually relevant raw materials for market structure and inference infrastructure. The July-to-August move from quality-based output pricing to forward-capacity contracts could indicate rapid belief revision, but the dossier supplies neither the evidence that caused the change nor a completed result. It could equally be disciplined learning or pre-product wandering. There is also no record yet of recruiting a complementary owner for provider relationships, market operations, or counterparty risk.

The exception case deserves attention. A two-person team can begin with an apparently narrow financial product, enter a giant existing value pool, and compound execution data into infrastructure. Falling inference prices—approximately 600-fold from 2020 to 2026—could create demand for budgeting and hedging, while executed trades could generate a proprietary forward-price history. But the same decline makes long-dated token commitments dangerous, named models become obsolete quickly, and the network effects remain hypothetical. This is an execution-heavy marketplace pattern I will fund without scientific novelty only after seeing evidence that liquidity compounds faster than funded competitors can copy it. No exit fixation, harmful business model, or permanent subsidy dependence is visible; the red flag is simpler: prevailing inference APIs wrapped in an unproven order book.

My 36-month base case is that Touchmark pivots from resellable named-model token forwards into enterprise inference-procurement and routing software because its secondary books never sustain liquidity. I pass today. One result would reverse that decision: $25 million of third-party notional executed within six months, with at least 20% coming from genuine secondary resales and positive contribution margin without provider subsidies. That would prove this is becoming market infrastructure rather than a pricing interface.
