# Inkbox -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important missing fact is not revenue. It is what broke for these founders. Inkbox identifies a real obstacle—agent developers must stitch together Gmail, Twilio, and ngrok—and has shipped a coherent workaround. But the dossier never shows Ray, Dima, or Alex encountering a specific failure, what they did next, or how quickly it changed the outcome. Building the product proves technical ability. It does not yet prove the kind of resourcefulness I use as the first gate.

The technical proof is much stronger. A single API can provision a mailbox, stable HTTP/WebSocket/TCP ingress, phone service, iMessage, scoped context, and a zero-knowledge credential vault. This is functioning infrastructure, not a diagram. Sixteen public repositories, more than 20 external pull requests, and plugins for Codex, Claude Code, OpenClaw, OpenCode, and Hermes suggest that other technical people can inspect and extend it. The founders’ networking, ML, web-automation, and telephony work makes this breadth credible. Inkbox fits my unstated preference for programmable primitives assembled before the category is settled.

Vendo is the first sign of a narrow well. It assigned identities to five agents spanning engineering, operations, GTM, and founder assistance. That is considerably better than a waitlist because the product is being used inside a named company for concrete jobs. But Inkbox published the case study itself, and there is no usage duration, payment, retention, or evidence that those agents pulled the product into an unexpected workflow. Nearly 100 Discord members and 34 GitHub stars show attention, not need. Likewise, listing $30 and $180–$200 monthly plans is pricing, not revenue.

There is genuine schlep here. Inkbox must manage 10DLC registration, carrier restrictions, iMessage initiation rules, abuse controls, phone numbers, credentials, and concentrated communication data. Those burdens may deter prettier but shallower competitors. Yet the dossier only infers that the founders arrived here through Bluebox and prior telephony work; it does not establish that Inkbox grew from a need they personally had. Nor does the move from Bluebox web automation to agent identity demonstrate flexibility, because no customer observation or rejected hypothesis explains the change.

The strongest case against my caution is that this is exactly what an unusually technical pre-seed company should look like: three founders with prior shared execution, a broad working substrate, external contributors, a named deployment, self-serve distribution, and no dependence on a ceremonial launch, large partner, or prematurely hired sales organization. The usual red flags are mostly absent. But the known-exception route does not rescue it. A working product can compensate for some missing history when either the proof is independently measured or users unmistakably pull it back. Inkbox has neither yet. AgentMail, Twilio, and ngrok also make the counterfactual uncomfortable: customers can assemble the components unless persistent identity and shared context produce measurable retention.

I would not write the check today. Inkbox looks like a good product before it has proved it is a company, and its strongest evidence lies in the dimension that is easiest for this team—shipping infrastructure—while its weakest lies in what they do when reality resists. One condition would flip me: the founders personally get ten unrelated fleets through the 10DLC and iMessage constraints, with each fleet still running at least five agents weekly and paying after eight consecutive weeks. That would simultaneously show the obstacle, the workaround, and the pull.
