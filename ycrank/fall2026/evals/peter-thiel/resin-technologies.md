# Resin Technologies -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

What does Resin Technologies believe about electronics design that Flux does not? A possible secret is that controlling the entire requirements-to-board workflow makes reliable automation possible where separate design assistants fail. That would justify forming a company. But this is a hypothesis I can construct from the product, not a disputed proposition established by the dossier. Resin publishes no experiment showing that integration produces better boards. The secret remains unstated, rather than refuted; it receives the absent-evidence default.

The competitive problem is more concrete. Resin combines editable schematic generation, component libraries, and proprietary routing, but Flux already offers an integrated ECAD workspace with AI assistance. Quilter and CELUS attack substantial portions of the same workflow. Combining their feature categories does not establish a monopoly. Resin identifies neither a bounded customer segment it can dominate nor a measured technical advantage within one. Editable exports help adoption, but they also weaken the hypothesized switching costs. “Proprietary” describes ownership of the routing code; it does not establish superiority.

The founders provide the strongest reason to resist an immediate dismissal. Anmol Jawandha brings enterprise AI deployment experience; Koosha Rezaiezadeh has actually worked in electronics and robotics, including at Bosch and Kodak. More importantly for founding structure, they met studying Engineering Physics together at UBC, rather than assembling around a fundraising opportunity. That earns some credit for a relationship predating the company. It does not establish that they previously shipped together, that both are now full-time, or that their ownership aligns them for a decade. You.com’s continuing public attribution of Anmol is insufficient evidence of current outside employment; I would not manufacture a part-time-founder red flag from an undated biography.

The strongest bull case is that this team has built substantially more than a conversational interface: its own editor, editable workflows, and a routing engine. This fits my revealed interest in software that advances physical production. If Resin can eliminate fabrication iterations, engineering savings could dwarf its subscription price. But the relevant exceptions to competitive-market aversion require independent technical proof or live customer behavior that resolves the underlying concern. Neither appears here. The terms acknowledge incorrect wiring and component selection; that disclosure is not evidence of technical failure, but manual review is not evidence that the automation works either. A public launch roughly twenty days earlier and 176 LinkedIn followers establish visibility, not repeated engineering reliance.

There is at least one coherent revenue engine. At $199 monthly, approximately 42,000 continuously paying Pro users would produce $100 million ARR; at $149 monthly, approximately 56,000 Team seats would do the same. That is intelligible arithmetic, but customer expansion and margins after AI usage remain unestablished. The seven-day trial, promotional credits, and founder onboarding give Resin an actual acquisition route to electrical engineers and hardware leads. They have not yet demonstrated conversion or a delivery burden compatible with those prices. A $6.1 billion category can support a substantial company while leaving several competing vendors with ordinary economics.

I pass on investing my own money today. My central concern is value capture: capable founders may create useful engineering software without owning the resulting market. One result would reopen the decision—a customer-run comparative study in a precisely defined board category showing Resin delivers fabrication-ready designs ten times faster than Flux and the incumbent workflow, without reducing first-pass fabrication success. That would turn the integrated editor from a feature bundle into evidence of a secret and a potential monopoly mechanism.
