# Super Powers Mobility (SPM) -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

SPM reports $421,815 of realized revenue in one month after its September 2026 pivot to batteries and power export. That deserves attention. It does not establish what my equity would buy. The disclosed $1.3 million pre-seed establishes that Boost VC and others financed the company; it supplies neither a post-money valuation nor evidence of competing offers. I cannot call this an auction, and I cannot call it a cheap, scarce check. The dossier also leaves the next capital-funded experiment unspecified. Those two gates remain at their absent-evidence defaults.

Jonathan Powers gives me a concrete reason to keep reading. Cybertruck integration, electric-truck braking work at Workhorse, and Honda ATV/UTV engineering connect directly to SPM’s battery, electronics, and vehicle-integration work. Bench testing and demonstrations to special operations forces in Nevada and Utah extend that experience into the proposed customer environment. This fits my unstated preference for founders translating between difficult physical systems. But demonstrations establish access and engineering activity; they do not establish a procurement position. The claimed power-density advantage remains independently unverified, and Inventus already supplies custom military battery systems. Powers earns meaningful founder credit without my pretending SPM owns a protected military franchise.

The strongest argument against passing is that customers may already have identified the useful business. Selling battery packs and power-export equipment could let SPM monetize its engineering before financing complete vehicles. The reported September revenue is unusually informative at this stage. I keep it separate from the $792,000 qualified pipeline and the $769,000 of prior vehicle preorders, whose conversion is uncertain. But the dossier does not identify which customers bought, whether they reordered, or whether proprietary access caused those purchases. Direct inquiries, evaluation units, and proposed prime subcontracting are available routes to market. They have not yet demonstrated an owned distribution advantage.

Delete the next financing from the spreadsheet. Does SPM survive? Actual sales give that question more substance than a prototype alone, which earns some economic credit. Nevertheless, there is no disclosed gross margin, burn, inventory requirement, or collection schedule supporting a break-even calculation. The $1,799 consumer battery also has an early-2027 shipping target, leaving production execution ahead. My concern is the cash absorbed between building a pack and getting paid. My exceptions for unusually strong conversion, proven economics, or an alternative financing structure do not resolve that concern here: one reported revenue month supplies none of those complete overrides. Equally, the dossier shows no artificial fundraising deadline or explicit dependence on serial raises; I will not invent those red flags.

I pass on the investment presented. SPM could become a valuable specialist supplier, and its engineering background makes that prospect credible. What I cannot underwrite is the relationship between entry price, the truth this check reveals, and cash generation afterward. My one verifiable condition for reopening the decision is a signed repeat-production contract whose payment schedule and fully loaded delivery costs demonstrate that SPM can reach cash break-even using existing capital. That would turn the promising sales month into evidence capable of overriding a missing venture-moment plan. I would still need the proposed price before committing my money.
