# BioDelivera -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Five years of research tells me the founders can stay with a difficult problem. It does not tell me what they do when their solution stops working. Ikeda and Orikeda Trashi researched the delivery technology they are now commercializing, which gives BioDelivera a much more credible origin than someone deciding to start a fashionable biotech company. But the dossier contains no specific failed experiment, founder-built workaround, and elapsed time. That is the missing observation I care about most. I cannot infer resourcefulness from two PhDs.

There is something here worth examining. The founders describe virus-like particles targeting markers including HER2 and PD-L1, with bacterial self-assembly avoiding subsequent modification ([founder interview](https://albphdcircle.com/startup/)). If that process works reliably, manufacturing could be part of the invention rather than an unpleasant task postponed until later. Their doctoral work supports genuine contact with the problem. The technical evidence deserves less certainty: efficacy in tumors, muscle, and brain is company-reported, without quantitative results in the dossier ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/biodelivera)). I give provisional credit for reported experimental work, but cannot treat three tissue names as three demonstrated breakthroughs.

The strongest argument against passing is that exceptional scientific founders can produce decisive evidence before they have customers. BioDelivera fits my pattern of backing researchers commercializing problems they encountered directly. **Bindwell** supplies a relevant exception: laboratory validation can outweigh an uncertain conventional founder profile. The material difference is that its case included numerical technical results and laboratory validation; BioDelivera's dossier supplies broad efficacy claims. An independently validated biological result could replace some missing founder-behavior evidence here. A competition win cannot. First place at UT Dallas in 2025 establishes recognition, even with the conflicting $12,000 and $15,000 prize figures, rather than selective delivery performance or therapeutic-developer demand ([UT Dallas](https://news.utdallas.edu/campus-community/big-idea-competition-2025/)).

Who needs the crude version now? The dossier does not identify a developer repeatedly using these particles or requesting another experiment. That earns the absent-evidence demand score, without treating pre-seed silence as rejection. Licensing and research partnerships are inferred business models, so I would not accuse the founders of relying on a large partner to create growth. Nor does their shared doctoral background suggest a hastily assembled cofounder relationship. The discrepancy over a historical third founder needs clarification, but establishes no misconduct. Aera's competing protein nanoparticles make the practical question sharper: which payload and tissue combination makes a researcher choose BioDelivera despite the inconvenience of adopting it ([Aera](https://aeratx.com/science/))?

I would pass today. There is credible problem ownership, provisional technical evidence, and no documented evidence-driven route change; the latter receives the default rather than a penalty for rigidity. The founders have not demonstrated the action under resistance required by my investment gate, and the available proof does not justify an exception. My condition for reopening this pass is one documented experimental recovery: a dated targeting failure, the founders' specific redesign shipped within weeks, and independent replication showing improved selective delivery against a matched control. That would let me evaluate both what they built and what happened when building it became difficult.
