# Elite -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Elite has shipped software, but the founders have not yet demonstrated specific knowledge of healthcare operations. Raj brings GTM experience from Deel; Vihaan was founding engineer #3 at an acquired legal-technology company and generated 1,000-plus signups for a university campaign. Those are real execution signals. Yet the dossier documents no work inside a medical practice, no healthcare customer sold, and no patient-access workflow endured firsthand. The July 29 government application-review launch followed by the August 22 healthcare HIPAA notice shows speed, not accumulated knowledge of this exact problem.

The strongest evidence is technical breadth: protocols for 18 specialties, write-back into more than 19 EHRs, audit logs, encryption, BAAs, and a public voice demo. Integration work creates friction for a copier, but friction is not a moat. Assort already claims 23-plus specialties and proprietary interaction data, while Hyro and Syllable offer overlapping workflows with $95 million and $81.2 million raised. If a competitor copies Elite’s agent code, Elite retains configurations and integrations; the dossier does not show an exclusive dataset, proprietary model, scarce rights, or improving deployment loop.

The market is large, but the causal path is still loose. A $13.7 billion conversational-healthcare category projected to reach $106.7 billion is a parent market, not proof of room to maneuver. Elite’s practice wedge already bundles scheduling, referrals, refills, eligibility, intake, billing, and triage, yet it identifies neither a focused initial buyer nor an adjacent transaction layer unlocked by one accumulating capability. Outcome-based pricing is directionally sound because practices pay per resolved interaction, but a quoted pricing mechanism without a paying practice, completed interaction volume, or unit economics proves intent rather than value capture.

The unstated-pattern test is weaker. This is centrally operated workflow automation sold through demo-led direct sales. One practice does not make the product more useful or easier to acquire for the next practice on present evidence. Resolved calls could generate specialty-specific data, but “could” is not an asset: Elite has disclosed neither volume nor the right to reuse patient interactions across customers. There is no permissionless contributor network, fragmented supply marketplace, or user-created distribution loop accumulating while the company sleeps.

The bull case is speed. Two brothers with complementary sales and engineering experience have assembled unusually broad EHR and specialty coverage quickly, and per-resolution pricing could align revenue with labor saved. That can override missing pre-seed revenue when the prototype itself is strange or technically singular. Here, however, the prototype enters a legible category occupied by heavily financed direct competitors. The rapid government-to-healthcare transition is too little evidence to call founder instability, but it is also too recent to count as persistence. The dossier shows no integrity, unfair-dealing, or financing-pressure red flag; it simply lacks the exceptional mechanism needed to override absent domain knowledge, customers, and durability.

I pass. My 36-month base case is that Elite narrows into EHR-integrated referral and eligibility automation and remains below $3 million ARR rather than winning the general healthcare front office. One condition flips the decision: evidence from at least ten paying practices that retained deployment data measurably improves resolution rates across customers and is contractually reusable to build a proprietary model. That would turn integration labor into compounding leverage.
