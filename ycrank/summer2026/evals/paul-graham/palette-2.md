# Palette -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The first question is what broke and what the founders did next. Palette has sensible machinery for model failures: capability gating, fallback routing, substitutions, and credit refunds. But the dossier does not show Josephine Lee or Serena Pei encountering a dated obstacle, improvising a workaround, and changing user behavior. Product resilience is not yet evidence of relentlessly resourceful founders. On the most important criterion, I have to use the missing-evidence default.

The technical evidence is much better. Two recent MIT graduates have already shipped a public studio and API spanning images, video, music, storyboards, and editing. The routing layer has 130-plus regression tests and reportedly generates media 35% faster and 50% more cheaply while finding a task-capable model on every internal benchmark. Those measurements are self-reported, so I would not treat them like an independent benchmark. But this is functioning software built before permission, not a diagram of software they hope to build.

What is missing is the narrow well. Palette names creators, marketing teams, operations leaders, and learning-and-development leaders—four populations rather than one urgent user. The $0.01-per-credit price proves that charging is possible, not that anyone pays. The $500,000 financing, YC Launch post, and OpenAI backing are investor and launch signals, not demand. There is no named customer, repeated workflow, retention cohort, revenue figure, or user who tolerated the crude product and pulled it somewhere unexpected.

The origin is similarly plausible but unproved. The founders met through an art program, and Pei researched diffusion models and multimodal systems at MIT. That explains why they could build Palette. It does not show that either founder repeatedly suffered this workflow problem, nor that they discovered an ugly operational burden others refused to handle. Enterprise security, copyright permissions, likeness rights, and model substitutions could become real schleps. At present, the legal documents still identify themselves as starter templates and SOC 2 Type II remains a roadmap item. Those are unfinished obligations, not a moat.

The strongest bull case is that this resembles the mechanism that made Dropbox interesting: a working technical product can matter more than the crowded-market objection. Palette could become a programmable substrate whose routing data and brand context improve with use. The material difference is that Dropbox’s prototype came with unusually rapid founder correction around a problem the founder personally experienced. Here, Runway and Luma already aggregate models, while Palette has disclosed neither user pull nor an evidence-driven route change. Its internal proof spike satisfies one of my unstated patterns, but the missing external response prevents the known exception from applying.

I am passing now. My base case is that by August 2029 Palette has abandoned the broad “AI-native media platform” positioning and narrowed into a workflow product for one enterprise creative niche, remaining below $2 million ARR. The single flip condition is an eight-week cohort report showing at least ten named creative teams, six still active in week eight, and the dated product change that produced the retention improvement. That would demonstrate both user pull and the founders’ behavior under resistance.
