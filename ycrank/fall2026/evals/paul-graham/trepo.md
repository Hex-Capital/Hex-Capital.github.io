# Trepo -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Trepo’s founders have shipped something. What I cannot find is what happened when they got stuck. Matt Taylor’s Tesla and Nuro experience makes the hardware credible, but it does not supply the missing episode: a particular obstacle, a workaround he built, and how quickly it worked. That distinction matters because I am buying the founders’ ability to solve the next problem. Their college and water-polo history is encouraging evidence that the relationship predates the company. Neither fact establishes resourcefulness under resistance.

The product earns more credit. There is a [released iOS app](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/trepo/id6764135986), and receipt scanning, shared lists, and kitchen interactions give Trepo a concrete way to test its idea. This is beyond a diagram. But the difficult technical claim is keeping an inventory correct as people eat things, throw things away, and forget to tell the machine. The dossier provides no measured accuracy or maintenance burden. Taylor’s engineering background cannot fill that gap. Trepo resembles my pattern of backing builders who produce evidence before asking permission, but the evidence currently establishes functioning software more clearly than reliable household inventory.

The strongest argument for investing is that people appear willing to do some work for it. [Six beta hardware batches reportedly sold out](https://hellotrepo.com/), the app has 56 ratings averaging 4.9, and the founders report more than 150,000 item check-ins. Those are substantial reasons to investigate. A household repeatedly recording food might be revealing a surprisingly deep need. But the cumulative count does not distinguish many initial pantry scans from a small group returning every week. Batch sizes and preorder terms are undisclosed. The reported 35-fold growth over five weeks lacks a defined metric and baseline, so I cannot turn it into a growth rate for a business. I give demand intermediate credit, rather than treating these omissions as evidence that users dislike Trepo.

The central tradeoff is whether the kitchen device removes work or creates another chore. If it makes recording consumption nearly effortless, hardware could solve the problem that defeats an app. If users must remember another bookkeeping task, replenishment will eventually act on fiction. Samsung’s overlapping features do not settle this question; Trepo can serve existing refrigerators. Nor do unnamed grocery pilots establish a distribution advantage. Dependence on a large partner would trigger a red flag, but the App Store and founder TikTok activity show other acquisition routes. I also see neither the programmable substrate nor the independently measured proof spike that characterizes some of my strongest technical bets. The dossier does not establish a founder’s own collision with grocery management, or an evidence-driven product revision, so those dimensions receive their missing-evidence defaults.

I would pass at 43. The working-product exception is the strongest challenge to that decision: Trepo has shipped, attracted favorable reviews, and apparently found buyers for successive hardware batches. Those facts could conceal an excellent company. They do not yet supply unmistakable continuing pull strong enough to override the missing founder evidence. This is a decision about the available proof, not an accusation that the founders are passive. My single flip condition is a documented deployment in ten households where a named inventory failure led the founders to ship a fix within two weeks, followed by eight weeks of repeated use and paid renewal. That would connect the founder behavior I need to the household behavior Trepo needs.
