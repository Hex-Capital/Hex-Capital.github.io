# The Letter Company -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The Letter Company has shipped enough to make me interested in its founder, but the dossier stops before the most useful part of the story. Mathew Pregasen moved from a writing agency into software for content and growth. What broke at the agency? What did he build to fix it? How quickly did that change a customer’s situation? His prior work supplies relevant context. It does not yet supply the obstacle-and-workaround sequence on which I would base a personal check.

Lettertrace is the strongest evidence here. A public, self-hostable monitoring tool with customer-supplied API keys is an inspectable artifact, and a composable CLI fits my preference for tools technical users can assemble themselves. This earns substantial technical credit. But the measurement has an unresolved problem: the README describes approximating AI Overviews through Gemini with forced search. Running the software and measuring the thing customers care about are separate accomplishments. The dossier establishes the former more convincingly. It also provides no comparable proof for the advertised coding agent and IDE.

The strongest argument for investing is that the free tool may already be finding customers for the paid tools. Lettertrace reportedly has over 500 users; Phantomstory reportedly signed eight contracts in one morning. Combined with Pregasen’s agency experience, that suggests a plausible path from understanding a customer’s work to automating it. This could be founder-led selling expressed through software. But both figures come through founder-post snippets. We cannot tell whether the 500 users return, whether the eight contracts produced collections, or whether either group wants the coding agent. Phantomstory’s advertised $500 monthly price is a price, not established recurring revenue.

That distinction matters because the proposed mechanism is more interesting than the launch results. Free monitoring could repeatedly expose a problem that customers then pay Letter to solve. Product Hunt’s #3 daily placement on August 12 and the repository’s 75 stars and 13 forks show attention; they do not establish that loop. Nor do they prove the founders depend on launch-day attention, so I would not apply that red flag. The more consequential test of my programmable-tools pattern is whether users build recurring workflows with these utilities. Profound and AirOps already combine measurement with execution. Developer control becomes an advantage when particular users need it badly enough to keep using Letter.

I would pass today. The working-product exception deserves consideration, but an existing monitoring utility without unmistakable repeat demand or independently validated measurement does not override the missing founder-behavior evidence. There is also no documented customer observation explaining the move from agency to utilities to growth-engineering IDE; I cannot score that sequence as evidence-driven adaptation. Likewise, agency experience alone does not establish a personally encountered problem with a neglected operational barrier. These are missing facts, not evidence of passive founders. My single flip condition is a verifiable customer case showing a named obstacle, a founder-shipped workaround within a week, and that customer paying for and repeatedly using the resulting workflow for six weeks. That would connect the software already built to the founder behavior and urgent need I am buying.
