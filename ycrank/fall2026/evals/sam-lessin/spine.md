# Spine -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Spine has raised $3 million, but the dossier does not tell me the post-money valuation, competing-offer count, or what specific uncertainty that money resolves. Those are my first underwriting questions. Five named investors do not establish an auction, and YC membership does not establish an expensive deal. Both financing gates remain undocumented. I can like the brokerage business and still pass on writing a personal check.

The business mechanism is sensible: insurance commissions subsidize the administrative work founders already hate doing. Spine operates Rippling, Deel, Gusto, and ADP through a human service layer rather than asking customers to migrate. That fits my preference for businesses connecting systems whose participants struggle to coordinate. It also gives the AI a useful subordinate role. Plan analysis can improve delivery without requiring a new model breakthrough for the underlying brokerage to have value. The competitive question is whether commission-funded support leaves enough surplus when existing brokers and HR platforms can offer overlapping services.

The strongest argument against passing is that Spine already has a functioning commercial engine. Thirty-plus startup customers and approximately 4.4-fold commission-revenue growth over six months through July 2026 are meaningful at this stage. Someone is choosing the service, and carriers are paying. Embedded administration could make Spine the trusted operator customers call before changing benefits providers. But the 50-plus practitioner network does not yet establish a proprietary distribution advantage: the dossier supplies neither referral-attributed customer wins nor exclusivity. I give credit for customer adoption of the workflow, without treating the referral network as demonstrated purchase causation.

The economics could make that distinction less important. Acadian reports approximately 78% contribution margin, explicitly company-modeled. If realized after the actual specialist workload, that could support a durable business without endless financing. The sting in the bull case is that an ordinary-looking brokerage can compound perfectly well while software investors dismiss it as services. My reservation is specific: commission growth supplies no absolute revenue base, and modeled contribution margin does not establish cash remaining after acquisition, central overhead, or renewal support. The conflicting 15% and 25% savings claims also need a consistent measurement basis. Spine has evidence of commerce; it has not established that the $3 million gets it to self-funding.

Onur and Ece have built companies together before, and the consumer health app is a shipped product with 582 ratings, including reviews preceding its rebranding. That deserves more credit than degrees alone. It does not establish that either founder has operated an insurance brokerage at scale, and those consumer ratings cannot validate employer-service retention. Their engineering, legal, and operating backgrounds make the translation plausible. Direct receipts from both sides remain incomplete. The named CEO supplies accountability; I would not invent an equal-founder governance problem.

I pass today. The exceptions I would consider—exceptional conversion and retention, demonstrated cash economics, or unusually durable proprietary access—are not established strongly enough to override the missing deal and capital-purpose evidence. Nor does the growth hiring brief establish that the round merely funds another financing cycle. My verifiable flip condition is an executable financing package below $10 million post-money that budgets one dated test: reach company cash break-even within 12 months from collected brokerage commissions, including acquisition and fully loaded service costs, without another raise. That would connect price, capital, and a consequential result. Spine currently offers an appealing operating mechanism without that underwriting connection.
