# 83 Sciences -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Eric Riesel’s MIT doctorate in inorganic chemistry is 83 Sciences’s strongest asset, while Ian Naccarella’s roughly 3,000 LinkedIn followers are the closest thing 83 Sciences has to owned distribution. That pairing makes 83 Sciences a domain-missionary scientific operation with weak founder-brand leverage—not an audience-led software company. All three founders personally encountered the experimental-data problem, but customer acquisition still begins with direct laboratory solicitation and grants of up to $50,000. The asset may be illegible and valuable; the path to acquiring it is expensive and unproven.

**Drop.io** is the high rung on asymmetric conviction: I built against free incumbents because I had lived the file-sharing problem and was willing to take several painful tacks. 83 Sciences shares the lived-problem mechanism—Eric Riesel developed materials-data ML at MIT, Ian Naccarella worked at Sila Nanotechnologies, and all three founders report searching old experimental records themselves. Where 83 Sciences diverges from Drop.io is visible pre-pitch obsession: 83 Sciences offers no category essays, sustained public research program, or evidence that Ian, Eric, or Yankang Yang pursued this exact problem for three-plus years. The missionary signal is credible but not yet overwhelming, placing 83 Sciences between Pinterest-style founder conviction and the Drop.io high rung.

**Fin** is the low-rung warning on founder-owned distribution: strong operators and a clever Wizard-of-Oz process do not automatically produce a durable customer channel, which is one reason Fin ultimately pivoted into workplace analytics. That mechanism appears in 83 Sciences more than I would like. Ian Naccarella has approximately 3,000 LinkedIn followers, Eric Riesel has 403, and Yankang Yang has about 1,000, but 83 Sciences has no newsletter, scientific community, recurring user network, or organically acquired laboratory base. Those profiles can open meetings; they are not John Fish’s Bookshelved audience sitting on the cap table. 83 Sciences is therefore close to Fin’s low rung, with only modest founder-reach evidence pulling it upward.

The $170.4 million materials-informatics market makes 83 Sciences non-consensus in category but not demonstrably mispriced as a round. Over the next 18 months, this dimension compounds only if 83 Sciences turns scientific illegibility into an underwriting edge: exclusive failed-experiment datasets, a clearly cheap entry price, and laboratory partnerships that other investors cannot value. Today there is no disclosed valuation, round size, tier-one pass, signed partner, or dataset volume, so the value-seed price test stops almost immediately. 83 Sciences is harder and less fashionable than the canonical vertical-AI agent shell, but “AI-native materials discovery” can still attract hot-YC pricing. No round evidence means I stay near the absent-evidence default rather than awarding non-consensus points from industrial branding alone.

Grants of up to $50,000 make the capital structure of 83 Sciences look backwards until proven otherwise: 83 Sciences appears to pay laboratories for access before demonstrating revenue from them. The score flips into Invest territory if those grants purchase exclusive, durable rights to failed-experiment data and 83 Sciences retains a defined share of patents, licensing revenue, or commercialization proceeds. That would turn 83 Sciences into a research holdco or royalty-shaped asset rather than another SaaS SAFE, and I would happily underwrite that strange instrument. The public record instead leaves dataset, model, and invention ownership unspecified while 83 Sciences advertises a $120,000–$250,000 engineering hire. That is a potentially capital-hungry regatta with no disclosed downstream financing tack.

**Sublime Security** is the useful single-anchor parallel for category fit: expert workflows can be compelling before the next consolidation wave when incumbents have large suites but users still distrust the output. 83 Sciences has a similar opening against Citrine’s 550 deployed models, 380 projects, and 70,000 monthly experiment suggestions because unpublished failures may be more valuable than polished datasets. Yet 83 Sciences sits outside my strongest revealed-preference sleeves—crypto, DTC brands, prescription rails, creator infrastructure, and employee-equity structures—and its AI agent risks becoming commodity interface software. The partial fit is the Wizard-of-Oz possibility: if 83 Sciences manually turns discarded records into co-authored manuscripts before automating, the category starts to resemble Fin’s service-first operating method rather than generic AI infrastructure.

Citrine’s existing scale points to my 36-month forecast: 83 Sciences will pivot from a broad materials-discovery platform into a services-led materials R&D and data-cleaning business, reaching roughly $1 million–$3 million in annualized revenue through individually negotiated laboratory partnerships rather than becoming the default software layer. 83 Sciences can plausibly deliver manuscripts in under two months because its founders have unusually strong chemistry and deployment credentials, but every new laboratory brings bespoke instruments, ontology work, trust requirements, and IP negotiations. With no structural data-access barrier yet, 83 Sciences’s high founder conviction will keep it alive while its low owned distribution and ambiguous rights structure keep it service-heavy.

Eric Riesel’s generative work on chemical experimental data supplies the strongest bull objection: 83 Sciences could use $50,000 grants to capture the missing 90% of laboratory data, compound a proprietary failure corpus, and become the legibility layer that Citrine cannot reproduce. The **Ro** exception stings here—capital intensity can itself become the moat when it clears regulatory or operational schleps others will not finance. I still land Neutral because 83 Sciences has disclosed no signed partner, exclusive data right, benchmark, independent discovery, or IP-allocation term; spending ahead of those facts is not yet a venture moment. I would flip to Invest if 83 Sciences verifies one exclusive multi-year laboratory partnership that assigns reusable model-training rights to 83 Sciences and produces either a paid renewal or a jointly owned patent.
