# Super Powers Mobility (SPM) -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Jonathan Powers has acquired relevant knowledge through action. Cybertruck integration at Tesla, electric-truck brakes at Workhorse, and ATV/UTV engineering at Honda put him inside the interfaces where vehicle systems fail. SPM’s subsequent vehicle-mounted power demonstrations make that experience relevant to a product it has built. This earns substantial founder credit. It does not independently establish superior battery chemistry, manufacturing economics, or integrity; the dossier supplies no adverse evidence on integrity.

The September 2026 pivot is the strongest commercial signal. Super Powers Mobility reports **$421,815 in realized revenue in one month** after concentrating on battery packs and power export ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/super-powers-mobility-spm)). Someone paid for the current direction. I keep the $792,000 qualified pipeline and $769,000 of earlier vehicle preorders outside that revenue figure. The $1,799 consumer battery also establishes an asking price, although early-2027 delivery remains ahead. Direct value capture is credible; repeat demand and gross profit remain unproven.

What survives someone copying the design? SPM has physical integration work: batteries, power electronics, controls, and vehicle testing. Its advertised 15 kWh modules and 12 kW continuous AC output address a concrete operational need—powering equipment without running an engine ([SPM](https://spm.us/pek)). That is a more substantial starting asset than a reproducible interface. But hardware earns its advantage through performance, production, or qualification. The claimed power-density superiority remains independently unverified, and Inventus already supplies overlapping custom systems. I give credit for the integrated hardware already demonstrated, without pricing in exclusivity or future replacement costs.

The expansion path is plausible but incompletely established. Military power export can lead into industrial equipment and commercial mobility through shared battery and power-electronics capabilities; SPM’s industrial offering and consumer battery make those actual product directions. The cited **$1.6 billion military-battery market**, however, does not establish how much SPM can serve or how broadly its modules transfer. The distinction matters: a reusable product architecture can expand across buyers, while bespoke engineering can expand revenue and workload together.

The strongest objection to my restraint is that customers may already be revealing the answer faster than public technical evidence can. A founder with relevant operating experience, demonstrated hardware, and substantial reported sales immediately after a pivot deserves attention. My exception for an unusually capable founder with a working product applies here, even before a customer network exists. It keeps SPM in consideration. It does not yet overcome the uncertainty about what compounds. Against my unstated patterns, this fits the shift toward physical bottlenecks, but shows neither permissionless contribution nor users creating value for other users. Against my red flags, one commercially supported pivot is no evidence of habitual abandonment, and there is no documented unfair dealing or financing pressure.

I am **Neutral** on writing a personal check today. Super Powers Mobility could become a valuable supplier, but the dossier leaves open whether each deployment strengthens a reusable platform or mainly funds another integration project. My single verifiable flip condition is a documented repeat production order for the same standardized module, showing positive gross margin after manufacturing, warranty, and support costs. That would connect the founder’s specific knowledge to an asset the company can repeatedly sell—and materially strengthen the investment case.
