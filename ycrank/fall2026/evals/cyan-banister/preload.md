# Preload -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I want a robot to learn how tightly to hold a slippery object by watching someone work—and receiving the information the camera cannot see. Preload is collecting precisely those missing channels. The path requires capturing human contact, translating it across different sensor layouts, and making it useful to a robot with different hands. September’s Touch2Robot results and October’s TACROSS work provide early evidence that parts of that bridge can work. Those are outside research results, however. They make the future more credible without establishing that Preload can deliver it.

The founders give me a reason to investigate. Runming Wang’s EMG, motion-tracking, and neural-hardware research maps directly to the collection problem; Surya Pandiaraju brings experience across several neural-interface companies; Leo Li combines robotics training with neuroengineering at Neuralink. This is substantive technical preparation for an awkward problem involving bodies, signals, and hardware. But I cannot yet reconstruct the first-believer story: what they built before starting Preload, what failed, or whom they persuaded to use it. Relevant experience earns conviction; an independently pursued obsession would earn more.

Why collect demonstrations on an expensive robot when a person already knows how to perform the task? That question exposes a real engineering cost. It does not yet expose an institutional rent or a rule withholding someone’s independence. Preload could make training cheaper for robotics developers, but the dossier provides neither measured collection savings nor a clear transfer of economic control to the people supplying demonstrations. I like the potential enabling layer more than the present agency story. Its integrated video, tactile, and EMG stack addresses information that downstream manipulation systems need, although exclusive data rights and a scarce asset under the company’s control remain unverified.

The strongest argument for writing an early check is that waiting for revenue could miss the formation of that asset. Preload fits my pattern of funding the concealed infrastructure beneath a visible wave. **CosmicBrain AI** is a relevant exception to demanding paying-user evidence: both bets connect specialized founders to physical-AI infrastructure. The material difference is that CosmicBrain supplied a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept. Here, the October 1 launch attracted a reported 246,186 views, but attention does not establish that anyone repeatedly wore the equipment, contributed usable demonstrations, or trained a better policy. Missing revenue is ordinary at this stage; missing behavioral proof leaves that particular dimension at its default.

The objection that stings is that all three sensing channels may become available everywhere while useful robot transfer remains expensive, customer-specific work. Axonnic already offers EMG capture; Human Archive already collects multimodal wearable data. Preload could become a capable collection contractor without owning the dependency that makes the larger future possible. I see no documented founder-control abuse, severe privacy violation, or dependence on one closed model supplier. Nevertheless, collecting bodily signals makes consent and dataset custody relevant gates, and the dossier does not establish their architecture. Neither technical relevance nor my attraction to hidden infrastructure resolves those questions.

I am **Neutral**, with a specific reason to stay close. My verifiable flip condition is two independent robotics teams renewing paid dataset licenses after controlled evaluations show that Preload’s EMG adds manipulation performance beyond video and tactile data alone. That would connect the distinctive collection stack to both customer behavior and a defensible economic reason to exist. Until then, I can see the future this team might supply more clearly than I can see why that future must buy from Preload.
