# Greypoint Industries -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence is real but not yet quantified. Counter-UAS spending is projected to rise from $14.41 billion in 2026 to $55.25 billion in 2034, and destroying an operator or command node could be more consequential than intercepting one replaceable drone. But Greypoint Industries has not shown how LEGION and ARROW change the cost of defending a soldier, installation, or square kilometer by 10x—much less 100x. A large defense budget is not itself a large consequence.

The technical case remains an architecture, not a discontinuity. Passive emitter geolocation connected directly to autonomous interception is a sensible system design, and one successful aerial-platform geolocation test proves more than a rendering. It does not establish range, accuracy, latency, contested-spectrum performance, or swarm capacity. Anduril, Fortem, and Epirus already field competing effectors; Greypoint provides no measured 5–10x advantage against any of them. RF-silent waypoint drones are especially damaging to the thesis because they attack LEGION’s sensing premise, while “sensor fusion” is currently a mitigation label rather than test evidence.

The risk-removal sequence is also incomplete. A Canadian Armed Forces demonstration and signed government contract are unusually informative pre-seed signals, but the dossier does not say what the demonstration proved, what the contract purchases, or what failure threshold would stop the present approach. The next dollars should buy one hostile trial against mixed RF-emitting, jammed, and RF-silent targets—not manufacturing capacity, briefings, or procurement staff. There is likewise no bottom-up cost model for antennas, aircraft, interceptors, operator requirements, production yield, or cost per defeated drone. Defense urgency can conceal bad economics for several years; it cannot repeal them.

The strongest case for investing is the team. An infanteer is paired with systems, controls, embedded-software, and uncrewed-aircraft experience from General Dynamics, TerraSense, Bosch, UBC AeroDesign, and Sailbot. This is not four generic AI founders chasing a procurement announcement. Canada’s July 2026 Defence Drone Initiative, the contract, and the CAF demonstration create a domestic path through testing and procurement that many defense startups never obtain. The pattern of accepting certification, manufacturing, and government timelines fits my actual investing behavior better than a software-only reading of my criteria would suggest.

But the same evidence can support a less attractive interpretation: four capable students have assembled existing sensing, autonomy, and interceptor components into a defense integrator precisely when public procurement is expanding. No proprietary dataset, production learning, patent, structural barrier, or technical benchmark separates that outcome from a platform company. The usual exception for a tiny team entering a giant value pool does not apply yet because the dossier shows neither a demonstrated discontinuity nor evidence that the initial system can expand into indispensable defense infrastructure.

My 36-month base case is that Greypoint sells LEGION as a sensing or C2 subsystem with $1–5 million in annualized government revenue while ARROW remains demonstration-stage. I would not write the angel check today. One result flips the decision: an independently observed CAF trial in which LEGION and ARROW defeat at least 80% of a 20-drone mixed swarm, including RF-silent targets, at no more than 20% of the fully loaded incumbent cost. That would convert a well-timed integration story into a measurable technical option.
