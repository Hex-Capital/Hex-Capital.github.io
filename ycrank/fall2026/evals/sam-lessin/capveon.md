# Capveon -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Capveon is asking me to underwrite access to infrastructure customers’ internal workflows. That could be valuable. But the proposed post-money, competing offers, and use of proceeds are undisclosed. YC backing establishes an investor; it establishes neither scarce capital nor an auction. I use my missing-evidence defaults for both financing dimensions. There is also no named, dated experiment explaining what my check would prove. These are underwriting gaps, not evidence that two pre-seed founders have done something wrong.

The strongest case is that Capveon becomes the connective tissue between infrastructure demand and the systems that turn it into orders. Resolving project identities across email, specifications, CRM, and ERP could put the company inside decisions with actual economic consequences. This fits my pattern of backing translation layers in neglected operating systems. Human approval, source citations, and field-level audit trails also acknowledge that errors have costs. The important question is whether customer-specific mappings compound into an owned workflow—or whether every new customer buys another integration project.

Lancaster’s prior FinnedAI business, with approximately $40K MRR claimed in his founder profile, gives me some evidence that he can ship software people buy. That revenue belongs to his previous company. Moorhead brings enterprise selling experience from Bonsai, Harper, and HubSpot, and the founders previously worked together at Harper. I give the team modest credit above my biography default. But neither founder has documented operating receipts inside infrastructure contracting or manufacturing. They have demonstrated more of the software side of this bridge than the infrastructure side.

The distribution evidence does not yet answer who owns the customer relationship. The WEFTEC tool covers 1,147 exhibitors and recommends 8–12 booths; those are product specifications, not conversion. Houston customer meetings establish activity without establishing purchases. Research-led prospecting and expansion through private-equity portfolios could work, but the dossier records no resulting paid deployment or referral. Likewise, subscriptions and contracted integration work describe possible revenue models without showing contribution margin, burn, or a seed-funded route to break-even. I retain the defaults for distribution and economics.

The competitive threat makes that distinction consequential. Trimble’s April 2026 announcement that it would acquire Document Crunch and embed compliance capabilities into construction workflows shows an incumbent moving toward this territory. Capveon’s opportunity is the work spanning systems that individual vendors do not control. Its vulnerability is that implementation and human review consume the value it creates. Approval queues reduce the danger of unchecked automation; they do not establish profitable delivery.

I pass on the evidence available. My exceptions for expensive rounds and AI-centered businesses require hard operating results—paid conversion, retention, or unusually strong external validation. Capveon has no documented equivalent yet. My single verifiable condition for reopening this pass is a production customer renewing at a price that produces positive contribution after fully counting integration, model usage, human review, and support. That would begin to distinguish an owned workflow from bespoke labor. I would then have an operating asset to price; today, I have a plausible product architecture.
