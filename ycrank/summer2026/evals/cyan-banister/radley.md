# Radley -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the interesting version of Radley is not a radiologist using an AI assistant. It is a practice in which credentialing, work allocation, report preparation, quality control, billing, and follow-up operate as one machine, leaving physicians with the judgments that still require them. The dossier gives me two leaks from that world: Radley’s previous software went live under $2.2 million of signed contracts, and the team is attempting to acquire the practice rather than negotiate from outside it. But it does not show which clinical decisions agents can safely assume, how those capabilities arrive in sequence, or why Radley—not an incumbent processing 25 million examinations annually—owns that future.

The founders are the reason I keep looking. Maryam Jahed helped Carbon Health grow from 31 to more than 100 clinics, led products through an insurer acquisition and an FDA breakthrough designation, and previously built Airo Health to six-figure revenue. Seak Meng Lay was Carbon Health’s founding engineer and VP of Software Engineering during the same expansion. They have already worked together inside the ugly machinery of healthcare. That is much better than discovering radiology during a fashionable AI round: it demonstrates operating tuition, complementary commercial and technical ability, and a prior artifact customers paid to use. What remains unproven is the independently derived obsession specific to radiology and their ability to recruit the clinical leadership required to operate a practice.

I want to know why one in six scans lacks sufficient radiologist capacity. Is the binding constraint physician supply, reimbursement, credentialing across facilities, fragmented worklists, or simply poor utilization? Radley names pieces of the system—the September 2027 PowerScribe support deadline, multistate licensure, facility privileges, and split clinical systems—but it does not quantify the rent each creates or identify whose agency the new model restores. Owning the practice may remove a vendor-adoption boundary; it may also preserve the same institutional economics while adding acquisition debt and integration risk.

The strongest bull case is quite strong. A pending acquisition could put $4.3 million of annualized revenue beneath a two-person pre-seed company, while the prior software business provides evidence that this is more than a rendering. Buying the workflow could also produce the proprietary clinical and operational data that an external software vendor cannot access. If the agents improve turnaround time, radiologist productivity, and collections inside one controlled environment, Radley could finance each subsequent acquisition with demonstrated operating gains.

But today the $4.3 million belongs to a transaction described in the future tense. No closing terms, financing, integration milestone, proprietary dataset, clinical validation program, or measured productivity improvement is public. Rad AI already reaches more than 40% of U.S. health systems, Sirona has 12 million annual studies under contract, and Radiology Partners combines practices with AI at far greater scale. Radley’s unstated-pattern fit—using a service beachhead to build concealed infrastructure—is therefore still only a hypothesis. Clinical data custody and agent governance also touch my privacy gate: the dossier cites the ACR requirements but supplies no limiting architecture. There is no disclosed 50/50 control problem or leadership shell game, yet the absence of those red flags cannot substitute for proof of the controlled bottleneck.

My 36-month expectation is that Radley closes at least one acquisition and reaches roughly $8–15 million in annualized professional-services revenue, but is still valued primarily as a technology-enabled radiology group rather than a defensible AI infrastructure company. I am neutral and would not write the check today. One verifiable condition flips me: after closing the first practice, show two consecutive quarters in which deployed agents produce an independently validated improvement in turnaround time or operating margin without worsening diagnostic-quality measures.
