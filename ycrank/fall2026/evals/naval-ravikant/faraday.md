# Faraday -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Oscar Rangel’s reported work building factory systems at Worthington Steel is Faraday’s most relevant founder signal. Shipping medical devices at Stryker adds exposure to physical constraints; Datta Kaligotla’s enterprise engineering and Handshake AI experience supply complementary context. These founders have plausibly encountered the problem they want to automate. But the evidence comes from founder profiles, without dated implementations or corroborated operating outcomes. I give their specific knowledge some credit without treating the biographies as demonstrated exceptional execution.

Faraday has also built something measurable. Its harness reportedly scores 96.2 against GPT-5.5’s 71.7 baseline at 2.6 times lower run cost. That is a useful engineering result. The limitation is consequential: these are 74 synthetic episodes across 33 scenario families in the company’s own benchmark. The result supports better orchestration on that suite. It does not establish an asset that survives another team copying the orchestration. No exclusive dataset, proprietary model, or accumulated workflow lock-in is established.

The strongest bull case is that factory deployment creates that asset. Mapping permissions, dependencies, approvals, and corrections could turn operational knowledge into reusable machine capability. Faraday’s combination of factory experience and a working harness makes this more credible than an unbuilt ambition. This fits my pattern of backing infrastructure bottlenecks, provided deployment learning actually compounds. But customer-specific environments can also produce isolated integration projects. Optional robot controllers do not establish a hardware advantage, and the dossier does not show that learning from one manufacturer improves service for another.

The expansion path is understandable: enter through quoting or order coordination, then use the same records and execution framework across inventory, procurement, and production. Manufacturers and distributors are identifiable buyers; Didero’s procurement product and Tulip’s frontline platform establish adjacent categories. Their financing does not establish Faraday’s addressable spend. The opportunity earns credit for a coherent expansion mechanism, while the ambition to run everything from the office to the shop floor still needs a commercially narrow starting point.

Faraday’s terms contemplate subscriptions and paid pilots, but identify neither an actual payer nor a price or completed transaction. Monetization therefore receives the missing-evidence default. So does customer-created distribution: Fabtech conversations are founder-led prospecting, and no observed referral or user-to-user utility loop is documented. I see no evidence of integrity problems, artificial financing pressure, or founder instability. Neither the standard YC financing nor the small public audience changes my judgment.

I pass on the current evidence. The relevant known exception is backing a demonstrated prelaunch product before a user network exists. Faraday has a technical artifact, but the dossier does not establish the exceptional product behavior or capital efficiency that would justify that override. My concern is whether each installation purchases another engineering obligation. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: a second paid factory deployment showing that contractually reusable corrections from the first improve held-out workflow performance without equivalent bespoke engineering.
