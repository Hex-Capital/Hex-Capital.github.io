# Frontier Computing -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that biological brains might replace GPUs. It is that oxygen diffusion limits cultures to roughly one million neurons, and Michael Domarkas reports a proprietary culture method intended to get past that barrier. This is a real obstacle and a plausible workaround, but the dossier omits the sequence I most want: what failed, what he changed, how long it took, and whether the change reproducibly increased viable tissue scale. The planned 100-million- and 500-million-neuron systems are construction targets, not results. So I see evidence of resourcefulness, but not yet enough to pass my founder gate.

The Frogger experiment is the strongest evidence. Living tissue achieved a 92% peak crossing rate after one hour over 25 games. That is much better than credentials or a diagram: Domarkas built something that learns. His four years in neuromorphic computing, two years in organoid computing, and $20,000 Emergent Ventures grant at 17 make the artifact more credible because he began before receiving the company’s $10 million pre-seed round. His age therefore does not bother me by itself. The relevant exception is Bindwell: both cases pair extreme youth with direct scientific exposure and a numerical biological result. The material difference is that Bindwell had laboratory validation, while Frontier’s result is self-reported, based on a small sample, and does not demonstrate the claimed scaling breakthrough.

The missing half of the proof is a user. Frontier names no researcher or ML team using the substrate, no revenue, no pricing, and no repeated experiment performed by an outside group. Twenty YC launch votes, press coverage, and a syndicate led by General Catalyst show investor interest, not product pull. This matters more because Cortical Labs already sells a $35,000 system and cloud access, while The Biological Computing Company reports a paying customer. If Frontier’s larger cultures are not usable and reproducible, neuron count is merely a spectacular benchmark attached to someone else’s market.

There is genuine schlep blindness here. The company must solve media chemistry, tissue survival, maturation, electrode interfacing, credit assignment, cloud operation, ethics, and donor consent. Domarkas has spent years inside the underlying science, so this does not look like a fashionable “AI plus biology” idea invented for an accelerator. But the dossier does not identify the personal incident that produced the company, and it shows no discarded hypothesis or evidence-driven change of route. Those remain unproven rather than adverse.

The strongest case for investing now is that demanding customers normally arrive after a new computing substrate works, and Frontier has already produced more technical evidence than most pre-seed companies. The strongest case against it is harsher: the Frogger result may not reproduce, may say nothing about useful ML workloads, and may not survive the jump from a small culture to 100 million neurons. Meanwhile, a solo 19-year-old founder is recruiting a scientific leader for £100,000–£180,000 and up to 4% equity to attack several of the central unsolved problems. That could be sensible team formation, but it could also mean the decisive scientific work has not yet been done by the company.

I would wait rather than write the check today. Frontier has a fierce-nerd artifact and an unusually difficult wedge, but it fails the resourcefulness gate because the dossier does not document the founder’s response sequence, and the technical proof is not strong enough to override that failure. One event would flip me: a named external ML lab paying to run repeated training workloads on independently reproduced cultures of at least 100 million viable neurons. That would turn the company’s two weakest claims—scale and user need—into one inspectable fact.
