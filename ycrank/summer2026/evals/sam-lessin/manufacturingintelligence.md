# HERA -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Post-money valuation: unknown. Competing term sheets: unknown. First scarce check: unproven. Those omissions do not make HERA bad, but they make the investment unpriceable. A technically credible YC company can still be a terrible personal check if Demo Day has already auctioned away the uncertainty, and the dossier provides none of the terms or capital-scarcity evidence required to clear that gate.

The product does contain a plausible venture moment: determine whether company-specific rules, tolerance math, and traceable evidence can catch expensive drawing errors without sending sensitive prints outside the plant. A successful test would matter because one prevented error can protect a $40,000 part. But the financing plan never names the experiment. There is no round size, dated milestone, accuracy threshold, conversion target, or explanation of why the work cannot be funded through paid engineering engagements. “Hire and sell” is not a truth-revealing use of venture capital, and silence does not become a plan.

The strongest bull case is the three-plant deployment. This is more meaningful than the 200 interviews or LinkedIn reach because engineers have apparently allowed HERA into a production workflow involving confidential drawings. Customer-specific rules and an accumulating error record could become the proprietary dark pool here: not a generally available model, but adjudicated exceptions embedded inside each plant. The missing causal step is purchase. The only disclosed offer is a free, NDA-covered review of 20 drawings; there is no paid conversion, repeat usage, prevented-scrap tally, or retained deployment. The known-exception logic therefore does not apply: HERA has promising access, but none of the unusually hard commercial evidence that can override an undocumented deal.

Patel and So are the best part of the case. Patel completed manufacturing work at Pfizer and TE Connectivity alongside healthcare-ML research; So automated workflows at Wyze and helped build a water-treatment facility adding 40 L/s of capacity. That is genuine outsider-insider potential across physical operations and software, not résumé decoration. A live demo in three plants suggests they have shipped beyond a classroom prototype. Still, HERA joins two systems more specific than “manufacturing plus data”: standards-heavy drawing adjudication and production-quality software. The dossier does not yet show that either founder has owned drawing release, quality sign-off, or a deployed compliance system at scale.

Delete the next financing and the model becomes thin. HERA publishes no paid price, gross margin, burn, runway, procurement cycle, or controllable cash metric. Redprint charges as little as $1.98 per drawing, while CoLab, Axial, and Glypad attack overlapping review workflows; that creates a real possibility that automated checking becomes a cheap feature while on-premise customization remains expensive services work. The counterargument stings: a single prevented $40,000 scrap event could justify substantial enterprise pricing, and customer-specific deployment may protect HERA from commodity per-drawing competition. But neither willingness to pay nor delivery cost has been observed.

I am passing today, not because the founders lack credibility, but because the investable asset has not been separated from the promising product. My base case is that within 36 months HERA reaches $1 million–$3 million ARR from on-premise deployments at aerospace and semiconductor suppliers, while custom implementation prevents software-like margins. The one verifiable flip condition is three current plants signing annual paid contracts that disclose aggregate contract value, gross margin, and a dated accuracy benchmark, alongside a sub-$10 million post-money round funding that benchmark rather than the next raise.
