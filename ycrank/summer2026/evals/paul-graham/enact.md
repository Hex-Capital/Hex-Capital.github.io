# Enact -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important missing fact is not revenue. It is what broke. Enact says robot developers must watch rollouts, judge success, and reset tasks manually, but the dossier never identifies a deployment where these founders encountered that obstacle, what they built around it, or how quickly the result changed. Both founders are effectively anonymous in the public record: no background, prior work, repositories, or shared execution history. That leaves the main resourcefulness test unanswered. YC admission and a website dated August 2026 are evidence that the company exists, not that the founders bend systems when blocked.

The product description is a plausible loop: find policy failures, generate demonstrations and recoveries, retrain, then evaluate again. But it remains a description. Enact publishes no working-system result, dataset size, evaluation accuracy, cross-embodiment test, or improvement in policy success. This matters especially because Instance reports testing a success detector on more than 10,000 held-out episodes across seven robot platforms, while Axis has raised $12 million around overlapping post-training data work. My unstated pattern here runs in the opposite direction from Enact’s evidence: the interesting robotics companies usually produce a numerical proof spike before the financing story. Enact has not yet disclosed one.

Nor do we know who pulls the product back. “Request data” implies direct enterprise work, but it names no robotics developer, paid project, signed LOI, repeated prototype user, or urgent task. The strongest bull case is that this service-heavy entry point is precisely the opportunity. Real-world collection is slow and expensive, recovery data must match deployment conditions, and repeated projects could accumulate proprietary mappings from failures to successful interventions. With the physical-AI training-platform market estimated at $3.2 billion in 2025 and growing 41.35% annually, a two-person team that closes this loop could become an important post-training layer rather than another labeling shop.

But that bull case currently substitutes the market’s difficulty for evidence about the founders. The same collection costs could make Enact a low-margin project business, while NVIDIA Cosmos, Runway, Instance, Axis, and micro1 can each absorb parts of the loop. There is no disclosed proprietary substrate, customer exclusivity, or technical threshold protecting the integrated workflow. The dossier also shows no rejected hypothesis or evidence-driven route change, so I cannot tell whether the team behaves like a running back or is merely describing its first route.

I’m passing. None of the known-exception mechanisms applies: there is no working prototype strong enough to replace missing founder evidence, no unmistakable user pull, and no independently validated result. The ordinary launch and partnership red flags are not yet present, but neither are the concrete pre-round proofs that would override uncertainty. One fact would flip me: a named robotics developer showing that Enact’s generated recovery data improved a deployed policy’s independently measured task-success rate by at least 20%, together with a dated account of the blocker and the founders’ shipped workaround.
