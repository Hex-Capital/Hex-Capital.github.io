# Greypoint Industries -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first three numbers I need are absent: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and the amount Greypoint Industries is raising. That matters more than the $14.41 billion counter-UAS market because defense is currently fashionable and YC adds auction risk. I cannot tell whether this is a cheap first check into a neglected Canadian team or whether investors have already priced in the category’s projected 22.4% annual growth. Financing silence earns no presumption of scarcity.

The product does contain a credible venture moment, but the round does not yet name it. LEGION has passively geolocated an aerial target, the team has demonstrated for the Canadian Armed Forces, and a Canadian-government customer has signed a contract. The next truth-revealing experiment should be brutally finite: detect and geolocate RF emitters under contested conditions, then cue ARROW to defeat a defined swarm at a stated range and success rate by a stated date. “Build autonomous swarm warfare” is not an experiment; it is a capital-consuming aspiration. Without a milestone, deadline, and use-of-funds plan, I cannot distinguish risk capital from another hardware financing factory.

The best evidence is the translation layer. Darcy Zhang has served as a Canadian Armed Forces infanteer; Nic Chu and Jordan Khoo worked inside General Dynamics systems engineering; Jordan also worked on controls at TerraSense; Steven Xu brings Bosch embedded-systems experience. Together they have converted that access into more than résumés: a government contract, a military demonstration, and an operating geolocation prototype. This is exactly where young outsiders can question a procurement and sensing stack while still being legible to the people who must deploy it.

The strongest bull case is that the contract already overrides several missing gates. Canada launched its Defence Drone Initiative in July 2026 specifically to test, produce, and procure low-cost counter-drone interceptors, giving Greypoint a timely domestic channel that Anduril, Fortem, and Epirus cannot simply recreate through better software. A contract is more informative than 1,107 LinkedIn followers, press listings, or hackathon prizes. It suggests that the team’s government relationships can cause a purchase, although the undisclosed value and absence of repeat procurement stop me from calling this a proprietary distribution asset.

The bear case still wins today. The only disclosed technical proof is one geolocation test; there are no range, accuracy, contested-environment, or swarm-interception results. RF-silent threats attack LEGION’s sensing premise, while established competitors already field kinetic, net-capture, and high-power-microwave systems. The prospective dark pool—emitter signatures, deployment data, C2 integrations, and manufacturing learning—is inferred rather than accumulated. Four equal-seeming co-founders also create an unanswered governance problem: the dossier names a CEO, but not who has final authority over product, procurement, and financing when those priorities collide.

Delete the next round and the model becomes harder. Greypoint provides no contract value, gross margin, burn, runway, interceptor unit cost, or route from prototype manufacturing to break-even. My 36-month base case is $1 million–$4 million in annual Canadian defense revenue from pilots and limited deployments, paired with another financing requirement rather than a self-funding production business. I would flip to invest if Greypoint documents a non-auctioned price and one seed-funded, dated field test that demonstrates contested-environment geolocation plus autonomous interception while showing contract economics capable of financing follow-on units. Until then, I am passing—not because the product is unserious, but because the company has shown a promising bridge without showing the price or the exact card my money flips.
