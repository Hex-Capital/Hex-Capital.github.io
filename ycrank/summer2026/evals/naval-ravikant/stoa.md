# Stoa -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Stoa’s institutional RFQ marketplace is strongest as a potential network and weakest as permissionless leverage. Stoa can compound transaction data across buyers, dealers, lenders, and operators, but every transaction still passes through access applications, KYB, private quotes, payment release, physical delivery, and U.S. export rules. This is a network-shaped business built from permissioned atoms. Code coordinates the labor and capital; it does not replace them.

**Uber** is the high rung because each new driver improved availability for riders, each new rider improved utilization for drivers, and liquidity became the product. Stoa shares that reflexive mechanism: more completed RFQs could improve clearing prices, counterparty-performance data, and dealer participation. Stoa diverges where it matters most—Uber demonstrated transactions, while Stoa discloses no participant count, RFQ volume, or completed trade, and Compute Exchange already aggregates procurement across more than 100 providers. A marketplace without liquidity is software describing a marketplace.

**Anchorage Digital** is the low rung on permissionless architecture because its OCC-chartered custody product begins with institutional permission rather than escaping it. Stoa repeats the mechanism without Anchorage’s regulatory moat: Stoa restricts access to U.S. institutions, requires KYB, solicits counterparties directly, and leaves hardware inspection and export compliance to users. Stoa may digitize brokerage, but digital labor is still labor. The internet lowers coordination cost; it does not make GPU settlement permissionless.

**Kaan Yigit’s commodities-desk experience** gives Stoa real specific knowledge about quotes, market structure, and institutional workflows, while Eren Berke Saglam’s Series 7 and 63 registrations add financial-market fluency. Over 18 months, that knowledge can compound into better RFQ schemas, dealer scoring, and price histories for Stoa. The missing artifact matters: Stoa names no prior GPU-market operation, public repository, executed exchange, or founder-built hardware network. Credentials can be trained for. A proprietary feedback loop cannot.

**No public TAM or SAM data** is less important than Stoa’s available pivot surface. Stoa could move from physical GPU execution into price indices, collateral valuation, inventory financing, compute-capacity contracts, or broker workflow infrastructure. The market score would flip into the top tier if Stoa’s transaction data became the reference price for lenders and neoclouds across physical hardware and compute contracts; it would flip downward if the business remains a U.S.-only resale venue for named NVIDIA generations. Stoa has room to pivot, but physical inventory may consume runway while those pivots are discovered.

**Berat Celik’s Cornell Tech MEng, Kaan Yigit’s UIUC computer-science degree, and Eren Berke Saglam’s 3.99 Dartmouth record** establish intelligence and likely energy, but not yet integrity over time. **Notion** cleared this axis because Ivan Zhao’s long obsession survived repeated rebuilds and made the founder inseparable from the product. Stoa offers no public co-founder history, prior venture outcome, or evidence of how Stoa’s founders behave through disputes and failed settlements. Play long-term games with long-term people. Stoa has shown the résumés, not yet the decade.

**Compute Exchange’s July 2026 launch** points to my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Stoa will have pivoted into GPU pricing data and broker-workflow software generating $1 million–$3 million ARR, rather than becoming a liquid hardware exchange. Stoa’s strongest asset is the structured data created around RFQs; Stoa’s weakest asset is the permission-heavy transaction itself. Software will peel away from settlement because code scales and hardware disputes do not.

**Eligible data partners paying a 0% platform fee** is the strongest bull case against that forecast: Stoa could subsidize the supply side, bootstrap proprietary clearing data, and use Kaan Yigit’s commodity-market experience to become the institutional exchange before Compute Exchange consolidates the category. Anchorage Digital shows that permission can become a moat when regulation and trust concentrate liquidity. The analogy breaks because Stoa does not inspect hardware, guarantee counterparties, take title, or disclose a single completed transaction, while its named rival already reaches more than 100 providers. I would flip to Invest if Stoa verifies $100 million in quarterly GMV with more than half of volume coming from repeat counterparties without outbound sales.
