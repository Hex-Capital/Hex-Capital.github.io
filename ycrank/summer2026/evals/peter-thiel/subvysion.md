# SubVysion -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

SubVysion’s contested truth is that a contractor can replace unreliable markings and drawings with a rover-generated, centimeter-accurate 3D map usable through a phone. What would prove it wrong? A blind excavation test showing that moisture, clay, or small-diameter lines produce materially more misses than the advertised “100% visibility.” The proposition is falsifiable, but the dossier supplies neither an independent accuracy study nor a detection-rate dataset. A technical claim without the experiment is a possible secret, not yet a discovered one.

The founders are unusually relevant to the problem. Maxwell Stone built on-device inference systems deployed in more than 10,000 pharmacies and worked on railyard mapping; Wenson Tang built emergency-dispatch software and a LiDAR search-and-rescue drone. They have converted those capabilities into contracts and live construction-site deployments across the United States, with the first customer acquired through cold calling. This is real pre-seed execution. But the pair only joined forces in January 2026, and the dossier establishes neither a history of shipping together nor their ownership, salaries, or economic exposure. Technical aptitude cannot repair an untested foundation.

The narrow beachhead is contractors preparing to excavate, but it does not yet exhibit a monopoly mechanism. Exodigo has mapped 86.1 million square feet for more than 100 customers; 4M aggregates utility records nationally; RodRadar places detection directly on excavation equipment. Phone-based AR is a delivery feature, not proprietary technology, a network effect, or scale economics. SubVysion might eventually accumulate a valuable corpus of corrected subsurface data, but a hypothetical dataset earns no credit before its size, exclusivity, or compounding accuracy can be observed.

The bull case is sharper than the score suggests. This is an adversarial information system that makes hidden physical reality legible, built by founders with field-sensing experience and already used on active sites. The team has also partially escaped the usual hard-technology coordination trap: it did not wait for perfect certification before finding a contractor through cold calling. If each completed scan improves future detection and contractors repeatedly reuse the maps, a project service could become infrastructure. Yet the known-exception logic for complicated physical systems requires independently validated output tied to persistent customer demand. Live deployment proves willingness to try; it does not prove accuracy, retention, or a monopoly.

The economics remain indefinite. No price, revenue, gross margin, processing throughput, sales cycle, or recurring-access contract is disclosed. The cited U.S. market was only $294.4 million in 2025, while the inferred business model combines field collection with recurring map access; without an adjacent expansion mechanism, this resembles a specialized surveying operation more than a power-law software company. My 36-month base case is that SubVysion remains below $2 million in ARR-equivalent revenue and derives most income from project-based mapping rather than a proprietary data platform.

I would pass today. The distribution evidence is the strongest fact, but sales cannot manufacture a secret or monopoly after the fact. One event would flip the decision: a national contractor signing a paid, multi-year rollout after an independent blind benchmark demonstrates greater than 95% utility detection with less than five-centimeter positional error across varied soil conditions. That would simultaneously convert “centimeter accuracy” from slogan to technical proof and show that the resulting advantage matters economically.
