# SubVysion -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: a crew should be able to see the subsurface as a persistent, queryable layer before a bucket touches soil, while owners and insurers treat verified utility maps as part of the site record. SubVysion has a small leak from that world—a rover combining ground-penetrating radar with positioning data, followed by phone-based AR on live construction sites. But the dossier does not yet map the transitions from project scanning to persistent infrastructure: who maintains each map, how corrections compound, and why the resulting record becomes authoritative rather than another drawing crews distrust.

The present system is genuinely broken. Contractors depend on 811 markings and legacy records, and locator errors or inaccurate maps reportedly contribute to one-third of utility strikes. SubVysion returns immediate visibility to the crew closest to the risk. What remains fuzzy is the rent: the dossier does not quantify the cost of a strike, identify whether the contractor, utility, insurer, or owner reliably pays for prevention, or show which party controls the updated map. A painful failure is not automatically a business model; the person suffering and the person buying must be connected.

The founders are the strongest part of this company. Before joining forces in January 2026, Maxwell Stone built on-device inference software deployed in more than 10,000 pharmacies and worked on railyard mapping and defect detection. Wenson Tang worked on cross-jurisdiction emergency dispatch and built a LiDAR search-and-rescue drone. Those are shipped artifacts in sensing, mapping, and safety—not decorative résumés. Cold-calling their way to a first customer and investor, then putting the product onto job sites across the United States, also shows commercial nerve. I still cannot call this a lifelong obsession: the record supplies relevant technical tuition, but not a dated trail of underground-utility experiments or recruiting beyond the two-person founding team.

The product has crossed the concept boundary, but not yet the trust boundary. Contracts and live job sites require real customer effort, yet the claims remain founder-reported: no customer count, revenue, repeat deployment, or observed crew behavior is disclosed. More importantly, “centimeter accuracy” and “100% visibility” sit beside FHWA evidence that moisture and clay attenuate radar and small utilities are difficult to distinguish. In a safety product, a beautiful AR rendering can make an uncertain prediction more dangerous by making it feel certain.

The bull case stings because this resembles the kind of concealed infrastructure I like: a mundane field workflow could accumulate corrected subsurface data into an asset needed by construction, utilities, insurers, and autonomous equipment. The functioning prototype and contracts also invite the same pre-revenue exception I made for **CosmicBrain AI**. The material difference is proof: that company paired its prototype with signed industrial proofs of concept and a founder’s unusually long robotics record. Here, Exodigo already reports 86.1 million square feet mapped and more than 100 customers, while SubVysion discloses neither independent detection results nor a controlled corpus. The hidden-layer pattern is plausible, not yet demonstrated.

My 36-month base case is a $2 million–$6 million ARR project-mapping business by August 2029, valuable to selected contractors but not yet the default subsurface-data layer. I would not write the personal check today. The accountable CEO/COO presentation avoids a leadership shell game, but founder ownership and any 50/50 tiebreaker remain undisclosed, while custody of sensitive infrastructure maps needs an explicit architecture. One verifiable artifact would flip me: an audited case study from a national contractor showing a paid renewal across at least ten sites and a material reduction in utility strikes. That would prove the map survives difficult field conditions, changes costly behavior, and compounds beyond a rover-enabled service engagement.
