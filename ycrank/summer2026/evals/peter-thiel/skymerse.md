# Skymerse -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The contested truth is precise: flight operations are constrained less by missing information than by the failure to translate fragmented aeronautical data into machine-readable operational consequences. This is falsifiable. If Skymerse’s structured affected-element model cannot reduce dispatcher workload or missed operational impacts against incumbent briefing tools, there is no secret—only a more elaborate interface. The evidence is meaningful but incomplete: 3 million processed NOTAMs, 2,005,126 extracted exposures, and LOT Polish Airlines receiving filtered alerts within three minutes constitute a shipped experiment. Yet the dossier does not show that named peers reject this proposition, nor does it quantify the three-hour planning claim independently.

The strongest case for Skymerse is not “AI for aviation.” It is the narrow beachhead already underneath that slogan: structuring NOTAM applicability for airline network monitoring. LOT’s deployment, 3,800 registered users, and thousands of daily users show that this is more than a presentation. The company also fits my revealed preference for information systems that make fragmented, consequential reality legible. But value creation is not value capture. The 487-day processing history may be useful data, yet it is not identified as exclusive, and Jeppesen ForeFlight’s Airflow combines aviation data, customer data, certification knowledge, and incumbent distribution. Overwatch AI covers much of the same operational stack. Skymerse has demonstrated a differentiated product architecture, not a monopoly mechanism.

Damian Szumski has unusually direct founder-market fit: he is a licensed dispatcher and has worked across LOT, Lufthansa Systems, airline-software implementation, and Flightradar24’s API product. That explains why the product begins with operational applicability rather than a generic chatbot. The foundation is nevertheless unresolved. Earlier materials called Michał Pasierbski a co-founder; YC now lists Szumski alone. There is no evidence about the departure, prior shared work, ownership, or economic exposure. Missing cap-table evidence is not refutation, but a disappearing co-founder is affirmative uncertainty about whether the company was organized as a durable mission or reassembled around successive products. The 2025 transcription assistant, followed by Notamify and then an end-to-end operations platform, reinforces that concern.

Distribution has evidence but not yet economics. A $24.90 monthly Pro product, an iOS app, and 3,800 registrations create a credible self-service channel; LOT demonstrates that demo-led airline sales can produce production deployment. What is absent is conversion, enterprise contract value, sales-cycle length, renewal behavior, and implementation cost. Those omissions matter because self-service pilots and airline procurement are two different distribution systems. The $1.03 billion flight-operations software market could support a valuable business, but no disclosed ARR, margins, or customer expansion arithmetic yet shows how one revenue engine becomes a power-law outcome.

The safety boundary makes incumbent competition more dangerous. Notamify explicitly disclaims status as an official aeronautical-information source, while higher autonomy requires synchronized NOTAM, weather, MEL, schedule, manual, and aircraft-limit inputs under an evolving EASA framework. Skymerse has avoided the worst coordination red flag because LOT proves that one operational deployment works; it has not proved that the complete system can become authoritative enough to displace the systems it currently supplements. None of the known exceptions applies: there is no verified zero-to-one technical event, persistent demand despite legal exclusion, or independently validated deployment that neutralizes the coordination stack.

My base case is that within 36 months Skymerse reaches $2–5 million ARR and either remains an intelligence layer or is acquired by Boeing’s Jeppesen operation, rather than becoming the independent last mover in flight operations. The painful counterargument is that LOT, daily usage, and the accumulated schema are exactly how an unglamorous infrastructure monopoly can begin before outsiders recognize it. I would reverse my judgment on one verifiable condition: three airlines each renew contracts above $250,000 annually after evaluating Skymerse directly against Jeppesen Airflow or Overwatch. Until then, this is a promising secret attached to an unproven company structure and an exposed competitive position. Neutral—investigate, but do not yet invest.
