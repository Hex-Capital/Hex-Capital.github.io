# Decawork -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what broke. Decawork moved from autonomous IT administration for employees to an agent control plane after deploying the earlier product across several enterprises in India and the United States. That could be an excellent running-back move. But the dossier does not identify the customer observation that forced it, the workaround the founders shipped, or how quickly the result changed. A pivot is not itself evidence of resourcefulness. Without the before-and-after mechanism, I cannot tell whether the founders chased a sharper problem or merely a hotter noun.

There is nevertheless a real product here. Decawork gives each agent an identity and scoped credentials, routes tool calls through a gateway, gates sensitive actions, attributes logs, monitors failures, and supports retirement. Reported SOC 2 status by July 20, 2026 is especially useful: two founders completed an annoying institutional task before they had public traction. But this is still self-reported architectural proof. There is no production workload, reliability measurement, detection advantage, or current customer showing that the control plane survives contact with a large agent fleet.

The founders are unusually plausible builders for this problem. Sarthak Aggarwal worked on AI systems at NVIDIA and enterprise AI at Ema. Aman Raj built AI-compliance tooling reportedly used by more than 240 Barclays teams. This is better than an “AI governance” idea manufactured from a market report. They have lived near the users and the schlep: access controls, enterprise trust, compliance, and procurement. What remains unproved is that either founder personally encountered this precise agent-governance failure and built Decawork in response to it.

The strongest bull case is that the public evidence trails the company. The prior product had enterprise deployments, the current system appears operational, and SOC 2 suggests Decawork has already done work most pre-seed teams postpone. These facts resemble the pattern of technical founders assembling a difficult substrate before announcing scale. But the pattern is incomplete. No named IT team uses the current product repeatedly; no developer has forced an unexpected workflow; and no measured proof spike distinguishes Decawork from a competent enterprise-security startup.

Competition makes that missing pull decisive. Okta released overlapping agent registration, credentials, ownership, and governance in April 2026. Astrix, Noma Security, WitnessAI, and Zenity have each raised more than $55 million. This does not make the market too crowded. It means the wedge must be discovered through users rather than asserted through feature coverage. “Deployment and maintenance plus governance” is currently a positioning sentence, not evidence that a narrow group wants the crude product badly enough to resist bundling.

I would pass today. The operational gate fails because the dossier supplies neither a named blocker followed by a shipped workaround nor current-product pull strong enough to replace that evidence. None of the known exceptions applies: there is no independently validated technical result or unmistakable user response capable of overriding the missing founder test. The one verifiable flip condition is a named enterprise case showing that an IT deployment blocker caused the founders to ship a specific workaround within weeks and that the same customer subsequently ran agents through Decawork repeatedly in production.
