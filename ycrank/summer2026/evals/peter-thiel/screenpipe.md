# screenpipe -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The contested truth is that an agent becomes substantially more useful when it can retrieve a private, continuous record of everything its user saw, said, and heard—not merely the data that applications choose to expose. The falsifying event is equally concrete: users install screenpipe but do not retain, pay, or entrust it with sensitive work after the novelty of retrospective search fades. The dossier shows a shipped test rather than a slogan: cross-platform capture, local SQLite storage, OCR and accessibility extraction, audio transcription, MCP access, and permissioned agents, supported by 12,176 repository commits. Yet it never identifies the prevailing belief this contradicts. Microsoft Recall, Granola, Otter, and the former Rewind all accept some version of the premise; the disagreement is over scope, privacy, and architecture.

The strongest fact is not the claimed 300,000 users. It is that screenpipe converted an open-source audience into 202 paid subscribers and $3,480 MRR by February 2025 without paid marketing, while generating more than 4,000 monthly downloads. The repository’s 20,900 stars and 2,100 forks, followed by reported 20–30% weekly revenue growth and sixfold active-user growth, indicate an unusually efficient developer-led distribution route. Basic pricing at $21 per month and Business pricing at $42 per seat fit that route. But enterprise sales are still an intention expressed through hiring, not a demonstrated motion with named buyers, contract sizes, deployment times, or renewals.

Distribution is real; monopoly is conjectural. Cross-platform capture and local model choice differentiate screenpipe from Windows Recall, while full-day context distinguishes it from meeting assistants. But differentiation is not durability. The product is assembled from available operating-system APIs, SQLite, Whisper, OCR, and MCP, and local exportability prevents the accumulated history from becoming a strong data lock-in. Microsoft can bundle the horizontal memory layer, meeting products can expand their capture surface, and the source-available license may weaken the community mechanism precisely when screenpipe tries to capture more value from it. Twenty thousand stars prove attention and technical execution, not ownership of a bounded market.

Louis Beaumont has more than a résumé claim to founder-market fit. He reports maintaining an AI second brain since 2020, has prior infrastructure and machine-learning experience, and has built a repository whose scale makes outsourced core engineering implausible. This looks like a life organized around the problem rather than a company assembled for the current batch. Still, the dossier does not establish current ownership, compensation, or the role of the second team member. There is no adverse founding-structure evidence, but economic alignment remains unverified.

The bull case is uncomfortable because the product may already contain the beginning of its moat: a large open-source funnel, years of difficult cross-platform engineering, and an installed base whose private histories and agent configurations become increasingly costly to reproduce. The revenue engine is also coherent—individual subscriptions can seed $42-per-seat teams and eventually on-premises enterprise contracts. If reported weekly growth persisted even briefly, the February 2025 MRR figure would badly understate present demand. But the known exception for a genuine zero-to-one technical event does not apply: persistent desktop memory is useful, yet the dossier contains no independent result showing an agent becomes categorically more capable with screenpipe than with incumbent context systems.

I would investigate, not invest today. This is a technically serious company with organic distribution trapped inside a market whose central feature may be bundled by platform owners. The verifiable flip condition is twelve months of cohort data showing at least 70% annual retention among paying teams and expansion to $2 million ARR, with most new revenue attributable to accumulated organizational workflows rather than discounted individual subscriptions. That would demonstrate that screenpipe owns a compounding work-memory layer; absent it, this remains a strong product in a competitive category—value creation without proven value capture.
