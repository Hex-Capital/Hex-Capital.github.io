# Arbital -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Arbital has demonstrated demand, but not consequential change. Its $1.3 billion of cumulative volume in seven months and $377 million of current monthly volume prove that 2,600 traders will route capital through the product. They do not show which societal cost pool becomes radically cheaper or which scarce expert service becomes broadly accessible. A terminal combining perps, memecoins, tokenized stocks, and market-making is an aggregation of speculative workflows, not yet a 100x consequence.

The technical case is similarly incomplete. Vincent Toniwan previously operated Aevo infrastructure below two milliseconds at 100,000 events per second, and the product integrates multiple perpetual DEXs with inventory caps, loss thresholds, circuit breakers, and maker-only execution. Those are credible engineering credentials. But Arbital supplies no measured improvement in spread, slippage, fill rate, capital efficiency, or loss-adjusted return against Hyperliquid, Axiom, or another named incumbent. Multi-venue integration is implementation work until a benchmark proves discontinuity.

The strongest bull case is that three Aevo veterans cover the necessary functions unusually well: Karon Pangestu helped scale cumulative volume from $1 billion to roughly $100 billion, Steven Mulyawan onboarded more than 10,000 traders, and Toniwan built the execution infrastructure. That combination plausibly explains the rapid volume. It also invokes the known exception for a small fintech team entering a giant value pool with a product capable of evolving. The material difference from Block is that Arbital currently expands speculative trading convenience rather than access to productive financial infrastructure, while competitors already demonstrate formidable economics—Axiom reports more than $100 million in cumulative revenue and Hyperliquid generated $844 million in 2025.

The company’s product transition weakens rather than strengthens the option. Its website presents a decentralized market-making engine, while the YC page describes a forthcoming social, mobile-first multi-asset application; the reported volume is not separated between them. This could be fast belief revision after evidence, but the dossier does not establish that causal story. It may instead be movement from defensible execution infrastructure toward a crowded interface business. Nor is there a disclosed experiment that ranks regulatory exposure, venue dependency, inventory loss, or consumer retention as the first kill risk with a numerical pass/fail threshold.

The economics remain opaque. Reduced fees and exchange points may attract flow, but no pricing, revenue, retention, rebate dependency, or unit contribution has been disclosed. If exchange incentives disappear or venues internalize the routing functionality, $377 million of monthly volume may have little durable value. My 36-month base case is that Arbital retreats from the broad consumer terminal into B2B cross-venue execution software and remains below $5 million ARR; the team is strong enough to adapt, but the present product lacks a proprietary cost curve.

I pass. This is competent execution in a real and rapidly growing market, but it activates two red flags: a product differentiated mainly through interface breadth and integrations, and a category adjacent to gambling that creates more trading rather than more societal capacity. One verifiable condition would flip the decision: audited evidence that Arbital’s routing engine reduces all-in execution cost or required market-making capital by at least 5x versus the best named venue-native alternative, while producing at least $1 million in monthly net revenue excluding exchange incentives.
