# Peer -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: no proposed post-money valuation, no competing-term-sheet count, and no evidence that this is Peer's first genuinely scarce check. Those are not administrative omissions. Without them, I cannot tell whether I am buying an unfashionable freight experiment or paying YC-auction pricing for the already-consensus claim that agents will automate brokerage. The dossier offers no hard evidence capable of overriding that price gate: no shipments, customers, revenue, or proprietary carrier network.

The capital plan is equally undefined. “AI-native freight brokerage” describes a destination, not the card this round flips. Peer has not named a dated experiment—such as completing a specified number of loads at positive contribution margin while autonomously resolving a measured share of exceptions—whose success would dramatically reprice the business. The May-to-August 2026 pivot from screen-recording automation to freight makes such a test more important, because the money could otherwise finance simultaneous customer discovery, brokerage compliance, carrier acquisition, and a product rebuild. That is a capital program, not one venture moment.

The strongest case for Peer is the team. Aaron Zhang built voice agents for trucking fleets at Waylens; Felix Peng shipped internal AI tools used by more than 250 LADWP employees. They have therefore shipped automation into real operating environments, and freight is exactly the kind of neglected, phone-and-email system where software can reorganize physical work. The $21.28 billion U.S. brokerage market is large, and more than 40% of brokers reportedly planned AI or machine-learning deployments in 2026. A fast pivot here could represent learning rather than drift.

But the translation layer is incomplete. Neither founder is documented as having operated a brokerage, procured carrier capacity, handled a failed pickup at midnight, or managed the spread and credit risk on a load. Peer also lacks public broker authority, the required $75,000 bond, reliability data, and a shipper or carrier whose behavior proves the workflow works. FleetWorks already reports 10,000 carriers, while Cartage combines automation with human exception escalation. Peer currently owns neither side of that network and has disclosed no technical mechanism that makes its exception handling materially different.

Delete the next financing and the model disappears into another blank. The inferred per-load spread could support a profitable company, but there is no take rate, carrier payment timing, gross margin, burn, or controllable cash metric. Completed loads might eventually create proprietary lane-pricing and carrier-reliability data; today that is an imagined future moat, not a dark pool causing purchases. The known exceptions do not rescue this: there is no viral adoption, enterprise conversion, retention, acquisition offer, or other hard external receipt strong enough to compensate for missing terms and economics.

My 36-month base case is that Peer either shuts down or pivots from principal freight brokerage into AI dispatcher software sold to existing brokers, because the founders have demonstrated automation skill but not ownership of brokerage supply, compliance, or cash economics. I would reverse the pass if Peer documented one package of proof: a sub-$10 million post-money first institutional round funding a dated test that completes at least 20 loads for three repeat shippers at positive contribution margin, with measured exception-resolution reliability. That would convert the pivot from an AI market story into a scarce, truth-revealing check.
