# Waybill -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: AI can now automate discrete procurement tasks, hardware teams will buy reliable cross-border fulfillment, and the controlling variable is contribution margin per successfully delivered order after supplier, freight, customs, verification, and exception-handling costs. Waybill does not yet identify or measure that variable. A $10.1 billion procurement-software market and 10% forecast CAGR do not establish that managed hardware procurement has software economics.

The product is stronger than the market case. Waybill accepts a BOM, obtains quotes, routes payment, manages freight and customs, tracks delivery, updates inventory, and reconciles invoices. It also retains order, price, and lead-time history. This is an owned workflow, not an AI formatter: better models could improve supplier selection and exception handling without absorbing the operational system. The tradeoff is that every additional step also creates liability. Without fulfillment cost, gross-margin, or delivery-performance data, it is unclear whether automation compounds or merely coordinates a service business.

The first two weeks produced requests for an obsolete RF component, 20 tons of aerospace-grade titanium, and four Cessna aircraft. This demonstrates breadth of inbound curiosity, not product-market pull. None is documented as a completed or repeated order, and the extreme variation may be adverse: sourcing aircraft, titanium, and electronic components involves different suppliers, regulations, verification systems, and working-capital requirements. There is no disclosed revenue, named customer, pricing schedule, retention, or capacity bottleneck.

The technology discontinuity is also underspecified. Gartner forecasts agentic supply-chain software spending rising from under $2 billion in 2025 to $53 billion in 2030, but Waybill has not shown how agents change its landed cost, labor per order, delivery time, or margins. Cofactr, Forge, Jiga, and Luminovo already cover overlapping workflows. A regional supplier network and accumulated transaction history could become useful, but the dossier provides neither network scale nor an estimate of what it would cost a funded competitor to reproduce that data.

The strongest bull case is the founders. Rishi Laddha handled aerospace procurement, supply chain, and government relations at LAT Aerospace; Tushar Goyal built inventory and forecasting systems used across more than 400 Indian D2C brands. They understand both the operational and software sides of the problem, and the product could turn its first procurement relationship into distribution for freight, compliance, inventory, and financing. But the physical-business exception requires evidence that technology changes incumbent unit economics. Domain experience alone does not supply it, and the dossier documents neither prior founder outcomes nor a costly commitment demonstrating desperation.

I pass today. My base case is that Waybill becomes a narrower, operations-heavy cross-border procurement service rather than a large software-like platform. One condition would flip the decision: ten named hardware customers each completing at least three orders within six months while Waybill demonstrates contribution margins above 20% after freight, customs, verification, and exception labor. That would validate both repeat pull and the controlling economic variable.
