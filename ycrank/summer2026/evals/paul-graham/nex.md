# Nex -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The revealing interval is February to April 2026. Nex shut down a revenue-producing AI CRM, then quickly shipped WUPHF, which reached 1.2K GitHub stars, 94 forks, and a Show HN post with 260 points and 115 comments. That is evidence of founders who keep moving when a product route fails. But the dossier never identifies the observation that killed the CRM. I see a shipped workaround under pressure, not yet a complete proof of resourcefulness, because I cannot tell whether they learned something precise or merely changed nouns.

The technical evidence is stronger. A live context graph, granular RBAC, SOC 2 Type 1 compliance, and more than 1,000 integrations are substantial artifacts for a pre-seed company. WUPHF is inspectable software rather than a diagram, and 94 forks imply that some developers wanted to alter or reuse it. Francisco Dias helped build HubSpot CRM and its AI infrastructure, so this does not look like two outsiders wrapping an API after reading a market report. The founders began before permission and have already done some of the integration and security work that makes this category unpleasant.

The missing variable is pull. GitHub stars, Product Hunt points, and LinkedIn followers measure attention. They do not show that a revenue-operations team repeatedly lets Nex touch its CRM, email, and Slack data. The previous CRM reportedly had revenue and enterprise proofs of concept, but no customer, amount, retention figure, or current conversion is disclosed. A $100-per-seat price is an economic hypothesis until someone pays it. Nex has found an audience for its engineering, but it has not yet shown a narrow group pulling the current product back.

There is a coherent founder-originated wedge. Mohammad worked in HubSpot product management, Dias was HubSpot CRM’s fourth engineer, and the product attacks the fragmented systems they encountered directly. The ugly work is real: permissions, inaccurate agent output, custom integrations, and customer-specific context all have to be handled before a workflow can safely send a message or open a pull request. This is exactly where a real company might hide. It is also where a demo can look finished long before the product is trustworthy.

The strongest objection is that Nex may be good at shipping launches rather than discovering demand. It has moved from AI CRM to context infrastructure to “Claude Cowork for GTM” while Clay already has $100 million ARR and Gumloop and Relevance AI have raised $70 million and $37 million respectively. Those companies can reproduce natural-language workflow generation, and Nex’s earlier agency program depended on partners receiving 70% of revenue. If the context graph is not filled by repeated customer behavior, the supposed moat is merely an expensive integration layer beside better-funded competitors.

I would still invest, narrowly. The team passes my founder and technical gates, and its HubSpot history makes the schlep more credible than the fashionable label. My base case is that within 36 months Nex reaches $3–8 million ARR by narrowing from generic GTM automation to context-aware workflows for RevOps teams. The verifiable flip condition is usage: if by the end of YC the founders cannot show ten unaffiliated GTM teams running a Nex-generated production workflow in at least four of six consecutive weeks, I would change this to a pass.
