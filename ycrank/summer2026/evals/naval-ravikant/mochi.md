# Mochi -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Robert Qiu and Michael Ninh have earned attention through action. Qiu previously built a game reaching $200,000 in monthly revenue and lofi.town to 30,000 users; Ninh has operated consumer growth, managed hundreds of video creatives, and watched more than 400 anime series. More importantly, this team has already turned that knowledge into Mochi: 100,000 users, 300 million organic views, and 3,000 creators. These are not résumé claims. They are artifacts.

The strongest mechanism is permissionless production. More than 500 episodes are created daily, giving viewers new supply without Mochi employing a proportional studio workforce. This is real leverage, not a hypothetical network diagram. But volume alone does not prove compounding: the dossier supplies no creator retention, viewer cohorts, referrals, or evidence that an additional creator improves another participant’s economics. A content factory and a network can look identical from a distance.

If a competitor copies the code, too little clearly remains. AniShort already combines short vertical anime with AI-assisted creation, while YouArt couples production with creator monetization. Mochi has no demonstrated proprietary model, exclusive data, switching cost, or rights position. Its catalogue and community may become the asset, but “may become” is not a moat. Copyright exposure and reports of failed generations consuming roughly $5 for two or three scenes could also repel the very creators expected to build that asset.

The market is large enough to support a company but not yet broad enough to excuse an undifferentiated one. Anime streaming generated $5.8 billion in 2024, and microdramas are forecast to generate $14 billion in 2026. Yet Mochi has not shown the causal path from one-minute anime into a wider transaction layer. It has identified two large categories, not demonstrated room to maneuver between them.

The bull case stings. Three thousand creators and 500 daily episodes may be the early formation of the same kind of self-reinforcing behavior that justified overlooking Twitter’s unfinished business model. Mochi also acquired 300 million views organically in a category where incumbents can spend 90% of their budgets on marketing. The material difference is that Twitter’s users were the communication network; Mochi’s creators currently publish into a format that competitors can reproduce, and no cohort evidence yet shows the community stays when generation tools or economics improve elsewhere. The known exception therefore does not yet apply. I found no founder-integrity, artificial-deadline, or morale-dependence red flag; the problem is structural, not personal.

My 36-month base case is that Mochi pivots from an anime-viewing feed toward creator production and monetization and remains below $5 million ARR by September 2029. I would not write the check today. One result flips the decision: creator-made series must produce improving 90-day paid-viewer retention as creator density rises. That would establish that the participants—not merely cheap generative code—are the durable substrate.
