# Insurf -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: CMS is forcing prior authorization toward electronic workflows by January 2027, specialty practices already spend roughly 13 staff-hours on 40 weekly requests, and Insurf becomes a large company only if its system produces materially more collected revenue per case than staff operating existing payer channels. The dossier never measures that controlling variable. Selected pilots are mentioned, but there is no authorization rate, appeal recovery rate, staff-time reduction, payment yield, customer count, or threshold at which the economics compel adoption.

The institutional shift is real but not automatically advantageous to Insurf. Faster decision requirements and FHIR APIs should reduce integration friction, yet they also make this category easier for Waystar and other incumbent revenue-cycle systems to absorb. The cited denial-management market was only $1.32 billion globally in 2025. A larger outcome requires Insurf to expand from appeals into a reusable decision and payment-recovery channel; the dossier establishes neither that expansion nor a margin step change.

The product architecture is the strongest part of the case. Inveto spans intake, source-cited drafting, physician attestation, submission, follow-up, payer decisions, and posted payments. Versioned policies, submitted text, attestations, and payment evidence create workflow state that a better foundation model would improve rather than erase. This is meaningfully more durable than a letter-generation wrapper. However, PA Copilot currently depends on CoverMyMeds and athenaOne interfaces, while production PHI, BAAs, SOC 2, branch protection, and AWS compliance monitoring remain gated or incomplete. Insurf owns an emerging operational loop, not yet the underlying distribution or system of record.

The strongest bull case is that these constraints are ordinary pre-seed work: the founders have already assembled a clinically supervised workflow, selected specialty practices are piloting it, and CMS supplies a dated adoption catalyst. Chang Lu contributes medical-program and health-modeling context; Bryan Chung contributes applied mathematics, computer science, physics, and two IEEE publications. But this is not a known-exception team of scarce frontier researchers, nor does the dossier document prior startup learning, costly personal commitment, or an exceptional technical asset that overrides missing market evidence.

Competition makes the absent outcome data decisive. Silna reports 50,000 patients served, Anterior supports organizations covering 50 million lives, and Cohere processes more than 12 million annual requests. Insurf’s proposed payer-specific dataset is therefore not yet a moat: early cases are selected and non-random, public volume is undisclosed, and the cost or time required for a funded competitor to reproduce its policy and outcome records is unquantified. My base case is that by August 2029 Insurf has become a software-enabled denial-appeals service below $2 million ARR, rather than the decision infrastructure layer described today.

I pass at this stage. The single flip condition is a dated cohort of at least five specialty practices demonstrating that Insurf increases collected revenue per submitted case by at least 20% after fees over six months. That would validate the controlling economic variable, show product-market pull, and distinguish the workflow from automation that incumbents can bundle.
