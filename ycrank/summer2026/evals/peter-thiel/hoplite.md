# Hoplite -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Hoplite’s implied contested truth is that coding agents become substantially more useful when a company’s local sessions, dependencies, CLIs, and MCP configuration move intact into persistent cloud environments. The falsifier is simple: engineering teams given that continuity still prefer the integrated cloud agents already offered by Cursor or Factory. Hoplite has one encouraging experiment—its first business installation turned ignored Sentry errors into proactive pull requests—but the dossier does not show that peers reject the proposition. Their products are converging on it.

This makes the central problem competition, not technology. Local-to-cloud handoff, reusable environments, Slack initiation, persistent machines, and alert-triggered work already exist across heavily financed competitors. Hoplite adds iMessage, MCP migration, and hands-on configuration, but these are features rather than a demonstrated monopoly mechanism. Retained credentials and workflow history could eventually create switching costs; one installation cannot establish them. The narrow beachhead is also undefined: “businesses running multiple coding agents” is a customer description, not a small market Hoplite can plausibly dominate.

The founders are the strongest part of the company. Ryan Morrissey and Bence Redmond met in the same software-engineering program, built applications, freelanced, and founded previous products together. Morrissey dropped out, and their Stripe and AWS internships give them direct exposure to developer infrastructure. This is a real founding relationship rather than a convenience match. But the dossier does not establish current ownership, economic exposure, or whether every essential component is built in-house, so the foundation is promising rather than fully proved.

The bull case is that founder-led “Collison” installations reveal an operating thesis: manually learn how each engineering organization works, then automate the recurring configuration. Their previous CarbonCopy product reportedly reached $855,000 in trading volume, suggesting that this team can attract usage, and the Sentry installation shows more substance than a mere launch page. Yet that evidence belongs mostly to a prior product, while Hoplite has no disclosed revenue, accessible paid tier, retention data, or onboarding economics. Usage-based credits are inferred, and no arithmetic connects price, compute cost, or customer count to a power-law outcome.

No known-exception mechanism overrides the failed monopoly gate. There is neither an independently verified zero-to-one technical result nor persistent transaction demand strong enough to overcome commodity competition. The distribution route is at least concrete—founders install the system for businesses reached through YC and developer communities—but it remains labor-intensive and lacks a named buyer, sales cycle, conversion rate, or contract value. I would reverse this decision if Hoplite demonstrates 20 paying engineering teams retained for six months with automated onboarding and materially expanding agent usage. Until then, this is a good founding team inside somebody else’s market: founder, yes; monopoly, no. I pass.
