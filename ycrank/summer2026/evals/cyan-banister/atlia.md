# Atlia -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A rental owner should be able to hand a property to software that prices every night, screens guests, dispatches trusted vendors, verifies completed work, and resolves exceptions without maintaining a management organization. Atlia has one leak from that future: 80 live properties already carrying property-specific rules, calendars, vendor context, and pricing history. But the dossier does not map the intervening transitions—reliable inspection, autonomous exception handling, vendor-quality verification, and portable data rights. Those are not details. They are the bridge.

The present-day economics are clearer. Traditional managers reportedly collect 15–30% of booking revenue; Atlia charges 10%, includes photography, and passes repairs and supplies through at cost. That can return meaningful income to owners. It does not yet break a rule or remove a gatekeeper, however—it substitutes a less expensive manager. Evolve already charges 10–15%, while TIDY advertises overlapping AI-plus-human operations at 3.9%. The San Diego license cap and Fort Lauderdale’s $880 registration fee constrain owners, but Atlia accommodates those restrictions rather than restoring agency withheld by them.

Eighty live properties is the strongest evidence here. Owners are trusting Atlia with homes, guest relationships, maintenance decisions, and revenue-producing calendars; that is costly behavior through a functioning product, not a waitlist. The missing behavioral detail matters: there are no dated cohorts, retention figures, booking volume, service failures, or contribution margins. More sharply, YC calls the operation completely autonomous while Atlia says experienced operators supervise it. If two founders are quietly supporting 80 homes with high service quality, that is remarkable operating leverage. If local labor is doing most of the work, this is a discounted property manager wearing an AI label.

The founders have earned more than a generic-founder prior. Edmond Niu worked on voice and LLM agents and managed rentals for a year; Shaan Yadav designed GPU hardware, conducted BCI research, and managed rentals for 1.5 years. They are longtime friends and former roommates, which helps explain why they could begin operating together. What I cannot see is pre-company obsession expressed through dated tools, experiments, or recruited operators before this market became fashionable. I also cannot identify the ultimately accountable founder or a contractual tiebreaker. That is an unresolved diligence gate, not proof of bad governance.

The possible compounding asset is property-level operating memory across rules, vendors, calendars, pricing, and maintenance. Yet Atlia has disclosed no exclusive data rights, automation accuracy, or controlled technical bottleneck, while Arbio and Vacasa already combine software with local operations. Guest screening and persistent property memory also create consent and custody questions without a published limiting architecture. The known-exception logic does not rescue this: Atlia is not missing revenue while possessing an extraordinary technical artifact or lifelong founder obsession. Its challenge is proving that the service activity creates proprietary infrastructure rather than permanent labor.

My 36-month base case is 500–1,500 managed properties and $2–6 million in annual net revenue, with Atlia still operating as a human-supervised regional manager rather than a software-like enabling layer. The bull case stings: 80 properties this early may already prove that two technical operators have compressed a surprising amount of labor, and every turnover could improve vendor selection and exception handling. I remain Neutral because the dossier does not show that compression. I would flip to Invest on one verifiable cohort report showing the first 80 properties retained above 90% after twelve months while at least 70% of operational tasks are completed without human intervention.
