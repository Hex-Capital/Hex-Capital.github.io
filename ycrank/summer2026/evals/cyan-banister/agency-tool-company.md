# Agency Tool Company -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Jack Morrison and Davis Foster make Agency Tool Company a lived-experience infrastructure bet with an unusually weak tactile surface: eight years together at Scythe Robotics taught them the deployment pain, but ATC Deploy remains invisible plumbing behind somebody else’s robot. Agency Tool Company is strongest where biography becomes product specification and weakest where I want to touch the artifact myself. I’m never playing the game I seem to be playing: Agency Tool Company looks like developer tooling, but Morrison and Foster may actually be packaging the scar tissue required to operate physical autonomy.

**Anduril** is the high rung because Palmer Luckey carried years of hardware-building instinct into defense rather than discovering the category in a market memo. Agency Tool Company shares that mechanism: Morrison led Scythe from 2018 through its 2026 acquisition, while Foster’s engineering organization shipped hundreds of robots across more than 30 states. Agency Tool Company diverges because ATC Deploy is a tool Morrison and Foster wished they had, not yet another difficult machine they have personally placed in the world; three launch partners validate the memory, but Burro’s 750 machines remain Burro’s fleet, not evidence that ATC has reproduced Scythe-scale operations.

The consensus YC AI-agent darling fails my physical-world test because its value exists mainly in founder testimony and a browser abstraction. Agency Tool Company carries a milder version of that failure: a normal person cannot photograph byte-level delta transfer, resumable downloads, or target-hardware CI, even when those functions update a robot nearby. Agency Tool Company escapes the bottom rung because Burro, Tempo Works, and Gather AI are using Deploy on real agricultural, construction, and logistics machines, but the artifact I can touch belongs to the customer. This is closer to watching a mechanic’s torque wrench than riding an **Uber**.

Three launch partners can compound Agency Tool Company’s magically weirdness over the next 18 months if each intermittent connection and failed rollout becomes another recovery behavior that generic Balena cannot reproduce. Agency Tool Company begins moderately non-consensus—one like on its Open Robotics Discourse launch is almost comically quiet—but robotics deployment infrastructure is recognizable rather than aesthetically transgressive. Formant has raised $45.25 million and InOrbit already offers OTA configuration, so obscurity alone does not make Agency Tool Company underpriced; the magic must emerge from failure-handling knowledge accumulated across incompatible fleets.

Jack Morrison is explicitly named founder and CEO, giving Agency Tool Company a visible tiebreaker after Morrison and Foster worked together for eight years. Agency Tool Company would move into my highest governance tier if Morrison held documented operating authority with founder vesting and an exit-conflict protocol; without that evidence, the long Scythe history is reassuring but not dispositive. **HQ Trivia** taught me that affection and history do not answer who wins the unresolvable argument. Two repeat co-founders can be a beautiful marriage, but I still want to see the prenup.

**SpaceX** is the clean parallel for accepting technology risk attached to the physical world, but Agency Tool Company sits one layer away from that frontier. Agency Tool Company handles operating-system images, models, activation, and rollback on machines capable of causing physical damage, so reliability becomes a serious technical moat rather than ordinary SaaS uptime. Yet Deploy is software, Build is still “coming soon,” and no patent or structural barrier is disclosed. The swing becomes more interesting if Agency Tool Company’s hosted hardware runners evolve into certification infrastructure for safety-critical robot releases; today it is a pickaxe beside the rocket, not the rocket.

Burro’s fleet of more than 750 machines gives me a falsifiable 36-month forecast: Agency Tool Company reaches $3 million to $7 million ARR across at least 20 production robot fleets and is then acquired by Formant for under $100 million to make deployment its software-delivery layer. Agency Tool Company gets there because Morrison and Foster can translate eight years of operator trust into integrations, while deployment histories and approval chains make removal progressively dangerous. This is an infrastructure outcome, not yet a defining independent platform outcome.

InOrbit’s existing remote-update capability is the bear mechanism that stings: Agency Tool Company could be a polished internal Scythe tool searching for a market while InOrbit, Formant, Balena, and customer platform engineers absorb delta transfer and rollback as features. Agency Tool Company has disclosed no revenue, only three concentrated launch partners, and no reliability statistics despite touching operating-system images on physical fleets. The known-exception escape hatch is **DeepMind**—Foster’s dropout-to-Thiel-Fellow trajectory and both founders’ cross-domain operating experience could outweigh the product’s abstraction—but DeepMind possessed unmistakable technical discontinuity, while Agency Tool Company currently claims 20-times-faster transfers in a substitutable category. I would flip to a pass if none of Burro, Tempo Works, or Gather AI converts to paid production usage within 12 months; today the founders’ lived scar tissue wins by a rebuttal, not a hard no.
