# Gradon -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Gradon’s interesting possibility is making scarce engineering capacity abundant. Its homepage advertises doubled revenue per engineer. That would matter to firms constrained by technical staff. But the $12.04 billion AEC software market is a spending category, not a calculation of the engineering bottleneck Gradon could remove. The dossier establishes a useful productivity consequence; it does not quantify an orders-of-magnitude expansion in access to engineering. I would pass on this evidence. The upside needs a stronger foundation than the size of construction.

The strongest argument against my pass is concrete: Gradon reports that a 50-person civil firm completed a project in 180 hours against a 750-hour manual estimate. That is approximately 4.2x productivity, with 570 hours potentially released for other work. At pre-seed, this deserves attention. Execution inside Civil 3D, Revit, ETABS and Bluebeam could also eliminate handoffs that a narrow drafting tool leaves untouched. But an unnamed customer’s estimated counterfactual does not establish repeatable technical superiority. The dossier supplies neither a benchmark method nor equivalent-quality comparisons against SWAPP or Augmenta. SWAPP already advertises overlapping execution and firm-standard validation. Cross-discipline breadth becomes valuable only if correctness survives it.

The largest kill risk is whether the saved production hours remain saved after licensed review, corrections and rework. Gradon’s terms classify outputs as preliminary and require professional approval. That is a sensible responsibility boundary, but it does not measure the remaining labor. I want capital spent on resolving that uncertainty before expanding the workflow surface. The dossier gives no costed experiment, acceptance threshold or stop condition, so milestone discipline receives its missing-evidence default. This is an absence of process evidence, not evidence that the founders are spending recklessly.

Zahzah’s mechanical-design experience and Helal’s engineering and software background make this a credible team to investigate the problem. Their transition from Vendo to Gradon demonstrates a change of product, but the record does not show what evidence changed their beliefs. Nor does it document recruiting against a specific weakness. I therefore cannot award the learning-and-recruiting criterion credit for credentials alone. The unstated pattern that attracts me is software taking over expensive expert work. The corresponding red flag to test is whether implementation labor carries the apparent automation. On-site onboarding creates that question; it does not prove the answer. Firm Brain establishes customer-specific configuration, while the contractual prohibition on training with customer content means I cannot assume a shared proprietary learning loop.

Named seats with unlimited usage could let customers capture substantial savings, but public pricing, inference costs and implementation labor are undisclosed. Economic parity remains unmeasured. My known exceptions permit an early check without polished milestones when a documented instigator and an extraordinary consequence justify it; Gradon’s record does not yet establish that override. **My flip condition is one independently reviewed, paid deployment across three unrelated engineering firms showing at least 5x lower total engineering hours on comparable, accepted deliverables—including review and rework—with positive contribution margin after inference and implementation labor.** That would connect technical leverage to repeatable economics. Today, the reported result is promising enough to investigate and insufficient for my check.
