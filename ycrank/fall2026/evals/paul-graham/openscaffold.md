# OpenScaffold -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most interesting fact about OpenScaffold is the change from “Lakehouse-as-a-Service” to deployment agents. The October 26 directory update establishes a change in positioning. It doesn't establish what the founders learned. Did a customer refuse the lakehouse and ask them to finish an implementation instead? That could be the beginning of a good company. But without the observation that caused the change, I can't count the rename as evidence of resourcefulness or learning.

Rushil Knagaram and Param Mehta have worked together at USUL. Mehta was a founding engineer there and previously led an AI platform for transaction-error handling at JPMorgan. Those are relevant experiences. They make the strongest case against passing: this team may already understand the gap between software that works in a demonstration and software that works inside a customer's business. But the dossier supplies no particular deployment obstacle, founder-built workaround, or elapsed time. My largest criterion therefore stays at its missing-evidence default. The same applies to problem origin: relevant employment doesn't establish that OpenScaffold grew from a problem these founders personally encountered.

The product description contains a potentially useful insight. Each deployment has different requirements, so generating its own agent scaffold might work better than forcing every customer through one fixed workflow. The difficulty is getting from discovery notes to a correct implementation. OpenScaffold describes that capability, but the dossier contains no inspectable deployment or measured technical result. Mehta's prior work supports his ability to attempt it; it doesn't verify this product. My pattern of backing programmable infrastructure would become relevant if developers were already building on the scaffold. Neither that usage nor an accumulated deployment substrate is documented here.

Who wants this badly enough to use a crude version? “Deployment teams” still leaves that question open. There is no identified customer niche with repeat usage, payment, or even a named commitment. Zero job postings and a LinkedIn page tell me nothing about demand. Auctor, Tandem, and June establish that other companies are pursuing related workflows; their funding cannot establish OpenScaffold's pull. The opportunity could lie in the ugly integration work those products leave unfinished. That is a hypothesis worth testing personally with customers.

I would pass on the present evidence. This isn't an active founder red flag: the dossier doesn't show dependence on a large partner, premature delegation, or refusal to listen. It also doesn't supply the working prototype, unmistakable user pull, or validated technical proof needed for a known-exception override. All five dimensions remain at their specified defaults, which means the founder-action gate is unmet. One verifiable condition would reopen my decision: a customer-confirmed deployment history showing a specific blocker, the founders' shipped workaround within days or weeks, and that customer returning for another deployment. That would turn the product description into evidence about the people building it.
