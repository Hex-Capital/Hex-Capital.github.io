# Callbook AI -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have already converted specific knowledge into working systems. Diego Avellaneda brings six years of embedded-ML work, peer-reviewed TinyML research, and a production voicemail detector. Daniel Martinez previously built a conversational B2B product to more than $8 million in ARR. Their nine-year working relationship matters more than résumé polish: these are repeat collaborators who have shipped, sold, and operated inside the technical and commercial problem.

The product evidence is unusually substantial for pre-seed. Callbook reports 100,000 borrower negotiations each week, 23 million interactions, two million borrowers managed, and more than $11 million recovered for customers. It also claims to have won every head-to-head pilot. The regional voicemail model—trained on hundreds of Latin American recordings, exceeding 96% held-out accuracy and reducing related processing expenditure by 60%—shows that the team can turn narrow operational data into deployed leverage.

But deployment is not yet defensibility. Salient, InDebted, and Skit.ai already offer overlapping negotiation, compliance, and portfolio-decisioning systems with far more capital. Voice, messaging, CRM, and language-model components are broadly available. Callbook’s corpus may improve segmentation and channel selection, but the dossier does not establish exclusive data rights or a learning curve connecting additional interactions to better recovery outcomes. If a competitor copied the code tomorrow, integrations and regional data would slow it down; they would not clearly stop it.

This also fails the permissionless-network test. Each bank or insurer arrives through relationship-led enterprise sales, and one creditor does not make the product directly more valuable or easier to distribute to another. Callbook digitizes fragmented collections labor, but it does not turn that supply into a participant-created network. Its TinyML work fits my recent preference for infrastructure-like bottlenecks better than a thin AI wrapper, yet the durable substrate remains incomplete. None of the founder red flags—unfair dealing, artificial urgency, reassurance dependence, or indiscriminate pivots—appears in the dossier, although public research cannot prove integrity. The known-exception logic is unnecessary here: Callbook is not asking me to overlook missing product adoption, only to decide whether strong usage will become a compounding asset.

The market has room to maneuver. A system that can negotiate across voice, WhatsApp, SMS, and email from pre-delinquency through 90-day obligations can sell to banks, fintechs, BNPL providers, retailers, insurers, and cooperatives inside a collection-software market projected to grow from $5.98 billion in 2025 to $13.77 billion by 2034. The weaker evidence is value capture: named customer logos and $11 million recovered prove customer utility, but no contract value, revenue, take rate, or unit economics show what Callbook itself retains. Aggregate interaction totals may also include its earlier general calling and Zoho product.

My base case is that Callbook reaches $8 million to $20 million in ARR within 36 months as a sales-led collections platform, but remains a competitive workflow company rather than a self-reinforcing network. I would write a small option check because the founders and deployment evidence are exceptional for the stage; I would not size it as a defining position. The single condition that would materially raise conviction is an independently verified longitudinal customer cohort showing that recovery performance improves as Callbook’s proprietary interaction corpus grows under durable data-use rights.
