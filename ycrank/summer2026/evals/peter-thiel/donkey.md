# Donkey -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Donkey’s contested truth is precise: public shipment records can reveal the factories hidden by trading intermediaries, allowing one integrated counterparty to deliver the same goods materially cheaper. The proposition is falsifiable. If identified factories cannot repeatedly fulfill inspected orders at a lower landed cost after duties, financing, and claims, the secret is false. A $110,000 first-week quote book and one quote 15% below an incumbent supplier for goods from the same factory are meaningful experiments, but unsigned pipeline is not completed proof. The outdated tariff example also matters because landed-cost accuracy is the product, not an administrative detail.

The founders are the strongest part of the company. Martindale and Tan are high-school best friends rather than founders assembled for YC. They previously built Prelude together, acquired 31 paying Chinese factories in 39 days, and reached cash-flow positivity. Martindale speaks Mandarin and has sold into Chinese manufacturing; Tan is building the customs-record classification system in-house. This is unusual founder-market alignment. The dossier does not establish that every core founder is full-time, what the seven-person team owns, or whether anyone has taken secondary liquidity, so economic alignment remains unproved rather than adverse.

The problem is value capture. Independent wholesale distributors form a plausible beachhead, but Donkey does not yet exhibit a monopoly mechanism inside it. Inspectors across seven provinces constitute operating coverage, not proprietary technology. Supplier pricing and reliability data could compound after settled orders, but today that is a future moat inferred from transactions that have barely begun. Sourcify, Flexport, Inspectorio, and Zetwerk attack adjacent portions of the workflow. Taking title, extending credit, and assuming delivery risk make Donkey harder to imitate, but working-capital intensity is not itself defensibility; sometimes it merely makes a commodity business more dangerous.

The strongest bull case is that the apparent operational burden is the opportunity. Factory identification, inspection, customs, credit, and dock delivery may form a system that fragmented incumbents cannot coordinate, while every completed order improves supplier intelligence. It also fits the less obvious pattern of making an adversarial information system legible: buyers cannot presently see who manufactured their goods or what the intermediary captured. But the exception for coordination-heavy companies requires a deployed unit or persistent customer behavior strong enough to override the failure stack. Donkey has quotes and prospective purchase orders, not one documented completed order. Five synchronized risks—classification, inspection, freight, customs, and credit—therefore remain assumptions rather than one proven machine.

Distribution is definite but unvalidated. Martindale drove from Vermont to San Francisco to visit independent distributors, and free supplier checks create a concrete founder-led entry point. Yet there is no conversion rate, sales-cycle evidence, acquisition cost, repeat-order behavior, or contribution margin. The embedded-margin model could become large because each importer brings recurring purchase volume, but net-45 terms and title-taking can consume capital faster than accounting revenue grows. My 36-month base case is that Donkey processes $30–60 million in annual merchandise volume but produces less than $8 million in net revenue, remaining a capable trading company rather than becoming a durable technology monopoly.

I am neutral. This is neither a consensus AI wrapper nor an obviously broken team; it is a strong founding pair pursuing a real human secret without yet showing that the secret creates proprietary economics. One result would flip the decision: a documented cohort of 50 importers each completing a second order sourced through Donkey’s proprietary factory matching, with at least 10% contribution margin after freight, financing, inspection, and claims. That would convert quotes into repeat behavior, operations into data advantage, and a trading company into a possible last mover.
