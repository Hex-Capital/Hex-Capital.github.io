# Torus -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Marcus Lima has built a capital-intensive energy company after studying energy systems; Rahul Thayil previously built and financed an enterprise AI risk product. That is action, not résumé decoration. Torus is also serving multiple Fortune 500 companies and major EPCs on data-center and energy projects. The evidence does not prove that either founder previously solved cross-document engineering consistency, but it does show that they understand both sides of the problem: physical infrastructure and enterprise AI deployment. I give the founders substantial credit without pretending adjacency is identity.

The product attacks an expensive coordination failure. Extracting parameters across thousands of drawings, specifications, and datasheets, checking more than 50 inconsistency categories, and drafting traceable RFIs turns a potential $500K rework order into a direct value proposition. The initial wedge can expand from document checking into approvals, transmittals, project controls, and eventually engineering-package generation. The $10.1–$11.3 billion AEC software market supports room to maneuver, although the dossier has not yet demonstrated that Torus owns those adjacent workflows.

The weakness is leverage. Torus currently looks like capable enterprise software sold through founder-led pilots, not a permissionless system in which users create value for one another. No disclosed corpus, proprietary model, retention evidence, parameter graph, or deployment benchmark survives the counterfactual where Trunk Tools, Transcend, Augmenta, or another well-financed competitor copies the workflow. Code can reproduce the interface. The investment depends on whether deployments create an asset competitors cannot reproduce.

The strongest bull case is that the asset is forming before it is publicly measurable. Fortune 500 and major-EPC deployments expose Torus to difficult, customer-specific engineering relationships; sovereign deployments and human approval gates address real security and accuracy constraints; and accumulated parameter maps could eventually improve every subsequent project. But “could compound” is not the same as compounding. Torus has disclosed neither paid contract values nor evidence that one customer’s deployment improves the next customer’s product.

There are no disclosed integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-morale red flags. The deeper contradiction is structural: the company resembles the enterprise-agent pattern in which every new account demands another sale, integration, and validation cycle. Within 36 months, I expect Torus either to reach roughly $4–8 million ARR concentrated in data-center and energy-infrastructure document review, or to be absorbed into a broader project-controls platform; I do not yet expect an independent network advantage.

I would pass today, narrowly. Strong founders can build a valuable business without creating a venture-scale compounding system, and customer logos without prices do not prove value capture. My verifiable flip condition is ten paid production deployments showing that a shared proprietary parameter graph measurably improves inconsistency-detection accuracy or deployment time across customers. That would convert implementation into an accumulating asset.
