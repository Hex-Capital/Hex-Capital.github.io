# 83 Sciences -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Eric Riesel developed materials-data ML during his MIT chemistry PhD, while 83 Sciences names three founders and no CEO. That is 83 Sciences’s signature: unusually authentic contact with the scientific problem, paired with dangerously ambiguous authority over what gets built. Ian Naccarella brings chemical engineering and Sila experience; Yankang Yang deployed AI to 30,000 users. Biz and Tiz are present. The missing captain matters because 83 Sciences is attempting to negotiate laboratory access, IP ownership, research priorities, and commercialization simultaneously.

Anduril is the high rung for a founder whose biography made the problem inevitable: Palmer Luckey built physical systems before defense became a fashionable category. 83 Sciences shares part of that mechanism because Riesel built ML systems for materials analysis before 83 Sciences existed, and Naccarella worked inside Sila Nanotechnologies after studying chemical engineering. The divergence is load-bearing: nobody at 83 Sciences has publicly shipped a comparable scientific product to scale, and Naccarella’s HBS-BCG-Sila-strategy path leaves more market-analysis residue than Luckey’s garage-built artifacts. 83 Sciences is closer to lived experience than credential cosplay, but not yet *of the place* in the Anduril sense.

HQ Trivia is the low rung on founder authority because intoxicating product pull temporarily concealed a partnership without a durable tiebreaker. 83 Sciences has three people uniformly titled “Founder,” two with a shared BCG history, and no named CEO, equity structure, or conflict protocol. That pattern appears in 83 Sciences before any HQ-scale exception appears: no viral artifact, repeat usage, or independently verified discovery can overpower the governance risk. Three-way ambiguity is not a rebuttal; until someone can say who decides when publication goals conflict with patent ownership, it is a hard structural problem.

Citrine’s 550 deployed models and 70,000 monthly experiment suggestions show how the magically weird mechanism could compound—or disappear—over the next 18 months. Every failed experiment captured by 83 Sciences could make the next recommendation better, deepen laboratory switching costs, and turn scientific trash into proprietary treasure. But 83 Sciences discloses no dataset size, signed laboratory, or investor who passed because the idea looked ugly; meanwhile Citrine, MaterialsZone, and Kebotix already occupy recognizable versions of the thesis. Grants of up to $50,000 may purchase access, but purchased access is not yet the persistent, rejection-driven sourcing pattern that found Niantic or Flock Safety.

The $50,000 early-partner grants put 83 Sciences near hard technology without making 83 Sciences a frontier-hardware bet. The score flips upward if those partnerships produce a closed experimental loop—instrument integration, model-proposed conditions, physical synthesis, and independently validated material performance—where capital and scientific trust compound into a moat. Today 83 Sciences captures voice notes and instrument output through a software agent, while partners appear to own the laboratories. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing, but here the game still looks more like materials-informatics software than SpaceX, Crusoe, or Anduril.

Niantic could be played before its market could be modeled; I could feel Ingress in my hand. 83 Sciences offers a weaker but real physical bridge: instrument outputs enter the system, experiments happen in laboratories, and the promised result is a manuscript, patent, or material process within two months. Yet 83 Sciences provides no public prototype, photographable discovery, benchmark, or partner outcome. A scientist may eventually touch what 83 Sciences helps discover, but an investor currently has to accept the agent’s value through founder testimony.

83 Sciences claims it can convert discarded laboratory data into a co-authored manuscript in under two months. By July 2029, I expect 83 Sciences to have pivoted from generalized “AI-native materials discovery” into enterprise experiment-record infrastructure at roughly $2 million–$5 million ARR, with publications serving as customer acquisition rather than the product. The founders’ domain contact should win pilots; the lack of a clear captain and the IP-allocation burden will push 83 Sciences toward the narrower workflow that laboratories can actually purchase.

Eric Riesel’s materials-ML research, Naccarella’s Sila experience, Yang’s 30,000-user deployment history, and a materials-informatics market growing at 19.2% form the strongest bull case against that constrained forecast. The DeepMind exception stings: elite credentials can hide genuine polymath compounding, and 83 Sciences may turn proprietary failed experiments into a discovery flywheel that Citrine cannot copy. But the other exceptions fail—83 Sciences has neither HQ Trivia’s tactile pull nor HandUp’s biography-level mission override—and the red flags remain: Stanford, Harvard, HBS, BCG, three founders, no CEO, no disclosed partner, and no validated discovery. I stay Neutral, but I would flip to Invest upon one verifiable condition: 83 Sciences publicly names a single CEO with documented final operating authority.
