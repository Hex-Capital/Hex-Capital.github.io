# Waybill -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the attractive version of Waybill is obvious: a hardware engineer submits a BOM, then software and operators quietly resolve suppliers, payment, authenticity, customs, freight, and inventory. The dossier does not yet map how that world arrives. Gartner’s forecast of $53 billion in agentic supply-chain software spending by 2030 describes a market wave, not the required transitions or Waybill’s control point. The best leak from the future is stranger and more concrete: within two weeks, users asked it to find a lifetime supply of an obsolete RF component, 20 tons of aerospace-grade titanium, and four Cessna aircraft.

Those requests make me curious, but a request is not a procurement event. No customer is shown paying, accepting delivery, exposing a production schedule, or repeatedly entrusting Waybill with a BOM. The pre-revenue exception does not rescue this by itself: that requires a functioning artifact, signed proof of use, and unusually strong evidence that the team can cross the remaining technical distance. Here, the costly behavior has started at the inquiry stage, while fulfillment and repeat usage remain unproven.

The founders are the strongest part of the case. Rishi Laddha handled aerospace procurement, cross-border operations, supply chain, and government relations at LAT Aerospace. Tushar Goyal built inventory and demand-forecasting systems used by more than 400 Indian D2C brands. That is complementary operating tuition in exactly the hidden machinery Waybill is assuming. They are not claiming that two engineers can rebuild procurement over a weekend; their product specification acknowledges brokers, authenticity checks, licensed customs agents, fallback suppliers, duties, invoice matching, and last-mile delivery. I see relevant biography and one accountable CEO, but not yet the dated pre-company experiments, independent artifacts, or recruiting proof that would establish obsession rather than excellent résumé fit.

The broken system is real but insufficiently decoded. Catalogs, resellers, email, WhatsApp, and spreadsheets fracture purchasing context and produce duplicate orders or undocumented delays. Waybill returns coordination to the hardware team through one bundled payment and one responsible operator. But who captures the existing rent? How much engineering time, working capital, brokerage margin, or customs delay disappears? Without those numbers, I cannot tell whether this becomes infrastructure or an operations-heavy intermediary whose margin is consumed whenever a shipment goes wrong.

The strongest bull case is that those bizarre inbound requests are the consumer beachhead in disguise: begin by sourcing impossible objects, accumulate supplier reliability, landed-cost, substitution, and customs-resolution data, then become the procurement memory for Indian deep-tech manufacturing. That pattern fits my attraction to unglamorous workflow repair beneath a more visible hardware wave. The bear case stings more today. Cofactr, Forge, Jiga, and Luminovo already occupy adjacent parts of the stack; Waybill has disclosed neither transaction scale nor a measured sourcing advantage, and its supposed data moat does not exist until completed orders compound. Brokerage, counterfeit exposure, customs mistakes, and international freight also make every new category a fresh operating surface rather than free software expansion.

I am neutral at this evidence level. My 36-month base case is that Waybill narrows into managed cross-border procurement for Indian aerospace and deep-tech teams and reaches roughly $1 million–$3 million in annualized revenue, rather than becoming a broad hardware procurement platform. One result flips me: show a cohort of at least ten hardware teams completing three or more purchases each, with landed cost or delivery time improving measurably as Waybill’s supplier history grows. That would prove both the weird behavior and the controlled enabling asset.
