# Rise Reforming -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when something broke. Rise Reforming changed its feedstock from plastic waste to biogas in 2025, but the dossier does not identify the observation that killed the first route, the workaround the founders shipped, or how quickly it changed the result. The next named obstacle is more concrete: real wastewater gas contains hydrogen sulfide, moisture, siloxanes, and other catalyst poisons. Yet the live-biogas pilot will not operate until 2027. This may be a resourceful team, but the evidence does not yet show resourcefulness under that constraint.

The strongest evidence is the machine. Lucas Zubillaga led construction and operation of a bench-scale reformer that reportedly sustained more than 1,800 hours of syngas production. Three young molecular engineers began building during finals week in 2023 and produced a containerized pilot before obtaining permission from the market. That is real fierce-nerd behavior. But Rise has proved stable syngas production, not continuous conversion of dirty wastewater biogas into saleable DME or methanol. The difference is the technical risk presently governing the company.

There is a plausible narrow well. Small wastewater plants have stranded gas, and Rise has obtained one binding supply agreement, several producer MOUs, and a conditional DME offtake agreement. These agreements are more informative than a waitlist, but they do not yet show a user pulling the crude product back: the host is not publicly named, no chemical volumes have been delivered, and no revenue has been reported. The business also requires Rise to finance equipment, maintain it, secure feedstock, and sell commodity chemicals. This is genuine schlep blindness, but it creates several simultaneous customer dependencies rather than proving demand.

The plastic-to-biogas pivot could be evidence that the founders are running backs rather than prisoners of their first idea. It could also be a change toward a more fundable story. The dossier supplies no falsifying observation, decision interval, or current-product result, so I cannot distinguish the two. Their prior work at Argonne, Avangrid, Mantel Capture, Nexamp, and an electrochemistry lab is adjacent to the problem, but there is no documented founder-originated need comparable to actually operating a wastewater plant or suffering its gas economics.

The bull case is the known exception represented by Bindwell: very young technical founders can overcome missing commercial history with a measured artifact and direct exposure to a difficult industrial problem. Rise shares the youth, numerical proof, and willingness to build hardware. The material difference is that Bindwell had laboratory validation on the actual biological problem, while Rise’s decisive live-feedstock experiment remains ahead of it. M2X has already completed multiple field deployments and raised $40 million, so stable operation on cleaner inputs cannot substitute for crossing that boundary.

I am not investing yet. The technical proof is unusually good for pre-seed, and none of the usual theatrical red flags—press as distribution, premature executive delegation, or an unnamed giant partner—dominates the dossier. But the operational gate fails: the founders have not yet shown what they do when the actual feedstock fights back, and contracts have not become product use. One verifiable event would flip me: 500 continuous hours processing live wastewater biogas into specification-grade DME or methanol, with the founders’ contaminant workaround documented and the output accepted by a contracted buyer.
