# SpaceFlow Technologies, Inc. -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: improving agent capabilities now let enterprise procurement teams automate work across ERPs, email, and supplier documents, while the economic variable determining SpaceFlow’s scale is whether each customized deployment reaches reliable production faster than implementation costs accumulate. The dossier supports that variable but does not yet quantify the threshold. SpaceFlow claims first-month shadow deployment and reports real workflow gains, yet provides no deployment-cost, gross-margin, or customer-by-customer implementation data. This is the one unresolved variable around which the company turns.

The product-market pull is unusually strong for pre-seed. SpaceFlow has six-figure contracted annual revenue, hundreds of daily production users, and a national restaurant customer whose supply-chain executive invested personally. Reported outcomes—4x faster RFQ cycles, 3x more RFQs processed, 42% less maverick spending, and $120,000 in annual savings—describe operational behavior rather than registrations or pilots. The $400 million in supplier spend is throughput, not revenue, and the results remain company-reported, but paid production use makes this more than a polished agent demo.

The architecture also owns meaningful workflow state. SpaceFlow maps customized systems, passes through customer identities, enforces approval thresholds, records corrections as evaluations, and maintains signed audit logs inside the customer’s network. Better models should increase task completion while SpaceFlow retains permissions, integrations, business rules, and responsibility for execution. That is durable relative to a prompt wrapper. It is not yet a structural moat: customer-specific evaluations may remain trapped within each deployment, and SAP, Zip, ORO, and Levelpath can bundle overlapping agents through existing distribution.

The technology-shift case is credible but less proven than the demand signal. MCP and model-agnostic endpoints reduce the cost of connecting models to enterprise tools, while the reported cycle-time and savings figures suggest a genuine productivity step change. But MCP is shared infrastructure, not proprietary advantage. The bear case is that customized SAP tables, air-gapped environments, and decades of approval logic make every rollout a bespoke integration project. In that world, revenue grows only with services headcount, SAP absorbs the agent layer, and SpaceFlow becomes a capable procurement consultancy rather than a large software company.

The founders improve the odds of navigating that complexity. Hakan Enes Aksu previously generated $40,000 from an automation service and sold a tender-matching engine for five figures; the team also built a procurement engine with a medical manufacturer before YC. Emre Isik and Ali Eren Aytekin bring machine-learning research, while two founders left university paths to build. This establishes relevant commercial learning and commitment, although the dossier does not document the deeper personal desperation or substantial prior-company learning that would earn full credit.

I would invest, with deployment repeatability as the governing diligence item. My 36-month base case is $8–15 million ARR from procurement-agent deployments across several enterprise accounts, provided the context layer becomes reusable rather than repeatedly rebuilt. The verifiable flip condition is clear: if the next three unaffiliated customers cannot reach paid production within 45 days using fewer than 20 person-days of custom integration each, I would reverse to a pass because the apparent software economics would actually be services economics.
