# Telamont -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Telamont’s potential secret is that construction losses can be prevented by continuously reconciling revisions before conflicting requirements become contractual facts. What would prove that wrong? A prospective comparison showing that existing workflows catch the same consequential discrepancies with comparable reviewer effort. The dossier documents a working product, but neither a rejected industry belief nor that comparative result. Continuous monitoring is a concrete product proposition; its status as a secret remains unproved.

The decisive problem is ownership of the opportunity. Telamont advertises revision comparison and technical/code checks beyond Document Crunch’s documented drawing limitations. Those differences matter. But Document Crunch already offers project-level risk detection, action, and tracking; Trunk Tools offers proactive document agents. Trimble now owns Document Crunch and can place overlapping intelligence inside existing project-management and ERP workflows. Telamont has construction-specific playbooks, but no demonstrated exclusive data, technical discontinuity, or bounded customer segment it can dominate. Its competitors need to close a feature gap; Telamont needs to establish a monopoly mechanism.

The strongest objection to passing is that customers may already be discovering something the competitive comparison misses. Canmec, TCI+, and other named logos provide more substance than a generic construction-AI pitch. Company-reported savings of $1.4 million in avoided rework and $2–3 million recovered on subcontract packages suggest potentially enormous willingness to pay. Continuous revision monitoring might become the authoritative record linking an obligation to the notice that preserves a claim. That would be a valuable position. However, the outcomes concern unnamed clients, and “billions of dollars” in covered projects measures underlying construction value. Neither establishes repeat purchases, exclusive workflow control, or Telamont’s revenue. My known exceptions depend on demonstrated technical discontinuity, persistent transaction demand, or a concrete deployment path that resolves the relevant risk. This evidence does not yet resolve competitive ownership.

Goulet’s construction exposure and infrastructure engineering, combined with Gagnon’s industrial AI experience at Poka, make the product credible. This resembles my unstated preference for operator-led infrastructure companies more than a team choosing a fashionable market from outside it. But professional relevance does not establish founding alignment. Shared attendance at Université Laval proves neither a long working relationship nor current economic exposure. Full-time status, ownership, and prior joint execution remain unspecified. I find no documented part-time-founder, outsourced-engineering, or capitalization red flag; I retain the default founding-structure score.

Distribution is Telamont’s strongest relative evidence. An Autodesk marketplace listing, demo requests, and the October 2026 CONSTRUCT exhibition identify actual routes into construction organizations. They earn modest affirmative credit, although sales cycles, conversion, implementation effort, and contract values remain unknown. Subscription terms also identify one coherent revenue engine. Without pricing or margins, however, the reported customer savings cannot support arithmetic for a multibillion-dollar outcome. The broader $1.83 billion risk-software estimate does not supply that arithmetic either.

I pass on this check. Telamont could build a useful business while integrated competitors retain the customer relationship and much of the economic surplus. One verifiable flip condition would change my judgment: a prospective, independently audited deployment study in a defined contractor segment showing that Telamont uniquely detects material revision risks missed by the incumbent workflow, followed by paid renewals attributable to that advantage. That would connect technical difference to durable customer preference—the missing bridge between useful software and an investable monopoly.
