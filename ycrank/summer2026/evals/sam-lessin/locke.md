# Locke -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Parth Badhwar’s White House, campaign, Deloitte policy, and Clipbook GTM history makes Locke a service business with unusually credible delivery DNA—and no evidence that its YC round is unusually cheap. That pairing is Locke’s signature: strongest as a potentially bootstrappable, Wizard-of-Oz government-affairs operation; weakest as a value-seed purchase, because Locke supplies neither price nor cap-table terms that establish mispricing. “AI-native government affairs” sounds venture-shaped, but the underlying asset may be a relationship-driven policy firm that should compound from retainers rather than successive SAFEs.

**Marina Mogilko / LinguaTrip** is the high rung on capital structure because profitable operations, owned distribution, and a $1.7M earnings-and-IP instrument made the asset financeable without pretending it was SaaS. Locke shares the load-bearing mechanism: human lobbyists perform the hard work while AI handles monitoring, briefs, stakeholder maps, and proposals, creating a credible service-first tack before automation. Locke diverges because no revenue, pricing, gross margin, or retainer count is disclosed, while two openings paying up to $200,000 plus as much as 3.00% equity could convert Locke from default-alive boutique into founder-drainage before the service economics are proven.

**Pinterest** is the low rung on price discipline: it was already a Silicon Valley darling, and only Ben Silbermann’s missionary temperament plus personal-network access rescued an otherwise consensus entry. Locke has no equivalent override. Locke discloses neither a discounted valuation nor a tier-one pass, and “AI-native” plus YC S26 is precisely the language likely to attract a hot-deck premium; the full-service lobbying angle makes Locke less legible than Quorum or FiscalNote, but illegibility is not mispricing until I see the price. I apply the 9/30 absent-evidence default rather than manufacture value-seed alpha from missing terms.

Parth Badhwar has worked across the White House, the Harris campaign, Deloitte policy, and Clipbook, so Locke’s founder conviction can compound over the next 18 months through trusted introductions, sharper policy judgment, and repeated exposure to customer workflows. Locke’s problem is that domain continuity is not yet asymmetric conviction: Badhwar has roughly 4,000 LinkedIn followers, but the dossier identifies no category essays, durable contrarian claim, or three-year public campaign for this operating model. Locke can become missionary if Badhwar turns those relationships into a written thesis and proprietary workflow; otherwise founder-led sales simply compounds a consultancy.

Parth Badhwar’s approximately 4,000 LinkedIn followers put Locke above a founder with no public surface area but well below an owned-community asset. Locke would flip this dimension into invest territory if Badhwar’s audience produced measurable inbound—say, 10,000 engaged policy operators, a newsletter with repeat consultation conversion, or a pre-existing community that reliably sources customers. Today Locke directs prospects to schedule consultations and reports only a growing pipeline, so the founder’s reach is professional credibility rather than distribution sitting on Locke’s cap table.

**Fin** is the relevant single-anchor parallel for category fit: start with a manual service, observe the work, and automate only what reality permits. Locke’s human lobbyists plus AI agents follow that Wizard-of-Oz pattern, and government affairs is unsexy enough that a service-with-software business could escape the generic SaaS factory. Locke still carries the exact red flag embedded in “AI-native”: Quorum already serves more than 2,000 organizations, FiscalNote has $84.1M ARR, and Polintel and Sporos also advertise agentic analysis. Locke fits my service sleeve only if the humans generate proprietary outcome data; otherwise Locke is another AI wrapper sitting atop public policy inputs.

Locke’s “first few partners,” undisclosed revenue, and two six-figure hiring plans lead to a falsifiable forecast: by July 2029, Locke will be a 15-to-30-person technology-policy and lobbying firm producing $3M-$7M in annual revenue without an institutional Series A, rather than a software platform competing directly with Quorum. Locke gets there through relationship-heavy retainers and proposal work, while AI raises staff leverage but does not become the moat. The regatta has a viable services tack; I do not yet see a venture-scale starting line.

Quorum’s more than 2,000 customers and Locke’s founder-market fit create the strongest opposing case: Quorum reportedly took no venture capital before its July 2026 strategic investment, so Locke could similarly bootstrap, accumulate proprietary policy-and-outreach data, and then become legible to a strategic buyer. The **Ro** exception strengthens that bull case because regulatory complexity can turn operational burden into a venture moat. Locke has not yet earned that exception: no named customer, verification process, proprietary dataset, revenue figure, or demonstrated compliance advantage separates Locke from incumbents licensing the same models. I would flip to Invest if Locke verifies at least $1M ARR from ten or more retained customers with strong retention and software-like gross margins at a sub-$12M post-money entry; absent that, I would not write the personal check today.
