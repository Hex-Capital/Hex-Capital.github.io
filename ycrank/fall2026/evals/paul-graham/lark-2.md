# Lark -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Interline Hardware was using Lark less than a month after kickoff. That is the most interesting fact here. Connecting purchasing software to a distributor’s actual ERP means encountering data and rules that a demo can ignore. The founders got something into use. But the case study leaves out the part I most want to hear: what broke during installation, what Jason and Michael did about it, and how quickly it worked. Deployment proves a product exists. It does not by itself prove how these founders behave when blocked.

The technical evidence is nevertheless substantial for this stage. Lark has implemented customer-specific purchasing workflows across an inventory of 60,000-plus items. That earns considerably more credit than an architecture diagram or a promise to build agents after funding. Interline’s reported saving of approximately 60 hours monthly supplies separate evidence that the software does useful work. Distributor buyers reconciling inventory and preparing replenishment orders are a specific enough group to build for. The separate claim of 50 hours saved weekly at an unnamed customer is less persuasive, and I would keep the two claims separate. Neither establishes retention, payment, or growth.

The strongest argument for investing is that Lark has already crossed the boundary where many enterprise AI companies stop. An actual distributor let the product into its purchasing workflow. Jason’s industrial supply-chain agent work at Traba supplies relevant experience, and Michael’s distributor outreach and conference attendance look like founders trying to recruit users themselves. Customer-specific mapping and operating rules could become accumulated knowledge that makes each subsequent installation easier. The danger is that every installation remains a fresh consulting project. Lark’s stated one-to-two-month setup period makes that a real question, especially with Didero already executing overlapping procurement workflows. Subscription language in the terms does not establish that customers will pay enough to support the implementation work.

I also cannot reconstruct how the founders arrived here or changed their minds. Jason’s reported 400K ARR in eight weeks belongs to Datost, not Lark, and the dossier gives no obstacle-and-response sequence behind it. His prior work makes the choice of market plausible, but does not document a personal collision with this purchasing problem. There is no rejected product hypothesis or evidence-driven route change to assess. Those are unknowns, not evidence of passivity. Being brothers reduces concern about an application assembled around strangers, though it does not establish shared execution under pressure. Michael’s departure from university is likewise immaterial beside an actual customer deployment.

Lark fits my preference for working systems and willingness to do ugly integration work. It has less evidence of the unusual technical substrate or unmistakable user pull that could justify overriding my founder-behavior gate. The working-prototype exception deserves consideration, but one customer testimonial does not resolve that missing evidence. I would hold off on a personal check. My flip condition is one verifiable implementation history showing a named blocker, the founders’ shipped workaround within weeks, and confirmation from the customer that the revised workflow remains in regular use. Lark already has enough product evidence to make that conversation worth having.
