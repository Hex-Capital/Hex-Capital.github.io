# ProvenMetal -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have real, action-earned knowledge. Doyle and Carkner previously built Syncra’s hardware, ran a 17-day office pilot, collected more than 20 million datapoints, and reported a 95% reduction in phantom energy use. Carkner also built PCB testing at Lumindt. Thirty-plus industry conversations and five LOIs show they continued learning through the market rather than merely adopting a fashionable reshoring narrative. This is strong founder evidence, although it stops short of proving that they have manufactured and sold PCBs at commercial scale.

The problem is that ProvenMetal currently owns the coordination layer, not the scarce substrate. Network PCB performs the assembly, offers overlapping sourcing and testing directly, and advertises a three-day turnkey service against ProvenMetal’s five-day fastest turn. If Network PCB copies the one-hour quoting and EDA workflow—or simply takes the customer directly—the manufacturing capability remains with the supplier. The KiCad plugin is useful distribution, but version 0.2.0, without installation or retention data, is software rather than a moat. Inferred future value from BOM histories and inspection records cannot substitute for a proprietary dataset that already compounds.

The bull case is sharper than a generic contract-manufacturing story. ProvenMetal is moving fragmented domestic capacity toward a searchable, managed transaction layer; the KiCad integration reaches engineers at the moment of component selection, and the same procurement, inspection, and compliance capability can serve drones, defense, robotics, medical devices, and industrial equipment inside a $16.12 billion North American PCB market. Policy pressure for domestic electronics production strengthens that path. If orders eventually route across multiple factories and every completed board improves quoting, supplier selection, and failure prediction, the coordinator could become more valuable than any single factory.

But that network does not yet exist. One manufacturing partner is concentration, not permissionless supply. Each order still requires procurement, program management, fabrication, assembly, and inspection; no customer currently creates utility or distribution for another customer. The five LOIs also came before the documented transition from an AI-enabled benchtop X-ray product to turnkey PCBA, so they cannot establish demand for the present service. The prototype-before-network exception therefore does not apply: there is neither a singular proprietary product nor evidence of capital-efficient user pull strong enough to override the missing loop.

Direct value capture is plausible because customers request project quotes, but no payer, completed order, price, take rate, or revenue is public. The offering transition is the most important red flag—not proof of weak conviction, but evidence that the team has not yet found the durable layer. My base case is that by August 2029 ProvenMetal has either narrowed into defense-grade PCB procurement and inspection software below $3 million ARR or been disintermediated by its manufacturing partners, rather than becoming a broad PCBA network.

I would not invest yet. The founders are good enough to keep watching, and the market is broad enough to permit a valuable pivot, but founder quality cannot be counted twice as defensibility. The single flip condition is verifiable: show twelve months of repeat orders routed across at least three independent factories, with gross-margin retention and proprietary inspection or supplier-performance data demonstrably improving quote accuracy or defect rates. Then ProvenMetal would own a compounding network rather than a convenient interface around someone else’s factory.
