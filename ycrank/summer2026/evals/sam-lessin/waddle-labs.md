# Waddle Labs -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I cannot underwrite the deal before I know the deal. Waddle Labs discloses neither post-money valuation nor competing-term-sheet count, so I cannot tell whether this is a cheap first check or an auction around “agents for robotics.” More importantly, no use-of-funds plan identifies the one experiment my capital would buy. “Build the API” is not a venture moment. A credible version would specify a robot class, a production task, a deployment deadline, and the success rate or programming-cost reduction that converts a pilot into payment.

The technical work is real. Song brings program-induction and multimodal-model research; Ye has worked on robot policies; together they have operated robots for six months and run roughly 1,000 overnight LEGO pick-and-place repetitions. The product’s editable programs and execution-feedback loop are also a legitimate alternative to direct VLA control. This is the strongest part of the company: two young researchers have crossed from model work into functioning physical execution rather than stopping at a paper or simulation.

But internal repetition is not external demand. Waddle’s six months of traces and shared skill library could become a proprietary data asset, yet the preview API, 971 X followers, and early-access registration show no purchase, retained deployment, or customer workflow that the company owns. Public code-as-policy research, CaP-X, and ASPIRE make the architecture available to others. Meanwhile, Skild AI, Physical Intelligence, Wandelbots, university labs, and robot vendors can pursue adjacent loops with substantially more capital, data, or distribution. The dark pool is therefore prospective, not demonstrated.

The bull case is that Waddle has already assembled the beginning of a compounding system: every executed task can create reusable code, failure traces, and intervention data, while the founders understand both program generation and robot policy. That could let a tiny team advance faster than foundation-model competitors attempting to learn every behavior end to end. The problem is that the dossier supplies no external deployment count, standardized benchmark, or evidence that one customer’s learning transfers economically to another. Performance and task cost also rise with third-party model capability and reasoning budget. If the next model release improves everyone’s code generation, Waddle may own an integration layer while its suppliers own the economics.

Delete the next financing and there is no business model left to inspect: no pricing, revenue, gross margin, burn, or controllable cash metric. Autonomous physical code also introduces safety and certification work that can turn a nominal API into a services-heavy deployment business. The earlier teleoperation and data-collection product suggests the team may ultimately move back toward robotics data infrastructure; within 36 months, I expect Waddle either to pivot into teleoperation/data tooling for robotics labs or remain below $1 million ARR rather than establish a broad robot-control platform.

I am passing, despite strong technical founder-market fit. The known exception case would require hard external receipts—paid enterprise adoption, repeat deployment, or exceptional retention—not merely a plausible data flywheel. My verifiable flip condition is one named industrial customer paying for at least three production tasks, with Waddle demonstrating that reusable skills cut deployment time or cost by at least 50% while maintaining a disclosed safety threshold. That result would reveal the missing truth: the system is a purchased workflow, not an impressive laboratory loop.
