# Dydema -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Dydema has published what a prediction costs, but not what owning the business costs. Wizard charges $0.003 per prediction window; the reported September 2026 convertible note supplies neither a valuation cap nor evidence of investor competition. I cannot infer cheapness from a $500,000 financing, or an auction from YC membership. Price remains unknown. So does the exact truth this check purchases: the dossier names no funded experiment with a deadline and a measurable result. Those are missing underwriting inputs, not evidence that the founders have done something wrong.

The potentially valuable asset is the recording operation. If Dydema can collect consented EEG recordings that developers cannot obtain elsewhere, retain useful reuse rights, and demonstrate that those recordings improve a task somebody pays to perform, there could be a proprietary business here. But each link matters. The dossier establishes an offering for commissioned recordings; it does not establish exclusive data rights, differentiated model performance, or customer purchases caused by that access. Public API onboarding and brain-controlled TikTok demonstrations show something works. They do not yet show who owns an economic advantage. EMOTIV already combines hardware, APIs, and brain-data infrastructure, making the distinction commercially consequential.

The strongest argument against passing is that Weaver and Oosthuizen have already crossed a difficult boundary. Weaver brings EEG/fMRI research and computer-science training; both founders worked at Syntra, collaborated on a neurotechnology experiment, and have now shipped a documented classification service. That activates my preference for people who translate between technical ecosystems. Their disclosure that zero-shot imagined left-versus-right movement achieves 52% accuracy against 50% chance—and their decision not to offer that task—is also more credible than pretending generalization is solved. Calibrated classification could support a useful specialist business well before a general consumer interface works. The founder dimension earns affirmative credit; scaled operation or established access to EEG buyers remains unproven.

That narrower business is the bull case I take seriously. Commissioned recordings might finance proprietary data accumulation while the API supplies repeat usage. But the published $1 model-creation fee, $1 monthly hosting fee, and usage pricing do not establish recording margins, inference costs, burn, or a route to cash break-even. I would not call the economics bad without those numbers. I also cannot delete the next financing from the plan and see a self-supporting operation. The disclosed theoretical demonstration ceiling of approximately 1.3 bits per second adds a specific adoption question: which task is valuable enough that a customer accepts calibration, equipment, and errors?

I pass at 31/100. Dydema fits the translation-layer pattern, but it has not demonstrated the proprietary access or cash engine that would justify overriding missing deal terms and an undefined venture moment. The known exceptions require documented conversion, exceptional external demand, or a concrete alternative financing structure; none is established here. Nor does the dossier establish auction pressure, equal-founder paralysis, or an assumption that errors magically disappear. My decision rests on the missing economic mechanism. One verifiable event would reopen it: an independent customer renewing a contribution-positive recording contract specifically because Dydema’s rights-cleared proprietary data delivers a measured improvement over available alternatives. That would turn the most interesting possibility into something I could underwrite.
