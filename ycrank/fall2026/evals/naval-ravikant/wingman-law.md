# Wingman Law -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Nicholas Le’s 700+ prior injury cases matter more to me than the degrees. He has encountered the evidence, insurers, and procedural failures Wingman Law proposes to automate. Sun Woo Lee’s reported $350K in legaltech sales during law school adds evidence that she can sell into legal workflows. These are actions with consequences. They give the founders substantial specific knowledge, although neither those sales nor StrongSuit’s $4M ARR belongs to Wingman’s current business ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/wingman-law)).

The strongest argument for Wingman is that owning the practice lets these founders change how legal work gets done. A software vendor must persuade an incumbent to reorganize. Wingman can direct its own attorneys and agents, then collect a contingency fee when the client recovers. That creates a plausible incentive to improve outcomes and reduce work per case. But the dossier establishes the fee arrangement, without a disclosed percentage, completed recovery, or contribution margin. Direct value capture is designed into the business; its economics remain untested.

The decisive question is what accumulates after each case. Wingman’s public visualization uses licensed public litigation data. Its internal technology has no demonstrated exclusive dataset, proprietary model, or measured learning advantage. Meanwhile, EvenUp and Supio sell overlapping automation to existing plaintiff firms. Those firms already have clients and case histories. Wingman must demonstrate that operating the entire practice produces an advantage those customers cannot obtain by buying software. An additional client currently provides no documented improvement in utility or distribution for another client. Founder introductions and branded-car advertising acquire attention; they do not establish a network.

The bull case deserves weight: experienced practitioners could discover that a few changes to case execution produce dramatically better recoveries with much less attorney labor. Owning the workflow would let Wingman implement those discoveries immediately. Founder quality can justify an unclear initial wedge, and a distinctive prototype can justify investing before distribution appears. Those exceptions keep me interested here. But the dossier establishes relevant experience without the unusual product proof that would justify an override. Wingman also only partially matches my pattern of converting fragmented offline services into scalable systems: it delivers the service directly, while the customer-created contribution mechanism remains hypothetical. No documented integrity or fundraising-pressure red flag warrants a veto.

There is enough market to build a substantial practice. The cited $57.8B U.S. personal-injury legal-services estimate supports that, although it does not establish Wingman’s accessible market or a repeatable expansion mechanism beyond California ([market estimate](https://law.co/blog/intelligence-in-personal-injury-tort-law-market-research-report)). Attorney-directed agents could carry shared capabilities across jurisdictions, but professional obligations and local operating arrangements still require execution. My concern is how much additional labor and acquisition spending each increment of revenue requires.

I would keep Wingman Law in consideration without writing a check today. The founders have earned confidence in their understanding of the problem; the business has not yet demonstrated an asset that compounds beyond their work. My flip condition is a documented sequence of completed case cohorts showing that a proprietary insurer-response model improves recovery per attorney-hour on subsequent comparable cases beyond what the same workflow achieves with commercially available tools. That would turn a plausible learning loop into evidence of a durable advantage.
