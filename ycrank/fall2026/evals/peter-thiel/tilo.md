# Tilo -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Tilo’s proposed secret is that reliable computer-use agents require applications designed around shared, typed actions rather than increasingly sophisticated imitation of human clicks. That proposition is testable: compare completed workflows on Tilo against existing-interface automation, including the cost of adapting applications. The published implementation gives the thesis substance. But the claimed improvement of up to 10,000× has no independently reproduced benchmark, and Simular’s or Browser Use’s architectural choices do not establish that either rejects Tilo’s proposition. I see a concrete technical hypothesis, with its most important result still unproved.

The investment question turns on who must change their behavior before the hypothesis becomes valuable. Tilo requires native applications to describe their items and actions to obtain its deeper capabilities. Developers must therefore invest in the ecosystem before that ecosystem has demonstrated demand. The strongest bull case is that integrating permissions, collaborative state, history, and undo makes an agent application substantially easier to build and trust; developers could adopt Tilo because assembling those primitives separately is worse. That could eventually produce an application network. Today, however, external developer adoption is undocumented, no bounded customer segment is established, and MIT-licensed framework code offers no exclusive ownership of the architecture. Implementation complexity is real; a monopoly mechanism remains unproved.

Emmerling and Wolff have relevant technical context: both list TUM and BDPE affiliations, and Wolff built patent-workflow automation during 2025–2026. Shared institutions could conceal a durable working relationship, but they do not establish one. Full-time status, prior joint execution, ownership, and economic exposure remain undisclosed, so I apply the founding-structure default. There is no documented part-time founder, outsourced core team, or destructive capitalization. Tilo also resembles my unstated preference for infrastructure beneath AI applications, but the dossier does not establish the concentrated operator network or proprietary founder-discovery mechanism behind other such investments.

Distribution and economics are separate unresolved questions. The public SDK and browser waitlist make experimentation accessible; they do not yet identify a paying buyer or a repeatable acquisition route. The October 21 catalogue authorization expiry shows a concrete delivery failure, although documenting renewal procedures and monitoring is a useful response. No pricing or business model establishes how Tilo captures the savings it promises. The quoted $16.7 billion AI operating-system category includes mobile and embedded deployments and supplies no arithmetic for this particular revenue engine. None of this is unusual at pre-seed. It nevertheless leaves both dimensions at their absent-evidence defaults.

I pass on writing a check today, while leaving the technical thesis open to investigation. The decisive concern is the dependency between application migration and ecosystem value: Tilo needs evidence that a small market will adopt the new architecture before I can underwrite its expansion. My known exceptions require demonstrated technical discontinuity, persistent transaction demand, or a concrete deployment path; this dossier establishes none strongly enough to override that concern. One verifiable flip condition would reopen the decision: an independently audited deployment in one named customer segment showing repeat paid usage and at least a tenfold reduction in total cost per successfully completed workflow, including application integration. That would connect Tilo’s technical hypothesis to an actual beachhead.
