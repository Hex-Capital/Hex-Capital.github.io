# Pacific -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important fact is not that Pacific has chosen data centers. It is that the founders abandoned a digital-twin product that reportedly reached £10K MRR in May 2026 and, by August, were proposing to manufacture physical compute pods. That is a large change made quickly. But the dossier does not say what the digital-twin product taught them, which customer observation caused the change, or what they shipped afterward. It proves motion, not yet resourcefulness. A running back changes direction because he sees a defender. Here I can see the turn but not the defender.

The current product is still mostly a set of claims. Diamond-wafer dry cooling could be valuable, particularly where water and power constrain deployment, but Pacific publishes no thermal capacity, power-density benchmark, reliability result, certification, or field test. Nor is there evidence that the team has built the transformers, switchgear, and power distribution it proposes to integrate. Ralph Heward-Mills’s Airbus thermal-engineering experience makes the attempt more credible than a generic “AI infrastructure” pitch. It does not turn the attempt into a measured artifact. Fierce nerds usually show you the strange thing working before explaining how large it will become.

The best bull case is the combination of the founders’ aerospace background, the previous product’s first-month revenue, and discussions with large aerospace companies. Perhaps the digital-twin business was simply the first route into a harder goal, and those customers revealed that managed physical compute was the real need. JLL’s figures support the urgency: 57% of data-center projects suffered delays of at least three months in 2025, while modular-system and micro-data-center sales are forecast to rise from $11 billion to $48 billion by 2030. A two-person team willing to leave software revenue for transformers, permitting, cooling, and manufacturing may possess exactly the appetite for ugly work that others lack.

But this exception requires the missing proof. Bindwell’s young founders were unusually credible because numerical performance and laboratory validation replaced the weak prior created by their age. Pacific has the relevant training but not the equivalent result. “Working with” unnamed aerospace companies could mean anything from a design conversation to repeated operation of a pod. YC and NVIDIA Inception are endorsements, not users. The £10K MRR belongs to the discarded product and cannot be spent twice as evidence for demand for the current one.

The schlep is real, but it may overwhelm rather than protect them. Pacific intends to manufacture precisely the components facing roughly 33-week lead times, reuse sites that still require local approvals, and compete with Armada’s deployed units and Crusoe’s billions of dollars of integrated infrastructure. Vertical integration becomes a moat only after it works repeatedly. Before that, it is several companies that must all succeed simultaneously. The space extension makes the story broader while the terrestrial machine remains unverified.

I am passing. The operational gate fails because there is neither a named blocker followed by a shipped workaround nor a current technical or user proof strong enough to substitute for it. My one flip condition is straightforward: demonstrate a full-power Pacific pod, with independently measured cooling performance, running a named customer’s workload continuously for 30 days. That single result would test the cooling claim, the integration work, and whether a real user will tolerate the crude product.
