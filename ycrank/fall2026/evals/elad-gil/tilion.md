# Tilion -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Browser agents create a new buyer for reliable web access: developers whose automated jobs stop at anti-bot challenges. Tilion’s controlling variable is the cost per successfully completed customer workflow, including retries and network egress. If Fortress materially improves that number, it can become infrastructure developers depend on. The company-reported 86.4% successful-load rate across 92 protected sites gives that proposition a measurable starting point. It does not yet establish completion economics: loading a protected page is only one step in finishing a valuable job.

There is real technical substance here. Fortress patches Chromium’s C++ and supplies coherent device identities rather than relying on JavaScript injection. Existing Playwright and Puppeteer applications connect through CDP, giving Tilion an integration point inside an established development workflow. BrowserArena’s October 22 result—156 ms median lifecycle latency and 99 successful runs out of 100—provides independent evidence that the hosted implementation works on that benchmark. However, neither result quantifies customer savings against alternatives. The announced $0.03/browser-hour price excludes the information I need about utilization, retries, support, and egress costs. More blocking can increase demand while simultaneously making delivery more expensive.

The strongest argument for investing now is distribution. Fortress reports more than 100,000 installs and engineers at 170 companies using it; the repository has 779 stars and 52 forks. For a Fall 2026 company, that is meaningful discovery and experimentation, even after discounting company-reported usage. Free development use could feed the $2,000/month organization license without requiring a separate acquisition channel. The September licensing change introduces an important test of that mechanism: will developers pay for maintained production access when earlier BSD releases remain available? Installs and claimed millions of daily operations do not answer that question, and the hosted-access announcement conflicts with the current waitlist.

Tilion fits my pattern of buying infrastructure exposure across a technology discontinuity. But the browser infrastructure market is already crystallizing around funded competitors: Browserbase has raised $67.5 million, Kernel $22 million, and Browser Use $17 million. Native patches are an implementation advantage, not demonstrated exclusivity. Better models should increase browser execution demand, but Tilion has not documented ownership of credentials, persistent customer state, or outcome feedback that makes its particular service harder to replace. The founders’ browser development, AWS experience, and shared research support execution credibility; they do not establish prior startup learning or costly personal commitment. The exceptions for scarce frontier research talent or a strategic opening abandoned by incumbents are not supported here.

I would pass on writing a personal check today. This is a credible infrastructure product with early distribution, but the evidence does not yet connect technical performance to durable economic capture. My flip condition is one documented production cohort: at least three organizations paying the published $2,000 monthly license for three consecutive months, with workload measurements showing lower cost per completed job than their previous setup and positive contribution margins after support and egress. That would turn the central market belief into customer evidence without requiring Tilion to look like a mature company.
