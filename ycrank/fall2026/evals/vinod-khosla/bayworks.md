# Bayworks -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Bayworks could return billable time to automotive technicians and prevent repair work from going unpaid because someone omitted a measurement. That is useful. The investment question is how much expertise and administrative labor disappears if this works exceptionally well. The dossier supplies no baseline for documentation hours, avoidable warranty losses, or cost per accepted claim. The cited $32.13B in dealership warranty revenue measures the surrounding activity; it does not measure the value Bayworks can release. I cannot yet calculate the consequence that would justify buying a low-probability option.

The strongest argument for writing a check is the reported deployment: 80+ dealerships in 110 days, including an unnamed top-50 dealer group. Those dealerships reportedly handle 4,000 repairs daily, although that does not establish 4,000 daily Bayworks transactions. Getting into an operating dealership is meaningful at pre-seed. My portfolio’s execution-heavy businesses are a reminder that scientific novelty is not the only route to a consequential company. A technician application could become the entry point into broader repair operations. But that expansion remains a hypothesis, and the customer’s $3B+ revenue supplies no evidence of Bayworks’s own economics.

The product has a sensible intervention: capture the explanation while the technician remembers the repair, request missing findings, and return a reviewed narrative to the DMS. The binding question is whether this produces a capability advantage that survives native-platform competition. Tekion already offers technician voice documentation. Bayworks supplies no comparative measurement of labor saved, missing-information detection, or warranty acceptance. Retrieval of previous repair stories could improve drafting, but dealership ownership of operational data leaves cross-customer learning rights unresolved. This creates exposure to the AI-feature red flag; it does not establish that the founders merely wrapped an API.

I would spend the next dollar on accepted-claim accuracy and total labor per repair. The dossier documents no experiment with a pass/fail threshold, budget, or response to failure. Deployment establishes commercial access, but it does not answer those questions. Likewise, Shubham Goyal’s backend experience at Navi and internships at Scapia and Cisco support the ability to build this workflow; they provide no documented example of belief revision or recruiting someone to own an automotive-domain weakness. I use the missing-evidence defaults here. Two founders, undisclosed revenue, and limited public biographies are ordinary pre-seed conditions.

I pass with my own money. The exception routes require evidence of an unusually consequential destination or a documented instigator capable of turning the wedge into much larger infrastructure. This record establishes neither. Pricing, inference costs, integration support, and human review costs are also undisclosed, so unsubsidized economic advantage remains unproven. One result would reopen my decision: an independently audited, 90-day dealership trial showing at least a 10x reduction in combined technician and administrator labor per OEM-accepted warranty claim against the existing workflow, with no deterioration in acceptance and lower fully loaded cost. That would turn a plausible convenience into evidence of an economic discontinuity.
