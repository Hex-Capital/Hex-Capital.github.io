# Ekpa -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A personal research agent watches every household asset, reads each new filing, remembers the owner’s constraints, and explains what changed before breakfast. Ekpa has one artifact from that world: users connected more than $3.4 million in holdings and ran 2,400 analyses. But the causal path remains blurry. The dossier gives me cheaper inference as “why now,” not the intermediate transitions that turn a portfolio dashboard into a trusted financial intelligence system.

The strongest evidence is behavioral. People surrendered sensitive portfolio context and repeatedly requested analysis from a functioning product; that matters more than the unsupported claim of a 1.5% one-week return. Yet I cannot tell whether 2,400 analyses came from 24 obsessed users or 2,400 tourists, whether anyone returned after an alert, or whether Ekpa Plus has produced a dollar. Assets connected are not assets managed, and research activity is not yet costly conviction.

Anant Asthana has built agentic drug-discovery pipelines; Yuga Patel combines computing, economics, investing experience, and prior work on a youth coding-and-robotics program. That is credible technical range and some first-believer proof. What is missing is the operating wound: no dated record shows either founder spending years wrestling with retail portfolio research, financial regulation, or trust-sensitive consumer products. Two MIT founders jointly introduced the company, but the dossier also does not identify the accountable leader or a governance tiebreaker. I do not assume a 50/50 problem; I require it resolved before investing.

Ekpa correctly sees a time leak: people with jobs and families cannot continuously process filings, news, macroeconomic data, and analyst revisions. It returns attention and some decision-making control. But this is inconvenience, not yet a decoded rent. Which institution profits because research remains fragmented? Which rule prevents the user from obtaining a continuous fiduciary-grade explanation? The unresolved investment-adviser boundary may become the central system constraint, while the current “research and education” positioning avoids rather than exploits it.

The bull case stings. Read-only brokerage access limits custody risk, CSV fallback reduces SnapTrade dependence, and cross-broker aggregation could become more useful than any broker-native assistant. Those 2,400 analyses may be the first trace of a longitudinal dataset showing how people investigate holdings and respond to changing evidence. If repeat use is concentrated and users pay, Ekpa could move below the interface into a valuable personalization layer.

Today, however, it sits above infrastructure and below distribution. PortfolioPilot already supplies personalized advice, SoFi acquired Composer, and Robinhood can place similar intelligence beside execution. Ekpa controls neither brokerage connectivity nor an identified proprietary model, dataset, or scarce technical bottleneck. Sensitive holdings could eventually create defensibility, but without explicit consent, retention, and deletion architecture they create a privacy liability first. My flip condition is concrete: show a six-month cohort in which at least 40% of connected users run four or more analyses monthly, produce $250,000 in annualized subscription revenue, and document that this behavior improves an Ekpa-controlled personalization system without retaining brokerage data beyond explicit user consent. Until then, I pass.
