# CONUS -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

CONUS has a plausible business connecting device manufacturers to hospital workflows. I cannot yet underwrite the check. Post-money valuation, competing term sheets, and whether my capital would be scarce are all undisclosed. So is the experiment the round would finance. Those are missing facts, not evidence of an auction or a bad capital plan. But they leave my two primary investment gates unresolved.

The strongest argument is the distribution mechanism. CONUS reports more than 20 European hospitals live and relationships with Johnson & Johnson, Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, and B. Braun. Free hospital access could create a useful loop: clinicians get organized device information, manufacturers pay to maintain useful portfolios, and hospital-specific inventories make the product harder to replace. That fits my preference for bridges between systems with different incentives. Live deployments earn credit for actual adoption; named relationships alone do not establish purchases, exclusive access, or repeat usage.

Manufacturer services explicitly financing operations is the most economically interesting fact in the dossier. It suggests CONUS may have a functioning business beneath the AI option. The advertised €9.99–€49.99 monthly subscriptions are prices, however, not demonstrated subscription revenue. Without service revenue amounts, delivery costs, gross margins, or burn, I cannot tell whether manufacturer payments support a repeatable product or labor-intensive onboarding. Delete the next financing: there is a possible survival mechanism here, but no documented route to cash break-even.

The team gives that mechanism credibility. Schulz developed biomedical electronics and embedded software at Miltenyi Biotec; Becker worked across hardware and automation software at Cleansort. Those are operating receipts beyond academic affiliations. Five chief-surgeon founders or advisors provide access to the clinical side of the bridge. This is stronger than three AI engineers discovering healthcare through a market map. It still leaves open who owns manufacturer sales and hospital implementation. The named CEO supplies some leadership clarity; three founders do not establish the equal-founder committee problem I avoid.

The bull case against my pass is substantial: manufacturer-funded distribution could let CONUS embed itself before charging hospitals, while proprietary local inventories and protocols become the durable asset. But 500,000 papers and 2,000 manufacturer documents establish coverage, not ownership of a dark pool. C8 Health and Incision already organize adjacent clinical workflows. Source-linked answers and mandatory verification help define a bounded product, but the economics must include that verification burden. The institutional prohibitions on paid preferential sorting and manufacturer access to hospital-specific usage data protect trust; they also mean I cannot casually assume the company can monetize that data. My exceptions for expensive AI businesses require demonstrated conversion and retention, while my exceptions to finite experiments require explicit capital plans and unusually strong founder or financing structures. CONUS has not documented either override.

I pass at the evidence available, with genuine interest in the supplier-funded business. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an executable financing agreement below $10 million post-money that fully funds a dated, 12-month test of whether recurring manufacturer contracts can cover operating costs. That would connect the price, the capital, and the truth being purchased. Twenty hospitals make CONUS worth investigating; they do not yet tell me what my money proves.
