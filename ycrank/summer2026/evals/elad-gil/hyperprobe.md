# HyperProbe -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: AI now writes production code, backend teams still buy observability and debugging tools, and HyperProbe becomes large only if access to live runtime state materially improves coding-agent resolution rates enough that companies instrument many services rather than merely use one free service during unusual incidents. The 80% developer AI-tool adoption figure establishes the shift, while the product supplies the missing capability: a coding agent can inspect line-specific variable state through MCP without a redeploy. The controlling variable is identifiable, but HyperProbe has not stated the required improvement in resolution rate, service expansion, or retention that would validate it.

The technical mechanism is more compelling than a generic “AI for DevOps” story. HyperProbe places read-only, non-blocking probes inside live Node.js, Java, Kotlin, Scala, Python, and TypeScript services; reports less than 1% overhead at 3,000 requests per second; and surrounds sensitive-state capture with PII redaction, audit logs, probe expiry, approval gates, and private-VPC deployment. This changes an agent from suggesting debugging steps to gathering production evidence inside the workflow. Better coding models should increase the value of that evidence rather than absorb the instrumentation layer outright.

CheQ Digital and Housing.com provide useful, though still narrow, product-market evidence. One production mismatch was found on the first attempt after previously requiring days to reproduce; one race condition was exposed and fixed within an hour during a traffic spike. Those are concrete completed jobs, not waitlist registrations or generic pilots. But they are company-hosted testimonials without payment, repeat usage, retention, or expansion data. The $99-per-service monthly price—with a three-service minimum—creates a clean test: whether one successful incident causes customers to instrument the rest of their estate. That expansion loop is not yet demonstrated.

The strongest objection is that this becomes an excellent feature with weak independent distribution. Lightrun has raised $110 million, Dynatrace already owns Rookout, and Multiplayer combines full-stack recordings with agent-generated fixes. A coding-agent platform or observability incumbent could integrate comparable probe control and bundle it into an existing enterprise relationship. HyperProbe has no documented proprietary dataset, exclusive integration, or structural barrier; the inferred corpus of runtime states and fixes deserves no moat credit until its reproduction cost and customer portability are known. The narrow probe may open the workflow, but diagnosis, remediation, verification, and incident history determine who ultimately owns it.

The founders partially offset that risk. Karan Raina and Shailendra Singh previously built HyperTest around SDK-based integration testing and application traces, then carried the same team into this adjacent production-debugging problem. That is relevant second-time learning and direct technical context, not résumé prestige. The pivot also suggests persistence, although the prior product’s 100-plus teams cannot be counted as HyperProbe traction and the dossier documents neither a prior exit nor a specific personal sacrifice.

I would make a small pre-seed investment because the market and technology gates clear, but size it around evidence that the first probe becomes reusable distribution across services. My base case is that HyperProbe reaches roughly $3 million to $8 million in ARR by August 2029 or becomes an acquisition target for an observability platform; the bear case is absorption before independent distribution develops. The verifiable flip condition is service expansion: if fewer than ten organizations are paying for at least three services after twelve months, I would reverse to a pass because the product would be behaving like an incident-specific tool rather than an owned production workflow.
