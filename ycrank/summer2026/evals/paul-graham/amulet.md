# Amulet -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The decisive missing fact is what happened when something broke. Nithik Bala and Varun Puru have built a technically credible system, but the dossier names no customer obstacle, engineering failure, or distribution barrier followed by a founder-built workaround. Their Meta, Rockset, Amazon, and backend experience explains why they can build this. It does not show how they behave under resistance. For a people-first investor, credentials cannot substitute for that event.

The product itself is the strongest evidence. Workspaces reportedly mount in under 100 milliseconds, sustain 8.4 GB/s per mount, and produce p99 reads of 1.9 milliseconds. Copy-on-write forks, content-addressed commits, short-lived credentials, and compare-and-swap publishing form a coherent answer to parallel agents corrupting shared state. This is more than a diagram. But the benchmarks are self-published, no independent workload result is available, and private-beta access prevents outsiders from determining how much of the system works under pressure. I give substantial but discounted credit for the technical proof.

Who wants the crude version now? Amulet names automated-research labs, agent-compute providers, and data-intensive AI teams, but it names no actual user. Small-batch private beta is controlled availability, not pull. The $500 Growth plan is a price, not revenue. Eighty-four LinkedIn followers—on a profile still describing the previous product—tell us almost nothing. There may be a narrow well here, but the dossier has not shown anyone drinking from it.

The July-to-August movement from a post-sales product to an agent filesystem could be rapid correction. It could also be two smart engineers replacing one plausible noun with a more fashionable one. The evidence does not identify the rejected hypothesis, the observation that killed it, or a stable goal connecting the products. Their infrastructure backgrounds provide genuine founder-market alignment, yet there is no documented personal collision with disposable compute losing data. And while distributed storage is full of unpleasant work, Archil, E2B, Daytona, and JuiceFS demonstrate that competitors are already willing to do it. This is not yet a neglected schlep.

The strongest bull case is that Amulet is building a programmable primitive for autonomous actors before the category has settled. Its versioned writable forks and provenance records are more specific than generic “AI infrastructure,” and the founders appear capable of shipping difficult systems without waiting for a large team. That pattern matters. But the known-exception logic does not rescue the investment: a working product can overcome missing conventional signals only when accompanied by independently validated proof, extraordinary founder motion, or unmistakable user behavior. None is documented here. There is also no fatal red flag—no dependence on a ceremonial launch, large partner, or delegated sales team—but absence of bad behavior is not affirmative evidence of resourcefulness.

My 36-month expectation is that Amulet pivots from a standalone filesystem into a narrower provenance-and-storage API for agent-sandbox vendors and remains below $1 million ARR. I would pass today. One fact would flip the decision: a dated case study showing that a named beta customer hit a concrete production blocker, the founders shipped the remedy within two weeks, and that customer then paid while using at least 100 workspaces weekly for eight consecutive weeks.
