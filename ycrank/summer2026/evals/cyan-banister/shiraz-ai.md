# Shiraz AI -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A contract manufacturer receives a new product on Monday, a line supervisor records the assembly once, and robots begin useful work without a robotics team rewriting the cell. Weekly production changes stop being automation resets. Shiraz AI has one credible leak from that world: robots are already operating at customer sites after learning from video. But the dossier does not yet map the intervening transitions—exception recovery, safety validation, cycle-time parity, and deployment economics—or show how far each remains from production readiness. This is a plausible future chain, not yet an inevitable one.

The founders are the strongest part of the company. Navid Aghasadeghi worked on Spot commercialization at Boston Dynamics and industrial manipulation at Rethink Robotics; Mojtaba Mozaffar led perception, policy-learning, and manipulation work at Amazon Robotics after robotics research at Northwestern. Navid also built GenZArt to a reported one million users, which adds commercial evidence to an otherwise deeply technical biography. These are not two people who discovered robotics through a fashionable funding round. What is missing is the first-believer story: the dated experiment that became Shiraz, evidence of how the pair recruited each other, and which insight they independently derived before single-video learning became a competitive category.

The broken system is visible but only partly decoded. Contract manufacturers change lines weekly, cannot consistently find labor, and leave rigid automation unused. That is a real operating wound. Shiraz could return control to plant operators by letting them teach tasks instead of waiting for specialist integrators. Yet the dossier never quantifies the idle equipment, integration bill, reprogramming delay, or labor-hours recovered. It also does not identify who captures today’s rent. “Automation is expensive” is the beginning of my why-question, not the answer.

Robots at customer sites matter much more than 204 X followers or a market projection from $37.8 billion to $78.9 billion. Someone allowed this two-person startup onto a factory floor, which is a costly trust decision. The strongest bull case is that these founders combine an unusually good technical prior with the willingness to own the ugly hardware and deployment work; generalist competitors may have larger models while Shiraz quietly accumulates the exception data that production actually requires. But “first customers” comes without names, deployment counts, repeat task changes, successful-cycle rates, uptime, or retention. Founder-reported operation therefore reaches the current-evidence ceiling: the artifact works somewhere, but the dossier does not show repeated weird or costly user behavior around it.

The full-stack delivery model is both the opportunity and the trap. Supplying hardware, model, and installation could let Shiraz control the feedback loop. Today, however, no proprietary dataset, robot asset, or integration advantage is disclosed, while Skild S1 advertises the same one-video, no-fine-tuning mechanism with more than $2 billion behind it. The company may sit beneath the application wave eventually, but right now it sells an application of that wave. The known pre-revenue exception is CosmicBrain AI: both bets pair industrial prototypes with deeply technical robotics founders. The material difference is that CosmicBrain’s founder record, signed proofs of concept, and robot-agnostic enabling position established the independent obsession and controlled layer; Shiraz’s customer claim has not yet exposed either.

I find no evidence of a CEO shell game, reckless dismissal of incumbent complexity, or severe surveillance design. The unresolved co-founder control structure is a diligence question, not an adverse fact. The active concern is subtler: a fashionable physical-AI capability, directly matched by capital-rich competitors, without proof that Shiraz owns what those competitors will need. My 36-month base case is $2 million to $6 million of ARR from fewer than 20 manufacturers, with Shiraz operating as a capable deployment-heavy systems integrator rather than a foundational robotics layer. I would not write the check yet. One condition flips me: three named factories showing six months of weekly task changes with at least 95% successful production cycles and declining deployment time as Shiraz’s proprietary execution data grows.
