# Elithia -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Elithia attacks expensive expertise: physicians spend hours or days reconstructing histories from thousands of medical-record pages. Making that work radically cheaper would matter. But the dossier does not quantify how much physician capacity becomes available, the spending displaced, or the population served. I can identify the bottleneck. I cannot yet underwrite an orders-of-magnitude consequence. An attractive option needs a consequential payoff as well as a limited initial loss.

The technical question is whether Elithia removes expert work or merely prepares material that experts must still reconstruct. Deduplication, chronology generation, and source-page citations are useful capabilities. DigitalOwl already offers overlapping functionality, however, and Elithia supplies no comparative measurement of review time, consequential omissions, or cost. Citations make verification possible; they do not establish that verification is cheap. This activates my concern about AI products competing through workflow packaging, although the evidence does not establish that Elithia is simply a model wrapper.

The strongest argument against passing is that Sarthak Jain has already reached buyers. Reported sales to physician expert witnesses and medical-legal firms carry more information than a speculative market slide. His Winton unstructured-data experience is relevant, leaving MIT Sloan shows commitment, and the medico-legal conference appearance provides access to a specialized customer community. My actual investing pattern allows execution businesses and narrow workflows that can become platforms. But the proposed expert-correction learning loop remains an inference: data rights, accumulated corrections, and performance gains are unverified. The known exceptions to my technical and milestone requirements require an unusually consequential opportunity or a demonstrated instigator; leaving a prestigious program alone does not establish that override. Nor does the record show belief revision or recruiting against a specific weakness.

Elithia’s decisive uncertainty is reliable reconstruction at substantially lower total expert effort. The dossier names the accuracy risk but supplies no completed experiment, threshold, budget, or decision branch. I would put the next dollars into that test. Expansion into insurers and third-party administrators cannot resolve it. Economics remain equally open: the first 1,000 pages are free, while paid prices, inference costs, and human checking requirements are undisclosed. That trial is a distribution tactic, not evidence of permanent subsidy dependence—but neither is it evidence of cost parity.

I pass at 25/100. The missing measurements receive the stated evidence defaults; I am not treating a small team or undisclosed revenue as adverse signals. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently adjudicated, matched-case trial against DigitalOwl showing at least 10x lower fully loaded cost per completed physician review, including correction time, with no increase in clinically consequential omissions. That would demonstrate a genuine collapse in the cost of expertise and give me a reason to revisit the check.
