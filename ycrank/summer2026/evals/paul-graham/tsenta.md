# Tsenta -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important obstacle was not writing résumés. It was making software survive 30 different ATS platforms, account flows, CAPTCHAs, and changing forms. Srivastava and Gupta encountered this after submitting more than 3,000 applications themselves, then shipped a system spanning 50,000 career pages and more than two million jobs. That is real resourcefulness. But the dossier does not give the clean before-and-after story I want: which integration broke, what they built that week, and how completion changed. So the founders pass my gate, though not at the highest level.

The technical proof is harder to dismiss. Tsenta is working software used through web, mobile, messaging, Chrome, and MCP/CLI interfaces. Supporting 30-plus ATS platforms is not a diagram pretending to be a product. It is an ugly production system. The footprint—45,000 users, 5,000-plus Android downloads, and 1,000 Chrome users—shows that the artifact works outside the founders’ laptops. The reported application error and inability to edit in one iOS review matter because mistakes here can cost users jobs, but they are evidence of an imperfect shipped product, not vaporware.

There is also a narrow well. Students applying to 200–400 jobs will tolerate a crude product because the alternative consumes dozens of hours. Tsenta reportedly grew from about 1,100 to nearly 8,000 users in two months, reached 9,000 customers, doubled users month over month, and grew revenue fivefold in June 2026. Pricing begins at $19 rather than hiding behind a waitlist. The ratings are mixed enough to be believable: 4.2 on Android and 3.8 on iOS. My reservation is that the claimed 70% paid-user interview rate is company-reported, while Tsenta said it could not directly observe outcomes. Applications submitted are an activity metric. Interviews caused are the useful one.

The founders found the problem organically, and the schlep is the opportunity: keeping thousands of employer pages current, handling hostile browser flows, preserving user facts, and adapting when ATS vendors change their defenses. The same schlep is also the danger. Simplify and Jobright already overlap substantially, while browser agents can commoditize form submission. Tsenta’s present advantage is therefore pace and operational coverage, not a structural moat. The two-million-job database and outcome-linked feedback could become one, but the feedback loop does not yet exist.

The strongest bear case is that Tsenta has built a faster spam cannon. Its pricing rewards application volume up to 4,500 submissions per month, while employers increasingly detect automated materials and users cannot always verify what was sent. Paid short-form creators could conceal weak organic pull, and hiring distribution before fully measuring user outcomes resembles the red flag of scaling sales before learning why customers stay. But this does not yet outweigh the proof spike in my unstated pattern: a young technical team assembled an unusually concrete substrate before its round, then users and revenue arrived quickly. I see no dependence on a large partner, ceremonial launch, or delegated product development. None of my known exceptions is required; Tsenta clears the ordinary founder, product, and demand gates.

I would invest, but not because “AI job search” is an attractive noun. I expect Tsenta to reach roughly $10–25 million in ARR within 36 months if it turns submission receipts into a measured application-to-interview feedback loop; without that, competitors will copy the visible product and employers will attack the underlying behavior. The missing evidence is adaptation: the dossier shows one route scaling rapidly, not a rejected hypothesis followed by a better route. My flip condition is simple: if a directly instrumented cohort of 1,000 paid users shows no statistically meaningful improvement in interviews per 100 applications versus comparable self-applicants, I pass.
