# Jcode -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A developer is no longer writing code beside one assistant; she is supervising a small machine organization—agents dividing work, exchanging context, surviving restarts, and moving among models as price and capability change. Jcode has one artifact leaking from that future: each additional session consumes approximately 10.4 MB, versus 21.6 MB for Codex CLI, 157.5 MB for Cursor Agent, and 212.7 MB for Claude Code. The Rust harness already combines coordination, messaging, persistent memory, and background tasks. What is missing is the rest of the causal map: which work becomes economically possible at twenty agents, how supervision changes, and why this harness captures that value rather than merely making today’s sessions cheaper.

Jeremy Huang’s strongest credential is not his coursework. It is the object sitting on GitHub. A 21-year-old solo founder produced 6,949 commits, attracted 16.9K stars and 1.9K forks, and separately built a Rust renderer with 1.6K stars. That is unusually convincing first-believer behavior. He has demonstrated Tizz—the independent compulsion to build—and some technical Rizz through open-source adoption. The dossier does not yet show when the obsession began, whom he recruited, or Biz: customer discovery, pricing power, and command of the route from popularity to enterprise value.

Jcode identifies a measurable irritation, but not yet a broken system. An extra Claude Code session imposes roughly 212.7 MB of memory and 3,436.9 milliseconds to first frame; Jcode attacks both the resource tax and the waiting. Multi-provider support and local Ollama or LM Studio operation also return some control to developers. But I cannot identify who captures the current rent, which artificial restriction keeps it in place, or whether users will pay to escape it. The hosted plan is especially peculiar: $10 buys $20 of credit, followed by inference at 90% of provider API prices. That may be a clever adoption subsidy, but it is not yet durable economics.

The behavior is more meaningful than a waitlist. There are 815 persisted sessions, 1,973 todo calls, 2,012 hill-climbability ratings, and 1.9K forks of a functioning product. Developers have spent time installing, running, and modifying it. Still, stars arriving at more than 670 per day during a Trending run can measure curiosity rather than dependence, and the dossier provides no dated retention cohort, paid users, or examples of teams reorganizing work around the swarm. The company’s own usage and benchmark reporting therefore stops short of proving repeated costly behavior.

The strongest bull case stings: one founder has already built a dramatically lighter, provider-neutral control plane while billion-dollar competitors optimize their own vertically integrated agents. If agent count explodes, orchestration—not any individual model—could become the scarce layer, and the open repository could become its distribution standard. But MIT licensing, reproducible company-authored benchmarks, and no identified structural barrier mean the controlled asset is presently execution speed and community attention. Anthropic, OpenAI, Cursor, or another open-source project can adopt the same architecture while Jcode subsidizes inference underneath them.

I would not write the check yet. This is a technically real and unusually well-distributed artifact, not a consensus wrapper, but the future leak has not become an economic mechanism. My flip condition is verifiable: show a cohort of at least 25 independent teams retaining Jcode for eight consecutive weeks while each regularly runs five or more coordinated agents, with at least ten paying for orchestration rather than discounted inference. That would prove the swarm changes work instead of merely winning a benchmark.
