# Cova -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Cova’s contested truth is that a home-care agency can scale without non-caregiver headcount because software agents can perform enrollment, benefits coordination, insurance, and billing. This is falsifiable: if each additional cohort still requires proportional human exception handling—or if Michigan’s four-form, photograph-by-text application process prevents faster enrollment—the supposed AI agency becomes an ordinary provider with an AI interface. Being licensed and live is evidence that the system touches reality; it is not yet evidence that the secret is true. The prospective caregiver quoting a $22-per-hour Medicaid rate confirms onboarding activity, but no completion time, administrative cost, error rate, or paid claim demonstrates a 10-times operating result.

The strongest fact is the founding structure. Jordan Ibe and Ansh Tandon worked together on Palantir’s healthcare team, then spent the year before Cova building systems for home-care agencies and hospitals. They combined deployment, engineering, and bioengineering experience before converting Omnition into the agency’s internal operating system. This is a company assembled around an operating thesis, not two people who met to apply to YC. The dossier does not disclose ownership, salaries, or secondary liquidity, so economic alignment remains unverified, but there is no evidence of part-time founders or outsourced core engineering.

Michigan family caregivers are a suitably narrow beachhead. The problem is not whether the market exists; delayed enrollment and unpaid relatives are concrete failures, and Cova has crossed the licensing boundary to bill as the provider. The problem is value capture. A Michigan license is permission to compete, not a monopoly mechanism. Aidaly already runs software-powered family-care agencies across five states, while Honor and Cera demonstrate that well-financed providers can automate care operations. No exclusive payer relationship, proprietary dataset, dominant local share, measured network effect, or technical result prevents replication. The company is narrow, but not yet dominant; capitalism requires escape from competition, not merely competent entry into it.

The revenue engine is coherent—provide care and bill Medicaid, MCOs, long-term-care insurers, and private-pay families—but its arithmetic is hidden. There are no reimbursement rates, payer contracts, gross margins, completed claims, or customer counts. Direct billing could support a large multistate provider, yet geographic expansion repeats licensing, payer enrollment, and regulatory execution. The broad $162.35 billion market does not answer whether one agency can retain enough economics to produce a power-law outcome.

The bull case is sharper than the current evidence: two aligned healthcare operators might use a licensed agency as a laboratory, accumulate exception-handling data, and prove that an agency with effectively zero administrative staff has structurally superior margins. That would convert a labor business into proprietary operating infrastructure. But this is only a possible future moat, and the known exceptions do not rescue it: there is neither an independently verified zero-to-one technical result nor persistent transaction behavior overcoming an otherwise failed gate. The named referral routes—MCOs, hospitals, discharge planners, case managers, social workers, and families—also lack contracts, conversion rates, sales-cycle evidence, and acquisition economics.

I pass today, although narrowly. Cova has founders rather than professional managers and a definite operational experiment rather than a generic healthcare-AI slogan. But the experiment has not yet established the secret, and the market already contains a direct family-caregiver analogue. One verifiable condition would flip my decision: publish or diligence-verify at least six months of paid claims showing that one non-caregiver employee can support ten times the active cases of a conventional Michigan agency, with positive contribution margin and a repeatable payer or hospital referral channel. That would turn automation from a claim into proprietary technology—and a licensed agency from a commodity into the beginning of a last-mover position.
