# Mnemonic Labs -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The most useful number in Mnemonic Labs’ dossier is the company-attributed $20M annual PnL from Kalki’s work at Tower Research. It belongs to his history, not Mnemonic’s revenue. But it describes the right kind of learning: making decisions in a system that charges for mistakes. Videh’s quantitative research at Citadel and Walleye adds relevant experience. These founders have operated inside the problem. The reported six-week pipeline build gives that experience a current expression, although the prior PnL remains unverified and the present training run has no disclosed outcome.

Mnemonic’s investment case turns on whether that specific knowledge becomes an asset outside the founders’ heads. Its own architecture and a training run exceeding 25TB establish a concrete research effort. They do not establish an advantage that survives someone copying the code. No demonstrated model performance, exclusive data rights, or accumulated execution feedback answers that question yet. I assign the absent-evidence default for durability. Prodigy’s overlapping foundation-model effort makes the distinction consequential: several competent teams can train models; only some will discover an advantage worth owning.

The strongest objection to passing is that these founders may already understand something their employers’ operating horizons prevented them from pursuing. A small team with direct trading experience could produce a proprietary model before it produces customers, public benchmarks, or a sales organization. My exceptions for exceptional founders and unusual working prototypes matter here. But Mnemonic has disclosed the training effort without the result. The proposed scaling law remains the central experiment. Founder ability earns substantial credit; it cannot also stand in for proof that the experiment worked.

The commercial possibilities are broad but still inferred. Proprietary trading and licensing create different businesses, and Mnemonic has not selected a public payer, price, or initial customer. The cited $26.3B algorithmic-trading market establishes surrounding economic activity without establishing a route from this model into adjacent demand. Revenue therefore receives its default, as does market expansion. There is also no observed mechanism by which one participant improves another participant’s product or acquisition. My preference for permissionless contribution and customer-created value finds no affirmative evidence here; a private execution-feedback loop would need separate proof.

I would pass at this point, at **39/100**. The founders are the reason to keep watching. The dossier contains no evidence of dishonest conduct, artificial financing pressure, or dependence on investor reassurance; their earlier robotics direction alone establishes none of those red flags. The missing piece is an economically useful proprietary result. One verifiable flip condition would be an independently checked six-month live trial showing Mnemonic’s trained model consistently outperforming a conventional baseline using the same inputs and risk limits, after execution costs. That would turn a credible research team into evidence of an ownable advantage.
