# Orgo -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

What does Orgo believe that E2B and Daytona believe is false? The dossier establishes a useful product: persistent cloud desktops with files, browsers, terminals, and programmatic control. It does not establish a contested truth. The possible secret is that agencies deploying business agents need a persistent operating environment sufficiently different from developer sandboxes to support a separate company. That proposition is testable through comparative customer behavior, but it remains my hypothesis. Orgo’s comparison with general-purpose hosting does not answer its competition with purpose-built agent infrastructure.

The decisive problem is ownership of the value created. Daytona already offers overlapping persistent computers across operating systems; E2B includes graphical Linux desktops; Cua supplies open-source building blocks. Orgo’s templates and saved configurations could make migration inconvenient, but inconvenience becomes an investment advantage only when customers demonstrably stay or competitors cannot reproduce the economics. No such mechanism is established. Selling infrastructure across the AI production stack fits my allocation pattern, but infrastructure can become a commodity just as readily as an application. A growing number of agents does not determine which provider keeps the profits.

The strongest objection to passing is that Orgo may already have discovered its distribution wedge. Vasilescu operated computeruse.agency, the founders identify tutorials, podcast appearances, and managed-agent agencies as acquisition channels, and a customer reports agents operating inside three businesses. Peter Steinberger is identified as the first paying customer. The company also reports more than 1,000 teams and thousands of production desktops. Those are meaningful pre-seed signals: the team has shipped something people use. Agencies could concentrate many downstream deployments into a single customer relationship. But the dossier does not establish paid conversion, repeatable acquisition economics, or customer dependence on something unique to Orgo. The known exceptions to my monopoly requirement depend on a demonstrated technical discontinuity or customer behavior that directly removes the relevant risk. These usage claims establish demand; they do not remove substitution risk.

The founding evidence warrants neither a premium nor a red flag. Kinney and Vasilescu lived in the same Berkeley hacker house before joining forces, and the team includes a technical founder in Wang. That is more substance than convenience matching at a fundraising event. It does not establish all three founders’ prior working history, full-time status, ownership, or economic exposure. I would retain the stated default for founding alignment. There is no evidence here of outsourced core engineering, investor-majority ownership, or substantial founder liquidity.

Orgo has one intelligible revenue engine: subscriptions at $29, $99, and $399 monthly, supplemented by capacity purchases. At the Scale subscription price, $100 million in annual revenue would require approximately 20,900 accounts before additional capacity charges. That is arithmetic, not a forecast; customer spending expansion and compute margins remain unestablished. I pass because direct substitutes already contest the proposed position while the secret remains unspecified. One verifiable result would reopen the investment: an independently reproduced, tenfold advantage over E2B and Daytona in fully loaded cost per successfully completed persistent-agent workflow for a named customer segment. That would give Orgo a reason to own this market beyond reaching customers early.
