# OpenTag -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when something broke. These three founders have shipped a real product and brought ten teams into early access in two weeks, but the dossier never names an obstacle, the workaround they built, or how quickly it changed user behavior. Their previous work together at Lilac Labs makes them a credible team; it does not prove resourcefulness under resistance. Nor does the unexplained sequence from Lilac Labs and Gini to OpenTag show a running back changing routes on evidence. On my controlling criterion, the evidence remains at the default.

The strongest fact is the product itself. OpenTag is not a diagram waiting for funding. It runs delegated work in isolated cloud machines, connects to company tools, routes across more than 80 models, maintains source-linked memory, and puts approval gates and audit logs around external actions. Ten teams have apparently used the deployed system. That is substantial pre-permission technical proof. I stop short of the top score because every performance and security claim comes from OpenTag’s own materials, with no benchmark, independently measured threshold, or documented task completed better than the alternatives.

The ten early-access teams are promising but ambiguous. Six are named, and the website says they are doing real work, which is stronger than a waitlist. Yet there is no repeated-use figure, paid conversion, retention, referral, or workflow that users forced the product to support. The $50 monthly plan and $100 free trial establish an economic mechanism, not revenue. The simple question is still unanswered: which team needs this badly enough to keep summoning it after the novelty wears off?

The market evidence makes that question more urgent. Anthropic introduced the shared-agent-in-Slack interface roughly two months before OpenTag’s launch, while Dust and Lindy already cover adjacent shared-agent workflows. Model routing and Microsoft Teams support are useful differences, but they currently look like features rather than a narrow well. The founders have relevant enterprise, infrastructure, security, and healthcare-software experience, yet the dossier does not show that one of them personally collided with this problem. Without that origin, OpenTag risks being a technically impressive response to a category announcement.

There is a real bull case. The team has prior shared operating history, has already assembled a programmable workflow substrate, and reached ten teams unusually quickly. Those facts fit my unstated preference for technical founders who build infrastructure before declaring a platform. The usual red flags—an application-assembled team, partner-dependent distribution, premature hiring, or a launch as the whole strategy—are absent. But the concrete proof spike is still missing. None of the known exceptions supplies an override: there is neither independently validated technical performance nor unmistakable user pull strong enough to replace the failed resourcefulness gate.

My 36-month guess is that OpenTag will pivot from a general model-agnostic coworker into a narrower IT-operations and approval workflow inside Slack and Teams, remaining below $2 million ARR until it does. The team looks capable of building; the weak point is discovering what must be built. I would not write the check today. One condition would flip me: a dated account of a blocker encountered by one of the current ten teams, a workaround shipped within a week, and that team then using the changed workflow weekly for four consecutive weeks.
