# River -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

River reports $500,000 of YC financing, but that tells me neither the proposed post-money nor whether my check would fund something otherwise unfundable. Competing offers and round terms are undisclosed. I cannot call this an auction, and I cannot call it cheap. The second question is equally open: what specific truth does the next dollar reveal? The dossier describes a functioning product, but no capital-funded experiment with a metric and deadline. Those are missing underwriting inputs, not evidence that the founders have a bad plan.

The interesting asset is the workspace where River can act on local files and signed-in browser sessions. If researchers actually move their working lives there, the team could own an important customer relationship. But access to a customer's documents does not automatically give River proprietary data or durable distribution. Notion and Superhuman already have workflows, customers, and overlapping capabilities; standardized connections make access easier for competitors too. River needs evidence that its particular workspace causes people to stay and pay.

The strongest objection to passing is that two people have already shipped something with substantial reported adoption. The launch figures—50,000 users, 130,000 agents, and 200,000 files—are more interesting than a presentation about an AI computer. Supple brings Roboflow engineering experience; Joseff brings research experience and a founding-team role at Progressly before its acquisition by Box. That combination fits my pattern of founders translating between technical systems and an operating environment they understand. I give them execution credit. I do not yet have evidence of a proprietary route to researchers' purchasing decisions.

The homepage's newer claim of 100,000 creators makes that distinction more consequential. There could be a valuable business hiding inside those users. The dossier supplies no activity definition, retained cohort, or paid conversion, so the count cannot establish it. My exceptions to the price and AI gates have required hard economic evidence: paid enterprise demand, measured conversion and retention, acquisition interest, or a financing structure that materially changes the downside. River's reported file creation supports product execution, but does not establish those overrides. Nor does the absence of conversion data establish failed conversion.

The $14, $36, and $100 plans, metered usage markup, and separately billed overages provide a plausible way to charge for expensive computation. The included AI credits are billing units; I cannot subtract them from subscription prices and declare a loss. Equally, a markup does not establish company profitability after support, acquisition, and operating costs. Delete the next financing from the model and I still cannot calculate the destination. External models and overlapping incumbent products make retained customer cash contribution the useful metric here. The two CEO listings also leave decision authority unresolved, although they do not prove equal ownership or committee governance.

I pass at **30/100**. River's strongest evidence concerns the team's ability to build; my largest economic questions concern whether anyone must keep buying what they built. One verifiable flip condition would be a proposed round below $10 million post-money whose budget funds a dated experiment demonstrating that an identifiable cohort of research teams renews with enough cash contribution to reach break-even without another raise. That would connect the shipped product to scarce capital, owned workflow, and an independently viable business.
