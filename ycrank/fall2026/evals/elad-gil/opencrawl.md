# OpenCrawl -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Agent adoption creates demand for continuously maintained data, but OpenCrawl’s investment case depends on whether developers buy bespoke retrieval infrastructure instead of assembling it from increasingly complete platforms. The controlling variable is the cost of maintaining a reliable customer-specific collection relative to those alternatives. The dossier describes the product, but supplies no measured maintenance advantage or threshold at which customers switch. I can identify the bet; I cannot yet establish that its economics work.

The market is already crystallizing. Exa reports more than $100 million in annualized revenue, while Firecrawl’s September 2026 Alexandria release combines source discovery, connectors and hosted indexes. Those figures establish demand and competitive investment around the category, not demand for OpenCrawl. Bespoke pipelines could address requirements that shared indexes handle poorly, but Firecrawl’s custom connectors make that boundary less comfortable. Investing across an infrastructure transition makes sense when each company captures a distinct layer. Here the layers already overlap.

The strongest bull case is that OpenCrawl actually operates the collection-to-retrieval workflow. Its disclosed product discovers sources, hosts crawlers, processes content and maintains indexes, with access through MCP, CLI and API. That is substantive operational functionality. Better models could improve source selection and extraction while the company continues running the underlying infrastructure. MCP’s adoption across more than 10,000 public servers by December 2025 also provides a concrete distribution interface. However, server adoption does not quantify OpenCrawl’s deployment savings or margins, and maintained indexes alone do not establish costly-to-reproduce data or durable switching costs.

Patel’s ingestion work at Pocus and Utkarsh’s query-compiler and agent experience make this bull case credible enough to take seriously. The founders have worked together, and their previous OnePatch product reportedly ingested 32 TB of telemetry and detected or investigated more than 600 incidents. That history supports their ability to build operational infrastructure. It does not establish repeat demand for the new search product, documented lessons from a prior startup, or personal sacrifice. I would neither transfer OnePatch’s usage into OpenCrawl’s traction score nor manufacture founder motivation from their résumés.

OpenCrawl’s $25 starter grant and $10 minimum top-ups provide an onboarding mechanism, but the unpublished rate card leaves the commercial unit unclear. The dossier supplies no current search customers, paid consumption or repeat usage. Those receive the specified missing-evidence defaults; LinkedIn followers add nothing. The positive workflow evidence is real, but neither frontier research nor a demonstrated economic step change supplies a reason to override the missing market proof.

I would pass with my own money today. My single flip condition is a documented 90-day cohort of five independent paying customers that renew their bespoke collections after comparing them with Firecrawl, with measured maintenance costs supporting positive contribution margins. That would test the actual proposition: whether customer-specific retrieval creates a repeatable business that existing platforms leave open.
