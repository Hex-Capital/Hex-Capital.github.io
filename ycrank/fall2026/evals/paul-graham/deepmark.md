# DeepMark -- Paul Graham Evaluation

DeepMark has shipped software and put it into production. What I cannot find is the episode that would tell me most about its founders: something broke, they found a way around it, and a customer’s situation changed. Six years working together is encouraging. So is turning university watermarking research into a commercial product. But neither establishes how Slavko and Kosta behave when their first solution fails. I would keep looking at DeepMark, but I would not write a personal check yet.

The technical work makes this worth investigating. The public AWARE implementation and benchmark tooling give me something more substantial than credentials or an architecture diagram. The proprietary Vigil model also addresses a specific difficulty: keeping a watermark detectable through telephone transformations. But the published comparison uses simulated telephone paths, and its speed comparison excludes AudioSeal’s streaming checkpoint. DeepMark itself says actual customer routes need evaluation. I give substantial credit for the functioning artifact, with limited credit for the claimed advantage. A telephone simulation cannot establish reliability on the call a bank actually receives.

The strongest argument for investing is that customers have already crossed the research-to-production gap. The founders report a platform license signed before YC, and Confident Security supplies a named testimonial describing production use. Pencil is another named customer reference. These are meaningful pre-seed signals even without disclosed contract values. They fit a pattern I like: technical founders building an underlying capability that other companies put into their own products. The working-product exception to my founder-evidence requirement deserves consideration here. But neither the customer accounts nor the technical results establish such overwhelming pull that I can comfortably substitute them for evidence of resourcefulness. A license proves someone bought; it does not yet show how indispensable the product became.

The important commercial question is what the customer is buying. Meta supplies AudioSeal under an MIT license, including optional identifiers. DeepMark therefore needs to make deployment and authorization sufficiently useful that customers prefer paying it to assembling those pieces themselves. The founders’ prior watermarking research gives their choice of problem credibility. Telephone integration and downstream identity resolution also contain real, tedious work. But research exposure does not establish that they discovered an urgent customer problem firsthand. Nor does the April 2026 Ortana Cubix integration establish distribution for telephone authentication. I would worry about dependence on partners if that were the entire acquisition plan; the separate production license keeps that red flag from being established here.

DeepMark also has no documented sequence of a rejected hypothesis, customer observation, and revised product. Moving from media provenance toward voice-agent identity might eventually supply that evidence, but the dossier does not establish that causal history. I will not award flexibility points for an inferred pivot. The uncertainty is concentrated: these founders demonstrably know how to build watermarking technology, while their speed of learning under customer resistance remains largely unobserved.

One verifiable event would change my decision: a named production customer documenting a failed real telephone route, a founder-built fix shipped within weeks, and continued paid deployment after that fix met the customer’s stated acceptance threshold. That would connect the technical work to founder behavior and an urgent user need. DeepMark already has enough substance to make that a plausible next result. It has not supplied it yet.
