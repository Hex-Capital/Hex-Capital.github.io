# Wingman Law -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI case-execution tools could let Wingman Law serve accident victims with substantially less labor per recovery, but the economic variable is contribution profit per attorney-hour after client acquisition, case expenses, and financing costs. The dossier does not establish that variable. A $57.8B personal-injury legal-services market is large enough; its reported 1.03% annual growth means Wingman must gain share or change delivery economics. Contingency fees give the firm exposure to successful outcomes, while also making collection timing and case selection central to the business.

The timing is credible, but the economic discontinuity remains unmeasured. Supio’s May 2026 launch of agents spanning intake through resolution suggests more of the work is becoming technically addressable. It also gives incumbent firms access to overlapping capabilities. Wingman therefore needs an advantage from reorganizing the practice around automation, beyond access to automation itself. There are no measured labor savings, faster recoveries, or margin improvements in the dossier. I give the dated capability expansion modest credit, without assuming it creates superior firm economics.

Direct representation is Wingman Law’s strongest architectural choice. Its attorneys direct agents processing claim data, policy language, medical evidence, and insurer behavior, and the firm takes responsibility for the client’s matter. Better models could improve a service Wingman already delivers rather than replace a standalone document feature. That earns meaningful workflow credit. However, documented integrations, persistent case state, and outcome-driven feedback loops are absent. The public visualization uses licensed litigation data; a proprietary learning advantage remains a hypothesis.

The strongest argument against passing is that these founders may understand exactly where existing practice economics break. Nicholas Le brings experience across more than 700 prior injury cases, and Sun Woo Lee previously co-founded Cromatic and generated a reported $350K in legaltech sales during law school. That is relevant operating experience, not merely prestigious credentials. It makes the integrated-service thesis more credible. Those figures nevertheless belong to prior work: Wingman has disclosed no current client counts, fees, recoveries, or repeat usage. Branded-car advertising and requests for introductions establish acquisition activity, without establishing product-market pull.

I would pass on a personal check today. Wingman fits my pattern of backing software that performs institutional work, and the service-business exception could apply if technology materially changes delivery margins. That reconstruction has not been demonstrated. The scarce-frontier-research exception does not apply on the supplied evidence, and the red flag to watch is a conventional labor-intensive practice receiving software-style underwriting. My single flip condition is an independently reviewable cohort of 50 resolved matters showing at least twice a comparable conventional practice’s contribution profit per attorney-hour, after acquisition, case expenses, and financing costs, with comparable recoveries and error rates. That would establish the mechanism this investment needs.
