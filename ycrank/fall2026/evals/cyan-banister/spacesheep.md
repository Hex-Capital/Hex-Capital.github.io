# Spacesheep -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine asking an agent for a decision and receiving a small, usable world: change an assumption, inspect the consequence, leave a question exactly where something looks wrong. Spacesheep’s interactive HTML surfaces, anchored comments, and agent edits are a little leak from that future. But publishing those surfaces is only the first transition. Teams must also learn to understand them, trust them, and preserve the decisions made inside them. The dossier establishes a working publishing mechanism; it does not yet establish that larger change in behavior.

Michael Makarov is the strongest reason to take this seriously. He built internal deployment tools and the first CC Artifacts version at Anthropic, after staff-engineering work at Pinecone and Twitter. Those are relevant things built inside the problem, rather than impressive employers pasted onto an unrelated idea. Spacesheep fits my pattern of an operating irritation becoming a product specification. Still, that record establishes technical proximity more clearly than independent obsession: the dossier supplies little pre-company experimentation, complementary recruiting, or evidence that Michael can turn this particular tool into a business.

Why does reviewing an agent’s work still mean digging through its conversation? Spacesheep identifies a specific tax: people reconstruct context from generated text and code, then manually translate feedback into another request. Location-specific comments and retained versions could return some of that time and control to teammates. That is useful workflow repair. The missing piece is evidence of how much labor disappears. A prettier surface can also make an unexamined conclusion more persuasive; the Product Hunt question about whether the examples actually reduce overload goes straight to the investment mechanism.

The strongest argument for writing a small check is that Michael has already built the relevant machinery, the product exists, and James Briggs reportedly uses it for coding plans, explanations, and reviews. I do invest before revenue when the artifact and founder record carry the uncertainty. But Spacesheep’s October 27 snapshot—22 Chrome-extension users, one Chrome rating, and two Firefox reviews—does not show the repeated, inconvenient behavior that makes me lean forward. The named use case matters more than 180 Product Hunt upvotes, yet its frequency and depth remain unspecified. Prices of $4.50, $9, and $10 per team member establish an offer, not willingness to pay.

The uncomfortable counterfactual is that people love this behavior and adopt it inside Notion instead. Notion already supports external agents and interactive HTML blocks. Spacesheep’s acceptance of multiple agents avoids the explicit dependence on one closed supplier that would trouble me, but independence alone does not establish a scarce enabling layer. Organizational memory might eventually become one; the dossier labels that advantage inferred. Private defaults, revocable connections, and sandbox isolation are affirmative privacy mitigations, so I would not manufacture a severe-privacy red flag from the presence of sensitive documents. They also do not settle consent and custody for every connected workflow.

I would hold my personal check today. Spacesheep has a credible builder and a future I can inhabit, with weaker evidence that its workspace becomes indispensable. My one flip condition is an inspectable eight-week cohort of five independent paying teams that repeatedly conduct agent-output reviews in Spacesheep, with usage records showing shorter review cycles than their previous workflow. That would connect the lovely little surface to an actual change in how people work.
