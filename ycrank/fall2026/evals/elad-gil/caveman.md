# Caveman -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Long-running agents create a buyer for Caveman: engineering teams whose inference bills grow faster than their confidence in cheaper configurations. The controlling variable is provider-billed cost per successfully completed task, including Caveman’s fees. If the product reliably lowers that number, deployment can follow. Token reduction alone does not establish the business. The dossier identifies a real purchasing bottleneck—uncertainty about changing configurations—but does not yet establish where optimization becomes economically compelling.

The strongest available economic test is uncomfortable. JetBrains measured 8.5% fewer output tokens for the earlier skill, yet aggregate billed cost increased 11.6% because of an outlier. That does not falsify the Cloud product launched October 13, 2026; it does show why optimizing a component can worsen the customer’s actual bill. Caveman’s reported 89% token removal with 94% evidence retention is a technical result, discounted as company-reported evidence. It does not establish equivalent task completion or net savings. The agent-spending wave is credible; Caveman’s ability to capture its economics remains unproven.

The newer architecture is considerably more interesting than the original compression proposition. Recording workloads, replaying proposed changes against evaluations, and deploying through shadow tests and approved canaries creates an operational loop. Better models could increase the number of useful changes Caveman can safely deploy. This fits my preference for infrastructure that gains value as the surrounding model ecosystem improves. Documentation supports that architecture, although customer-specific histories becoming a durable switching cost remains an inference.

The opposing case has substantial distribution behind it. Approximately 115.5K GitHub stars, 6.7K forks, and 20,000 users of the earlier browser extension are meaningful evidence that Julius Brussee can attract developers. GreenPT’s integration adds an actual distribution relationship. A founder who converts even a small fraction of that audience into production optimization customers could build quickly. But those assets largely precede Cloud: they establish an audience and some earlier-product usage, rather than repeat demand for the present paid workflow. Brussee’s agent-runtime and retrieval experience supports execution credibility; his CV does not document the costly commitment or prior startup learning that would earn additional founder-conviction credit.

Market structure is the other constraint. LiteLLM already mediates model traffic, and Edgee combines compression, routing, and attribution. Caveman must persuade customers to introduce another control layer while gateways can add overlapping functionality. Apache-2.0 code makes the implementation reproducible; the harder asset would be trusted evaluation and deployment history, whose reproduction cost is not established. My pattern of investing across a technology transition makes this layer interesting, but does not establish Caveman’s winner case. Neither the rare frontier-model team exception nor a demonstrated transformation of incumbent economics supplies an override here.

I would pass with my own money today. The strongest evidence concerns the workflow Caveman could own; the weakest concerns the economic outcome customers would purchase. That distinction matters more than the founder’s polish or the size of the repository audience. My flip condition is one production cohort test: three paying teams sustaining at least 20% lower provider-billed cost, after Caveman’s fees, for 90 days at an unchanged, predefined task-success threshold. That would connect the architecture to customer economics and repeat purchasing in one verifiable result.
