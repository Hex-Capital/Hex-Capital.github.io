# FlowManual -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

FlowManual addresses hundreds of monthly administrative hours and occasional $5K–$10K clause mistakes. Those are real costs. They are not a 100x consequence. The dossier offers a $7.7 billion software TAM but never translates the product into an order-of-magnitude reduction in construction cost, project delay, material waste, or expert labor. This is currently a useful back-office tool, not an option on rebuilding construction economics.

The technical case is weaker. FlowManual combines bid-to-contract review, invoice reconciliation, takeoffs, and change-order workflows, but breadth is not discontinuity. There is no measured accuracy, review-time reduction, or loss-prevention benchmark against Document Crunch, Trunk Tools, or manual review. Extracted text goes to Anthropic, overlapping competitors already provide source-linked document analysis, and customer data is not pooled without opt-in. That produces privacy, not a proprietary learning system. The company is competing through workflow packaging around a third-party model—the precise AI pattern I distrust.

The bull case stings. Two founders began building on April 18, interviewed hundreds of construction professionals within two months, entered YC, and shipped a free product spanning an unusually complete purchasing workflow. Document Crunch’s use by 500-plus companies and Trimble acquisition validate an expensive existing demand pool. My unstated pattern includes execution-heavy software when an initially modest wedge can expand into infrastructure. The known exception for an incremental product entering a giant transaction pool therefore deserves consideration.

It does not apply yet. That exception requires an extraordinary team learning faster than incumbents and a wedge already showing platform pull. David Bae and Michael Lin are capable young engineers, and Lin has experience associated with Dijie’s growth from $3K to $336K MRR, but the dossier documents neither construction operating experience, a material belief revision, nor recruitment of someone who owns construction risk. College roommates from adjacent Harvard engineering backgrounds are not yet risk-tuned gene-pool engineering.

The first $500K should attack one kill risk: can the system find financially consequential discrepancies with reliability high enough that contractors repeatedly trust it? Instead, FlowManual exposes a wide surface across drawings, bids, contracts, invoices, submittals, RFIs, and change orders without publishing a pass/fail threshold, controlled comparison, or repeat-usage result. SOC 2 work and audit logs are necessary, but they do not answer the technical question. My 36-month base case is that FlowManual narrows to buyout-to-invoice reconciliation and remains below $1 million ARR because broader document intelligence commoditizes faster than contractor-specific data compounds.

The economics are similarly unproven. A free tier and custom-priced Pro plan reveal neither inference cost nor willingness to pay, and no scale model shows savings exceeding incumbent labor and software costs. I would not invest my own money today. One verifiable result flips the decision: three named contractors must each process at least 100 projects, retain for six months, and demonstrate an independently audited fivefold reduction in review labor or avoidable losses versus their prior workflow. That would convert a wrapper into evidence of a discontinuity.
