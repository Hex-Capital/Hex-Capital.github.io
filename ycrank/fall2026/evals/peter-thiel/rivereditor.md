# River -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

River’s strongest possible secret is that owning the whole working environment makes AI substantially more useful than adding agents to existing applications. That proposition is testable: give researchers comparable tasks, files, and model access, then measure completed work and sustained preference against incumbent workspaces. The dossier establishes that River built the environment. It does not establish the claimed advantage, identify peers who reject the proposition, or report a comparative result. I assign the missing-secret default; an unproved secret is different from a disproved one.

The more immediate problem is ownership of the resulting market. Researchers, consultants, and authors describe several audiences, without identifying a beachhead River can dominate. Superhuman Docs already combines documents, AI, and external connections; Notion brings agents to an established workspace. Local files and signed-in-browser access distinguish River’s implementation, but the dossier provides no evidence that this distinction produces proprietary technology, network effects, or durable switching costs. This is affirmative competitive overlap, which warrants a score below the absent-evidence default for monopoly.

The strongest objection to passing is that River may already have discovered something through use that its public description fails to explain. The company claims 100,000+ creators; its founder reports 50,000+ AI Desktop users, 130,000+ agents, and 200,000+ files. Those figures deserve attention for a two-person company. River’s individual-user entry point also fits the consumer-software side of my actual investment pattern. But these are overlapping, differently defined claims, not a growth series or evidence of retained demand. The OpenAI exception shows why uncertain monopoly need not prevent investment when a genuinely new technical capability is demonstrated. River shares the monopoly uncertainty; it has not supplied the technical proof that justified that exception.

The founders are the strongest dimension. Supple and Joseff reportedly spent ten months building together in Joseff’s garage, giving their relationship more substance than a team assembled for an application. Supple’s engineering background and Joseff’s experience on Progressly’s founding team support their ability to build, although credentials cannot establish economic alignment. Neither part-time employment nor outsourced core engineering is documented. Full-time status, ownership, compensation, and decision authority remain unverified; the two CEO titles require clarification rather than an invented governance indictment. The evidence supports an intermediate founding-team score.

River has one intelligible revenue engine: subscriptions at $14, $36, and $100 per person monthly, plus usage billing. At $36, one million paying seats would generate $432 million in annual subscription revenue—a scale illustration, not a projection. The unresolved question is how much value remains after serving those seats. Included AI credits are retail allowances, not disclosed supplier costs, so they establish neither attractive margins nor losses. Free signup, desktop downloads, and reported adoption provide some distribution evidence, but no identified acquisition source, paid conversion, or repeatable channel economics.

I pass on the current evidence. The decisive red flag is broad competition without an observable mechanism for retaining value; founder misconduct and technical refutation are not established. River’s reported activity does not directly remove that competitive risk. One verifiable flip condition would reopen the decision: a controlled, paid deployment in a named research niche demonstrating at least a tenfold improvement in completed-work throughput over incumbent workflows, sustained for three months. That would connect River’s architectural claim to a bounded market worth owning.
