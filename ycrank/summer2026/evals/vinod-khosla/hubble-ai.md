# Hubble -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Hubble has found an expensive nuisance, not yet a 100x consequence. Medical records still move through faxes, portals, release queues, and human phone calls; converting hours of paralegal work into minutes and recovering $340K in denied claims over 90 days proves real customer pain. But the dossier quantifies neither the total cost of this labor nor how many patients or organizations could see an order-of-magnitude improvement. An $8.57B projected interoperability market is attractive. It is not evidence that Hubble rewrites healthcare economics.

The strongest evidence is the product itself. Forty-plus production tools span Epic, Cerner, athenahealth, UnitedHealthcare, and Aetna. Combining APIs with browser and voice agents attacks a concrete incumbent failure mode: critical data remains inaccessible whenever FHIR coverage ends. The reported improvement from hours to minutes is potentially greater than 10x, while patient cost-estimate accuracy moving from 50% to 94% shows consequential workflow value. Yet these are anonymized outcomes without retrieval volume, completion rate, accuracy methodology, or cost per successful record. This may be clever technical infrastructure, or it may be implementation labor hidden behind an API.

Prabha Dublish and Aaron Leon have unusually direct founder-market fit. Dublish was Grow Therapy’s first product hire; Leon built Amazon Care’s data platform and One Medical infrastructure serving more than one million patients. Their decade-long working relationship reduces team risk. What is missing is evidence of the behavior I care about most: a material belief revised after data, a difficult complementary recruit won, or a weakness assigned to a specialist. Relevant résumés establish competence. They do not establish exceptional learning rate.

The largest kill risk is whether retrieval agents can reliably overcome hundreds of changing portals, consent regimes, and release processes at software margins. Hubble does not name a decisive experiment, threshold, cost, or stop condition for that risk. Protected-record rules fragment the one-consent promise, external systems control latency, and even the published legal workflow takes one week. Pricing and unit economics are also absent, so I cannot tell whether browser and voice escalation becomes cheaper with scale or merely accumulates operations cost. Capital should first establish completion rate, median latency, human-intervention minutes, and contribution margin by retrieval source.

The bull case stings. A two-person team already has production integrations and customer outcomes that many pre-seed companies only promise. My actual investing pattern allows execution-heavy infrastructure when usage creates a proprietary learning loop, and Hubble could learn which retrieval route succeeds for every provider and record type. The exception case would be a modest initial tool evolving into a broad patient-data control plane. But no retrieval volume, retention, or learning-rate evidence establishes that loop today, while Particle Health, 1upHealth, Health Gorilla, and PicnicHealth have each raised at least $49M. The current differentiation could be copied by well-capitalized competitors or compressed by improving exchange infrastructure.

My August 2029 base case is that Hubble becomes a $2M–$5M ARR workflow vendor concentrated in dental denials or legal record retrieval, rather than the horizontal intelligence layer for patient information. I pass today. I would flip if three named customers independently demonstrate at least a 10x reduction in fully loaded retrieval cost, above 95% record-completion coverage, and positive contribution margin over six months; that would convert an integration service into a scalable technical discontinuity.
