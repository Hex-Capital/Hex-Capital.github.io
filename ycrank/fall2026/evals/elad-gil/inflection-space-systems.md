# Inflection Space Systems -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Inflection Space Systems is betting that constellation demand now supports a standardized spacecraft factory, with customers accepting a fixed platform in exchange for lower prices and faster delivery. The controlling variable is **annual demand that fits Exponent without custom engineering**. Enough compatible orders would spread engineering and factory costs across identical units. Customization would put the company back into the bespoke economics it wants to replace. The published 100 kg payload and 1.5 kW power envelope makes that proposition testable, but the dossier does not establish the order volume or manufacturing cost at which it works.

The pricing curve exposes the challenge. Inflection quotes $1.5M for one spacecraft and $700K each for one hundred—a 53% reduction. That is a customer discount, not evidence of a production-cost advantage. At $700K per spacecraft, the target of 10,000 annually implies $7B in annual sales, close to the cited $7.31B forecast for the entire satellite-bus market in 2031. Neither estimate establishes a ceiling, but together they show that the factory ambition requires substantial market expansion or extraordinary share. A standardized product is a credible mechanism; its economic threshold remains unproven.

There is a real institutional change behind the timing. SDA’s December 2025 awards committed approximately $3.5B to 72 tracking satellites. Those awards establish funded demand, although they went to teams led by established contractors and do not establish demand for Exponent. Meanwhile, Apex has raised over $718M and already pursues productized spacecraft manufacturing. This market is crystallizing around funded suppliers. Inflection needs a specific production advantage and a route into procurement; additional industry spending alone will not create either.

The strongest argument against passing is that this team has worked directly on the bottleneck. Matt Hanley automated satellite integration and testing at Loft Orbital, and Alex Lisso’s experience spans spacecraft design, manufacturing, testing, and operations. Inflection also reports completing more than half its internally designed stack, including flight and ground software. That gives the company a plausible foundation for owning the manufacturing and operational loop as automation improves. It fits my pattern of backing physical businesses where expertise can change economics. However, the reported stack completion does not establish automated throughput, qualification yield, or gross margin. The biographies establish relevant experience, but contain no documented prior founder learning or costly commitment warranting additional motivation credit.

I would pass with my own money today. The booked Q1 2028 launch is a development milestone; it is not customer pull, and followers, investor logos, and recruiting do not substitute for orders. Missing demand receives the pre-seed default rather than being treated as failed adoption. My exceptions for research-stage investments require distinctive frontier research and a credible capital path, while my physical-business exceptions require demonstrated economic reconstruction; neither is established here. **One verifiable flip condition:** a customer-backed production run showing that Exponent can be delivered within its published envelope at a positive gross margin at the $700K volume price, without bespoke engineering. That would connect demand, standardization, and manufacturing economics—the connection currently missing.
