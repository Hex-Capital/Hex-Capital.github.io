# Robocurve -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock forward six years. Robots are doing useful work across factories, warehouses, laboratories, and homes, and buyers no longer accept a beautiful demonstration as evidence that a machine will survive Tuesday afternoon. Someone must provide the measurement layer across models, embodiments, and jobs. Robocurve has found a real leak from that future: it completed a frontier-model pilot on physical hardware, while Inspect Robots records grader scores, configurations, transcripts, traces, and visualizations. But the dossier does not yet map the intervening steps by which one pilot becomes the trusted institution for robot capability.

The founders fit the work unusually well. Jay Chooi contributed to Inspect Evals at the UK AI Security Institute; Aris Zhu worked across Amazon Robotics, Amazon AGI Labs, and Yondu Robotics. Their public footprint includes approximately 400 commits, 13 repositories, an IEEE robotics paper, and research at ICML, ACL, and EMNLP. That is more than two smart graduates encountering a fashionable category. It shows evaluation fluency joined to physical-systems experience, although the dossier does not establish a long pre-company obsession, exceptional recruiting pull, or prior commercial command.

The most promising mechanism sits beneath the visible robotics wave. Every model laboratory can publish its own success video, but robot purchasers need comparable evidence across hardware and jobs. Inspect Robots is already cross-embodiment, MIT-licensed, available through PyPI, and supported by 134 GitHub stars and 19 forks. Those are useful artifacts, not merely pitch-deck claims. If evaluations accumulate into a trusted cross-model history, Robocurve could own the reference layer even while giving away the software.

Here is the sting: openness may distribute the framework without concentrating the institution. Positronic already runs physical VLA benchmarks, Instance is automating rollout judging and resets, and ReSim orchestrates testing across simulation and hardware. Laboratories can also internalize this work. The company has not named the specific rule, fee, procurement failure, or economic rent that forces customers toward an independent evaluator. “Labs grade themselves” is an institutional weakness, but it does not yet tell me who pays, how much failed evaluation costs, or whose agency Robocurve restores.

The first real-robot pilot is therefore less informative than the GitHub activity initially makes it feel. There are no disclosed repeat evaluations, paying engagements, named customers, or users rearranging expensive hardware schedules around the benchmark. The pre-revenue robotics exception does not require revenue, but it does require unusually strong substitution evidence—repeat prototypes, signed industrial commitments, or behavior proving that the product has become operationally necessary. One pilot with results still pending does not clear that bar.

I find no evidence of a leadership shell game, severe surveillance architecture, or contempt for the hidden work of operating physical systems; Robocurve appears to understand that reproducibility requires maintained hardware, controlled setups, resets, and domain experts. My concern is the subtler red flag: a crowded 2026 AI category paired with an asset everyone may reuse and a future dataset that remains hypothetical. I would not write the check today. I would flip to invest if three independent robotics organizations paid for repeated cross-embodiment evaluations and permitted Robocurve to publish a shared score history that neither an internal lab nor a software fork could reproduce.
