# CarSignal -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the compelling version is not “auto shops use AI.” It is that every vehicle carries a continuous service history, routine diagnosis begins before the car reaches a bay, and independent mechanics regain time currently lost translating phone calls, sticky notes, parts catalogs, and payment systems into one another. CarSignal shows the present-day leak—a working vehicle record spanning intake through payment—but the dossier does not map the transitions from today’s workflow software to that future. Voice agents becoming available in spring 2026 explain the current product wave; they do not explain what CarSignal uniquely enables six years from now.

The founders have the right wound. Both reportedly grew up in mechanic shops and watched their families operate back offices through paper and disconnected tools. That is much better than discovering “automotive AI” from a market report, and Michael Muzzin’s electrical-engineering and computer-science coursework is directionally useful. But I cannot yet see the first-believer trail: no dated pre-company builds, prior shop experiments, recruiting history, or evidence that either founder personally mastered the operational economics of estimates, parts margins, technician utilization, and customer trust. The biography establishes proximity, not yet obsession.

The strongest fact is behavioral. Shops in 14 states have apparently adopted the product, and every shop that began migration reportedly completed it. Changing a shop’s system of record is costly behavior: repair histories, estimates, payments, and daily technician work all have to move. That signal stings against my pass case. The denominator is undisclosed, however, and there are no dated cohorts, paying-shop counts, retention figures, or examples of shops repeatedly trusting the diagnostic copilot. A 100% migration statistic could mean two shops. CarSignal has exposed the artifact; it has not shown the ritual around it.

The economics are sensible but do not reveal a broken rule. Charging 1% only on paid invoices, capped at $200 per shop each month, lowers purchase anxiety and avoids per-seat punishment. Unlimited users may give a shop owner more control than feature-tiered software does. Still, the dossier identifies workflow fragmentation rather than the rent behind it: who profits from the fragmentation, what delay or fee it imposes, and how much agency returns to mechanics remain unquantified. At the cap, CarSignal also needs roughly 417 fully paying shops for $1 million in annual recurring revenue, so founder-led demos and $500 referral payments must become unusually efficient distribution.

The bear case wins below the interface. CarSignal depends on PartsTech, Stripe, Plaid, QuickBooks, and unspecified AI capabilities while controlling no disclosed model, proprietary diagnostic asset, distribution channel, or structural bottleneck. Parrot is already selling agents to independent shops, and Shopmonkey and Shop-Ware already cover much of the operating workflow. The privacy architecture is appropriately cautious—shop data is not used to train shared models without explicit consent—but that also makes the inferred cross-shop data moat less automatic. I see no proven surveillance violation or leadership shell game, yet the unnamed second founder, undisclosed governance, and absence of an accountable decision-maker are diligence gaps I would not waive.

I pass today. The bull case is real: founders raised around mechanic shops have shipped an integrated product, persuaded businesses across 14 states to attempt painful migrations, and aligned price with completed work. That could become durable vertical infrastructure. My verifiable flip condition is a cohort of 50 paying shops retained for twelve months with at least 70% of their repair orders moving from intake through payment inside CarSignal. That would show that the company owns the operating loop rather than merely bundling fashionable features.
