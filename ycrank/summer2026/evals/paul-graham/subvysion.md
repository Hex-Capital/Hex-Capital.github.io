# SubVysion -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The hardest obstacle the founders have visibly overcome is getting into the field. They did not wait for a construction partner or hire salespeople. They cold-called, found their first customer and investor, secured contracts, and put the product on job sites across the U.S. That is real founder behavior. But the dossier does not identify the rejection or operational failure they encountered, how quickly they responded, or what they changed afterward. I see resourcefulness, but not yet the complete proof of it.

The stronger evidence is technical. Maxwell Stone previously built on-device inference software deployed in more than 10,000 pharmacies and worked on railyard mapping; Wenson Tang built a LiDAR search-and-rescue drone. Together they have assembled a rover-based system combining ground-penetrating radar, positioning, geospatial processing, 3D maps, and phone AR, and it is reportedly operating on construction sites. This is much harder than making a convincing demo. The missing measurement matters, however. “Centimeter accuracy” and “100% visibility” remain marketing claims until tested against known underground assets, particularly in wet or clay soil and on small-diameter utilities.

There is a narrow well: contractors about to dig where an inaccurate utility map can cause an expensive strike. These users should tolerate a crude interface if it reliably prevents one. Contracts and live sites are stronger than a waitlist, but SubVysion does not disclose a named contractor, revenue, repeat projects, or crews changing their workflow around the product. The $355,000 SAFE financing proves investors were persuaded, not that contractors were. Demand therefore clears the initial gate only narrowly.

The bull case is that the labor is the wedge. Every scan, excavation, and correction could improve a proprietary subsurface dataset while mobile AR becomes the interface crews use in the field. Cold calling and personally serving sites would then be learning, not a scalability defect. But this mechanism is still hypothetical. Exodigo already claims 86.1 million square feet mapped and more than 100 customers after raising over $200 million; 4M and RodRadar attack adjacent parts of the same workflow. SubVysion must learn something from each job that compounds faster than the fieldwork grows. No dataset size, correction loop, throughput figure, or switching cost is yet visible.

The unstated-pattern test is only partly satisfied. The team shipped a difficult system before institutional permission, but it has not produced the numerical proof spike that would make the technical claim unusually credible. The red-flag test is mixed: the founders joined forces only in January 2026, so there is no demonstrated history of surviving disagreement, but they are selling personally and are not depending on a ceremonial launch, large partner, or premature sales hire. None of the known exceptions resolves the short cofounder history yet; those exceptions require either independently measured proof or unmistakable user pull.

My 36-month base case is that SubVysion becomes an operations-heavy utility-mapping vendor with $1 million to $3 million in annualized revenue, rather than a national software platform, because the team has proved it can ship the instrument but not yet that it can alter the route when sensing limits or project economics resist it. I would not write the check today. One condition would flip me: three contractors paying for a second site within 90 days of their first, after blind comparisons show the maps found utilities missed by their previous process. That would simultaneously prove repeat pull and turn the accuracy claim into an inspectable fact.
