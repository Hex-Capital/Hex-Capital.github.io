# Litmus -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important fact is missing. Shaivi Rau and Elena Zhao encountered technical recruiting firsthand and built a company from their Columbia dorms, but the dossier never says what broke, what blocked them, or what they shipped in response. Founder-led demos and free assessment builds show work, not resourcefulness under resistance. I cannot infer the decisive founder trait from $60K ARR because that revenue belongs to the demand evidence. On the criterion I weight most, Litmus remains at the absent-evidence default.

The product itself is much stronger. Litmus turns an employer’s repositories, tickets, and job descriptions into assessments, lets candidates use their normal environment and AI tools, and combines recorded workflow with deterministic sandbox tests and configurable LLM graders. More than 3,000 assessments have passed through this deployed system. That is a real technical artifact built before institutional permission, not a diagram. But the company has not published grading error rates, predictive-validity results, or evidence that its proprietary data produces better hiring decisions. The machine works; whether it measures the right thing remains unproved.

The narrow well is visible. Neo Scholars and its portfolio use a Litmus-built hiring system, Autumn AI and Sleuth appear as customers, and contracts have reached $60K ARR. These employers are not merely joining a waitlist; they are paying to replace artificial coding screens with company-specific work trials. This is also the sort of concrete pre-round proof spike that repeatedly matters more than a polished market story. Still, Litmus does not disclose customer count, renewals, assessment frequency, or weekly growth, so I cannot tell whether customers pull the product back repeatedly or merely try one free custom build.

The origin and the schlep are credible. The founders became friends while undergoing technical recruiting; Zhao worked as an engineer at Meta and Two Sigma, while Rau had startup, venture, and hiring exposure. The ugly work includes repository ingestion, ATS integrations with Ashby, Greenhouse, and Lever, custom assessment construction, candidate recording, employer security, and grading calibration. Those burdens make this less like a manufactured “AI for recruiting” idea. But there is no documented rejected hypothesis or before-and-after product route, so flexibility under contrary evidence also stays at its default.

The bear case stings because Litmus may be a useful feature surrounded by large vendors. HackerRank, CodeSignal, and Karat already possess customer relationships, assessment data, AI-enabled workflows, and ATS channels. They can copy repository-derived tasks while Litmus must persuade employers to transmit sensitive code and candidates to surrender prompts, audio, video, and assessment-expression rights. The youthful-enterprise-selling red flag is partly answered by actual revenue, and the team is doing founder-led sales rather than waiting for press, hires, or a magical partner. But the known-exception test does not rescue the missing evidence: a working product and early pull are not substitutes for a documented rapid correction or an independently validated result.

I would not write the check yet. Litmus has crossed the technical-proof and user-demand thresholds, but it fails my operational founder gate because I have no observable instance of the founders changing reality after a named obstacle. One condition would flip me: a dated case study showing that a named employer rejected the initial system over security or validity, that the founders shipped a specific workaround within weeks, and that the employer then renewed or expanded.
