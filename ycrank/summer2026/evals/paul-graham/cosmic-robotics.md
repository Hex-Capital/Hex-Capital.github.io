# Cosmic Robotics -- Paul Graham Evaluation

James Emerick deployed autonomous earthmoving equipment at Built Robotics, while Cosmic Robotics’s weakest evidence is not technical but relational: no public record shows how Emerick and Lewis Jones met or built together before Cosmic Robotics. This is a schlep-maximal company with a cofounder-chemistry blank. Cosmic Robotics has already completed tens of thousands of panel installations in dusty, remote jobsites, yet I cannot tell whether its founders behave as a durable pair when a robot breaks at 3 a.m. The first fact makes Cosmic Robotics unusual. The second keeps it from being an automatic check.

**Coinbase** is the high rung for schlep blindness because compliance was not incidental to the product; enduring the grind became the asset. Cosmic Robotics has the physical version of that mechanism: 90-pound panels, uneven terrain, millimeter-level placement, contractors, trailers, suction grippers, and workers fastening every unit. Cosmic Robotics diverges from Coinbase because the schlep is not exclusive. Gritt has 2.8 GW under contract, Terabase reports 40 MW installed, and Charge Robotics attacks the same workflow. Cosmic Robotics gets nearly full credit for choosing ugly work, but not for being alone in noticing it.

The polished AI-wrapper company fails because its founders were assembled around a market slide rather than years of shared work. Cosmic Robotics avoids the worst version of that pattern: Emerick and Jones are two technical founders, not a solo founder or a pair waiting to hire engineers after funding. But Cosmic Robotics provides no evidence that they previously worked, roomed, or hacked together, and both biographies emphasize credentials rather than the relationship between them. The **Loopt** exception shows that one extraordinary animal can override structural uncertainty, but Cosmic Robotics’s dossier contains no comparable force-of-personality evidence. I therefore treat the pair as plausible, not proven.

Tens of thousands of installation cycles are the strongest tracks Cosmic Robotics’s animals have left. Over the next 18 months, Cosmic Robotics can turn every broken suction seal, GPS correction, and crew intervention into reliability data that makes the next deployment less manual. More than doubled labor productivity and one panel every 30–40 seconds show a working artifact rather than a deck. But the $4 million pre-seed round arrived before any disclosed revenue, and the dossier names no cereal-box stunt, rejected-customer reversal, or improvised sale by Emerick or Jones. Cosmic Robotics has shipped impressively; it has not yet shown that its founders can manufacture survival from nothing.

James Emerick’s Built Robotics history puts Cosmic Robotics close to an organic idea, because Emerick personally worked on autonomous construction equipment before Cosmic Robotics began automating solar construction. The score would move into the top rung if Emerick could name the exact jobsite failure he repeatedly suffered, the tool he first built for himself, and the contractor who kept using it before fundraising. Without that chain, Cosmic Robotics may be a sharp adjacent-market choice rather than a Dropbox-style “I needed this myself” idea. If Cosmic Robotics instead originated from investor-driven analysis of robotics markets, the score would fall a tier.

**Dropbox** is the useful hacker parallel because Drew Houston’s demo mattered more than his résumé. Cosmic Robotics likewise has the artifact: an eight-wheel electric platform, camera-equipped arm, suction gripper, and high-accuracy GPS operating across tens of thousands of installations. Cosmic Robotics therefore outruns the absence of public GitHub repositories from both founders. Still, Jones’s SpaceX, JPL, JSC, and Relativity Space history is institutional engineering, not evidence of the Lisp-prize or open-source genealogy that made the Collisons legible before Stripe. The machine is persuasive hacker evidence; the founders’ public trails are not.

Gritt’s 2.8 GW commitment makes the next 36 months a deployment race rather than a robotics demonstration. My falsifiable forecast is that by July 2029 Cosmic Robotics will produce $8–15 million in annualized revenue from repeat utility-scale solar EPC deployments, while data-center construction and NASA lunar work remain pilots rather than material businesses. Cosmic Robotics gets there by compounding field reliability around the narrow panel-placement workflow, not by becoming a general construction robot. Cosmic Robotics misses the forecast if it chases cooling pipes, data centers, and the Moon before contractors can run Cosmic-1 repeatedly without founder-heavy supervision.

Terabase’s 40 MW of installations is the bear case that stings: Cosmic Robotics may be a technically competent entrant in a crowded category, financed with a $4 million pre-seed round and an eight-person organization before disclosing a single dollar of revenue. Gritt, Charge Robotics, and Terabase could capture the EPC relationships, while Cosmic Robotics’s lack of public hacker trails, unknown cofounder history, and expansion into three adjacent markets become manager-mode diffusion. Even the **Airbnb** exception cuts against Cosmic Robotics here: Airbnb had cereal boxes proving resourcefulness, while Cosmic Robotics has capital and coverage. Tens of thousands of completed installations and more than doubled labor productivity defeat that bear case for now because they are field evidence, not adjectives. I would flip to Pass if Cosmic Robotics cannot verify, within 12 months, two repeat paying EPC customers and at least $2 million of recognized deployment revenue.
