# Donkey -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The strongest fact is not the $110,000 quoted in week one. It is that Martindale and Tan already persuaded 31 Chinese factories to pay for Prelude OS in 39 days, made it cash-flow positive, then abandoned that product for a harder transaction business. Martindale subsequently drove from Vermont to San Francisco to visit independent distributors himself. These are actions, not founder adjectives. But the dossier never identifies the observation that forced the pivot or a specific obstacle followed by a timed workaround, so the resourcefulness proof is good rather than complete.

The product is also more real than the phrase “AI-native trading company” makes it sound. Donkey parses customs filings, distinguishes factories from forwarders, identifies manufacturers from photos or markings, coordinates inspectors across seven Chinese provinces, and produced a quote from the buyer’s existing factory at a 15% lower price. That is a working system crossing the boundary into physical trade. It is not yet a measured technical breakthrough: there is no disclosed entity-resolution accuracy, completed delivery, defect rate, or evidence that the factory data is proprietary. The proof is the transaction machinery, not the AI.

This problem came out of the founders’ actual work. Martindale speaks Mandarin, sold to Chinese factories, and advised Sany and Sinoboom on overseas expansion; Tan built the customs-data machinery. They have also known one another since high school. The ugly parts—taking title, inspecting goods, clearing customs, financing net-45 terms, and accepting responsibility through delivery—are the opportunity. Most software founders would try to remove these schleps from the pitch. Donkey has made them the product.

The unresolved question is who wants that product now. Independent wholesale distributors are a plausible narrow well, but $110,000 of quotations and purchase orders described as “signing” are still pipeline. The 31 Prelude customers validate the founders’ ability to sell in this ecosystem, not demand for Donkey’s new offer. My 36-month base case is therefore specific but restrained: Donkey reaches $25–50 million of annualized merchandise volume by August 2029, while remaining a working-capital-intensive trading company rather than becoming a pure software platform.

The bear case stings because correctness is the product. Donkey’s public savings example still includes a temporary 10% tariff surcharge whose authorization expired July 24, 2026 absent an extension. A company selling landed-cost certainty cannot be casually wrong about landed cost. Classification errors, buyer defaults, defects, or tariff changes could consume an embedded margin before a proprietary supplier dataset exists. The current proof spike is also weaker than the pattern I prefer: a quote is not a completed order, and seven-province coverage is not yet a defensible substrate.

I would nevertheless write a small check. The founders pass the operational gate: they have sold a crude prior product quickly, changed routes, built the difficult transaction stack, and are doing customer acquisition personally. There is no dependence on a ceremonial launch, a large partner, or hired salespeople, and the long cofounder history removes another common failure mode. The verifiable flip condition is simple: if by November 2026 Donkey cannot show at least three completed deliveries with correct landed-cost estimates and one repeat order, I would conclude that the quotations measured curiosity rather than pull.
