# STASIS -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Sixteen billion eyes counts anatomy. It does not quantify the consequence of success. STASIS could make continuous ocular sensing useful by connecting measurements to lens tint and screen adjustments. But what changes if it works: hours of comfortable vision, access to specialist care, or the cost of managing a defined condition? The dossier establishes none of those magnitudes. I can accept a 90% probability of technical failure. I still need to know what the remaining 10% buys. Missing consequence evidence receives my default, rather than an assumption that eye strain is unimportant.

The largest kill risk is whether the feedback loop produces a meaningful benefit beyond ordinary glasses and sensible screen settings. Detecting ocular activity, interpreting fatigue, and choosing an effective intervention are separate engineering problems. The founder’s reported 30-day wear test establishes personal use; it does not establish that the interpretation or intervention works. Claimed patents without identifiable claims supply no measurable technical advantage. IXI addresses autofocus, while Emteq measures facial activity; neither comparison establishes STASIS’s superiority. The dossier also supplies no decisive experiment with a threshold, cost, and stop condition. Technical discontinuity and risk-removal discipline therefore remain at their absent-evidence defaults.

The strongest objection to passing is the team. Chakraborty and Goradia have actually worked together on ocular hardware, combining bioengineering, neurology-AI research, and EECS. That is more interesting than two founders discovering a wearable category through market research. Their background fits an unstated pattern I value: several disciplines converging on a difficult physical workflow. The separate GlaucoGlasses project’s reported 348 physician intent forms and seven distributor LOIs totaling $168,000 suggest exposure to professional demand. Those claims belong to that product, however. They establish neither STASIS adoption nor a documented learning-driven transition. Without an observed belief revision or risk-specific recruiting achievement, I keep the founder criterion at its default.

The economic question remains open. Indexed hardware prices conflict between $95 and $240, followed by optional tracking at $15 per month after year one. Neither price reveals sensor cost, manufacturing yield, returns, support, or the cost of delivering useful analytics. I cannot infer permanent subsidy dependence, but I cannot credit unsubsidized parity either. JINS MEME’s discontinued consumer offering makes sustained usefulness a relevant question; the dossier does not establish why it ended. My red flag would be scaling distribution before validating benefit. There is no evidence that STASIS is doing that, so I do not deduct for it.

I pass at 25/100. My exceptions permit missing commercial plans or incomplete milestones when a documented instigator and an enormous quantified consequence justify buying the option. STASIS’s relevant builders make that possibility worth watching, but the current evidence does not establish the override. One verifiable result would reopen my decision: an independent randomized crossover study showing at least a 50% reduction in a prespecified validated eye-strain score against ordinary glasses with optimized screen settings over six weeks. That is a proposed test, not a company milestone. It would establish that STASIS changes an outcome people care about, giving the team something consequential to engineer and price.
