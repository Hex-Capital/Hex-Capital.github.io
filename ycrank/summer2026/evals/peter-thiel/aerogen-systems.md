# Aerogen Systems -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The secret is precise: molecular contamination should be destroyed continuously inside semiconductor tools, not captured by facility-wide filters and nitrogen purges. It would be disproved if independent fab testing showed that Talos cannot sustain sub-1-ppb contamination under production conditions—or that any yield benefit fails to exceed integration costs. Aerogen Systems has advanced beyond a fashionable semiconductor thesis: multiple hardware generations, two years of NASA-backed research, and a reported 7N-purity result in 16 minutes constitute a falsifiable technical claim. But the decisive test remains company-reported, based on one 30-minute PID run rather than an independently qualified semiconductor process.

The founding structure is the strongest evidence. Noah Vogel and Colin Lavery did not meet to manufacture a YC application; they worked together in the University of Michigan’s BLiSS research group, co-authored NASA’s 2024 VOC-removal report, and retained the photocatalytic reactor through successive applications. Vogel brings SpaceX manufacturing experience; Lavery built gamma-ray imaging systems at H3D. This is a long-aligned technical team pursuing a natural secret with core engineering apparently in-house. The dossier does not establish current ownership, salaries, or the full-time status of every member of the four-person team, so economic exposure remains unproved rather than adverse.

Aerogen’s market is less convincing than its technology. Semiconductor-tool contamination control is a bounded beachhead, but not yet a monopoly: Entegris already sells point-of-use and tool-level purification, Camfil supplies qualified AMC tool filters, and Purafil offers catalytic adsorbents. Patent-pending IP and a consumable-free reactor could become proprietary technology, while tool-specific qualification data could create switching costs. Neither has yet been demonstrated. A patent application is not a monopoly mechanism, and hypothetical qualification records cannot be scored as existing lock-in. The movement from spacecraft purification to industrial ducts and then semiconductor tools also leaves open whether the team discovered a great market or is searching for one that will accept the reactor.

The strongest bull case is that advanced packaging has made parts-per-billion contamination a new yield constraint precisely when Aerogen has a compact reactor ready for pilots. The team has already attracted more than $500,000 of pre-company R&D, ID Ventures funding, and at least one unnamed in-tool pilot. If Talos produces a measurable yield improvement, a fab’s willingness to pay could dwarf the equipment cost, and qualification itself could make displacement difficult. This resembles the narrow circumstance under which a coordination-heavy physical system can overcome category aversion: one validated unit removes uncertainty from several downstream steps at once. Aerogen has not yet supplied that validation.

Distribution is definite in form but indefinite in economics. The company names fabs, equipment OEMs, and manufacturing teams; it proposes an on-site assessment, equipment integration, and multi-system deployment. Yet it names no buyer, customer, price, sales-cycle duration, or paid deployment. Custom integration may produce high contract values, but it can also turn a proprietary reactor into a low-margin engineering project. With no pricing, gross margins, or estimate of systems per fab, the $6.163 billion contamination-control market does not establish that one revenue engine can create a multibillion-dollar company.

I would not invest today. Aerogen Systems is a serious technical project with an unusually credible founding history, but the investment case currently crosses too many unverified boundaries at once: laboratory performance, fab qualification, incumbent displacement, custom integration, and repeatable sales. None of the known-exception logic applies yet because there is neither independently verified zero-to-one performance nor persistent customer behavior overriding these risks. One verifiable condition would flip the decision: a named semiconductor manufacturer or equipment OEM completing a paid production-line qualification that independently confirms sustained sub-1-ppb performance and a measurable yield or consumables-cost improvement.
