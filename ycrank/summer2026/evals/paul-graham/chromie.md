# Chromie -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The best fact about Chromie is the one-way ticket to Washington and visits to 50 GovCon offices. When the founders lacked customers and domain access, they did not hire a salesperson or wait for a channel partner. They went door to door. That is real resourcefulness, but the dossier does not connect a named rejection to a dated product change or show how quickly those visits produced the three customer logos. The action is stronger than the causal proof.

There is a crude product doing useful work. Chromie monitors federal and state sources, builds a company-specific profile, traces extracted requirements to their sources, and preserves replayable audit logs. More importantly, Snovation says it surfaced an RFI that the contractor’s existing pipeline missed ([Chromie](https://www.chromie.dev/)). That is a specific user and a specific job, not merely a waitlist. But one testimonial, three logos, and an undisclosed user count do not yet show payment, repetition, or pull. Who opens Chromie every week? Who would complain if it disappeared? The dossier cannot answer either question.

The sequence of products is harder to interpret. During 2026 the team moved from a Chrome-extension builder through browser-agent infrastructure and insurance workflows before relaunching for government contracting. This could be a fast running back changing routes. It could also be two capable engineers searching for a sufficiently legible market. No customer observation is identified as the reason for any change, and neither founder is documented as having encountered government procurement through prior work. Visiting contractors after choosing the market is good founder behavior, but it is not a founder-originated need.

Government contracting contains plenty of ugly work: fragmented portals, recompete prediction, solicitation extraction, internal-document ingestion, and security requirements. But schlep is only protective when competitors remain blind to it. GovDash already reports nearly 200 customers, Procurement Sciences more than 300 organizations, and both have raised at least $40 million; GovSignals has FedRAMP High, SOC 2 Type II, and ITAR controls. Chromie has no disclosed security certification, proprietary dataset, revenue, or switching-cost evidence. An $8.1 billion market growing 9.2% annually is large enough, but market size does not explain why this two-person company wins.

The strongest bull case is that I am penalizing a pre-seed company immediately before its proof arrives. Ananth previously built an acquired product with more than 40,000 users, the founders have known one another since high school, they are selling personally, and the current software has already found something an incumbent workflow missed. This also avoids several of my red flags: there is no reliance on a ceremonial launch, major partner, or prematurely hired sales organization. But it does not match my unstated pattern of a concrete pre-round proof spike: three logos are not 40,000 active users, a measured benchmark, or a difficult proprietary substrate. Bindwell is the closest known exception for young technical founders; the material difference is that Bindwell had numerical technical validation and direct exposure to its problem, while Chromie presently has neither.

My base case is that within 36 months this team broadens into regulated-industry workflow automation, with government contracting contributing less than half its revenue, because the founders’ strongest evidence is their willingness to keep moving rather than a deep attachment to this problem. I am neutral today. The operational gate barely passes on founder action and a working product, but the company has not proved that this route was discovered through unusually strong pull rather than selected from a sequence of plausible AI markets. I would flip to Invest if at least five contractors paid to renew after repeated weekly use and each could identify an opportunity Chromie found before its existing tools.
