# Atomarine -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Data-center developers no longer wait four to seven years for a grid interconnection; they order 75–100 MW compute barges from shipyards, pair them with whatever power vessel is economically available, and replace the power source without rebuilding the data center. Atomarine has worked backward from that future through two real transitions: gas turbines make the 2028 pilot possible before marine reactors are licensed, and separating compute from power turns nuclear availability from a prerequisite into an upgrade. The present-day leak is concrete but early: a projected 35–49 GW firm-power shortfall by 2030, a new NRC–Coast Guard maritime-nuclear framework, and reported relationships with barge builders and several SMR vendors.

Why does a data center belong on land? Because electricity, fiber, cooling, construction labor, and permitting historically converged there. Atomarine is attacking the resulting rent most precisely at the grid queue: developers lose four to seven years waiting for scarce interconnection capacity while land, water, and community constraints compound the delay. A shipyard-built platform returns siting and scheduling control to the developer. The dossier does not yet quantify the queue’s carrying cost or show that ports and subsea connectivity create less expensive gatekeepers, so the agency transfer is plausible rather than proven.

The founders fit the machine unusually well. Emile Germonpre researched offshore reactor design during an MIT nuclear-engineering PhD; Dimitris Koutentakis combines EECS, naval architecture, mechanical engineering, optimization work, and Greek-shipping relationships. This is not two software founders discovering energy during an AI boom. Still, credentials are not the same as pre-company obsession: I do not see dated vessels built, reactor artifacts shipped, customers recruited, or institutions persuaded before Atomarine. The partnership claims help, but unnamed counterparties cannot establish Rizz, Tizz, and Biz by themselves.

The bear case stings. Samsung Heavy Industries already has a classified 50 MW design, shipbuilding capacity, Supermicro validation, and an industrial consortium. Nautilus has operated a 6.5 MW barge. Atomarine has no public prototype, environmental testing, connectivity architecture, exclusive supply, patent, or operating data; its 1.1 PUE, 4–5× deployment advantage, and 1.5 GW annual capacity are modeled claims. This could be a systems-integration company facing better-capitalized integrators while depending on several simultaneous miracles: marine reliability, customer procurement, port access, reactor licensing, and high-bandwidth connectivity.

The gas-powered pilot keeps those miracles from being fully simultaneous, and the replaceable power vessel is the clever systems choice: prove marine compute first, then substitute nuclear when the regulatory and supply layers mature. But the known pre-revenue exception does not clear Atomarine yet. CosmicBrain had a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept; Atomarine currently has neither. Partnerships are supply-side preparation, not costly customer behavior.

I would make a small personal investment because the six-year map, founder-stack fit, and modular architecture are sufficiently unusual, but this sits near the bottom of my invest range. My one verifiable flip condition is a named hyperscaler, neocloud, or data-center developer committing money to the 2028 pilot contingent on independently specified vibration, corrosion, cooling, and connectivity tests. Without that customer taking technical and procurement risk alongside Atomarine, the barge remains a beautiful rendering of what is coming rather than an artifact that proves it has begun.
