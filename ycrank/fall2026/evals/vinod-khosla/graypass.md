# GrayPass -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

GrayPass addresses a genuine security gap: authenticating someone at login does not establish who controls the session when money moves or permissions change. If action-level assurance works, applications could prevent losses while demanding fewer interruptions from legitimate users. But the dossier never quantifies either consequence. A $3.8 billion behavioral-biometrics market is a supplier revenue estimate; it does not establish the losses GrayPass can eliminate or an order-of-magnitude improvement in protection. I can lose one check. I still need evidence of what extraordinary outcome that check purchases.

The critical uncertainty is whether behavioral continuity adds useful discrimination at an acceptable false-rejection rate. GrayPass combines typing, pointer, and scrolling signals with contextual evidence, then issues short-lived, action-bound proof. That is a coherent enforcement design. The signed proof, however, carries the decision; it does not establish that the decision is accurate. TypingDNA already supplies behavioral authentication, and Twosense connects behavioral mismatches to remediation. GrayPass provides no comparative accuracy, latency, or attack-detection benchmark. Its disclosed inability to withstand a fully compromised endpoint also bounds the protection it can deliver. This earns the technical absent-evidence default, without assuming the undisclosed models are trivial.

The strongest argument against passing is that the founders are already selling into an established need. European pilots, a reported customer win, and the CEO’s claim of five figures in revenue are meaningful at this stage, although the revenue period is unspecified. BioCatch’s reported revenue above $185 million ARR establishes that buyers spend substantially in the adjacent category. Execution-led financial infrastructure can become important without a scientific breakthrough. But that exception needs evidence that the initial wedge can expand into a much larger value pool. Here, overlapping incumbent capabilities are documented; durable customer dependence and expansion are still hypotheses. Ordinary product usage could improve behavioral models, but the dossier establishes neither a proprietary learning loop nor rights to a uniquely valuable dataset.

Shadow evaluations and initially unenforced action policies deserve limited process credit: they let GrayPass observe decisions before exposing customers to blocking errors. The dossier does not supply a numerical acceptance threshold, experiment cost, or an if/then deployment decision. Mathur’s software experience and Ranjan’s computational neuroscience research provide relevant, complementary foundations, but neither demonstrates belief revision or recruiting against a named weakness. Those remain missing evidence. Likewise, $500 monthly starting pricing establishes an offer, while inference costs, support burden, integration effort, and customer savings remain undisclosed. There is no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, premature organizational scaling, or deliberate architectural imitation.

I pass with my own money today. The known exceptions for exceptional instigators or enormous quantified consequences do not have sufficient support here. My flip condition is one independently audited, preregistered customer trial showing at least a 5x reduction in successful unauthorized sensitive actions against the customer’s existing controls, with legitimate-action rejection below 0.1% and lower total operating cost. Those are proposed investment thresholds, not reported results. Meeting them would turn GrayPass’s central claim into evidence of a consequential capability.
