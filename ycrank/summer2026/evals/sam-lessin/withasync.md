# Async -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I first write down three numbers: post-money valuation, competing term sheets, and the size of the first check. Async discloses none of them. For my money, that is not neutral: I cannot tell whether I am buying a neglected belief or paying the YC auction price for “AI agents for small business.” The market is already crowded with Harvey, Luminai, Relevance AI, and Lindy, so the absence of deal evidence leaves the most important underwriting question unanswered.

The functioning asset is narrower and better than the headline. Async has deployed agents at four named law firms, and Clifton Law uses them for intake, conflict checks, engagement letters, and task assignment without migrating systems. Across customers, the agents have reportedly automated thousands of hours. That is real workflow penetration—not followers, integrations, or a demo—and the combination of approval logic, customer memory, isolated infrastructure, and SOC 2 Type I controls matters in regulated operations. The dark pool, if one exists, is the accumulated map of how small law firms actually work.

But Async is trying to generalize before proving that asset economically. Every published customer story is legal, while the company markets simultaneously to healthcare, real estate, and childcare. Those are not four distribution channels sharing one product; they are four regulated operating systems requiring audits, customization, integrations, and maintenance. If the agents remain bespoke, Async may build a useful legal-operations service with software leverage. Calling the same delivery process a horizontal agent platform does not create platform economics.

The founders have more receipts than their résumés initially suggest. Ben Smith and Connor Park previously built outcome-based AI billing infrastructure, then legal agents under Skope, and now have current deployments inside law firms. Smith brings selling experience and Park has shipped software, but the dossier does not show that either founder has operated a law firm, clinic, property business, or childcare provider. They can build one side of the bridge and have gained access to the other; they have not yet demonstrated deep operating authority on both sides.

The strongest bull case is that the pivots are learning, not drift. The team moved from billing infrastructure to legal agents, retained enough continuity to produce four named deployments, and designed outcome-based rather than seat-based pricing. This could become the AI exception where a base business works before the speculative platform story. The material difference from **Ando** is evidence: Async reports hours automated, but no prices, conversion rate, retention, customer revenue effect, gross margin, burn, or route to break-even. Hours saved are an input; cash is the metric.

I am passing today. There is no named capital-funded experiment, deadline, or pass/fail milestone, and deleting the next financing from the model exposes an unknown: whether outcome pricing covers forward deployment and ongoing maintenance after model, integration, and human-approval costs. My verifiable flip condition is six months of cohort data from at least ten paying law firms showing 90% or better retention and positive contribution margin, paired with a disclosed sub-$10 million post-money round whose proceeds fund a dated test of repeatable deployment. That would convert promising embedded workflow into a one-check venture moment.
