# Lyon -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first three numbers I need are missing: proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and what this check specifically finances. I will not infer scarcity from Lyon being pre-seed, nor infer a venture moment from the fact that model development costs money. Without terms or a finite experiment—one hypothesis, metric, and deadline—I cannot tell whether I am buying cheap uncertainty or merely funding an attractive AI category.

The product evidence is real. Lyon trained inside a fintech’s cloud on 28 billion transactions, produced four times the precision of existing premium-card-conversion rules, and is now being deployed for credit; separate work with an insurer suggests this was not a one-off demo ([YC, Aug 2026](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/lyon)). Customer-VPC operation, SOC 2 Type 1 compliance, and 35 monitored controls also show that the team has crossed more enterprise infrastructure than the average two-person pre-seed company ([Lyon Trust Center, Aug 2026](https://trust.lyon.so/)). This is the strongest part of the case.

But whose dark pool is it? The transaction history belongs to the financial institution, training occurs separately for each customer, and the resulting intelligence remains inside that customer’s cloud. Lyon may accumulate deployment automation, schemas, and security receipts, but the dossier supplies no evidence that learning compounds across customers. If every installation requires another from-scratch model and bespoke procurement cycle, the apparent foundation-model company can become a technically excellent consulting business.

The hard bull case is that the 4x result already caused broader usage: the fintech moved from premium-card conversion toward credit, which is exactly the cross-task behavior Lyon claims. That could be the first proof of a reusable representation rather than custom feature engineering. The relevant AI exception is Ando, where model claims became investable only after 100% pilot-to-paid conversion and retention. Lyon’s material difference is that neither payment nor retention is disclosed; expansion inside one unnamed institution is product validation, not yet cash validation.

Gabriel Noya has shipped on the technical side: Stanford EE/CS, NVIDIA experience, an edge-AI competition win, and—far more important—the 28-billion-transaction deployment. No banking or insurance operating history is documented, however, so I do not yet see the outsider-insider translation receipt on both sides. YC lists one active founder and a team of two while LinkedIn shows three employees; that does not prove a co-founder governance problem, but it leaves decision ownership unverified.

Delete the next financing and the dossier gives me no model: no contract value, revenue, gross margin, burn, training cost, or deployment time. That matters because customer-specific VPC training faces heavily funded substitutes including Taktile, Pecan AI, Feedzai, and H2O.ai. My base case is that Lyon can reach a handful of institutions but remains services-heavy unless it proves repeatable deployment economics. The one fact that would flip me is a second institution converting to a paid production contract of at least $250,000 annually using substantially the same tooling rather than a bespoke rebuild. Until then, I pass—not because the model failed, but because the product proof has not yet revealed an investable capital structure.
