# Hop Aero -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first problem is not whether rockets can move cargo quickly. It is that Hop Aero has raised $3 million without disclosing the post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or whether this was genuinely scarce capital. I cannot tell whether I am buying an unfashionable experiment or paying YC pricing for a fashionable defense-space story. Financing silence receives little credit because price is part of the thesis, not paperwork to inspect after deciding I like the founders.

The capital also lacks a named card to flip. Rook’s ultimate claim—250 kilograms transported 750 kilometers in roughly 15 minutes—is wonderfully falsifiable, but Hop Aero does not say what the $3 million specifically funds, by what date, or what technical result ends the experiment. The disclosed proof is one tethered small-scale flight and an engine-and-igniter hot-fire. Those are legitimate receipts, but the gap from tethered testing to hypersonic re-entry and landing on an unprepared surface contains propulsion, guidance, thermal, regulatory, and operating risks simultaneously. “Build the rocket” is a program; it is not yet one bounded venture moment.

The strongest bull case is the team. Matija Milenovic sold propulsion IP, led a 20-plus-person supersonic-UAV team, and scaled Ukrainian loitering-munition production from 10 to more than 100 units per month. Jacob Balaj has operated inside Marine Corps aviation and worked across Falcon 9/Heavy integration, Virgin Orbit, Masten’s lunar lander, and SpinLaunch. This is unusually strong outsider-insider coverage of the bridge between military logistics and launch systems. The $1,249,909 Air Force Phase II SBIR award is also an actual customer action, not follower theater: a government buyer paid Hop Aero to advance “Rapid Operations Over Kilometers.”

But the contract purchased R&D, not demonstrated cargo delivery. Hop Aero has one disclosed federal customer, no untethered point-to-point flight, and no mission pricing, gross margin, burn, runway, or path to break-even. Delete the next financing and the model becomes difficult to defend: a six-person company must still complete a vehicle, secure FAA launch and re-entry authorization, build operations, and compete for government budgets against SpaceX, Rocket Lab, Inversion, and Outpost. Physical complexity can create a durable operating system, but here it currently creates capital dependency faster than proprietary flight data.

My 36-month base case is that Hop Aero remains alive as a defense-development contractor, accumulates roughly $1 million–$5 million in follow-on government awards, and shifts toward propulsion, test vehicles, or shorter-range contested-logistics systems without operating a commercial 250-kilogram, 750-kilometer cargo service. The objection that stings is that this is precisely how hard defense infrastructure begins: exceptional operators, a paid government wedge, and progressively larger demonstrations. I accept that possibility. I do not accept treating an SBIR award as evidence that the full mission economics or procurement path already works.

I am passing today. The one verifiable flip condition is an Air Force follow-on contract that pays a fixed mission price covering Hop Aero’s disclosed gross cost and is contingent on completing an untethered point-to-point payload flight. That single event would convert the current story into a bounded experiment, show that owned technical capability causes a purchase, and make the business less obviously dependent on another speculative round.
