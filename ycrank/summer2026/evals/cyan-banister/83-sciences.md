# 83 Sciences -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Materials labs no longer treat failed syntheses, instrument traces, and notebook fragments as experimental exhaust; every attempt becomes searchable input for the next attempt. Getting there requires reliable extraction from messy records, contractual permission to reuse them, and proof that accumulated history improves real experiments. 83 Sciences has one leak from that future: its Dalton agent reportedly revised synthesis conditions beyond an anonymous battery lab’s prior capacity plateau and helped produce a manuscript in under two months. That is more meaningful than the $410.4 million market forecast, but it is not yet the full causal map—the result remains company-reported, the customer unnamed, and the manuscript unpublished.

The founders make the traversal plausible. Ian Naccarella worked in battery-materials strategy at Sila, Eric Riesel completed an MIT chemistry PhD spanning experimental analysis and generative models, and Yankang Yang led enterprise AI work across a 30,000-person organization. They also report years of collaboration on superconductors and cooling textiles. This is complementary technical and commercial fluency, not three people who discovered “AI for materials” during YC. What is missing is dated evidence of a pre-company obsession producing several independent artifacts or recruiting outcomes; the biography is strong, but the first-believer record is still compressed into credentials and the current company.

The broken system is unusually concrete: 83% of laboratory data allegedly disappears into notebooks, file servers, and instrument exports, forcing researchers to reconstruct work that already happened. The rent is paid in scientist time, repeated experiments, and slower iteration, while Dalton returns some control to the people running the lab. I want the economics made harder: hours saved per researcher, experiments avoided, and the value of reaching a viable formulation earlier. Without those numbers, this is a recognizable institutional leak rather than a fully demonstrated transfer of agency.

The strongest bull evidence is behavioral. A laboratory reportedly handed over enough unpublished history for 83 Sciences to alter an experiment, exceed a prior performance plateau, and co-author a manuscript. That is costly participation involving sensitive data, laboratory labor, and professional reputation—not a waitlist click. Dozens of additional partnerships are only “in progress,” however, and grants of up to $50,000 may be subsidizing adoption. The next proof is not another partnership announcement; it is a named lab returning with a second materials problem after the grant or initial discovery contract ends.

The wonderfully strange asset is not the agent. MatterGen is open, Meta has released OMat24, and CuspAI, Orbital, Citrine, Entalpic, and MaterialsZone are already attacking adjacent layers. The asset could be the organized negative space: failed runs and unpublished conditions that public models cannot see. Every discovery contract passing through one structured platform could compound into enabling infrastructure for batteries, PFAS replacements, catalysts, and other downstream products. But that only works if 83 Sciences may legally reuse derived knowledge across partners. The dossier discloses no exclusivity, foreground-IP allocation, or cross-partner data rights; without them, the apparent data flywheel collapses into separate consulting engagements.

I would invest, narrowly. The working artifact, complementary team, and lower-stack data asset outweigh the absence of revenue at pre-seed, and no disclosed governance fact triggers my leadership gate—Ian is identified as CEO. The case against me stings: this is a crowded AI category, the public validation is anonymous, and the most important asset belongs to customers whose confidentiality requirements may prevent compounding. My verifiable flip condition is contractual: if diligence shows that partner agreements prohibit 83 Sciences from retaining reusable, provenance-safe learning from completed experiments, I pass because the platform becomes labor-intensive discovery services sitting on commoditizing models.
