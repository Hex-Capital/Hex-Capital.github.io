# Reason Machines -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Reason Machines has shipped something substantial. What I cannot find is the episode that would tell me most about its founders: what stopped them, what they did about it, and how quickly it worked. Adi Singh’s previous IDE company and Sven Myhre’s production engineering experience make their technical ability plausible. Building and living together across four cities also gives their relationship some substance. But those facts do not establish resourcefulness under resistance. That distinction matters when I am deciding whether to invest my own money.

The strongest evidence is the product itself. A persistent controller that moves between local and cloud execution environments addresses an actual difficulty: keeping agents working while developers handle something else. Reason’s disclosed parallel execution, deferred tool discovery, and cache-preserving prompts show attention to the machinery underneath the interface. The [October technical report](https://reasonmachines.com/blog/introducing-reason-agent-1-1) claims 22% lower cost per solved task than Codex and 10% below Pi using GPT-6 Astra. I discount those company-run comparisons, but the functioning software earns substantial credit independently of the claimed advantage. These founders have built enough to be examined.

The interesting demand signal is the founder’s October 22 report of **300 active customer Slack channels**, alongside 10,000 users acquired over the preceding 14 days. Engineering teams are a sufficiently specific initial audience. Slack could put Reason inside their ordinary work rather than require another destination they must remember to visit. But an active channel does not establish that users repeatedly delegate consequential tasks. The distinction becomes particularly important when YC companies receive $1,000 in credits. The $50 and $200 monthly plans establish an offer; they do not establish payment or retention. I give the reported activity meaningful, provisional credit.

Reason Machines fits my pattern of technical founders building programmable tools that users might bend into unexpected workflows. Its approximately 1,600 repository stars and 138 forks suggest developer attention, although they do not yet demonstrate that appropriation. Here is the strongest case against withholding a check: a two-founder team has shipped working infrastructure and reports reaching hundreds of customer channels quickly. Waiting for a long revenue history could mean missing the period when the founders learn fastest. My working-product exception deserves consideration. But the dossier does not yet show either independently validated exceptional performance or unmistakable repeated user pull sufficient to replace the missing founder evidence.

Competition makes that distinction consequential. Devin already offers shared agent context and external agents; Factory offers model choice and controlled deployment. Reason’s separable controller could become valuable if users depend on it across environments, but architectural flexibility alone does not establish that dependence. Nor does the Ara-to-Reason transition demonstrate learning: the dossier supplies no rejected hypothesis or customer observation that caused the change. Earlier IDE work supplies domain context without establishing the founders’ personal collision with this particular problem. Those two dimensions therefore remain at their absent-evidence defaults. I would not infer a manufactured idea, launch dependence, or premature delegation from their absence.

I am neutral on Reason Machines and would not write a check today. Its technical evidence clears my product threshold; its documented response to obstacles does not clear my founder threshold. One verifiable development would change that decision: a customer-confirmed account showing the founders overcoming a specific adoption blocker within two weeks, followed by that team paying and repeatedly delegating work for eight weeks. That would connect the software to both founder behavior and durable need—the missing connection in an otherwise interesting company.
