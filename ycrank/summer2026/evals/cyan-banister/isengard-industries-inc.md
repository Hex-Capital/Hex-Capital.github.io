# Isengard Industries Inc -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Allied militaries are not shipping every damaged drone back through a distant prime contractor; they are assembling interoperable strike and counter-UAS systems locally, updating autonomy from battlefield data, and replenishing fleets faster than an adversary can exhaust them. Isengard Industries has leaked a piece of that world into the present: four reportedly operational systems, two production cells totaling 18,200 square feet, Ukrainian frontline deployments, and a U.S. Army evaluation. The missing piece is the causal map. The dossier does not show how those cells progress from a 3,000-unit monthly target to verified sovereign-scale output, or which procurement and export constraints must fall in what order.

Francisco Serra-Martins is the strongest reason to believe Isengard could traverse that distance. He served as an Australian combat engineer and previously led Terminal Autonomy, which reported $60 million in revenue. James Tennant adds military experience, defense investing, private credit, and research into economic warfare. This is not a team that discovered defense because capital became fashionable. Thirty employees, six system designs, and facilities in St. Petersburg and Warsaw are expensive first-believer acts. The complementary commercial and operational fluency is real, although Eugene Skokowski’s unspecified role and the absence of any public co-founder history leave me unable to see how decisions actually resolve among the three founders.

The broken system is also concrete. Allied procurement runs through multi-year cycles and prime-subcontracting channels while the battlefield consumes low-cost systems at a rate traditional production cannot replenish. Isengard’s answer is shared propulsion, airframes, autonomy, and supply chains distributed through sovereign local cells. That could return production control to allied governments instead of leaving it concentrated with a few primes. But the dossier neither quantifies the incumbent rent nor proves that localization escapes it: DDTC manufacturing approvals, foreign-production licensing, and government acceptance may simply move the queue rather than remove it.

The bull case has teeth because users are doing more than joining a waitlist. Named Ukrainian units reportedly field the systems, one product is in rate production, another is in flight test, and the U.S. Army is evaluating the technology. Those are costly institutional behaviors around a functioning artifact. They are also all company-reported. No unit output, interception rate, navigation reliability, targeting accuracy, reorder, or paid contract is public. My flip condition is one independently verifiable procurement record showing a named allied customer paid for and reordered at least 100 fielded systems after measured operational use.

Here is the part that stings: the company combines lethal AI targeting with an AES-256 datalink, yet the dossier says nothing about human authorization, target-data custody, auditability, or failure containment. “Operational” is not enough when the artifact can select or strike a target. Add unclear founder decision rights, a 3,000-unit production aspiration presented beside no actual output, and competition from Anduril, Helsing, and Firestorm with dramatically greater financing, and this can become a well-connected defense story faster than it becomes a controlled manufacturing system. A verified governance structure and explicit targeting-control architecture are gates, not diligence decoration.

I would still invest, but at an ownership and price appropriate to those gates. The team’s prior operating tuition and the physical evidence earn the check; the unverified battlefield claims, absent future-chain milestones, and lethal-autonomy architecture keep it from being a conviction-sized one. Isengard is building complete weapons rather than a scarce enabling layer beneath the sector, so its advantage must ultimately appear in measured production economics and field performance—not in the breadth of its six-system catalog.
