# Elite -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important missing fact is what broke. The brothers launched Stratum’s government application-review product on July 29, 2026, then had Elite’s healthcare HIPAA notice in effect by August 22. That proves they can change nouns quickly. It does not prove resourcefulness. The dossier identifies neither the observation that invalidated the government route nor a blocker the founders personally worked around. Without the obstacle, action, elapsed time, and result, I cannot distinguish a running back from a team searching for a field.

There is nevertheless a real artifact. Elite has a public voice demo and claims specialty protocols for 18 specialties plus write-back to more than 19 EHRs. Building those integrations while implementing audit logs, encryption, US data storage, HIPAA processes, and BAAs is more substantial than making an AI receptionist demo. But the evidence remains self-reported. There is no measured completion rate, correction rate, latency result, or production volume. This matters because Elite proposes to alter scheduling, eligibility, refills, referrals, and billing records. A system that performs nine-tenths of those actions correctly may be worse than one that performs none.

Who wants the crude product now? The dossier cannot name a practice. It reports no current-product usage, revenue, signed agreement, or repeat behavior. Per-resolved-interaction pricing is sensible because it makes Elite earn money only when it finishes a job, but a pricing mechanism is not evidence that anyone pays. Nor is a booking form distribution. The $13.7 billion market estimate says nearly nothing about whether one practice will trust these founders with its patients and EHR.

The strongest case for investing is the team’s speed and range. Raj has sold at Deel; Vihaan was founding engineer number three at Cadastral before its acquisition and previously generated more than 1,000 campaign signups in 48 hours. They are brothers rather than application-assembled cofounders, and Elite shows none of the usual launch-day, big-partner, premature-hiring, or manager-mode red flags. A small team that can deploy integrations faster than Assort, Hyro, and Syllable could win independent practices neglected by those heavily funded competitors. Outcome pricing could then turn technical speed into an unusually sharp wedge.

But the known exception does not rescue the present evidence. Bindwell also involved unusually young technical founders, but numerical technical proof, laboratory validation, and direct exposure to agriculture supplied the override. Elite has no documented healthcare-operations collision and no comparable production measurement. Its recent shift into a crowded AI category therefore resembles a solution searching for a sufficiently painful user more than an organic need opening a neglected market. It also misses the concrete pre-round proof spike that appears repeatedly in the companies I actually back.

I would pass now. My 36-month base case is that Elite pivots from healthcare front offices into broader regulated application or claims processing and remains below $1 million ARR, because the team has shown more evidence of rapid construction than of attachment by healthcare users. One verifiable result would flip me: a named practice case study showing that the founders overcame a specific integration or safety blocker within two weeks and then resolved at least 1,000 patient interactions over 30 days with repeat usage and a disclosed human-correction rate.
