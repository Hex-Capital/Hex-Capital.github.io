# Stacktrace -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a small business leaving agents working overnight without leaving a human awake beside them. Getting there requires knowing what happened, deciding what is permitted, and stopping an action before the damage occurs. Stacktrace has built part of that path: transcript inspection and component attribution. Its remote-support demonstration exposes a wonderfully ordinary problem—an unattended agent leaking credentials. But that is evidence of the problem arriving. It does not yet establish that Stacktrace controls the passage from supervised automation to dependable autonomy. The endpoint sidecar reads logs; blocking requires the separate inline gateway.

The founders are the strongest reason I would keep looking. Vinod Kone’s Apache Mesos contributions and PMC leadership are actual work on the machinery beneath other people’s applications. His software supply-chain security responsibility at Harness gives that work a relevant continuation. Micheal Benedict brings infrastructure, security, and AI-platform experience from Airtable; Prasanth Ganta spent 2011–2026 building at Amazon. This is substantially more informative than three people discovering agent security during fundraising season. Still, the dossier establishes technical depth more clearly than independently derived obsession. It does not show the early experiments or customer encounters that made this particular company inevitable for them.

Why does a person have to babysit software hired to save that person time? Stacktrace identifies a concrete operating failure: unattended agents expose credentials, stall, and wander away from instructions. Returning that supervision time matters. But the dossier does not quantify the burden or identify a gatekeeper extracting an artificial rent. The free Team allowance—100 agents and 200 session-hours monthly—makes experimentation accessible; it does not demonstrate that users have recovered meaningful operating freedom. The CLI’s October 27 release and browser sandbox establish something I can touch. Undisclosed design partners provide some behavioral evidence, but no dated account of repeated reliance or changed working habits.

The strongest argument against my hesitation is that the apparently modest monitoring tool could become the permission layer enterprises need before delegating serious work. That fits my pattern of looking beneath the visible application wave. Local transcript processing and attribution to installed components give the thesis technical substance, and open-source collection could help distribution. Yet an enabling function is not automatically a controlled bottleneck. OpenAIDR and OpenACA are reusable under Apache-2.0, detection currently relies on pattern rules, and Noma already advertises enforcement and steering. Stacktrace must earn authority inside the workflow while competitors are trying to occupy the same position. A larger security market does not settle who earns that authority.

My pre-revenue exception for deeply technical founders with a working prototype is relevant here; absent revenue is not my objection. What is missing is the unusually convincing bridge from their technical work to a future they can own. Nor does the dossier establish the red flags that would justify an outright rejection: Kone is identified as CEO, there is no documented governance conflict or dismissive claim about incumbent complexity, and local processing is a constructive privacy choice. It does not establish the full consent and custody architecture, however. I am **Neutral**: interested in these builders, unconvinced by the investment mechanism. My one flip condition is a verifiable 90-day customer deployment in which Stacktrace’s gateway repeatedly blocks policy violations before execution and that evidence leads the customer to authorize a previously prohibited unattended workflow. That would show the product changing what people are willing to entrust to machines.
