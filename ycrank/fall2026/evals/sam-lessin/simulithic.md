# Simulithic -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Simulithic has a verification product I can understand and an investment proposition I cannot yet price. The reported $500,000 from YC establishes backing; it establishes neither the proposed post-money nor competing offers nor why my capital would be scarce. Those are missing facts, not evidence of an auction. The dossier also names no capital-funded experiment with a deadline and a measurable result. Automatically discovering application journeys is a product hypothesis. What this particular check proves remains unspecified.

The strongest reason to take the founders seriously is that they have already shipped together. GitWrap’s reported 100,000-plus developer users belong to that separate project, but they demonstrate more than matching résumés. Ryan Ning brings AI-agent research and engineering experience at Uber and Shopify; Satyam Singh brings experience at Ramp and Bree. Both report encountering release-verification problems at prior employers. That is a credible bridge between agent development and application engineering, consistent with my preference for people who can translate between systems. It earns the strongest dimension here, although building a widely used developer project does not establish operating depth in production QA.

The bull case has teeth: coding agents increase the volume of changes, manually maintained tests become the constraint, and Simulithic discovers journeys instead of asking engineers to specify every test. Founder-reported deployments at several companies, dated October 15, suggest someone is trying the product outside the founding team. But the proprietary asset is still hypothetical. Neither those unnamed deployments nor the founders’ LinkedIn reach demonstrates that owned access caused purchases or retained usage. Customer journey maps could become a dark pool of workflow knowledge; the dossier does not yet show that customers depend on it. The documented-conversion and proprietary-access exceptions to my usual gates therefore do not apply.

Simulithic also needs to establish what customers actually buy when exploration has boundaries. Its documentation says the explorer never submits forms and agents avoid destructive controls. Those may be sensible safeguards, but transactional paths can contain precisely the failures a buyer most wants caught. Meanwhile, QA Wolf markets autonomous exploration and coverage mapping, and BrowserStack already distributes testing infrastructure and agents. The decisive question is whether Simulithic accumulates customer-specific knowledge that improves useful coverage faster than these alternatives. General improvements in browser agents are available to competitors too. Nothing here proves the founders are relying on errors disappearing, but nothing yet establishes an advantage that survives better shared models.

Delete the next financing from the spreadsheet. The published allowances—2,000, 8,000, and 25,000 monthly sessions, with phone sessions counting triple—describe consumption, while undisclosed prices and absent operating costs leave the cash engine unknown. I give missing economics the prescribed default, rather than assume an uneconomic business. My personal-money verdict is **Pass**: demonstrated shipping ability is carrying an otherwise undocumented underwriting case. One verifiable condition would reopen it: five independently confirmed customers renewing after 90 days at prices that produce positive contribution after inference, device infrastructure, and human QA costs. That would turn early deployment claims into evidence of a functioning base business strong enough to reconsider the missing gates.
