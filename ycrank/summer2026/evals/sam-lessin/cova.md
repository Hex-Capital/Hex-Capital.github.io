# Cova -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I first write down three numbers: $500,000 raised, unknown post-money valuation, and unknown competing-term-sheet count. The first number is normal YC financing; the missing two prevent me from knowing whether Cova is a scarce, non-consensus check or another auctioned AI company. A Michigan home-care agency is unfashionable enough to interest me, but “AI-native” and YC are fashionable enough that I will not infer cheapness from the operating category.

The strongest part is the founders’ translation layer. Jordan Ibe and Ansh Tandon worked inside Palantir Healthcare, spent the following year building AI systems for home-care agencies and hospitals, converted Omnition into an internal operating system, and obtained a Michigan provider license. They are joining software deployment to regulated care operations rather than selling an abstract workflow tool. Being licensed and live demonstrates more than résumé fit, although it does not yet prove that they can manage reimbursement, caregivers, and clinical exceptions at scale.

Cova’s product architecture is directionally right: bill Medicaid, managed-care organizations, insurers, and families as the provider, then use automation to remove non-caregiver overhead. That is a real physical-world operating system, and owning the agency should create better workflow data than selling seats to incumbents. But the disclosed experience still requires applicants to complete four state forms and text photographs of every page. The purported dark pool—payer-enrollment data and exception handling—is prospective. One Reddit report of enrollment in progress and a quoted $22 hourly caregiver rate shows a person entered the funnel; it does not establish a completed enrollment, paid claim, repeat referral, or retained care relationship.

The bull case is that the license, direct billing, two-person structure, and zero non-caregiver headcount let Cova prove unusually lean agency economics before a conventional competitor can reorganize itself. That would resemble the mechanism that justified overlooking AI risk in Ando: automation sitting on top of a measurable base business. The material difference is that Cova discloses no paid claims, reimbursement cycle, conversion rate, forecast accuracy, retention, or customer revenue effect. Aidaly already operates software-powered family-care agencies across five states, so merely automating a Michigan agency is neither proprietary access nor a novel hypothesis.

The missing venture moment matters more than the missing revenue at this stage. The dossier never says what the $500,000 must prove, by when, or what result would change the company’s value. “Run the agency with no non-caregiver headcount” could become that experiment, but it needs a finite test: for example, 100 continuously paid caregiver relationships, a specified enrollment time, clean-claim rate, gross margin, and cash collection deadline. Without that, Cova risks becoming a capital factory whose next round finances more states before Michigan economics are known.

My base case is that within 36 months Cova remains a sub-$3 million annual-revenue Michigan home-care operator or pivots into back-office software for other agencies, because the founders can ship the bridge but have not yet demonstrated proprietary demand or self-financing economics. The objection that stings is straightforward: a licensed agency with direct payer billing may generate evidence privately long before it appears publicly, and a two-person team operating live care is meaningful pre-seed execution. I would flip to investing upon verified evidence that at least 50 caregivers have produced paid claims for six consecutive months at positive contribution margin, together with a disclosed round price and a dated plan to reach break-even without another financing.
