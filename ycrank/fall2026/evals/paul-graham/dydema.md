# Dydema -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Dydema has shipped something. What I cannot find is the episode that tells me how these founders behave when something stops working. Julian Weaver and Christopher Oosthuizen have built together since a college neurotechnology experiment and later worked together at Syntra. That is useful evidence about the partnership. Their live EEG classification API is useful evidence about their ability to build. Neither establishes the particular thing I would be buying with my money: how quickly they turn an obstacle into a result.

The strongest evidence is Wizard itself. Developers can upload labeled EEG trials, fit and evaluate classifiers, and receive hosted predictions. The demonstrations extend to controlling 2048 and scrolling TikTok. This fits a pattern I like: technical founders making a difficult capability programmable. But the numerical evidence draws a boundary around the achievement. Dydema reports 52% zero-shot imagined-movement accuracy across nine datasets against 50% chance, and does not offer that task. Withholding it is sensible. The working calibrated service deserves credit; reliable, general-purpose brain control remains unproved ([documentation](https://developers.dydema.com/docs/task-models)).

Who needs the calibrated version badly enough to keep using it? That is the missing link. A researcher repeatedly running labeled EEG experiments seems a more immediate user than someone replacing a mouse. The reported theoretical ceiling of approximately 1.3 bits per second, before filtering and errors, makes the consumer proposition particularly dependent on finding the right task. Company demonstrations establish that commands can happen. They do not establish that anyone prefers making commands this way. The 82 LinkedIn followers add no evidence of that preference. Likewise, charging $1 to create a model, $1 monthly to host it, and $0.003 per prediction window establishes a way to collect money, without establishing that customers pay ([pricing](https://developers.dydema.com/pricing)).

The case against passing is substantial. Weaver combines neuroscience and computer science with EEG/fMRI research; Oosthuizen brings machine-learning research and startup engineering. They have a longstanding working relationship and have exposed their product through self-service onboarding. These are plausible founders for discovering an unexpected use of this primitive. **Dropbox** illustrates the relevant exception: a working prototype can justify investing before conventional traction. The material difference is that its prototype demonstrated the job users wanted done. Dydema demonstrates classification and control, while the compelling recurring job remains unidentified. Its commissioned-recording service could uncover that job, but the dossier supplies no resulting customer behavior.

I also cannot award a pivot merely because the API is narrower than the consumer ambition. There is no dated account of a rejected hypothesis and a shipped revision. Nor does prior neuroscience research establish that the founders personally encountered this particular problem. Recording data, handling consent, and managing re-identifiable embeddings could become valuable operational work, but an acknowledged burden is not yet evidence of a founder-built advantage. EMOTIV already combines hardware, APIs, and EEG infrastructure. Dydema has not disclosed the corpus size or measured improvement that would establish its own accumulated substrate. Conversely, I see no evidence of the red flags that would make this an active rejection: waiting for a partner, premature delegation, or ignoring contrary results.

I would pass today. This is an engineering bet with a credible team, but my central founder test remains unanswered, and neither demonstrated demand nor exceptional technical validation supplies an override. One verifiable customer episode would reopen the decision: a named EEG research team encounters a blocking failure, Dydema ships a workaround within two weeks, and that team subsequently pays and uses it weekly for a month. That would connect the founders’ response to resistance with someone’s actual need.
