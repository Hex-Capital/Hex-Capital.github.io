# Trident -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Software changes continuously, so an enterprise’s defenses are attacked continuously; autonomous systems reproduce an exploit, follow it through cloud permissions, patch the code, and attack again. Trident has built a plausible piece of that loop. What is missing is the path from today to that future: which new capability compounds, which bottleneck disappears, and what asset Trident owns when continuous autonomous testing becomes ordinary. “AI agents pentest faster” describes the present wave. It does not yet tell me what comes next.

The founders are the strongest part of this company. Junaid Mahmood reports writing game exploits at fifteen, has shipped an open-source project with 470 GitHub stars, and previously worked at Tesla, Wealthsimple, and smallest.ai. He and Dekai Li built Vector together before Trident; Li brings AI-systems and research experience. That is real pre-company tinkering and recruiting proof, not two people discovering cybersecurity after a financing announcement. I still cannot see the commercial tuition: no dated sequence from their earlier artifacts to this product, no named design partner, and no evidence that either founder has operated an enterprise security program.

The broken rule is legible. Annual pentests freeze a narrow scope while applications, APIs, IAM permissions, and data stores keep changing. Trident replaces that snapshot with a loop: verify the weakness, map its cloud reachability, open a patch pull request, and rerun the exploit. That returns time and control to security teams. But the dossier never quantifies the rent—consulting expense, remediation delay, false-positive labor, or breach exposure—so I cannot tell whether customers are escaping an artificial gatekeeper or simply buying a more automated version of the same service.

The sharp bull case is that a working artifact already exists and attacks the industry’s ugliest failure mode. Trident says it reproduces findings rather than trusting model output, while surveys report that 90% of users perform significant manual validation and 78% have seen automated tools miss critical vulnerabilities. It also reports finding critical vulnerabilities in 97% of companies served and some vulnerability in every black-box-test customer. Those are meaningful completed actions at pre-seed. They are not yet costly or weird user behavior: there are no customer counts, repeat runs, payments, named deployments, or examples of a patch being accepted and surviving retesting. Company-reported percentages can conceal a denominator of three.

Competition changes the meaning of every technical claim. Horizon3.ai has performed more than 150,000 pentests for over 3,000 organizations; Pentera reports more than $100 million ARR; XBOW has raised over $270 million; Terra already combines agent swarms with human oversight. Trident’s application-to-cloud attack paths could become an enabling security layer, but today there is no disclosed exploit corpus, benchmark advantage, proprietary distribution, or controlled technical bottleneck. The July 2026 movement from AI-agent monitoring and red-teaming toward web, API, and cloud pentesting makes this look more like searching for position inside a fashionable market than following one independently derived system map.

The trust boundary is the final problem. Trident connects to sensitive applications and cloud identities while SOC 2 Type 2 remains planned, and the dossier does not explain data custody, exploit containment, model-provider dependence, or customer consent architecture. Clear CEO and CTO titles avoid an obvious leadership shell game, but they do not resolve the security gate. My verifiable flip condition is narrow: show three named customers that ran Trident continuously for six months, accepted its remediation pull requests, and measured materially fewer manual-validation hours than an incumbent tool—along with explicit isolation and data-retention controls. Until then, I believe the founders more than I believe the company’s claimed position in the stack, so I pass.
