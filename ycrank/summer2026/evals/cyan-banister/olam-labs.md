# Olam Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years from now, autonomous agents will not merely complete isolated tasks. They will bargain over resources, form coalitions, conceal information, and coordinate with other agents inside companies. Before institutions delegate real authority, someone must make those social behaviors measurable. Olam Labs has built one small leak from that future: deterministic multiplayer environments where humans and models receive identical information and actions, leaving replayable evidence of what each participant said and did.

The artifact is unusually alive for a pre-seed company. Thousands of early-access users generate more than 1,000 matches and nearly one billion processed tokens per day; Social Poker alone has produced 86,729 graded table-talk turns. People are spending time negotiating with machines rather than merely clicking a benchmark button. That is the strongest evidence here. It also creates a proprietary corpus connecting transcripts, actions, and game states—more valuable than another leaderboard of pairwise preferences.

The painful objection is that Olam may be measuring who plays poker well rather than who can safely operate a business. No published evidence connects its game rankings to production-agent performance, and preliminary results depend on LLM grading. Patronus AI is already pursuing simulated digital environments with $70 million raised, while Braintrust and Galileo could add multiplayer evaluations to established enterprise platforms. Olam’s corpus becomes an enabling layer only if behavior inside these constrained worlds predicts behavior outside them. Otherwise, nearly one billion free tokens per day is an expensive gaming community masquerading as infrastructure.

Om Buddhdev and Shreshth Sharma are credible builders for this experiment. Buddhdev worked on agents, infrastructure, and product systems at AI Dungeon; Sharma brings reinforcement-learning and production data experience from RBC. Their relationship predates the company by years, reaching back to a middle-school snack store. That gives me more confidence than two founders assembled around a fashionable benchmark market, but the dossier does not show a dated trail of independent multi-agent experiments or a prior operating wound that made Olam inevitable. I see relevant fluency, not yet the full pre-company obsession-and-commercial-command combination.

The economic mechanism remains blurry. Olam has not identified a rule, fee, or institutional gatekeeper whose rent it removes, nor whose autonomy is returned. Private evaluations, datasets, and custom arenas are inferred products rather than disclosed purchases. The free arena could be a clever consumer beachhead financing a difficult evaluation substrate, but there are no named design partners, revenue figures, or transfer studies showing that frontier labs value the resulting signal. User participation proves fascination; it does not yet prove the buyer’s problem.

I pass for now, narrowly. The exception case is real: a working artifact, striking usage, relevant founders, and a controlled dataset can justify moving before revenue. It does not override the central gap because Olam has not shown that its strange user behavior predicts anything a lab will pay to improve. The verifiable flip condition is one frontier-model lab purchasing a repeat private evaluation and publishing or permitting disclosure that an Olam score predicted a material deployment failure better than static or workflow benchmarks. Before writing my own check, I would also require explicit retention, consent, and access controls for private reasoning traces; anonymity is a promise, not a custody architecture.
