# Yam -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Yam has built something people appear willing to spend hours inside. What I cannot yet see is what its founders did when something important broke. The dossier contains no named obstacle, founder-built workaround, or elapsed time to a result. Shan Zhong’s reported experience growing a previous fintech to 100K users is relevant experience, but it does not answer that question. I would leave resourcefulness at its missing-evidence default. That is the largest unresolved part of this investment.

The product deserves substantial credit. A creator can describe a scenario, edit characters and settings, generate artwork, and publish a playable link. There is a functioning catalog, not merely an explanation of what AI might eventually enable ([creator guide](https://www.hiyam.ai/guide)). Yu Qin’s work on TikTok AI creation tools supplies relevant technical context; Crystal Shan’s product-design experience supplies another useful capability. Neither résumé proves the current system works unusually well. The shipped creation-and-play workflow is the proof. It also fits my interest in tools that let users make things, though the dossier does not yet show creators inventing uses the founders never anticipated.

The strongest argument for investing is that Yam may already have found a deep well. Women who consume fanfiction and dating sims are a specific audience. More than three hours per daily active user is a striking claim, and the leading games display 1.8M and 516.4K plays ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/yam), [catalog](https://www.hiyam.ai/)). Dismissing those figures because this looks like entertainment would be a mistake. But plays do not establish unique users or returning cohorts, and the duration claim lacks a denominator and measurement period. My framework permits a working product with unmistakable pull to override missing founder evidence. Yam has the product; the evidence of pull is promising rather than unmistakable.

The founders’ own participation in fanfiction and dating sims makes the origin more credible than a generic search for an AI market. It remains a retrospective account, without a documented personal obstacle or neglected operational burden. Earlier posts positioning YAM as a design-first coding tool show a changed destination, but no customer observation explaining the turn. I cannot award evidence-driven adaptability merely because two descriptions differ. Nor would I invent red flags: there is no documented dependence on a major distribution partner, premature sales hiring, or dismissal of contrary feedback. Unknown cofounder history is simply unknown.

The important commercial question is whether creators and players keep returning as Yam becomes a business. The $9.99 and $19.99 monthly plans establish an offer, not payment or profitable engagement ([pricing](https://www.hiyam.ai/pricing)). Three-hour sessions could support a valuable habit, while also creating substantial serving costs. The popularity of games using existing anime characters adds a separate question about whether that habit survives rights enforcement. I would not write a check yet, but this merits another conversation. My flip condition is one documented founder-led intervention that fixes a measured retention problem and produces a sustained improvement in a returning-player cohort. That would connect the promising product to the founder behavior I am investing in.
