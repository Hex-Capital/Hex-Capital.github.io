# Cardinal Square -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine a child moving through school at her own pace while an educator notices the frustration, curiosity, and social trouble that a lesson engine misses. Cardinal Square’s proposed path—online supplementation first, accredited schools later—could lead there. But what must become true between those two businesses? Better independent learning, families trusting a new institution, and a school operation that can deliver consistently. The dossier establishes the proposed sequence, but supplies no company-specific artifact showing those transitions have begun. I can imagine the future; I cannot yet locate Cardinal Square inside it.

Why does a family need another education bill? What does that bill buy back: a parent’s evening, a teacher’s discretion, a child’s ability to escape an unsuitable pace? The dossier does not identify a particular institutional restriction, quantify its cost, or show who captures the rent. Synthesis already offers adaptive instruction at an advertised $29 monthly or $119 annually. That does not foreclose Cardinal Square, but it makes the educator’s contribution consequential: the company must demonstrate something families cannot obtain from inexpensive automated practice. Tuition and subscription economics remain hypotheses.

The two-person team could have exactly the operating wound that makes this worth building. I cannot award that story to founders whose identities and histories are unavailable. There is no documented pre-company experiment, recruiting act, or experience running instruction. This is missing evidence, not evidence of opportunism. Likewise, inaccessible research URLs do not establish a customer outage, and an unidentified leadership structure does not establish a governance problem. My pattern of backing unglamorous workflow repair would become relevant if these founders had discovered a specific educational failure through operating it; the dossier does not establish that connection.

The strongest argument against passing is the combination of educators and AI. The cited June 2025 mathematics study found that teacher-designed hints mitigated the harm associated with unrestricted AI assistance. Human involvement could therefore be central to the learning mechanism rather than an expensive decoration. Supplemental instruction also offers a narrower place to test that mechanism before opening schools. But this is external research supporting a possibility, not Cardinal Square’s product proof. My exceptions for pre-revenue companies depend on affirmative substitutes—working prototypes, committed counterparties, or demonstrated founder obsession. None is documented here. Nor is there evidence that the company controls an enabling asset beneath the application, such as a validated assessment system or proprietary instructional dataset.

I pass at **26/100**. This is an unestablished fit, not an active contradiction of my thesis. There is no documented severe privacy problem or closed-supplier dependency; consent and student-data custody would nevertheless matter before I entrusted children’s information to the product. My one verifiable flip condition is a completed, independently assessed Cardinal Square pilot showing that students repeatedly return and improve on **unaided** assessments relative to their starting level. That would give me an artifact to investigate. Until then, neither completed exercises nor the ambition to become a school tells me whether children gain lasting control over their learning.
