# isoquant -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A small business can afford software that keeps trying, checking, and correcting instead of charging a human for every failed attempt. Getting there requires cheaper open-model execution, reliable specialists, and evaluation that catches mistakes before those mistakes become actions. Isoquant’s banking experiment is a small leak from that world: a Qwen3-8B specialist reportedly reached 91.8% accuracy against 74.7% for GPT-5.4 mini, at an estimated 91.7% lower inference cost. That is a concrete transition worth investigating. But 3,079 classification requests do not yet establish that the optimization loop travels across tasks. The future is plausible; the remaining distance matters.

Why does a developer have to become a GPU operator to escape an expensive API bill? That is the irritating system underneath isoquant. Developers surrender either money to a provider or engineering time to serving infrastructure. Its published $0.07 input and $0.15 output pricing per million tokens makes the proposed alternative tangible. Yet the dossier identifies an operating burden more clearly than an artificial gatekeeper or captured rent. Together, Fireworks, and Baseten already offer overlapping capabilities. Lower prices return some control to developers, but endpoint compatibility also lets that control walk straight to the next cheaper provider.

The most appealing part of the company’s origin is that the founders encountered the infrastructure problem while building Agent FM, then commercialized their optimizations. An application exposing a useful layer underneath it is a pattern I like. Aniket’s model-training and reinforcement-learning infrastructure work complements Mugdhaa’s AI developer tooling and distributed-video experience. Those are relevant operating skills. Still, the dossier gives me much more evidence of professional preparation than independently pursued, pre-company obsession. Their nearly ten-year relationship does not establish recruitment ability or decision rights. I see a credible team; I cannot yet see the first-believer story that makes me unusually confident in this particular team.

There is something here to touch. Isoquant publishes model-specific latency comparisons, and Sho Kaneko reports roughly twice-as-fast agent inference after switching. The reported 1.2 trillion tokens in a month and 1,000-plus customers could represent extraordinary early pull. The strongest objection to my hesitation is that a two-person team may already be learning from enough real workloads to compound its engineering advantage faster than larger competitors. But those totals remain company-reported, without a paying-customer breakdown or retention cohorts. Agent FM’s 60-plus paying users belong to the earlier application. I will not turn token throughput into evidence of customers repeatedly choosing isoquant at sustainable prices.

I can back infrastructure before meaningful revenue when the technical artifact and founder history make the destination convincing. Here, that exception only partly applies. Kernel, memory, caching, and orchestration work place isoquant beneath applications, but the dossier does not demonstrate control of a scarce dependency. Nor does it establish the closed-provider dependence that would trigger my strongest objection. Written consent for training and model-specific zero-retention controls address privacy concerns, although their implementation remains unverified. My own money stays on the sidelines: **Neutral**. One verifiable result would change that decision—a customer-confirmed, 90-day production deployment showing isoquant’s optimization loop maintaining agreed quality while reducing cost across three materially different workloads, with positive gross margin. That would connect today’s useful engineering to the future I want to fund.
