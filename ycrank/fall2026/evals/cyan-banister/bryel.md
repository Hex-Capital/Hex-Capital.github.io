# Bryel -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I would like a small business to teach its own models from its own work, keep them on its own machines, and improve them without repeatedly asking an outside laboratory for permission. Bryel’s customer-owned models point toward that world. But ownership is the destination sign, not the road. Someone still has to turn messy work into training examples, construct reliable rewards, and make improvement routine. Foundry proposes to handle those transitions; the dossier does not yet show them happening together. I can imagine the future here, but my imagination cannot count as Bryel’s evidence.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that this team has already moved beyond a presentation. YC’s October 2026 announcement describes delivered customer models: classification running 20× faster, search costing 5× less, and agents matching frontier performance at 5–8× lower cost. Those are consequential claims, even without published methodology. Customers apparently entrusted the team with real tasks. Yet delivery does not establish that customers repeatedly use, pay for, or improve these models. I give the working artifact credit without converting performance claims into demonstrated customer devotion.

Kevin Miao’s BAIR vision-transformer research, public software, and Apple machine-learning experience give the technical work substance. Frankie Li brings AI-product experience from Fundamental Research Labs. This is more than two impressive school names. What I cannot establish is the obsession before the company: which problem Kevin kept rebuilding around, what Frankie persuaded someone to try, or how their collaboration survived an ugly deployment. My pre-revenue exception at **CosmicBrain AI** rested on a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a lifelong building record. Bryel shares the technical foundation; the documented first-believer behavior that completed that exception is missing. Lack of revenue is not the deciding gap.

Why does a business need an embedded training team in the first place? Because owning examples is different from knowing how to turn them into a dependable model. That expertise gap is the system Bryel could repair, returning deployment and operating control to customers. This fits my quieter pattern of backing unglamorous infrastructure beneath a visible wave. But it matters enormously whether Foundry transfers that expertise or whether Bryel’s people keep supplying it. Fireworks already offers specialized training, serving, ownership, and continued improvement. Bryel controls software, but the dossier establishes no scarce technical constraint that its software uniquely removes. Classification, search, and agents provide several possible destinations; they do not establish a defensible bridge.

Customer ownership and proposed on-premises deployment also push against my red flag of dependence on one closed model supplier. They do not establish consent or data-custody safeguards, although the dossier supplies no affirmative evidence of a privacy violation. I would **pass at present**: the most persuasive evidence concerns a technically credible team delivering useful work, while the six-year transition remains unproven. My one verifiable flip condition is a named customer demonstrating repeated Foundry-driven training and deployment cycles on customer-controlled infrastructure, with measured performance improvements and no continuing embedded Bryel team. That would show the customer receiving a capability that keeps working after the experts leave.
