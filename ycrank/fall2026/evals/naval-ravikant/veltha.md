# Veltha -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Veltha’s founders have worked where rules meet messy evidence. Roland Jaloszynski’s Palantir work on mortgage underwriting automation is particularly relevant: translating documents into regulated decisions is learned through deployment. Edin Citaku brings government AI deployment experience. Those are useful actions, beyond credentials. But neither establishes mastery of workers’ compensation claims. Their current product extends that experience into the exact problem; its reported performance still lacks named deployments or independent validation.

The strongest case for investing is that becoming the administrator lets Veltha capture the value its automation creates. Selling software leaves the customer to reorganize the work. Operating the TPA could let these founders reorganize it themselves. Their October launch claims six hours of preparation reduced to ten minutes and four times the caseload per adjuster. If those figures survive production, the opportunity is substantial. But preparation speed is only one component of claims economics. Licensed review, exceptions, customer acquisition, and liability still determine what remains for the owner.

My decisive question is what Veltha accumulates that a competitor cannot buy or reproduce. Source-linked regulatory determinations are useful product functionality. The dossier establishes no exclusive claims data, proprietary model advantage, or demonstrated switching costs. Adjuster corrections could become an asset, but ownership and measured improvement remain unverified. Sedgwick already combines claims operations, AI, and proprietary data through Omni. Veltha must show why its learning compounds faster than an incumbent’s existing operating history.

There is enough market to make that question worth answering. Workers’ compensation administration represented approximately $5 billion in 2023. Document collection, evidence extraction, and rule application also offer a plausible bridge into other insurance lines; Reserv’s broader P&C business demonstrates adjacent commercial demand. That supports room to maneuver. It does not establish proprietary distribution. The founders’ July account of more than 50 conference leads shows active learning about buyers, while its relationship-dependent sales process supplies no evidence that customers attract or improve the product for one another. Contract pricing, realized revenue, and contribution economics remain undisclosed, so direct value capture receives only the missing-evidence default.

I would pass today. Veltha fits my pattern of founders turning specialized knowledge into scalable output, but the scalable part remains the unresolved investment claim. Its regulated buying process offers little permissionless distribution, and no customer-created network is demonstrated. I see no documented integrity or artificial-deadline red flag; the move from Co-Adjuster software to a TPA could be sensible adaptation. My exceptions for exceptional founder behavior or an unusually compelling early product deserve consideration, but adjacent operating experience and unverified efficiency claims do not yet establish that override. I would reopen on one result: an independently audited deployment showing that Veltha’s exclusively controlled correction history materially lowers handling cost on new claims at unchanged decision accuracy. That would demonstrate an accumulating asset behind the service.
