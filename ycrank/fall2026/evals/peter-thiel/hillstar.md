# HillStar -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

HillStar’s possible secret is that utilities will entrust procurement decisions to software once it preserves the institutional reasoning behind an award. That is more interesting than faster document processing. But the dossier does not establish that utilities or named competitors reject this proposition, or report a test that could disprove it. Evaluating 19,800 pages in nine minutes demonstrates processing speed; it does not establish decision correctness. I give the secret the absent-evidence default, without treating its absence from public research as evidence against the founders.

Who owns the advantage after the documents have been read? HillStar has chosen a recognizable beachhead: utility procurement committees reconciling technical requirements and vendor submissions. Capturing evaluator judgments, exceptions, and supplier outcomes could eventually make the product difficult to replace. Today, those are advertised capabilities. There is no demonstrated exclusive dataset, retention effect, or technical advantage that preserves the value HillStar creates. Keelvar and Fairmarkit overlap with the workflow, although the dossier does not prove they are interchangeable substitutes. The monopoly mechanism remains unproved rather than refuted; that warrants the default, not a commodity-company penalty.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ unusually relevant operating experience. Behroyan founded Paxon Energy and reports a 2025 exit; Stone co-founded PlanGrid and has worked in geothermal software. This fits the unstated preference for companies formed by operators who have encountered neglected institutional problems. The company-published examples also describe consequential work: a seven-day sourcing cycle and two bidders missing mandatory certification, including the cheapest bidder. HillStar may understand that the scarce product is a defensible award decision. But operating credentials do not establish founding structure. Shared working history, current full-time commitments, ownership, and economic exposure remain undisclosed, so alignment receives exactly its default. Stone’s *former* fractional role and a part-time staff vacancy do not establish a part-time founder red flag.

The live prospect-RFP walkthrough is a concrete sales entry point: let the procurement buyer inspect the product on work already requiring attention. That earns modest distribution credit beyond an unspecified enterprise-sales claim. Behroyan’s utility background adds plausibility to buyer access, but cannot establish repeatability. Contract values, conversion, sales cycles, and implementation costs are unknown. Likewise, enterprise software accompanied by procurement professionals is only an inferred revenue model. Without pricing or delivery margins, I cannot distinguish a scalable software engine from expert services with software assistance. The power-law dimension therefore stays at its default.

I would pass on writing a check today. HillStar fits the operator-network pattern and touches the infrastructure constraints surrounding AI, but those patterns do not establish ownership of an enduring market. The known-exception overrides require independent technical proof, persistent transactional demand, or a concrete validated deployment path; anonymous, company-published outcomes do not yet remove the monopoly uncertainty. There is also no evidence of the regulatory, capitalization, or technical-collapse red flags that would justify a stronger rejection. One verifiable event would move me to active diligence: an independently confirmed utility renewal where the buyer documents choosing HillStar over an alternative because accumulated procurement history materially improved its award decisions. That would begin to show why the next procurement belongs to HillStar.
