# Bernard -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Bernard’s contested truth is that appliance repair can be diagnosed before dispatch because fragmented service histories, symptom evidence, parts catalogs, and manufacturer guidance contain enough information to predict the required repair. The obvious falsifier is equally concrete: across completed jobs, Pre-ID fails to improve first-visit resolution or parts-selection accuracy. This is a potentially real secret, not merely “AI for home services,” but Bernard publishes neither accuracy nor first-visit-fix data. Live usage across thousands of daily calls shows that the workflow exists; it does not yet prove the underlying diagnostic claim.

The beachhead is appropriately narrow. Appliance servicers confront model-specific parts complexity that broad field-service software does not necessarily solve. Completed repairs can feed a shared knowledge base, and Bernard’s terms permit aggregated customer data to improve its models. But a permitted feedback loop is not yet a monopoly mechanism. There is no dataset size, measurable accuracy advantage, retention evidence, or exclusive supply of repair outcomes. ServiceTitan already owns customer and job data while adding AI intake and dispatch, and Probook is building a broader AI operating system. Bernard may have defined a real small market—or merely drawn a narrow circle inside an incumbent’s product roadmap.

The foundation is better than the technology evidence. Katzman brings operations and partnerships experience; Goodfellow managed fraud and geo-compliance products and previously worked on enterprise automation. Both present as full-time founders, the nine-person team appears to keep the product in-house, and their shared USC history is better than a networking-event match. Yet attending the same university does not establish that they previously built together, while ownership, salaries, and economic exposure are undisclosed. None of the severe foundation red flags is documented, but long alignment cannot be inferred from matching biographies.

The strongest bull case is the deployment pattern: Bernard claims large authorized service companies, thousands of calls per day, and identifiable use by Mr. Appliance of West Loop. Demo-led sales into servicers, manufacturers, warranty companies, retailers, and service networks can attach the product to an urgent operational expense rather than discretionary experimentation. If every repair produces proprietary outcome data, early distribution could compound into technical advantage. That is also the known-exception logic that could override a crowded market—but only after a verified technical event. Here the claimed activity is substantial while the decisive outcome remains unmeasured.

Economically, Bernard has one coherent subscription engine, including annual contracts, enterprise pricing, and volume commitments. It does not disclose price, revenue, gross margin, sales-cycle length, conversion, or expansion. A $1.90 billion appliance-repair-software market can support a valuable vertical company, but it does not by itself establish a power-law outcome. The unstated pattern fits only weakly: software is reaching into a neglected physical workflow, yet Bernard controls neither the appliances, parts supply, nor service network. The market-definition red flag therefore remains unresolved—specialized data layer or competitive field-service feature?

My 36-month base case is $1–4 million ARR followed by a narrowing toward appliance diagnostic and parts intelligence rather than a standalone “AI employee” operating system. I would not invest today. One verifiable condition would flip the decision: cohort evidence from at least ten multi-location servicers showing a 25-percentage-point improvement in first-visit fix rates and at least 120% net revenue retention after twelve months. That would convert the secret from assertion to proof and the feedback loop from possibility to monopoly mechanism.
