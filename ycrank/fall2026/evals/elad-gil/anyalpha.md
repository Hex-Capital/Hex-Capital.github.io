# AnyAlpha -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AnyAlpha’s market turns on whether prediction-market traders will keep paying to delegate execution of their own theses. Natural-language agents could reduce the work between identifying an opportunity and placing a trade; Polymarket’s May 2026 US launch potentially expands the buyer base. But AnyAlpha’s US integration is unconfirmed, and the dossier does not establish the controlling economic variable: how much recurring customer value this automation captures after subscription costs, trading costs, and execution errors. I would pass with my own money today.

The product has more substance than a research chatbot. AnyAlpha describes market discovery, condition setting, backtesting, and execution through connected accounts. Those actions give stronger models somewhere useful to go: better interpretation could improve a workflow that actually places trades. That earns architectural credit. It does not yet establish durable ownership of account permissions, execution history, or a feedback loop competitors cannot reproduce. Composer and Capitalise.ai already combine strategy creation, testing, and execution in adjacent markets; Olas addresses autonomous prediction-market trading directly. The category is sufficiently formed that “agents for trading” needs a sharper winner case.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ demonstrated ability to build and monetize products. Their previous apps reached a reported $500K ARR, and they have built together for three years. That is relevant startup learning. Shirley’s Coinbase and LSEG experience and Jerry’s six years developing quantitative trading systems also make this a credible team to attempt the workflow. Jerry committing personal capital to trading is more informative than his employer names, although his reported investment returns do not establish that customers can profitably use AnyAlpha. The plausible bull case is that this team combines consumer distribution experience with execution expertise before a default prediction-market interface emerges.

That case runs into attribution. The 42 iOS ratings and 100+ Android downloads include an earlier product; they cannot establish demand for the current agents. The demo’s $53 gain on $100 demonstrates neither repeat customer use nor company revenue. Listed purchases of $4.99–$79.99 establish an offer, with billing periods and agent entitlements still unclear. I assign the current-demand default rather than treating these figures as traction or interpreting missing traction as churn. Likewise, the dossier supplies no measured reduction in research time, execution cost, or delivery expense to justify an economic step-change premium.

AnyAlpha fits my preference for integrating an entire workflow, and its founders have useful prior operating experience. But my pattern of backing multiple layers of a technology shift does not remove the need to identify where value accrues. The relevant red flag is reproducibility: no exclusive execution dataset or demonstrated learning advantage is established. My exceptions for scarce frontier-model talent do not apply to the evidence here, and the outdated sports-information terms leave the current offering less clearly defined. I would reopen the investment on one verifiable result: at least 60% 90-day paid retention among a cohort of 50 funded accounts using the current agent product, at a disclosed recurring price. That would begin to distinguish a recurring execution business from an appealing trading demo.
