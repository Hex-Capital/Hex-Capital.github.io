# Palisade -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Fnu Prince left Caltech at 18 after beginning professional red- and blue-team work at 16. Palisade is a contrarian kernel hacker’s product trapped inside a commodity-SaaS capital envelope: founder nonconformity is Palisade’s strongest dimension, while fit with my required-capital profile is its weakest. Palisade may become useful security infrastructure. Useful is not the same as “too important not to do.”

**OpenAI** (2019) is the high rung for defying consensus: an unconventional organization pursuing a world-state most experts considered implausible. Palisade shares the instinct to attack beneath the application layer; Prince abandoned the credential path and built kernel-level remediation around eBPF, syscalls, and memory events. Palisade diverges where it matters: no public thesis contradicts security orthodoxy, no GitHub work establishes intellectual leadership, and Cisco’s Tetragon already performs in-kernel enforcement. My unstated pattern favors either a prior exit or a publicly legible intellectual reputation. Prince’s 207 LinkedIn followers and absent public repositories do not yet satisfy that pattern.

**Slash** (2024) represents the low rung on required capital: commodity software economics made my capital depth unnecessary, and only a documented founder pivot overrode the mismatch. Palisade discloses no training program, hardware fleet, research program, or deployment plan requiring even $50 million before commercialization. Palisade appears fundable through ordinary per-endpoint SaaS economics. I can write a large check, but capital is not a moat when Palisade’s principal competitors—Cisco, Aqua Security, Sysdig, and CrowdStrike—already possess distribution and deeper balance sheets.

The **$95.9 billion projected endpoint-security market** describes Palisade’s opportunity but not its consequence. If Palisade compounds successfully for 18 months, fleet remediation could contract from weeks to minutes, producing more telemetry, better policies, and safer automated rollouts. Yet Palisade does not collapse endpoint security; Palisade moves spending among endpoint-security vendors. No named labor pool disappears, no physical constraint breaks, and no civilizational variable moves. That is a meaningful automation mechanism, not an **Impossible Foods**-scale replacement of an incumbent production system.

**Cisco’s Tetragon already provides eBPF observability and runtime enforcement**, making Palisade’s technical-risk score depend on what remains unsolved rather than on the vocabulary of kernel telemetry. Palisade would move up a tier if it demonstrated autonomous remediation across Linux, Windows, and macOS with independently measured false-positive rates, deterministic rollback, and protection against previously unseen exploit classes. Palisade currently shows three named CVEs—CVE-2024-1086, CVE-2025-6019, and CVE-2025-32463—but publishes no detection rates, remediation-error rates, or platform matrix. A demo is evidence of engineering. It is not yet evidence of a technical miracle.

**Fnu Prince reports encountering OS vulnerabilities through offensive and defensive security work since age 16**, but Palisade provides no evidence of a pivot or explicit thesis update. The relevant single-anchor parallel is **Slash**: Slash’s public Kanye-driven pivot revealed learning rate under pressure, while Palisade’s history remains a single linear plan. Palisade receives the absent-evidence default here—not because a Summer 2026 founder should have a long history, but because learning rate cannot be inferred from precocity.

**Three demonstrated CVE remediations without disclosed customers** produce my 36-month forecast: Palisade will narrow into Linux cloud-runtime policy, remain below $5 million ARR, and be acquired by Cisco/Isovalent for under $100 million rather than become an independent endpoint-security platform. Palisade’s high founder-defiance signal can generate valuable engineering, while Palisade’s low capital requirement and absent structural moat make acquisition more probable than category collapse. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself” does not mean every good subsystem becomes a great independent company.

**DoorDash** (2013) is the strongest objection because a capital-light YC company can outrun a thesis mismatch when founder ambition and execution create a new economic flow. Palisade could similarly turn automated kernel remediation into a trusted control plane, and Prince’s age-16 security experience plus three working CVE demonstrations are real pre-seed signals. The objection still loses today: Palisade has no disclosed deployment, proprietary dataset, rollback benchmark, or answer to Tetragon’s existing enforcement capability. I would flip to Invest if Palisade reaches $1 million ARR from at least three large fleets while publishing independently reproducible false-positive and rollback results across two operating-system families.
