# Robocurve -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Jay Chooi’s UK AI Security Institute evaluation work and Aris Zhu’s Amazon Robotics experience make **Robocurve** an intellectually contrarian evaluation institution with a commodity-capital profile. That is **Robocurve’s** dimensional signature: strongest when challenging laboratories that grade their own robots, weakest where my money needs a credible path for deploying a $50M check. What economic mechanism breaks if Robocurve works? Today, none. One completed physical-robot pilot improves measurement; it does not yet collapse an incumbent system.

**KiOR** (~2008) is the high rung on defying expert consensus because it converted petroleum orthodoxy into falsifiable claims about catalyst yield, feedstock economics, and refinery integration. **Robocurve** shares the instinct: its founders reject the accepted practice of vendor-run evaluation and claim no independent group continuously benchmarks physical robots. The analogy breaks where it matters. **Robocurve** has published no full comparative pilot results, Chooi and Zhu largely extend their AI-evaluation and robotics careers, and neither founder has publicly committed to a controversial technical proposition. This is institutional contrarianism, not yet the expert-defying instigation that drove KiOR.

**DoorDash** (2013) is the low rung on required-capital fit, not company quality: capital-light logistics normally made me over-resourced, and only the prospect of reorganizing consumer commerce justified the exception. **Robocurve** offers MIT-licensed software, 96 GitHub stars, one pilot, and an inquiry form with no published pricing or revenue. Nothing in **Robocurve’s** roadmap explains how $50M would accelerate a proprietary technical milestone rather than subsidize evaluations that robot vendors can run themselves. DoorDash had network effects and eventually a $72B outcome; Robocurve has 13 forks and no demonstrated economic flywheel.

Forty-plus VLAs and more than 260 language models give **Robocurve** the beginnings of a consequence mechanism: every physical run could compound into longitudinal data, benchmark authority, and a default interface for robot procurement. Over the next 18 months, however, **Robocurve** must turn coverage into control—results that determine which robots factories purchase, insure, or deploy. Without that authority, Robocurve merely measures the possible displacement of labor; it does not cause the displacement. I invest against the $15T labor variable, not in a scorekeeper adjacent to it.

**KitchenBench** and **DataCenterBench** make the technical counterfactual concrete. I would move **Robocurve** into an investable tier if reproducible testing across kitchens, data centers, and robot form factors required proprietary metrology, standardized physical facilities, and more than $50M of operated infrastructure. The dossier instead describes optional digital twins, an MIT-licensed framework, and laboratories that already own the necessary hardware and telemetry. **Robocurve** faces difficult engineering and benchmark-design work, but no named physics, hardware, or capital miracle that repels consensus VCs.

**Slash** (2024) earned an exception because its documented Kanye West pivot revealed learning rate, not because consumer business banking was civilizational. **Robocurve** provides no comparable thesis update: Chooi moved through MATS, Inspect Evals, and the UK AI Security Institute, while Zhu moved through Amazon Robotics, Amazon AGI Labs, and Yondu Robotics into robot evaluation. For **Robocurve**, there is no evidence of a prior pivot or explicit “we were wrong” moment—applying the absent-evidence default. Relevant experience is not evidence of learning rate; experts often extrapolate the past with more confidence than accuracy.

One completed physical-robot pilot is enough to make a forecast falsifiable: by July 2029, **Robocurve** will have pivoted into a vendor-funded robotics-assurance consultancy generating roughly $500,000-$2 million in ARR, rather than becoming an independent benchmark platform. **Robocurve’s** contrarian evaluation expertise can sell services, but its low capital requirements, open-source replication, and vendor-controlled hardware pull it toward consulting economics. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself” does not mean every useful institution becomes a venture-scale company.

Ninety-six GitHub stars, 13 forks, integration with 40-plus VLAs, and complementary founders form the strongest bull case: **Robocurve** could become the neutral standard just as **DoorDash** escaped my capital-risk criterion by building a category-level network. If laboratories contribute benchmarks while customers demand independent scores, **Robocurve’s** open source could become distribution rather than leakage. The evidence does not yet support that exception: vendors can substitute their own evaluations, the only pilot’s comparative results remain unpublished, and no customer pays Robocurve for recurring authority. One condition flips my verdict: by January 2028, three named frontier-robotics laboratories must sign recurring paid evaluations whose published results are used in customer procurement decisions.
