# RealPact -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Ranvir Deshmukh and Erik Peterson make RealPact a repeat-builder pair attacking a genuine schlep, but RealPact is weakest exactly where the best startup ideas usually begin: neither founder is known to have worked in a brokerage or personally suffered through a closing. CourseMe reached 4,000 monthly users, 1 million page hits, and roughly 90% of Dartmouth before being acquired. That tells me Deshmukh and Peterson can make and distribute things. It does not yet tell me they had to make RealPact.

**Stripe** is the high rung for cofounder configuration: two brothers who had already built Auctomatic together before turning their shared commerce pain into an API. RealPact has the important part of that mechanism. Deshmukh and Peterson previously built CourseMe together, then moved through SignPact into RealPact instead of meeting at a founder-dating event. RealPact diverges from Stripe because a Dartmouth campus product is a smaller test than Auctomatic, the duration of the founders’ working relationship is unclear, and the listed team size of three leaves the third person’s role unexplained. Still, this is a real pair, not two résumés stapled together.

**Loopt** is the low rung on organic founder pain: force of personality could override weak product timing, but the idea itself was not enough to justify the check. RealPact has the same missing piece without a Sam Altman-sized override signal. No brokerage employment, real-estate operating history, or personal use of RealPact appears in the dossier. RealPact seems to have been reached by iterating from SignPact and observing an inefficient market. That can work, but it is discovery by search rather than Dropbox’s forgotten USB stick. The explicit red flag is present: no founder is shown using the product as a customer.

CourseMe’s 4,000 monthly users and SignPact’s first 100 customers are the animal tracks in RealPact’s history. The important mechanism is not the $9.99 price; it is that Deshmukh and Peterson repeatedly shipped, found users, discarded an insufficient product, and sold RealPact to paying New Hampshire brokerages before institutional scale. Over the next 18 months, that resourcefulness can compound because every ugly county portal and manually corrected contract becomes another workflow RealPact knows how to automate. RealPact is below Airbnb’s cereal boxes because the dossier contains no comparably improbable stunt, but paying brokers and a Four Seasons Sotheby’s pilot are much better evidence than credentials alone.

No public GitHub repositories were found for either founder, which keeps RealPact’s hacker genealogy below the top tier despite Peterson’s React, React Native, Firebase, and Swift work. The counterfactual is simple: if RealPact has already built a reusable property-record ingestion system that survives changing schemas across dozens of counties, and Deshmukh or Peterson can show the underlying artifact rather than merely describe an AI agent, this score jumps. If RealPact is mostly model calls wrapped around brittle browser automation, it falls. CourseMe proves shipping ability; RealPact has not yet publicly proved technical taste.

**Coinbase** is the useful parallel for RealPact’s schlep. Coinbase’s compliance work looked like execution risk until the willingness to do it became the asset. RealPact faces jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction public-record integrations, stale source data, legally consequential contract fields, unfinished SOC 2 work, and brokerage onboarding. Those are unpleasant facts, which is encouraging. RealPact can turn them into accumulated operational knowledge if the founders personally live inside the corrections. SkySlope’s 900,000 professionals make the race harder, but SkySlope cannot make every county portal consistent merely by having more customers.

Paying New Hampshire brokerages and the Four Seasons Sotheby’s pilot lead me to predict that by July 2029 RealPact will reach $3 million to $6 million in ARR from roughly 50 to 100 brokerage customers, while remaining strongest in fragmented regional record retrieval rather than becoming the national brokerage “OS” it currently describes. RealPact’s strong founder pair should be able to grind through enough integrations to build a substantial business. RealPact’s weak organic founder-problem fit will make it harder to recognize which brokerage workflows matter before customers explain them.

SkySlope’s existing AI, compliance products, and 900,000-professional footprint form the strongest bear case against RealPact: incumbents could copy external-record retrieval while RealPact absorbs the liability for putting stale model output into contracts. The absence of public repositories triggers the hacker-genealogy concern, and the absence of personal brokerage experience triggers the organic-idea red flag. The counterevidence is behavioral: RealPact’s founders built CourseMe to 90% campus adoption, secured an acquisition, sold SignPact, found paying brokers, and obtained a firm-wide Sotheby’s pilot. That resembles the Kiko-to-Twitch pattern of learning through a prior product more than the polished AI-wrapper pattern. I would invest, but I would reverse the verdict if the Sotheby’s pilot does not convert and RealPact cannot show ten brokerages retained for at least six months by July 2027.
