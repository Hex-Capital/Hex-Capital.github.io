# Osmaura -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Yeabkal Abeje and Jity Woldemichael are MIT friends who left in 2026 to build Osmaura, which makes Osmaura a strong-pair, borrowed-pain startup. The pair is Osmaura’s strongest signal; the fact that neither founder worked inside a law firm is its weakest. Months of partner interviews and daily customer use show that the founders found a real problem. But finding a problem by interviewing customers is different from being Drew Houston repeatedly forgetting his USB stick. The first produces startups deliberately. The second produces them almost accidentally.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for co-founder configuration: two brothers with years of shared hacking history and complementary force, building something they already needed. Osmaura shares the important structural mechanism—a technical pair from the same institution willing to leave school together—and Osmaura’s founders cover NLP, infrastructure, policy research, Meta, AWS, and Jane Street between them. The divergence is history. “Friends from MIT” is not evidence that Abeje and Woldemichael built together for two years, survived a failure together, or have the almost telepathic working relationship the Collisons had. Osmaura has the right shape, but the dossier does not yet prove the bond inside the shape.

**Loopt** (2005) is the low rung on organicity: weak product timing was overridden by Sam Altman’s force of personality rather than by evidence that a working personal need had pulled the product into existence. Osmaura has an adjacent weakness. Abeje researched policy prediction and Woldemichael researched computational astrophysics, but neither founder was a law-firm partner spending 10 hours a week chasing clients. Osmaura emerged from months of interviews, which is respectable customer research but also the standard way founders manufacture founder-market fit after choosing a market. Daily use says Osmaura may be useful. It does not retroactively make the idea organic.

Customers reportedly log into Osmaura daily, and that can compound into animal resourcefulness if Abeje and Woldemichael spend the next 18 months manually inspecting false positives, briefing partners, and learning which events actually become legal matters. The contrary evidence is unusually specific: Osmaura spent two months negotiating with a major law firm and withdrew rather than surrender months more attention. That could be good discipline. It could also be schlep blindness in reverse—the founders discovering the unpleasant part and routing around it. Osmaura has shipped an artifact, but there is no cereal-box stunt, named customer, disclosed revenue, or other track left by an unmistakable animal.

Kali Abeje’s public repositories top out at one star, while Jity Woldemichael’s `basis` has three. Osmaura would move up a full tier on hacker genealogy if the founders could point to a technically surprising artifact predating fundraising—a widely used open-source system, a public event-matching engine competitors could not reproduce, or even a Hacker News demo that attracted legal users without outbound sales. MIT CS, two AWS internships, Meta, and a Jane Street fellowship establish technical competence. They do not establish the pre-startup hacker trail that made Aaron Swartz or Patrick Collison legible. Osmaura looks like it can build the software; Osmaura has not yet shown technical taste so strong that building it was inevitable.

**Coinbase** (2012) turned compliance grind into an asset because Brian Armstrong ran toward the part everyone else disliked. Osmaura has a real version of that test: noisy public signals, confidential CRM data, partner reputations, security scrutiny, and enterprise procurement all have to work simultaneously. Those are useful schleps because incumbents cannot solve them merely by adding an agent-shaped button. But Osmaura withdrew from the one disclosed major-firm negotiation after two months, while Nexl and Intapp already possess customer relationships into which overlapping features can be bundled. Osmaura is standing at the entrance to a good schlep zone. It has not proved it likes living there.

Nexl has raised $33 million and Intapp already sells AI-assisted pursuit intelligence, so my falsifiable forecast is that by July 2029 Osmaura will have pivoted from autonomous pre-need detection into a broader legal relationship-intelligence workflow and will be producing between $1 million and $3 million in ARR. Osmaura’s strong founder pair should be able to keep iterating until firms buy something, but Osmaura’s borrowed origin makes it more likely that customer requests pull the product toward familiar CRM workflows than that the founders preserve a strange new category. Competent pairs often find a business. Organic ideas are more likely to find a new default.

Customers using Osmaura daily are the strongest case against that forecast: firm-specific profiles could accumulate conversion data, deepen trust, and turn Osmaura into the system that knows a legal need before the client articulates it. **Loopt** shows that extraordinary founder force can override weak timing, and **Airbnb** (2009) shows that my first reading of an idea can be badly wrong. The bull case still loses today because Osmaura discloses neither customer count nor revenue, has no proprietary dataset yet, and stepped away from the only described major-firm procurement schlep. One condition would flip my verdict: ten named law firms paying Osmaura at least $1 million in aggregate ARR while retaining more than 70% weekly partner usage for six months.
