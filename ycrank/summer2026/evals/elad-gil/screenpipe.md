# screenpipe -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: MCP-enabled agents now need persistent context, knowledge workers can buy that context as a $21-$42 monthly product, and the controlling variable is whether captured history becomes indispensable to recurring agent work rather than remaining a searchable archive. screenpipe has evidence bearing on this belief—202 paid subscribers, $3,480 MRR in February 2025, 4K monthly downloads, and subsequent company-reported revenue growth of 20-30% week over week—but it has not disclosed retention, expansion, or the percentage of users repeatedly invoking that history through agents. The market gate passes, narrowly; the decisive behavior is visible but not yet measured cleanly.

The technical work is more substantial than the phrase “AI memory” suggests. A 12,176-commit repository captures screens, accessibility data, audio, and interface events across three operating systems, then retains local workflow state behind search, an API, MCP, encryption, and agent permissions. Better models should improve what can be extracted and executed from that history. They do not automatically reproduce the capture layer, permissions, accumulated state, or cross-application integrations. This is closer to owned infrastructure than a prompt wrapper, although screenpipe still stops short of taking responsibility for a completed business workflow.

The strongest objection is distribution and value capture. Microsoft can bundle Recall into Windows, meeting products can expand beyond calls, and every major component—SQLite, Whisper, OCR, MCP, and operating-system capture APIs—is available to competitors. The claimed 300K users also sits awkwardly beside the last disclosed 320 weekly active users, while local storage and exportability make the proposed data moat inexpensive to reproduce or abandon. Privacy and consent constraints could further push enterprises toward incumbent-controlled systems. If screenpipe remains a developer-oriented archive, model and platform improvements compress its value rather than compound it.

What keeps me on the investing side is demonstrated pull before the story was polished: paid subscriptions, organic acquisition, 20.9K GitHub stars, 2.1K forks, and sixfold active-user growth over two months. This resembles my unstated pattern of buying useful infrastructure across a discontinuity before the final application layer settles. The material limitation is that this market is already crystallizing around Microsoft, Otter, and Granola, so broad technical coverage is not itself a winner case. The known frontier-team and physical-economics exceptions do not apply; screenpipe must prove ordinary product-market fit and workflow ownership.

Louis Beaumont adds credible execution evidence without determining the investment. He previously founded AI companies, worked in data infrastructure and satellite intelligence, and reports maintaining a personal AI second brain since 2020. That supports founder-market fit and prior learning, but the dossier does not document a prior exit, costly sacrifice, or the desperation signal required for a top score. Hiring a Forward Deployed Engineer is coherent with proving deployments; hiring a Head of Virality before retention is disclosed risks scaling acquisition ahead of a durable loop, though it is not yet the broad-hiring red flag.

I would invest a small personal check and expect screenpipe to reach roughly $10-$25 million ARR by August 2029 if it becomes the permissioned context layer used by multiple agents, rather than the dominant end-user productivity suite. My one verifiable flip condition is paid-cohort evidence by February 2027 that at least 20% of seats still invoke screenpipe context through its API or MCP weekly after six months; failure would show that the repository’s popularity represents curiosity rather than an owned workflow.
