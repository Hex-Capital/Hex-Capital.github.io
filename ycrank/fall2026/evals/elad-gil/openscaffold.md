# OpenScaffold -- Elad Gil Evaluation

OpenScaffold’s market turns on the fully loaded cost of an accepted production deployment. Enterprise AI adoption creates implementation work; deployment teams become buyers if automation makes that work materially cheaper without increasing failures or human rework. Generating a custom agent scaffold matters only if it changes that equation. The dossier describes the mechanism but supplies no measured deployment outcome or economic threshold. I can formulate the governing belief; I cannot yet underwrite it.

There is a dated institutional change behind the opportunity: EY announced forward-deployed engineering roles in April 2026. That establishes that a major services organization is organizing around this work. It does not establish how much OpenScaffold can automate or what margins remain after customer-specific engineering. Auctor, Tandem, and June already address overlapping implementation functions; June announced $20 million in pre-seed funding in August 2026. This looks like a category forming around a real problem, but competitor financing supplies neither OpenScaffold’s demand evidence nor its winner case. My pattern of investing across layers of a technology shift makes the category interesting; it does not establish differentiation for this entrant.

The strongest bull case is that a deployment-specific scaffold becomes the persistent execution environment for the customer. Better models could then complete more work while OpenScaffold retains permissions, integrations, deployment history, and responsibility for the result. Its stated progression from customer discovery to autonomous operation points toward that possibility. However, those durable assets are not documented. Previous deployments are described as an input, without evidence of accumulated volume, data rights, or a measured learning effect. The red flag would be claiming an inexpensive-to-reproduce dataset as a moat; the present dossier establishes neither that claim nor a defensible dataset.

The founders make the bull case more credible. Both worked at USUL, Mehta was a founding engineer, and his biography describes leading an AI transaction-error platform at JPMorgan. That is relevant operating experience. It does not document a prior founder outcome, specific startup lessons, or costly personal commitment sufficient to raise my founder score. Nor does it establish the rare frontier-research capability that would justify my exception for investing before commercial pull. The other potential exception—technology demonstrably reconstructing service economics—also remains unproven. The October 26 positioning change warrants careful attribution of future results, without treating a recent repositioning as evidence of failure.

I would pass with my own money today. OpenScaffold has no disclosed revenue, repeat usage, or named customer commitment; that receives the missing-evidence default, with no additional penalty for being pre-seed. The same distinction applies to undocumented economics and workflow ownership: uncertainty is not disproof, but it cannot earn affirmative credit. My single verifiable flip condition is a paid deployment cohort demonstrating at least a 50% reduction in fully loaded cost per accepted production deployment against the customers’ existing process, including human intervention and rework. That is my underwriting threshold, not a reported result. It would connect the product’s central claim to customer value.
