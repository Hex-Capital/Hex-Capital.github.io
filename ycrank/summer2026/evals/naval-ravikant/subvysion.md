# SubVysion -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the founders’ résumés. It is that two students who joined forces in January 2026 built a multisensor rover, cold-called their way into a first customer and investor, and put the product on construction sites across the United States. Maxwell Stone’s deployed computer-vision work at Roboflow and railyard-mapping experience, combined with Wenson Tang’s LiDAR drone and emergency-dispatch work, make this action-revealed knowledge rather than claimed interest. They have already crossed from sensing software into a safety-critical field workflow.

The product also survives copying better than an ordinary AI application. Ground-penetrating radar, positioning, calibration, and field validation must work together before the phone can display a useful 3D map. But technical difficulty is not yet a durable asset. SubVysion discloses no proprietary dataset, performance advantage, repeat-use rate, or workflow lock-in, while Exodigo has mapped 86.1 million square feet for more than 100 customers and two other funded competitors attack the same subsurface problem. A hard product can still be a commodity if every deployment teaches only the company operating the equipment.

That distinction matters because SubVysion currently scales through labor rather than participants. Each site appears to require rover collection, alignment, quality control, and validation. No contractor contributes data or distribution that demonstrably improves the product for another contractor. The attractive unstated-pattern case is that scattered underground knowledge becomes a shared, price-discoverable infrastructure map. The evidence presently describes isolated projects, not that network.

The market is real but bounded as presented. The cited global underground-utility-mapping market grows from $1.57 billion in 2025 to $4.08 billion in 2034, yet the U.S. segment is only $294.4 million and the dossier does not establish a causal expansion from contractor mapping into a larger transaction layer. Contracts and live sites are meaningful pre-seed validation, but without disclosed price, revenue, take rate, or gross margin, they prove willingness to engage more than direct value capture.

The strongest bull case is the known-exception case: capital-efficient technical founders can deserve backing before monetization or network effects become visible, particularly when they have already built a strange working system and sold it through cold outreach. SubVysion fits that pattern and also fits my shift toward physical infrastructure. What prevents the exception from controlling the decision is the unverified claim of centimeter accuracy and “100% visibility” despite documented GPR degradation in wet or clay soils. I see no founder-conduct, integrity, or financing-pressure red flag in the dossier; the risk is that an ambitious technical claim is doing work that independent performance data should do.

My August 2029 base case is a capable, field-operations-heavy mapping provider at roughly $2 million to $7 million ARR, rather than a compounding subsurface-data network. One condition flips me: a blinded third-party field study showing that accumulated deployments materially improve later ones—at least 95% utility recall and sub-10-centimeter median error across wet and clay conditions without proportional growth in field labor. Until the learning compounds, the founders are stronger than the business mechanism.
