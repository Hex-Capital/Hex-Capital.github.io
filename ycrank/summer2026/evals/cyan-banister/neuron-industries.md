# Neuron Industries -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Dennis Ren built industrial control panels at Tesla and production hardware at Freeform before Neuron Industries put Cortex into chemical plants, wastewater facilities, and small modular reactor environments. Neuron Industries is a frontier-hardware founder-origin bet with one exposed wire: the product is unusually physical and difficult, while authority between Ren and Kenneth Rhee remains undocumented. I can touch the controller, inspect the factory, and trace Ren’s biography into the machine. I cannot yet see who wins an irreconcilable argument.

SpaceX is the high rung because capital intensity and technical risk were the moat before conventional investors recognized them. Neuron Industries shares that load-bearing mechanism: Cortex combines real-time control hardware, fieldbus connectivity, HMI, historian, testing, and a browser IDE, with a stated 0.5-microsecond P99.9 jitter target. Neuron Industries diverges where it matters: Cortex has no identified structural barrier or patent portfolio, and its SIL 1 path remains a production target. SpaceX had to make rockets work; Neuron must prove that integration becomes proprietary rather than merely impressive.

HQ Trivia exposed the low-rung failure mechanism: two talented founders without a durable tiebreaker can turn product magic into organizational shrapnel. Neuron Industries names Dennis Ren as CEO and Kenneth Rhee as CTO, which is better than shared titles, but Neuron Industries discloses neither its co-founder history nor majority operating authority, equity split, vesting terms, or conflict protocol. “CEO” is a label; tiebreaker authority is a fact. Until I see that fact, I apply the governance penalty despite the clean titles.

Dennis Ren’s Tesla, Apple Vision Pro, and Freeform hardware work gives Neuron Industries an autobiographical origin rather than a market-analysis costume. Over the next 18 months, that history can compound through installation judgment: every chemical-processing or wastewater deployment teaches Neuron Industries which fieldbus failures, ambiguous requirements, and operator behaviors never appear in a software demo. Kenneth Rhee’s WorkIPO exit and regulated Medidata experience add Biz and Tiz. The missing Rizz signal is environmental—I know the résumés, but not yet how these founders behave when a plant manager says the controller stopped a production line at 2 a.m.

Siemens and Rockwell make Neuron Industries weird in the productive sense: replacing a trusted PLC stack with natural-language-generated deterministic Python is the sort of industrial proposition sophisticated people dismiss before touching it. Neuron Industries earns more than the absent-evidence default because two hard-tech pilots were already live and hardware reached four demanding environments. The counterfactual is sharp: evidence of a six-fund bidding war would push the non-consensus score down a tier, while named industrial investors passing specifically because AI-generated controls feel unsafe would push it near the top. Today I see weirdness, but not proof that the round is underpriced.

Flock Safety is the single-anchor parallel because its truth was visible in the physical world before the spreadsheet caught up. Neuron Industries has Cortex hardware priced at $2,950 and $6,450, and Neuron Industries can be observed controlling equipment in chemical processing, electronics manufacturing, wastewater treatment, and small modular reactors. That is my kind of evidence: not a model benchmark, but a box wired into consequences. Cortex is less accessible than a neighborhood camera, yet a working controller in a live plant is more falsifiable than ninety percent of AI software.

Two live hard-tech pilots lead me to predict that by July 2029 Neuron Industries will have 40–80 paid production controllers deployed and $4 million–$9 million in annualized hardware and support revenue, concentrated in greenfield drone, chemical, and energy facilities. Neuron Industries gets there by avoiding wholesale replacement of Siemens and Rockwell installations and becoming the specification for new machines, where browser-based engineering and generated simulation tests compress commissioning time. The frontier-hardware strength creates the beachhead; the unresolved governance limits how confidently I extrapolate beyond it.

Cortex includes Synapse without runtime-license fees, faces entrenched Siemens and Rockwell channels, and asks industrial operators to trust AI-generated control code before its SIL 1 path is complete. The bear mechanism stings: Neuron Industries could become a beautifully engineered integration project with hardware margins, long certification cycles, and no recurring software economics, while a founder dispute arrives exactly when deployments become painful. Neuron Industries survives that objection because Ren has already lived the controls problem and pilots exist in four consequence-heavy settings—not merely in a YC demo. I would write the check, but failure to produce one named paid production deployment and independently verifiable SIL 1 progress within 18 months would flip me to Pass.
