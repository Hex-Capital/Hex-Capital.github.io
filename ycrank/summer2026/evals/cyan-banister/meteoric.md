# Meteoric -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Solar farms are no longer passive fields waiting for weather and interconnection approvals; operators dispatch autonomous aircraft to manage the atmosphere above already-installed capacity. Meteoric supplies two transitions that make this imaginable: electric-drone access at $30–$60 per hour instead of $2,000-plus aircraft, and AI weather forecasts produced in under a minute. The little leak from that future is a working chamber prototype that dissipated an artificial cloud by 13%. That is real enough to earn attention, but the distance from a chamber to coordinated fleets at 1–5 km still contains several unnamed miracles.

The system economics are unusually concrete for a pre-seed company. Overcast clouds remove 73–82% of incoming sunlight while 773 GW of proposed U.S. generation waits in the interconnection queue. Meteoric could return control to solar operators by extracting more energy from assets they already own rather than asking permission to build more. But why does this make money? The dossier does not say how many drone-hours recover one MWh, whether tens or hundreds of aircraft are required per site, or whether maintenance and fleet capital consume the modeled 10–30% annual uplift. The broken constraint is visible; the rent capture is not yet measured.

Mete Karslioglu and Eric Nilsson did not wander into weather modification because climate hardware became fashionable. Karslioglu worked on cloud-reflectivity spray nozzles and atmospheric instruments; Nilsson worked on digital microfluidics, LiDAR-lens droplet removal, and atmospheric measurement. Their Cambridge work from 2022 to 2026 converges almost suspiciously well on this machine. That is first-believer evidence and complementary technical fluency. I do not yet see the commercial half of the team’s education: recruiting proof, utility procurement experience, aviation operations, or evidence that either founder knows how capital turns into an approved fleet.

The strongest bull case is also the most dangerous one. A 13% chamber result from two deeply relevant engineers could be the primitive artifact preceding an entirely new atmospheric-control layer. Yet no chamber dimensions, repeated trials, natural-cloud measurements, or recovered solar output are public. The WMO says there is no generally accepted evidence that tropical cyclones can be modified, so the hurricane ambition currently dilutes rather than strengthens the solar thesis. Users have not paid, rearranged operations, or exposed a valuable site to testing. This is working physics without costly user behavior—the clearest weakness in the dossier.

Meteoric fits my concealed-infrastructure pattern better than a conventional climate application: control of droplets, forecasts, autonomous flight, and site-response data could eventually sit beneath solar generation, storm mitigation, aviation, and other weather-sensitive industries. Today, however, that layer is aspirational. Rainmaker already owns purpose-built drones, radar, forecasting, and mission-validation infrastructure, while Meteoric discloses no patent, proprietary dataset, permit portfolio, or deployed fleet. The active red flag is regulatory rather than fashionable-market dependence: baseline Part 107 rules conflict with 1–5 km, multi-drone, beyond-visual-line-of-sight operations. Governance is also undisclosed; with two founders, I would verify who has final control rather than inventing comfort from the CEO title.

I would write a small personal check, narrowly underwriting solar-farm cloud treatment—not hurricane weakening. My 36-month expectation is that Meteoric becomes a specialized solar and fog-management operator with a handful of paid sites and less than $3 million ARR by August 2029, while the hurricane program remains experimental. One verifiable condition would flip me from this cautious invest to strong conviction: an independent field study showing at least 5% net solar-output improvement, after drone energy use, across ten matched natural-cloud events.
