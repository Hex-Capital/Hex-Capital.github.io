# tash -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The crucial missing fact is what broke. Tash operated a card exchange that processed more than $22,000 across 710 trades and 250 listed cards, then replaced that route with regulated multi-card investment vehicles. But the dossier never says which observation caused the change, how quickly the founders reacted, or what they shipped around the resulting obstacle. SEC qualification remains pending. This is evidence that they build, but not yet that they become unusually resourceful when the path closes.

The founders’ collision with the underlying problem is much stronger. They have bought and sold more than $1 million of cards and collectibles, spent years purchasing at card shows and tracking prices, and worked in finance, data, and marketplaces. The regulatory, custody, authentication, sourcing, and liquidity burdens are genuine schleps. The team also has unusually durable social roots: three founders met in high school, all four attended Berkeley, and they became roommates. This does not prove operating ability, but it makes tash look like an organically discovered problem rather than “investing for cards” invented from a market report.

The prior exchange is a functioning artifact, not a diagram. Its 710 trades justify meaningful technical credit. They also provide some evidence that card traders will transact through software built by this team. But aggregate trades do not reveal a narrow group repeatedly pulling the product back, and they cannot validate a different product that has not launched. The current index has only a waitlist, no public pricing, no revenue, and no disclosed repeat behavior. Worse, the marketed CL50 history contains interpolated intermediate points while the final daily-series chart remains unfinished. For an investment product, measurement is part of the product.

The bull case is that the exchange was merely the founders’ first route. Their card experience exposed the burden of researching, pricing, authenticating, storing, and reselling individual assets, so they moved upward into diversified portfolios and recruited Rialto Markets and North Capital to handle regulated offerings. That would be a sensible running-back move. It also partly resembles the pre-round proof spike I favor: a real exchange preceded the more ambitious company. But the dossier supplies no customer observation connecting the 710 trades to the pivot. The current product therefore does not qualify for an exception based on unmistakable pull or independently validated proof.

The red flags reinforce this gap. Distribution currently consists of a YC launch and waitlist, while actual issuance depends on broker-dealers and SEC qualification. The partners are named, which is better than saying merely “we will partner,” but their timetable and the founders’ workaround if qualification stalls are absent. Rally, Collectable, Alt, and Dibbs already cover adjacent parts of the stack, and Collectable’s 2024 trading halt shows that legal issuance does not manufacture liquidity. Against a $13.51 billion market growing 7.9% annually, tash’s multi-card structure is a useful distinction, but without proprietary sourcing, performance history, or liquidity it is packaging rather than a moat.

I would not write the check yet. My 36-month base case is that tash abandons the regulated index and returns to a less capital- and permission-intensive card marketplace, portfolio-tracking, or asset-management product. The team knows the domain well enough to keep searching, but the dossier has not shown how they respond when the regulatory route resists them. One verifiable condition would flip me: an SEC-qualified first vehicle reaching $250,000 of subscriptions from at least 100 investors, with 30% of them buying a second offering within 90 days.
