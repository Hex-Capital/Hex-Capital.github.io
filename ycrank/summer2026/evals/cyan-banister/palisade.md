# Palisade -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the interesting version of Palisade is not a chat bubble. Marketplaces are continuously rearranged by agents representing buyers, sellers, and operators: buyers carry preferences between visits, sellers receive demand signals before changing inventory, and staff supervise rather than manually tune every funnel. Palisade has one leak from that world—buyer, seller, and staff agents are already live at Shorebreak and Vaulted Atelier—but the dossier does not map the transitions from two deployments to that future. Computer use is an available capability, not a six-year thesis, and $2.7 million in attributed GMV proves present utility rather than the destination.

The working artifact is the strongest evidence. Two operators let a one-person company connect to catalogs, booking engines, property-management systems, and CRMs, then influence more than $2.7 million in marketplace volume during the Summer 2026 batch. Installing those integrations is costly behavior; customers are placing real workflows and conversion opportunities in Palisade’s hands. The missing piece is attribution quality: I cannot tell whether the agents caused incremental transactions, merely touched them, or produced behavior stranger and more durable than conventional search and merchandising software.

Jonathan Salama has credible operating tuition. Amazon Traffic & Marketing, consumer-shopping agents at Sage AI Labs, and a one-million-subscriber Lulus TV channel collectively show familiarity with distribution, commerce, and product execution. This is more than a degree followed by an accelerator-shaped idea. But the biography still lacks the dated, self-directed artifacts and long pre-company obsession that would establish him as the first believer. Being the sole founder does at least make accountability unambiguous; there is no governance shell game hiding inside the team structure.

I keep asking which rule Palisade breaks. Search bars forget people, marketplace operators lose conversions, and sellers cannot see latent demand—but the dossier identifies no artificial restriction, captured rent, or gatekeeper whose removal returns meaningful agency. This is conversion optimization, not yet a transfer of occupational control. The product may make marketplaces much better, but “visitors browse without inquiring” is a symptom; it is not an explanation of why the system has its present shape.

The bull case stings because the traction arrived before a large team, and persistent histories plus deep operational integrations could accumulate into a marketplace-specific data asset. The bear case is that Rep AI, Constructor, and Nosto already occupy adjacent territory with substantially more capital, while general-purpose computer-use APIs expose the underlying navigation capability. Palisade currently controls neither a scarce model nor exclusive data rights. Its moat is therefore permission-dependent integration depth, and its privacy policy permits collection of click patterns, device identifiers, IP addresses, and communications without describing the explicit consent and custody architecture I require. Human approval for operational changes limits execution risk; it does not resolve surveillance risk.

My 36-month base case is that Palisade becomes a useful $1 million–$3 million ARR marketplace-conversion product, increasingly resembling an integration-heavy feature suite rather than an independent marketplace operating layer. I would not invest my own money today. One condition flips me: three named marketplaces must publish independently attributable results showing that buyer, seller, and staff agents each produce repeat incremental transactions while users can inspect, revoke, and delete the persistent memory used to generate them. That would simultaneously demonstrate the strange multi-sided behavior, the controlled asset, and the limiting architecture this dossier does not yet establish.
