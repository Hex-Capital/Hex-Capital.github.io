# Neuromorphic -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A laboratory operator describes an assay, points at whatever robot is available, and the machine assembles the workflow from navigation, manipulation, inspection, and tool-use skills. The interesting leak from that future is not the $64.46 billion robot-software forecast. It is one biotechnology customer reaching daily robot operations within a week. Neuromorphic has supplied a plausible causal chain—natural-language instruction, reusable skills, onboard execution, sensor feedback—but has not yet shown that the chain survives different robot bodies without fresh engineering.

The founders are the strongest part of this bet. They competed together in high-school robotics and won championships before physical AI became a fashionable financing category. Ege Doğanay researched contact-rich manipulation and policy generalization; Vatan Aksoy Tezer accumulated deployment and navigation experience across PickNik, Rapyuta, KABAM, and Augmentus; the team reports prior work involving more than 1,000 robots. That is pre-company obsession plus complementary operating tuition. The YC-period move into wet labs looks less like a tourist’s pivot because they signed the first contract within five days; they changed the beachhead, not the underlying technical fixation.

Why does robot onboarding take weeks? Because a deployment engineer must translate each operator’s intent into hardware-specific behavior, absorb integration failures, and remain in the support loop. Neuromorphic is trying to return that control to the laboratory operator. The prior $5,000–$10,000 per-robot monthly price makes the rent visible, but not yet its distribution: the dossier does not quantify integration labor, downtime, customer savings, or how much of the service remains human monitoring. This could eliminate a gatekeeper, or merely rebundle one inside Robotics-as-a-Service.

The signed contract and daily use are real pre-seed behavior. A customer trusted the system with physical laboratory work rather than joining a waitlist. Still, there is only one public deployment and no success rate, intervention rate, safety record, or cross-body benchmark. The pre-revenue robotics exception represented by CosmicBrain AI applies only partially: Neuromorphic has stronger commercial evidence, but the dossier does not yet provide the prototype measurements that establish embodiment independence rather than competent systems integration.

The sharp bear case is that four overlapping robot-brain companies have raised more than $4 billion while Neuromorphic controls no disclosed model, proprietary hardware, or demonstrated data advantage. Its moat is inferred from future workflow data, and its onboard LLM may place the company above somebody else’s closed foundation rather than beneath the wave as infrastructure. The rapid switch from facilities to wet labs could become excellent focus; it could also reveal that the general brain works only after vertical-specific deployment labor. My 36-month expectation is therefore a focused wet-lab automation business at roughly $3 million–$8 million ARR, not yet a broadly adopted embodiment-agnostic platform.

I would write a measured personal check. The founders and the one-week deployment make this more than consensus physical-AI language, while the missing controlled bottleneck keeps it far from a maximum-conviction bet. Three co-founders are not themselves a governance problem, and the dossier provides no evidence of a leadership shell game, but I would verify that one person is ultimately accountable. My technical flip condition is simpler: if the same core workflow cannot run on a second materially different robot body with less than one day of customer-specific engineering, I move from Invest to Pass because the claimed brain is really a services layer.
