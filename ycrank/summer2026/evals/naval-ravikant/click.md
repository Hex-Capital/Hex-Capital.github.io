# Click -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Aditya Asgaonkar has real technical knowledge, but Click has not yet shown specific knowledge of this buyer. Ethereum consensus research, formal-verification work, and writing about browser-agent context engineering are credible evidence that he understands protocols and agent infrastructure. They are not evidence that he has repeatedly shipped or sold retrieval tools to operations teams, researchers, or enterprises. The product has already moved from a frontend cursor, to operations automation, to research connectors. Adaptation is healthy; serial repositioning without documented user pull is not yet conviction informed by reality.

Click’s useful insight is that ordinary web search does not reach LinkedIn activity, live fares, financial datasets, Maps, or YouTube transcripts. One MCP installation spanning Exa, Crustdata, Apify, Fiber AI, Jinko, and other providers reduces integration work. This resembles moving fragmented supply into a searchable layer, one of the better structural patterns. But the supply is controlled by upstream APIs rather than contributed permissionlessly, and the customer does not make the system more useful for the next customer.

Copying the code removes most of the company. OpenAI already supplies web search and remote MCP support; Exa, Tavily, Linkup, and Parallel collectively have hundreds of millions of dollars behind overlapping retrieval infrastructure. Click discloses no exclusive data, proprietary model, contractual access, accumulated routing benchmark, or workflow lock-in. Cross-provider request data could eventually become an asset, but a possible future dataset is not a present moat. Provider access and pricing can change while the host platforms can absorb the interface.

The strongest case for Click is the combination of a technically credible solo founder, a real protocol transition—more than 10,000 public MCP servers by December 2025—and a concrete $49 monthly product covering several valuable data categories. A capital-efficient prototype can justify investing before conventional traction, and weak early monetization can be overridden by demonstrated obsessive usage. But the dossier shows neither repeat usage nor a paying customer: 157 company followers and NVIDIA Inception membership do not establish demand. A displayed price proves an intended business model, not value capture.

The market argument is broad but incomplete. A $16.28 billion AI-search estimate does not identify Click’s initial buyer, the expensive workflow being replaced, or the adjacent transaction the same capability unlocks. The many connector categories create room to maneuver, but they also make the product horizontally legible and easy to bundle. My 36-month base case is that the horizontal connector subscription remains below $1 million ARR and is discontinued or repositioned into a specific operations workflow.

I pass. The decisive asymmetry is unfavorable: Click bears upstream dependency and data-handling risk while its suppliers and host platforms retain the durable assets. The red flag is not that the product has changed; it is that three positions have appeared without public evidence that users forced any of them. I would reopen the decision if Click documents at least 100 paying business customers and shows that accumulated, Click-owned routing data measurably improves result quality or cost as usage grows.
