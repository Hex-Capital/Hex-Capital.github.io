# 6thSense -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Useful robots no longer learn contact-rich work by watching video alone; they feel slip, pressure, grip, and pose, then connect those signals to what a human demonstrated. 6thSense has produced a believable leak from that world: wearable sensors and cameras recording eight modalities on one clock, paired with tactile arrays molded to robot hands. The missing causal step is consequential, though. Pressure proxies are not ground-truth force, and no independent benchmark shows that the claimed one-to-one mapping produces better manipulation policies. I can see what is coming, but the bridge from synchronized data to capable robots still contains an unnamed technical miracle.

The stack position is the exciting part. Designing and manufacturing the tactile devices in-house gives 6thSense more substance than a thin model wrapper, and a proprietary library of episodes tied to deployed sensor configurations could compound. Yet Human Archive already operates more than 1,000 headsets, Daimon reports over one million dataset downloads, and Tacta has raised $75 million for an integrated glove-hand-model system. Those artifacts validate the bottleneck while making this a crowded present-day wave. Without demonstrated transfer performance, 6thSense controls hardware but has not established that it controls the scarce enabling layer.

Matt Wulff brings the strongest founder evidence: he worked on vision-guided robotics at Tesla and tactile-data capture at Mecka AI before this company existed. Ronak Agarwal, Alex Noh, and James Baek add infrastructure, AI, biomedical, and commercial experience, so the team can plausibly build across hardware and data systems. What I cannot find is the deeper first-believer trail—dated tactile experiments, independently shipped robotics artifacts, or years of obsession preceding the category’s sudden heat. Baek is identified as CEO, but four best friends founding together also makes decision control and a contractual tiebreaker a diligence gate, not a detail.

The product is real enough to be offered, but the user behavior remains narrated. “A handful of teams” are working with 6thSense, while the 20-place robotic-hand pilot asks $4,000 per pair at cost. An offer is not a purchase, and neither establishes repeated collection, renewal, or customers reorganizing development around the system. The bull case stings: inexpensive matched hands could seed deployments, every deployment could generate proprietary episodes, and a small hardware program could become the data network competitors cannot reproduce. Today there is no dataset volume, retention evidence, or paid conversion proving that loop has started.

I also cannot locate a broken institutional rule, captured rent, or agency transfer. 6thSense removes synchronization and calibration toil from robot-learning teams, which is useful, but the dossier neither quantifies that cost nor identifies an artificial gatekeeper whose control disappears. More seriously, egocentric collection in real homes can expose intimate environments, while no consent, custody, or anonymization architecture is public. That is a red flag because the prospective moat is the very data creating the risk.

I would not write the check yet. My base case is that within 36 months 6thSense becomes a specialized tactile-hardware and data supplier at roughly $1–3 million ARR rather than the nervous system for physical AI: technically useful, but without enough validated transfer advantage to own the layer. One event flips me— a named robotics customer publishes a controlled benchmark showing that 6thSense’s human-to-robot mapping materially improves task success over vision-only training and then renews the deployment as a paid data contract.
