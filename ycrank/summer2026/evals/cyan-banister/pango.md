# Pango -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the interesting version of Pango is not a better returns portal. It is the operating layer through which a merchant expresses intent while software negotiates carriers, warehouses, refunds, fraud, and customer exceptions. The dossier gives me one leak from that world: Pango says it already automates 99% of shipping and return operations across 30 signed brands and 40 countries. But it does not map the intervening transitions or identify what Pango must control as competitors acquire the same agency. That makes the future imaginable, not yet proprietary.

The present-day wound is clearer. A brand accumulates five to seven tools, then pays people to carry policy between them whenever a shipment, SKU, or country produces an exception. Pango’s 100-plus integrations and reported 20% operating-cost reduction suggest real systems tuition. Still, I cannot see the rent precisely: no cost per return, exception-handling time, incumbent fee, or evidence that the savings accrue to merchants rather than being recaptured through customized per-order pricing. This repairs an irritating workflow, but it does not yet return meaningful economic agency to an individual.

The team is the strongest part of the case. Steve Rahimi has spent seven years building e-commerce businesses and wrote a 2025 thesis on reverse logistics. Lukasz Reszczynski previously built a patented configurator used in more than $1.5 billion of quotes and a $300 million sales process. Those are independently derived, pre-YC artifacts, not two founders noticing an “agentic commerce” slide at the same conference. Their backgrounds also divide cleanly between the operating problem and configurable execution infrastructure. I would still investigate how they began working together and who has the contractual tiebreaker; the dossier names Rahimi as CEO, but supplies no governance history.

The product has escaped the deck. The Shopify app dates to October 2024, five reviewers have given it 5.0, five customers are named, and brands have granted Pango permission to edit orders, discounts, gift cards, store credit, and policies. That is costly behavior because a merchant is entrusting the system with money and customer relationships. The strongest bull case is that each integration makes Pango harder to remove and each resolved exception produces a proprietary operating record. Thirty signed brands at pre-seed is meaningful even without disclosed revenue.

The same permissions make the bear case sting. Narvar and parcelLab already sell autonomous post-purchase agents, while AfterShip has more than 1,300 carrier integrations. Pango has not published paid retention, error or reversal rates, comparative automation performance, or evidence that its workflow history improves decisions. ISO 27001 remains in progress despite sensitive customer data and broad write access. A system that can issue refunds and store credit needs explicit custody and audit architecture; otherwise the apparent moat is merely accumulated integration work sitting above infrastructure controlled by Shopify, carriers, and possibly a closed model provider.

My 36-month base case is that Pango reaches $4 million to $8 million in ARR by August 2029 as a credible commerce-operations vendor, but does not become the agentic operating system its language implies. I am neutral and would not write the check today: the founders and working artifact are ahead of the future-chain and controlled-bottleneck evidence. One result flips me—an independently verifiable cohort of at least 20 paying brands retained for 12 months while fewer than 0.5% of autonomous financial actions require reversal.
