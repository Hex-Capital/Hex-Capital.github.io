# Liquid Energy -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Liquid Energy proposes an escape from a four-year grid queue. I want to know where its founders have already escaped a smaller one. What prevented the first NanoDC from working, what did they change, and how long did that take? The dossier contains an eight-week contract-to-first-token promise, but no documented deployment showing how they made it happen. That is the missing fact most relevant to my check. Identifying an obstacle is different from demonstrating resourcefulness against it.

The problem is substantial. JLL reports grid-connection waits averaging over four years in primary markets, and bringing generation with the compute could remove a dependency that money alone cannot quickly resolve. But an unfolding solar array moves the constraint into generation, storage, cooling, and workload scheduling. Liquid Energy needs to show that those pieces produce useful inference together. Proprietary-module and cooling descriptions establish an engineering direction; they do not establish a functioning system. The [company’s terms](https://www.liquidenergy.world/terms) say advertised capabilities may never be offered. I would treat that as uncertainty about delivery, rather than evidence that the technology has failed.

The strongest argument for these founders is that they have done things outside an accelerator. Sid Ravikumar was a founding engineer at Jelly.fm and a senior engineer at Lotus Laboratories. Sajan Khosa previously signed three brokerages for AJMER and raised over $100,000 for Dasvandh. Those are more informative than follower counts. They make it plausible that this team can build and persuade people. But the dossier does not identify the resistance behind those achievements, or connect the founders’ prior work to a personal encounter with this particular energy problem. Likewise, the older storage and waste-heat description could reflect useful learning, but without the observation that prompted the change, I cannot score it as evidence-driven adaptation.

Who wants the crude NanoDC now? Liquid Energy says it sells inference to companies of every size. I would rather see one specific kind of customer willing to tolerate its limitations because waiting for conventional capacity is worse. Its 247 followers, YC participation, and NVIDIA Inception affiliation do not answer that question. Neither do competitors’ funding rounds. Rune’s use of existing solar generation makes the tradeoff concrete: carrying the array must deliver enough additional speed or flexibility to justify its burden. With no disclosed billing units, operating costs, or customer usage, that tradeoff remains untested.

Liquid Energy fits my interest in neglected operational difficulty. It has not yet demonstrated the measured technical proof or repeated technical-user adoption that makes unfamiliar infrastructure investable for me. None of the documented exceptions supplies an override here: there is no established working prototype, independently validated experiment, or unmistakable user pull. I also would not infer premature delegation from three executive titles, or a fragile partnership strategy from an affiliation badge. The evidence is missing, rather than actively disqualifying. Under this framework, each dimension therefore stays at its stated absent-evidence default.

I would pass today. One verifiable result could change that decision: a customer-confirmed NanoDC deployment, delivered within eight weeks despite a documented blocker the founders personally overcame, followed by 30 days of paid repeat inference with measured availability and positive contribution margin. That would connect founder behavior, engineering, and need in one observable sequence. I do not need a long revenue history from Liquid Energy. I need to see that sequence begin.
