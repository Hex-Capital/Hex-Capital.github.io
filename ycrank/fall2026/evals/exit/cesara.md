# Cesara -- Exit Evaluation

**Action:** Watch  
**Evidence confidence:** Low

### Company case

**Strategic acquisition by a revenue-cycle software vendor** is the strongest path. Cesara’s workflow prices claims against payer contracts at submission and prepares clause-backed appeals when payments fall short. A vendor could pay to own that capability if it consistently recovers more cash than the vendor’s existing tools and owning the contract and appeals workflow lets the vendor capture more of that value than licensing or building it. The memo documents a public launch and demo. The CEO reports that pilots identified over 90% of valid underpayments and recovered over 70% of those identified. Customer names, sample sizes, and dollars recovered are undisclosed. (*The Idea*, *Market & Traction*, *Defensibility*)

The operating milestone is a paid production rollout across multiple health systems with customer-verified incremental net recoveries over their existing workflows. Reaching it requires reliable contract, claims, and remittance feeds and validation of appeal accuracy. The memo gives conflicting financing figures and leaves the rollout budget and runway unknown. It also leaves IP ownership, customer contract assignment, and post-sale data access unverified. There is no separately supported route to cash for this holder. (*The Idea*, *Market & Traction*, *Key Risks*, *Key Facts*)

**Proceeds bridge.** The illustrative $500,000 check at a $10 million post-money valuation buys 5%, diluted to 3%, with no follow-on checks. The 5× objective requires $2.5 million in cash within eight years, or $83.3 million in distributable equity proceeds at 3% ownership. The hurdle is $125 million at 2% and $62.5 million at 4%. Both sale scenarios assume enterprise value equals distributable equity proceeds after zero net debt and zero other adjustments, with a simple pro rata waterfall and no preference deductions. All consideration is cash at close. Stock, escrow, earnouts, rollover, and compensation are each assumed to be zero. The prices and timing are illustrative. The memo supplies no usable acquisition pricing evidence. (*Market & Traction*, *Key Facts*)

| Scenario | Timing and assumed outcome | Investor cash | Gross multiple |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Modest sale | Year 5 at $40 million enterprise value | $1.2 million | 2.4× |
| Strong sale | Year 7 at $100 million enterprise value | $3.0 million | 6.0× |
| Prolonged private | Through year 8, growth plateaus, buyers stay away, public scale remains out of reach, and cash is reinvested. The investor retains the shares. | $0 | 0× |

At 2% ownership, even the illustrative strong sale returns $2.0 million, or 4.0×.

### Countercase

Incumbents already ingest claims and remittances and flag payment variances. They could add clause-linked appeals to their existing workflows or license the capability, limiting what they would pay to own Cesara. The memo shows no measured adoption or retention that would make the workflow hard to replace. That keeps the case at Watch. The evidence does not establish that a substitute matches Cesara’s recovery results or that buyer economics cap the price below the hurdle, so the Pass override does not apply. (*Defensibility*, *Key Risks*)

### Decisive diligence question

Could a revenue-cycle vendor justify paying enough to leave $83.3 million in distributable equity proceeds by owning Cesara? A buyer-side economic assessment grounded in customer-verified production recoveries, with an explicit comparison against licensing and building, would answer it. Evidence that the ownership advantage clears that hurdle would move the action to Reach out. Evidence that an equivalent substitute caps the price materially below it would move the action to Pass.
