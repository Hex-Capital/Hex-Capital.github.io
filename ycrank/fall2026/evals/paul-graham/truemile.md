# TrueMile -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Harpreet has managed a trucking fleet since he was 19. That makes me interested. But what happened when a driver canceled, a return load disappeared, or a profitable-looking lane lost money? What did he build next? TrueMile’s dossier supplies the occupational history without one such episode. The reported $7M from his family’s trucking operation establishes exposure to the business; it is neither TrueMile revenue nor evidence of a particular ingenious response to resistance ([founder profile](https://www.ycombinator.com/founders/harpreet-dhaliwal)). I would pass today because the strongest evidence concerns how he found the problem, while my first question concerns what he does when solving it becomes difficult.

The origin is nevertheless unusually credible. Someone who has coordinated real trucks is more likely to notice a useful problem than someone searching for an industry to put agents into. Owning the fleet could also force the team to confront the unpleasant details that software vendors can leave to their customers: driver constraints, cancellations, dispatch errors, and the cost of an empty return trip. This is the strongest argument against passing. TrueMile could learn something important precisely because it must live with its own decisions. That earns substantial credit for the wedge. It does not establish that the learning has happened.

The missing link is a measured result. The website describes mathematical optimization and coordinated sourcing, planning, dispatch, and communications agents, with human approval of loads and rate confirmations ([TrueMile](https://truemile.ai/)). There is no documented deployment result, empty-mile reduction, or improvement in contribution per truck. My pattern of backing technical teams with concrete proof before a round therefore supplies no additional support here. Fleet ownership creates somewhere to run the experiment. It does not tell us its outcome. And because aifleet already combines its own fleet with AI planning, the business structure alone cannot establish TrueMile’s technical advantage.

Who wants this now? Recurring truckload shippers in Texas and the Sunbelt are a sufficiently narrow starting point. Seeking two dedicated-lane customers could be sensible founder selling; calling them “partners” does not establish the large-partner dependency I distrust. But an inquiry form is not a booking, and the dossier identifies no repeat shipper or payment. The 94 LinkedIn followers contribute nothing to that test. Nor can I count the conflicting descriptions of carrier software and an owned-fleet business as evidence of an intelligent pivot: there is no dated observation, rejected hypothesis, and subsequent change.

The economic question is whether better decisions produce enough surplus to build an exceptional company. Industry operating costs of $2.336 per mile in 2025 make small mistakes consequential, although that figure says nothing about TrueMile’s own costs. Operational experience could let Harpreet find improvements outsiders miss. That is a serious bull case. But the known exceptions that would overcome my missing founder evidence require a working prototype, independently validated proof, or unmistakable user pull. None is documented here. Missing cofounder history is likewise uncertainty, not proof of a bad relationship.

One verifiable result would flip my decision: a dated four-week operating record showing a specific dispatch obstacle, Harpreet’s shipped workaround, and the same shipper subsequently rebooking and paying for loads at positive contribution after direct operating costs. That would connect founder behavior to an outcome and then to demand. Until then, TrueMile has a founder who plausibly knows where to look, but insufficient evidence of what he has found.
