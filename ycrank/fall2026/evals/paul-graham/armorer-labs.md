# Armorer Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Armorer Labs has evidence that its founders can build security systems. It has less evidence of what they do when something stops working. Begimher and Leo report three years building and evaluating AI security together at AWS. That is useful shared execution history. But I cannot find a specific obstacle, the workaround they shipped, and how quickly it changed the situation. For a people-first investment, that missing sequence matters more than their former employer’s name. I would pass on the present evidence.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that they have already made something concrete. Armorer has public Python and TypeScript integration SDKs, documented one-use authorization tokens, and a specific technical claim: CPU-based checks below two milliseconds. Its reported comparison produced 98% accuracy against 80%, at 1.9 milliseconds against roughly 168. Those are testable claims. But the comparison covered only 50 documents, and the runtime and model assets are supplied separately. I give meaningful credit for the implementation, with discounted credit for the company’s benchmark. An SDK makes the system easier to inspect; it does not independently establish that the whole protection loop works.

The interesting problem is preserving useful work while preventing dangerous actions. A security product that blocks everything is easy to build. Armorer says it generates fixes, checks that business workflows survive, and gets fixes ready for approval in about four hours. The founders’ prior security-agent and adversarial-evaluation work gives them a credible reason to understand this problem. Wrapping tool calls and eliminating direct credential paths also involves the integration work customers would rather avoid. That is a plausible schlep-heavy wedge, though the dossier does not establish the particular incident that caused these founders to start Armorer.

The Just Eat Takeaway engineering-director testimonial is the most useful demand evidence. A named engineering leader endorsing issue resolution tells me more than 45 GitHub stars or 451 LinkedIn followers. It still does not establish repeated production use, payment, or customers asking for more. The bull case is that two experienced builders have found a painfully specific job inside a large company and are already solving it. That case would sting if I passed and they grew quickly. My working-product exception deserves consideration here, but the public evidence does not yet establish either independently validated protection or unmistakable user pull strong enough to replace the missing founder-behavior evidence.

Armorer’s developer interface fits my preference for tools technical users can compose. Its three-year cofounder history also answers a real relationship concern. There is no disclosed dependence on a large distribution partner or premature sales organization. But proprietary files are not themselves a difficult proprietary substrate, and founder-led demo invitations do not establish manual customer conversion. HiddenLayer, Zenity, and Noma already cover adjacent security workflows. Armorer’s opportunity depends on customers repeatedly choosing its verified fixes despite the integration burden. Enterprise licensing is a plausible business model, but it remains inferred. The archived Gauntlet project proves neither an evidence-driven pivot nor obstinacy; I leave adaptability at its missing-evidence default.

One verifiable development would change my decision: a dated Just Eat Takeaway deployment account showing a specific integration blocker, the founders’ shipped workaround within weeks, and subsequent repeated production use. That would connect the team, the working system, and an urgent user need. Armorer currently has promising pieces of that proof. I would want to see them connected before investing my own money.
