# Asakana -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Yudai has actually worked in seafood distribution. That matters more than his MBA. He has seen the place where a restaurant’s message becomes someone else’s typing job. One distributor receives about 60 orders daily, some longer than 75 lines, and spends roughly 30% of representative time processing them. Asakana starts with an intelligible problem encountered by someone who lived inside the business. The unpleasant details—customer shorthand, changing catalogs, and old ERPs—are precisely where an opportunity can hide.

The founders have also crossed the distance between describing software and putting it into operation. Asakana reports live US seafood-distributor deployments. Its product uses order history to interpret messages and sends uncertain entries to people before touching the ERP. That is a sensible boundary for an early system: a wrong seafood order creates a physical mistake. Ben’s eight years at Google, including Gemini and Project Mariner, make the implementation credible, but I give technical credit for the deployed product rather than the résumé. The reported 40%+ time savings remain a company claim, without a disclosed accuracy benchmark or independently measured result.

What I cannot find is the incident that tells me how these founders behave when something breaks. Asakana says it builds connections where APIs are unavailable. That identifies difficult work, but it does not document a particular blocked installation, the workaround the founders built, or how quickly it worked. Founder-led introductions through Columbia’s Japanese business network show personal recruitment rather than dependence on a large partner. Neither fact supplies the missing obstacle-and-response sequence. There is also no documented rejected hypothesis followed by a product change. I would leave both judgments at their missing-evidence defaults; inventing either determination or rigidity would be equally careless.

The strongest case for writing a check is that this team may already have found its narrow well. Seafood distributors are using the product, broadline customers are reported, and the claimed savings concern everyday operations. A functioning product can sometimes outweigh uncertainty about founder history. But the dossier does not establish repeated order volumes, customer payment, or retention strongly enough to justify that exception here. Asakana partially matches my pattern of technical teams producing concrete proof before fundraising; it has the operating software, but not the unusually decisive proof spike. The cofounders’ shared execution history is unknown, which leaves that question open without establishing the red flag of a relationship assembled for an application.

Competition makes the next piece of founder evidence particularly consequential. Choco already markets message-to-ERP automation and claims more than 200 ERP connections; Pepper bundles order capture with other distributor tools. Asakana’s possible advantage is learning one customer’s messy operation faster and serving it more closely. Its transaction-volume commission could grow with usage, but undisclosed rates and support costs leave the economics untested. I am neutral and would keep my money for now. One verifiable event could change that: a customer-confirmed account of a blocked ERP installation that the founders rescued with their own shipped workaround within a week, restoring correct live order entry. That would turn the central uncertainty into evidence of the founders I want to back.
