# Dydema -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: someone wearing an ordinary headset issues a quiet command without reaching for a screen. Dydema’s brain-controlled 2048 and TikTok demonstrations are little leaks from that world. But what must happen between the demonstration and the habit? Decoding must remain useful across sessions, and calibration must cost less effort than the action it replaces. Wizard’s reported 52% zero-shot imagined-movement accuracy against 50% chance leaves substantial distance. That result does not invalidate its calibrated classifiers; it does stop me from treating “map to anything” as an established capability.

The founders give me more confidence than the consumer promise does. Julian Weaver combines neuroscience, computer science, and EEG/fMRI research; Christopher Oosthuizen brings machine-learning research and a public programming-language artifact, ABlaze. More tellingly, they describe a college neurotechnology experiment together, followed by working together at Syntra. There is a thread here that precedes this company. I see technical curiosity and evidence they can build together, although the dossier does not establish years of independently pursued neurotechnology work or their ability to recruit other first believers.

Why does a developer lack sufficient brain recordings today? Is collecting them expensive, are existing datasets poorly matched to the task, or are useful rights locked away? Dydema names the shortage but does not establish its economics. There is no quantified toll, identified rent collector, or demonstrated transfer of control to an individual. The commissioned-recording business could answer those questions through actual orders. For now, a self-service classifier removes implementation work; the dossier has not shown that it unlocks something an institution was withholding.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that the team has already assembled something usable: public onboarding, hosted classification, and transparent pricing of $1 per model creation, $1 per month for hosting, and $0.003 per prediction window. Revenue is not my prerequisite. **CosmicBrain AI** illustrates the relevant exception: a functioning technical artifact can justify a pre-revenue bet. The material difference is its documented industrial proofs of concept and lifelong founder obsession. Dydema supplies demonstrations, but no comparable evidence that an outside user repeatedly accepts the calibration effort or reorganizes an activity around the product. I want to discover what someone willingly puts up with because this feels like magic.

The recording service also fits my pattern of looking beneath a consumer beachhead for infrastructure. But that pattern requires an asset to accumulate. Dydema has not established the size, reuse rights, or performance advantage of its recordings, while EMOTIV already combines hardware, data, and modeling infrastructure. Privacy makes this particularly consequential: the terms acknowledge that embeddings can re-identify participants. Consent requirements and treating embeddings as personal data are constructive; they do not establish the custody and deletion architecture. I see an unresolved privacy gate, not evidence of misconduct. The identified CEO and CTO likewise provide no affirmative evidence of the governance failures I avoid.

I land at **Neutral**. These founders could build a useful specialist EEG business before they build an everyday consumer interface; that is where I expect the evidence to pull them. The single result that would change my decision is an independently auditable, consented deployment in which an outside customer repeatedly pays for a task that Dydema’s reusable recordings demonstrably improve over the available pretrained baseline. That would connect the working tool, the accumulating asset, and the future I can picture. Today, those connections remain hypotheses.
