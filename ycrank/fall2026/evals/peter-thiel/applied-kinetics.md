# Applied Kinetics -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Applied Kinetics becomes interesting if electrical-equipment scarcity is partly an allocation failure: qualified factories have usable capacity that developers cannot efficiently find and coordinate. That is a possible secret. The founders’ claim of transformers built and tested in seven weeks against market waits of up to 65 weeks gives it a falsifiable test. But the dossier names neither an accepted delivery nor an industry participant whose contrary belief that delivery overturned. I give the proposition some credit; I cannot yet give it the status of a discovery.

Who owns the benefit if the proposition is true? Applied Kinetics reports access to **40+ partner plants**, but access does not establish exclusive capacity, proprietary technology, or an advantage that strengthens with each order. The **10+ GW equipped belongs to the manufacturing base**, not this company’s delivery record. Its strongest initial wedge appears to be urgent transformer procurement, yet its catalog already spans switchgear, generation, and cooling. A broad catalog can increase sales without producing a narrow market the company can dominate. Heron Power and DG Matrix address different technical problems; their differences do not establish protection from equipment distributors.

The founders provide a more affirmative signal. Rohit Naras and Adhik Durga attended Georgia Tech and collaborated on Orion at Cal Hacks before Applied Kinetics. That is actual shared work, rather than a founding relationship inferred from matching biographies. Their Crusoe telemetry and IBM agent experience also make the proposed software credible as something they could build. However, prior collaboration supports alignment; it does not establish full-time commitment, retained ownership, or control of essential engineering. Those remain open questions, with no documented part-time-founder or capitalization red flag.

The strongest case against passing is that the customer may care much more about an accepted transformer than about proprietary AI. Compressing a project-critical procurement cycle could create substantial value before any sophisticated software moat appears. Applied Kinetics also fits the less obvious pattern of investment in the physical production stack behind AI: equipment availability can constrain compute as directly as chips or electricity. Its proposed inspections, testing, approvals, freight, and service address a real delivery problem. But the advertised timelines remain nonbinding until specifications and scope are confirmed. The known exceptions that justify overlooking an incomplete monopoly require a demonstrated technical event or live customer behavior; this dossier supplies neither.

The economic question is equally specific. Equipment quotations identify a plausible principal business, but there is no disclosed order value, gross margin, or expansion history from which to construct power-law arithmetic. Separately licensing agents introduces another possible revenue stream without demonstrating the first. The adjacent **$25.8 billion data center power market** cannot supply the missing economics. Founder solicitation of developers, EPC firms, and utilities is a concrete starting route, worth modest credit, but no conversion, sales-cycle, or implementation evidence yet shows that the route supports the contract economics.

I pass on the present evidence. The decisive gap is between coordinating valuable equipment and owning a durable position in its supply. This is an unresolved investment thesis, not a demonstrated founder or technical failure. One verifiable event would reopen the decision: **a named developer places a repeat order under an exclusive capacity agreement after accepting a seven-week transformer delivery, with the order’s positive gross margin independently verified.** That would begin connecting the proposed secret to customer behavior, retained value, and a business I could underwrite with my own money.
