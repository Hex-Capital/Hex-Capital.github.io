# Daqstra -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Darell Chua and Anchit Kumar spent more than 10 Mojave campaigns building and testing rocket engines before Daqstra connected NI hardware, Siemens PLCs, Modbus, OPC-UA, and custom TCP systems. Daqstra is a schlep-heavy organic idea whose weakest signal is not technical ability but animal resourcefulness. Daqstra has shipped the machine, but the dossier never shows the cereal-box moment: the improbable pre-funding act proving Chua and Kumar will manufacture a path when the obvious one closes.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep because compliance was not incidental to the product; enduring the grind was the product advantage. Daqstra has the same structure in miniature. Every heterogeneous controller, safety interlock, redline, abort sequence, and vendor-specific update is work sensible founders will avoid. Daqstra has already operated live LOX/IPA static-fire campaigns and deployed across multiple rocket-engine stands, so this is real grime rather than a slide about future integrations. Daqstra diverges from Coinbase because hardware integration is tedious without yet having Coinbase’s structural regulatory barrier; Nominal and Sift can spend their $155M and $67M funding piles attacking much of the same territory.

A polished AI wrapper with two MBAs and a plan to hire engineers after funding is the low rung for resourcefulness: all presentation, no artifact, no tracks in the snow. Daqstra is plainly not that. Daqstra’s active aerospace programs use the product day to day, and Chua and Kumar have crossed the software-hardware boundary enough times to make bidirectional control work in safety-critical tests. But resourcefulness is more than competence. The dossier contains no rejection overturned, customer won through an unscalable stunt, or side-channel revenue invented when money ran out. The palm-oil pilot targeting $100,000 in annual savings is evidence of founder-selling, but not yet evidence that either founder is an animal.

Drew Houston built **Dropbox** (2007) because he kept forgetting his USB stick; Chua and Kumar built Daqstra after personally reconnecting fragmented test infrastructure while developing liquid rocket engines at UC San Diego. That is the same organic-idea mechanism. Daqstra’s AIAA work on modular distributed data acquisition, Chua’s systems architecture experience at Rivian, and Kumar’s 10-plus Mojave campaigns make the founder-problem distance nearly zero. Daqstra is not an AI idea pasted onto a fashionable market. The AI arrived after the pain, which is the right order.

Chua’s 2025 AIAA paper and Kumar’s Mars-cave autonomy work give Daqstra more hacker genealogy than their missing public GitHub repositories initially suggest. Over the next 18 months, this dimension compounds only if Daqstra turns field knowledge into reusable adapters, test primitives, and failure-handling code; every successful NI or Siemens deployment should make the next mixed-vendor installation cheaper. Daqstra’s danger is becoming a clever engineering consultancy whose knowledge remains trapped in Chua and Kumar’s heads. Hackers build tools that eliminate the work they just did. Consultants repeat it.

UC San Diego’s 2024–2025 page named four Daqstra co-founders, while the current YC page names only Chua and Kumar. Daqstra still has the preferred configuration: two founders who met before the startup, built liquid rocket engines together, and co-authored the SAMDAS architecture. The score would flip downward if the earlier departures reflect unresolved founder conflict rather than ordinary roster correction. Daqstra would move to the top rung if Chua and Kumar had a documented multi-year record of shipping together beyond the university program; the present evidence proves shared ordeal, but not yet durable company chemistry.

Active aerospace programs using Daqstra day to day should carry Daqstra to $3–8 million in annual recurring revenue by July 2029, principally from aerospace and energy test operations, without an acquisition or a pivot into generic telemetry analytics. The mechanism is schlep accumulation: Daqstra learns the ugly edge cases in NI, Siemens, Modbus, OPC-UA, and custom systems, then sells the resulting reduction in setup time and test risk to the next program. The range is deliberately short of a breakout forecast because Daqstra has not yet shown the resourcefulness or repeatable sales motion required to escape founder-led deployments.

Nominal’s roughly $155 million in disclosed financing and Sift’s $67 million make the bear case sting: Daqstra could be a capable integration shop caught between funded platforms and customers’ internal controls engineers, with safety liability slowing every deployment and the unexplained four-to-two founder contraction foreshadowing strain. Daqstra nevertheless has evidence the financed competitors cannot erase: live static-fire operation, day-to-day aerospace usage, multiple rocket-engine stands, and a palm-oil pilot tied to more than $100,000 of annual savings. Those are artifacts, not positioning. I would invest, but I would flip to pass if Daqstra cannot produce three independent paying customers above $100,000 in annual contract value within 18 months; that would show the schlep is custom work rather than a compounding product.
