# Trepo -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Trepo’s market turns on whether households can maintain accurate grocery inventory with almost no effort. Camera and voice interfaces could make that possible for buyers who want grocery planning handled for them. But purchases are only half the ledger: consumption, disposal, and other household members constantly change it. Receipt scanning makes initialization easier; keeping that state correct determines whether replenishment becomes useful enough to delegate. The dossier identifies this mechanism but supplies no measured inventory accuracy or maintenance burden. I cannot yet underwrite the controlling belief.

The strongest argument for Trepo is that people are already engaging with an unfinished product. Six beta hardware batches reportedly sold out, the iOS app has 4.9 stars from 56 ratings, and the founders report more than 150,000 item check-ins. These are meaningful pre-seed signals. They make “nobody wants this” a weak objection. However, undisclosed batch sizes prevent treating sellouts as a demonstrated capacity constraint, and cumulative check-ins do not establish that households keep updating inventory after the novelty wears off. I give Trepo credit for demand without converting it into evidence that the inventory problem is solved.

The product also contains something worth owning: household inventory state connected to recipes and shared shopping lists. Better recognition models could improve that recurring workflow. This is more substantial than generating recipes from a prompt. The unresolved boundary is execution: the dossier does not document completed grocery orders, deployed retailer integrations, or feedback that reconciles predicted consumption with actual stock. Grocery-partner pilots could create distribution, but unnamed counterparties and undisclosed terms do not establish a reusable channel.

The economic discontinuity is less clear than the interface improvement. Instacart’s September 2025 developer tooling lowers integration work across the category, including for competitors. Samsung already combines recognition and replenishment, while Cooklist offers overlapping inventory functions. Trepo’s external device avoids requiring a new refrigerator, but neither hardware pricing nor evidence of changed delivery costs or margins is available. My pattern of backing several layers of a technology wave does not establish value capture for this particular layer. The physical-business exception requires technology to change the economics; the dossier has not demonstrated that change.

Matt Taylor’s Tesla and Nuro experience makes hardware execution credible, and Zach Slaughter’s marketing background fits consumer acquisition. Those credentials do not establish prior startup learning or costly personal commitment, so founder motivation stays at the absent-evidence default. Nor does the dossier establish the rare model-building capability that could justify a consumer technology exception. I would pass with my own money today: Trepo has encouraging product interest, but its market gates remain unproven. My one flip condition is a paid 90-day household cohort demonstrating that reliable replenishment continues without routine manual inventory reconciliation. That would connect the existing demand signal to the mechanism that could make this a durable business.
