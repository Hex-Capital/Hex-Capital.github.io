# 0.security -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI agents could move application security from periodic testing to continuous investigation and repair; developers and security teams buy if the cost per verified, accepted fix falls enough to make that transition economical. That is the controlling variable for 0.security. Scanning more code is useful, but the economic endpoint is a vulnerability resolved without consuming equivalent human review time. The dossier establishes a plausible mechanism, but gives no evidence on that endpoint. I would pass on a personal check today.

The market is already crystallizing. Aikido reported $20M ARR for 2025 and offers overlapping code-security and autonomous-testing products. That validates a budget category while making distribution and displacement central to the investment. The broader $10.65B application-security estimate does not tell me which budget 0.security wins. Its $30 monthly subscription and usage pricing provide an accessible entry point, but neither pricing nor free credits establish attractive delivery economics.

There is more substance here than a security chatbot. Repository context, deterministic scanning, execution sandboxes and remediation pull requests connect investigation to action. Better models should improve that system’s ability to investigate and repair vulnerabilities. That earns workflow credit. However, retained investigation history is only an inferred future advantage, and the public harness makes orchestration easier to reproduce. I see an operational product, with limited evidence that its accumulated state makes it increasingly difficult to replace.

The strongest argument against passing is that 0.security may already have the beginning of distribution. Its harness had 526 stars and 58 forks on October 27, 2026, and the company reports work with EY and organizations in pharmaceuticals, finance and defense. Those are credible early signals for a two-person company. Separately, the Spotify engineer’s report of scanning almost 30,000 files in under a minute supports technical throughput. It does not establish verified-fix quality or labor savings. The software reaching three billion devices likewise demonstrates reach of affected software, rather than adoption of this product. These distinctions keep promising evidence from becoming several independent proofs of product-market fit.

The founders strengthen the bull case. Doruk has cyber-defense experience and credited vulnerability research; both founders report prior exits, although the terms and lessons are undisclosed. That supplies some evidence of prior startup learning without establishing exceptional motivation. My pattern of backing several layers of a technology discontinuity makes autonomous security worth pursuing even with established competitors. But the exceptions for scarce frontier-model research teams or a strategic vacuum do not apply here: the dossier demonstrates applied security execution in an increasingly populated market.

I would reconsider after one verifiable result: three named enterprise customers renew paid deployments after six months, with deployment records showing lower fully loaded cost per verified, accepted fix than their previous testing process. That would connect the product’s technical architecture to the market’s controlling variable. Today, the founders and workflow are ahead of the economic proof. The missing proof is specific enough to investigate, but I cannot substitute technical credibility for it.
