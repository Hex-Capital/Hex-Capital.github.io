# ernd -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Ten startups onboarded in 48 hours is the most interesting fact about ernd. It suggests these founders can get people to try something quickly. But “onboarded” could mean ten applications or ten companies moving their AI spending. Those are different discoveries. The first demonstrates an attractive offer. The second might reveal a business. The [company-reported launch figures](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/ernd) establish a little early demand, with no evidence yet of repeated spending.

I would start with what happened during those 48 hours. What prevented a customer from switching, and what did the founders build or do to get past it? The dossier contains no such episode, so I cannot give ernd credit for resourcefulness under resistance. Connor’s Bridgewater work and Nikhil’s payments experience are relevant preparation. Nikhil’s previous games reaching 15,000 downloads also show that he has shipped software people wanted. But none establishes that this team has solved the hard part of ernd. The card remains in early access, and the dossier supplies no demonstrated transaction flow or measured technical result. These are unanswered questions, rather than evidence of inability.

The strongest case for investing is that the founders have already begun solving the purchasing coalition’s cold-start problem. They report recruiting both ten startups and five vendors almost immediately. The [doubled Firecrawl usage allowance](https://ernd.io/partners/firecrawl) makes at least one negotiated benefit concrete. A small group of companies with substantial AI bills could give ernd purchasing leverage, which could attract more spending and improve the next negotiation. That is a plausible wedge. Ramp’s size would not by itself frighten me away from founders who had discovered users it served poorly.

But the economics determine whether that loop actually closes. The [October 27 rewards terms](https://ernd.io/terms) say ernd funds the OpenAI and Anthropic rewards itself; those providers do not endorse the program, and exhausted budgets can leave new points pending. Recruiting buyers with a subsidy does not establish that pooled purchasing produces the subsidy. Restricted redemption also makes the advertised reward less straightforward than cash. Five vendor agreements supply something to sell, but they do not establish repeat customer acquisition. The partnership red flag would become serious if the founders expected those agreements to do the selling for them.

I would pass today. Ernd has a modest early-demand signal, but it misses my founder-action gate, and neither demonstrated technical proof nor unmistakable user pull supplies an exception. My recurring preference for concrete proof before the round is not satisfied by prior accomplishments or published offer terms. There is also no documented founder-originated purchasing problem or evidence-driven product revision; both receive the missing-evidence defaults. One verifiable result could flip me: a dated customer cohort showing that a founder-built fix to a specific switching obstacle led to repeated AI purchases over eight weeks, with vendor-funded benefits covering the associated rewards. That would connect founder behavior, user need, and economics in one observable sequence.
