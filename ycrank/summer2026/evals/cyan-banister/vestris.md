# Vestris -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Aahil Valliani and Joshua Tang processed settlement files for approximately six weeks before founding Vestris, which makes Vestris a lived-workflow company building an almost invisible back-office abstraction. Founder contact is Vestris’s strongest dimension; physical-world observability is its weakest. The founders touched the pain, but a normal human cannot touch Vestris: the public surface is a waitlist and demo form, while the real artifacts—Schedule B exceptions, prorations, and lender packages—remain behind a title professional’s desk.

Anduril is the high rung for founders whose biographies made the business inevitable: Palmer Luckey had already built frontier hardware before defense became the pitch. Vestris shares the mechanism in miniature because Valliani and Tang personally spent 8–10 hours processing files inside a settlement company before building Vestris. The divergence is depth. Six weeks creates authentic irritation, not yet the decade-long social license John Hanke brought to Niantic or Luckey brought to Anduril. Vestris was born from contact rather than a TAM memo, but I cannot yet say the founders are *of* title operations rather than briefly *visiting* them.

The consensus YC AI-agent darling is the low rung because its value exists in a slide, a model, and founder testimony rather than something I can ride, scan, or hand to a friend. That failure mechanism appears in Vestris: no public product is available, no repeat usage is documented, and the preview waitlist reveals nothing about whether a drafted commitment survives an underwriter’s red pen. Vestris does produce observable outputs—party portals, title schedules, closing disclosures—but those artifacts are legible only inside a specialist workflow. Vestris is more tangible than an API wrapper and much less tactile than Flock Safety’s camera or Niantic’s Ingress.

Qualia’s $160 million and Snapdocs’s $260 million make “AI for closings” sound more consensus than magically weird, but Vestris could become stranger over the next 18 months if county-by-county integration work compounds. Each ugly portal, rejected title, and firm-specific correction could make Vestris harder to copy. The present mechanism runs the other direction: Dono already automates property-record intelligence, RealPact builds transaction agents, and Vestris has disclosed neither exclusive rights nor the size of its supposed decades-long title dataset. Unsexy earns Vestris credit; crowded AI positioning takes much of it back.

Aahil Valliani is explicitly named CEO while Joshua Tang is listed as co-founder, so Vestris has a visible captain rather than two people sharing the wheel. The score would flip upward if Vestris documented Valliani’s tiebreaker authority, founder vesting, and a conflict or exit protocol; it would flip downward if ownership is effectively 50/50 and “CEO” is merely the external title. Vestris clears the naming test, but silence on governance still invokes my HQ Trivia scar. Love between founders is wonderful. Love is not a deadlock mechanism.

Vigil Labs is the useful parallel for Vestris: regulated financial plumbing can become defensible precisely because sensible investors dislike the compliance surface. Vestris handles contracts, land records, settlement charges, closing disclosures, and lender packages, so errors can carry real financial consequences and jurisdictional knowledge can compound. Vestris nevertheless stops short of the strongest version of that pattern: it is software-only, leaves commitments for underwriter sign-off, and discloses no security certification or compliance posture. This is unsexy-regulated adjacency, not yet a SpaceX-sized frontier swing.

Washington, D.C. and Montgomery County, Maryland are the only publicly listed coverage areas, so my 36-month forecast is that by July 2029 Vestris will have narrowed from “autonomous end-to-end closing” into a human-in-the-loop title-preparation module serving Mid-Atlantic settlement firms at $1–3 million ARR. Vestris can win a useful wedge because Valliani and Tang have felt the repetitive work, but 3,700-plus county systems and underwriter sign-off will keep Vestris from becoming an autonomous national closing platform on that timetable. I’m never playing the game I seem to be playing: the valuable business may be exception-handling infrastructure, not the robot closer on the homepage.

“Decades of approved and rejected titles” is the bull mechanism that stings: if Vestris controls that corpus, the founders’ six weeks of firsthand processing may be enough to identify the correct interface while every completed file strengthens a proprietary decision system. Vestris could then resemble the HandUp exception—lived contact overriding a modest-looking software profile—or grow into the regulated data moat others underestimated. The dossier does not establish dataset ownership, a paying customer, or even repeat prototype use, so the bull case currently rests on Vestris’s own description. I would flip to Invest if Vestris verifies ten paying title firms across at least ten counties, shows that more than 80% of files reach underwriter sign-off without processor rework, and documents exclusive or contractually reusable rights to the training and correction data.
