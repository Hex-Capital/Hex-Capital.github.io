# Callbook AI

I would not begin with AI collections; I would begin with the missing deal card. The dossier gives no proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or evidence that this is Callbook AI’s first scarce institutional check. It also names no experiment the round purchases—no dated milestone, pass/fail metric, or explanation of why capital rather than customer cash must fund it. Those are omissions, not proof of a bad auction, but I am investing my own money and will not pretend the strike price and use of proceeds are administrative details.

The strongest evidence is already inside customer workflows. Callbook reports 23 million collection interactions, two million borrowers managed, more than 100,000 weekly negotiations, seven named customer logos, and over $11 million recovered. It reportedly won every head-to-head pilot. This is not follower-count or integration theater: the system performs an economic action and money comes back. The remaining gap is attribution. The dossier does not disclose how many pilots occurred, whether customers pay, renew, or expand, or whether Callbook’s intervention produces incremental recovery after fees and compliance costs.

The founders have shipped real components of the system. Daniel Martinez previously built a B2B conversational product beyond $8 million ARR, while Diego Avellaneda’s embedded-ML work produced a voicemail detector with 91.4% recall and a reported 60% reduction in related processing expenditure. That supports the enterprise-software and production-voice sides of the bridge. It does not establish deep operating authority inside regulated collections, where Regulation F, negotiation errors, and creditor-specific policies determine whether automation remains an asset or becomes a liability.

The proprietary interaction corpus is promising, but “23 million interactions” is not automatically a dark pool. Callbook has not shown exclusive data rights, a measured learning curve, or negotiation-error rates. Salient, InDebted, and Skit.ai already offer overlapping multichannel negotiation and compliance products with substantially more capital. The defensible asset must therefore be the compact workflow and regional operating data that competitors cannot cheaply recreate—not temporary model performance or a voicemail classifier that general voice platforms can eventually match.

The economics are still opaque. There is no revenue, gross margin, burn, runway, or controllable company-level cash metric. A 60% reduction in one processing expense proves customer value, not Callbook’s profitable path. Delete the next financing and I cannot determine whether seven employees, inference and telecommunications costs, compliance operations, and enterprise deployment expenses can be supported by collections revenue.

The strongest objection to passing is that I may be punishing omitted financing data while ignoring unusually mature pre-seed operating evidence. Callbook has named financial customers, won competitive pilots, handled millions of borrowers, recovered real money, and assembled founders with both production ML and prior $8 million-plus ARR experience. The relevant exception is Ando: measured enterprise conversion justified underwriting an AI business despite category skepticism. The material difference is that Ando disclosed paid conversion, retention, and operating accuracy; Callbook discloses pilot wins and aggregate activity without revenue, retention, contribution margin, or error cohorts.

My 36-month prediction is that Callbook AI reaches $8 million–$15 million ARR as a Latin American enterprise-collections platform, but requires another institutional round because compliance and deployment costs prevent the current evidence from supporting one-and-done economics. I am passing today. I flip to Invest if a signed sub-$10-million post-money term sheet documents this as the first institutional check and funds a dated test that converts ten named pilots into 12-month retained customers at positive contribution margin.
