# Orca Aerospace -- Paul Graham Evaluation

What happened when Orca Aerospace’s software would not fit on the spacecraft? The joint Lyra study identifies a real constraint: model memory determines the compute board, and board power determines the spacecraft bus. But the dossier stops before the interesting part. It does not show what the founders changed, how quickly they changed it, or whether the revised system worked. That leaves my most important founder test unanswered. It does not mean they failed it.

The strongest reason to back these founders is that they have worked on the actual problem. Jacob Ososke spent over a decade in guidance, navigation, and control at Lockheed Martin and founded its first AI-GNC team. Hakan Chunton worked on onboard collision avoidance and trajectory optimization. This is substantial evidence of relevant technical experience. Combined with the avionics integration and hardware testing the product requires, it earns partial credit for finding an opportunity inside difficult work. But the dossier does not establish the particular collision with the problem that caused them to start Orca, or a subsequent decision to change routes when evidence demanded it.

Their proposed separation of AI objectives, deterministic planning, and command guardrails is sensible. The important question is what survives contact with hardware. An architecture description and an engineering vacancy do not establish a functioning operator, measured reliability, or acceptable power consumption. My pattern of backing unfamiliar technical businesses depends on concrete proof spikes; Orca has not supplied one here. Shield AI and Sedaro’s reported execution of 189 approved commands in August 2026 shows that this category can produce inspectable results. Those results belong to a competitor.

The Lyra assessment is the strongest counterargument to passing. A named spacecraft company is examining integration, and these founders have unusually relevant experience for resolving what that examination uncovers. They could be doing exactly the unglamorous early work that produces a valuable company. But a concept study establishes technical engagement, not a user who keeps demanding the crude product. Orca’s 302 LinkedIn followers supply no additional demand evidence. With no documented recurring use or commercial commitment, I cannot distinguish a repeatable software business from a succession of bespoke integration projects. The broad $5.88 billion market estimate does not resolve that distinction.

I would also avoid inventing red flags. Hiring a flight software engineer does not prove premature delegation. Working with Lyra does not establish dependence on one large partner. There is no documented obstinacy or waiting for funding. Conversely, the known exceptions require a working product, unmistakable user pull, or validated technical proof strong enough to overcome uncertainty. None is documented for Orca. Its current evidence therefore supports a pass on my own money, without an adverse judgment about the founders.

One verifiable result would change my decision: a customer-witnessed hardware-in-the-loop report showing that the founders overcame a named compute or power blocker within weeks and ran the revised operator successfully against agreed mission tests. That would connect their relevant experience to resourcefulness and a working artifact. Those are the missing links.
