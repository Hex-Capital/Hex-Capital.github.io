# STASIS -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Soham Chakraborty and Neil Goradia have earned knowledge in the right place. Berkeley independently identifies both founders on GlaucoGlasses; they built ocular devices together before STASIS. That matters more than their degrees. Chakraborty’s reported 30-day wear test adds evidence that he lives with the integration problems himself. I give the founders substantial credit for action, while keeping the glaucoma product’s 348 physician intent forms and $168,000 of distributor pilot intent entirely outside STASIS’s commercial record.

The investment turns on what survives replication. Inward-facing sensors, automatic tinting, and connected screen adjustments create a physical product that copying an application would not reproduce. But hardware earns its advantage through difficult performance, manufacturing knowledge, protected designs, or accumulated measurements. STASIS’s claimed sensor patents have no verified identifiers or scope, and its proposed longitudinal data advantage has no demonstrated dataset or predictive improvement. There is an engineering project here. The dossier has not established a durable asset.

The strongest case against passing is that these founders could discover that asset through use. They have worked on ocular hardware, stayed together, and put the new product on a founder’s face for a month. This fits my preference for founders attacking embodied bottlenecks. My exceptions for unusual prelaunch products also matter: waiting for conventional commercial proof can mean waiting until the opportunity is obvious. Here, however, the decisive uncertainty is whether the sensors measure something useful enough to change behavior. Founder wear establishes exposure to the product; it does not establish independently measured benefit or obsessive customer demand. The exception remains plausible, but insufficient to write my check.

“The WHOOP for 16 billion eyes” counts anatomy rather than demand. The initial buyer is a screen user experiencing eye strain. A larger opportunity would require showing how the same validated capability serves adjacent buyers or transactions; the dossier supplies no established route or independent spending evidence. IXI’s autofocus product demonstrates activity in neighboring eyewear, while JINS MEME’s discontinuation shows that sensing can reach consumers without securing a lasting product. Neither establishes STASIS’s market ceiling or its likely outcome.

There is also no demonstrated mechanism through which one wearer makes another wearer’s product better. Personal baselines could improve an individual experience, but cross-user improvement, proprietary training rights, and referral effects remain unproven. The proposed hardware purchase and optional $15 monthly subscription identify a direct payment model. Conflicting $95 and $240 preorder copy, with no verified completed orders, leaves willingness to pay unresolved. I see no documented integrity violation or artificial fundraising pressure; pricing ambiguity warrants clarification, not an accusation.

I pass at present. The founders’ specific knowledge is the strongest signal, but STASIS has yet to convert that knowledge into a demonstrated advantage customers will repeatedly pay to use. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an independently run, paid 90-day customer study showing sustained wear and repeatable reduction in eye-strain symptoms attributable to the sensor-driven adjustments. That would establish a useful feedback loop between measurement and intervention—the foundation on which the larger business currently depends.
