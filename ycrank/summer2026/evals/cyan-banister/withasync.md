# Async -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years from now, a small law firm may operate with software handling intake, conflict checks, engagement letters, scheduling, and task assignment while attorneys retain judgment. Async has one credible leak from that future: agents already perform those workflows at Martinez Legal, Clifton Law, Clemente Mueller, and Joshua Lowenthal’s firm, reportedly eliminating thousands of work-hours. But the dossier does not map the intervening steps from bespoke audits to a repeatable operating layer. “AI agents will grow quickly” describes the current wave; it does not explain what Async uniquely makes possible after that wave passes.

The strongest evidence is behavioral and wonderfully ordinary. Clifton Law allowed the product inside several consequential workflows without migrating systems. Four named firms have entrusted it with procedures that touch clients, legal conflicts, and engagement documents. That is much better than a waitlist or an agent rendering. Yet the “thousands of hours” claim lacks dates, customer-level attribution, repeat-use cohorts, and payment data. I can see the machinery moving; I cannot yet see customers making a costly commitment to it.

Why does this work cost another employee today? Async says owners lack the time and knowledge to redesign procedures, which is a real irritation, but it never identifies a rule, fee, institutional gatekeeper, or quantified rent. Outcome pricing could align Async with the owner rather than selling another software seat, but no contract values or measured outcomes are disclosed. The product currently returns time, not clearly economic or occupational control.

The founders’ history is ambiguous in a useful way. Ben Smith and Connor Park previously built together, Park shipped a GPT-4 Obsidian plugin, and Skope gave them experience with outcome billing and legal agents. The less flattering chronology is also precise: AI billing infrastructure in 2025, legal agents in early 2026, then four service verticals by August. That may be operating tuition, or it may be two capable founders following each new agent-market opening. The dossier supplies neither a long pre-company obsession nor evidence that one accountable leader can keep a customization-heavy business from dispersing across law, healthcare, real estate, and childcare.

The bull case stings because Async may already have found the correct unglamorous wedge. SOC 2 Type I, tenant isolation, approval queues, zero-retention agreements, and integrations into existing systems show more respect for hidden operations and privacy than the usual AI wrapper. Outcome-priced deployments could accumulate organizational memory and become difficult to remove. But those controls are deployment competence, not yet a controlled bottleneck. Async relies on Claude and other frontier models while competing against well-funded self-service and forward-deployed platforms; four verticals multiply delivery complexity before the four published legal customers establish repeatability.

I pass today. The working artifact is real enough to prevent a stronger rejection, but the known-exception case is incomplete: product proof can overcome missing revenue when paired with an unusually deep founder prior or a scarce enabling asset, and Async has shown neither. My verifiable flip condition is ten paying law firms where at least seven purchase a second workflow within 90 days while deployments sustain software-like gross margins. That would show the audits are discovering a repeatable legal operating layer rather than financing a talented automation consultancy.
