# Trope -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Trope’s contested truth is that ERP implementation can become software-like: agents can compress a six-to-eighteen-month, hourly consulting project into a fixed-price deployment measured in weeks. The falsifying event is simple: on several live Business Central migrations, Trope fails to shorten implementation time or reduce errors without replacing software leverage with expensive human review. The prior workflow product’s reported two-to-three-times speed improvement and 90% error reduction make this more than an AI slogan, but they do not yet prove the proposition for the ERP product launched in July 2026.

The initial market is appropriately narrow: Business Central buyers, especially firms leaving Dynamics GP before support ends in 2029 or NAV 2018 before 2028. Trope has built an in-house harness, field-level APIs, native side panels, release-wave tests, and approval gates for sensitive financial actions. These are useful technical components, not yet a monopoly mechanism. Microsoft, incumbent Dynamics partners, and well-funded substitutes can reproduce many of them. Deployment logs and migration mappings could eventually become proprietary data, but a possible future corpus is not present ownership.

The founders are the strongest evidence. Matthew Chow and Victor Vannara grew up in Vancouver, met at UBC, built a prior workflow product together, and now present themselves as full-time co-founders. Their production-software, AI-agent, and distributed-systems experience is credible, while the earlier paid pilots show that they can put automation into a real organization rather than merely publish a demo. No documented red flag suggests convenience matching, part-time commitment, or outsourced core engineering. But founder ownership, salaries, and the exact role of the unnamed principal consultants remain undisclosed, so economic alignment and whether essential ERP judgment resides inside the company are not yet proved.

The strongest bull case is that fixed pricing changes the incentive structure of ERP services. Hourly consultants profit from duration; Trope profits from automation. Add a time-bounded Microsoft migration wave, consultants credited with more than 200 implementations, and managed agents sold after go-live, and a services wedge could become a recurring operational layer. This resembles the unstated pattern of building the complete production stack rather than selling a standalone AI feature. Yet the current product has disclosed neither a customer nor revenue, while all paid-pilot evidence belongs to the preceding workflow product. There is therefore no verified zero-to-one event strong enough to invoke the exception granted to technically proven products whose monopoly remains uncertain.

Distribution is named but unmeasured. Direct scoping calls fit bespoke implementations; white-label relationships with systems integrators, VARs, and Microsoft Dynamics partners could provide reach. They could also subordinate Trope to partners that own the customer and can internalize its agents. Without contract values, sales-cycle evidence, partner conversions, or implementation margins, the same channel can describe either a scalable software-enabled integrator or a small consultancy with better tools.

My central concern is not whether Trope can build a good business. It is whether one revenue engine can produce a power-law outcome. Fixed-fee projects remain labor-sensitive, optional managed agents have no disclosed pricing, and platform concentration gives Microsoft strategic control over both product surface and distribution. I would reconsider after one verifiable result: at least five current-product Business Central deployments completed in under twelve weeks, with positive implementation gross margins and most customers converting to recurring managed-agent contracts. Until then, this is an intriguing secret without demonstrated value capture: investigate, but do not invest yet.
