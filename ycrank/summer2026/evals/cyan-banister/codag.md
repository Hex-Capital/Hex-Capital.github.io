# Codag -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: production agents investigate failures continuously, and feeding them millions of raw log lines looks as primitive as asking a human to read a database page by page. Codag has found a plausible leak from that world. Its engine preserved incident answers in 98–99% of hosted tests while using 5.9–7.5 times fewer tokens than raw logs plus grep. But the dossier skips the intervening machinery: why compression becomes an independent platform, why observability vendors cannot bundle it, and what Codag controls when they try. This is a real future-shaped clue, not yet a six-year map.

Michael Zhou has more builder evidence than biography theater. He worked on infrastructure at Okta and Shopify, produced a developer-tool repository with 655 GitHub stars, and shipped the current CLI, Drain implementation, API, and MCP server. A solo founder also gives me one accountable decision-maker rather than a 50/50 governance puzzle. The five reported pivots cut both ways: they demonstrate persistence and contact with users, but they do not establish that log compression was a pre-company obsession. I see Tizz; I do not yet see the recruiting proof or commercial command that completes the founder case.

The strongest bull case is wonderfully concrete. Anyone can install the CLI without an account, inspect the MIT-licensed code, reproduce part of the benchmark, and graduate from free usage to a $19 or $499 plan. The current repositories have 45 and 20 stars only months after the May 2026 launch, while several heavily funded observability companies validate that autonomous diagnosis matters. If the synthetic results survive messy production incidents, Codag could become a tiny compulsory component inside every debugging agent. That would be a much better business than another observability dashboard.

But users have not yet done anything costly or weird. GitHub stars are curiosity; they are not engineers routing sensitive production logs through Codag, renewing a paid plan, or trusting its output during an outage. The benchmark covers 51 synthetic incidents and was run internally. The exception I make for pre-revenue infrastructure requires unusually strong proof—a signed institutional deployment, repeated real-world use, or a functioning artifact that changes behavior immediately. Codag has the artifact, but not the exceptional behavior.

The broken rule is also underdeveloped. Token and context limits impose a measurable computational tax, yet the dossier does not quantify what an organization currently pays, identify a gatekeeper extracting rent, or show whose occupational control returns when that tax disappears. Meanwhile, raw operational logs create a genuine custody gate. Transient processing, exclusion of third-party LLMs, PII scrubbing, and enterprise VPC peering are thoughtful mitigations, but possible retention of de-identified logs for training leaves consent and deletion architecture unresolved. With this data, “de-identified” is a claim I need to attack, not a spell I accept.

Codag matches one of my unstated patterns: concealed infrastructure underneath the visible agent wave. The material difference is control. Its deterministic base adapts published Drain3 work, is MIT-licensed, and sits beside Dash0, Honeycomb, Sentry, and Cribl, which already own ingestion and customer relationships. My 36-month base case is that Codag pivots into broader agent-observability or telemetry routing and remains between $500,000 and $2 million ARR rather than sustaining standalone log compression. I am not writing the personal check yet. One verifiable flip condition would change that: three unaffiliated companies each renewing the $499-or-higher plan for three months after using Codag on production incidents under a contractually explicit no-retention or customer-controlled-storage policy.
