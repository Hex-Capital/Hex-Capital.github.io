# Bayworks -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Bayworks sells into a simple economic tension: time spent writing repair narratives competes with billable technician work, but incomplete narratives can jeopardize warranty reimbursement. AI-assisted capture could let dealerships recover both. The controlling variable is net economic value recovered per repair after technician review and correction. The dossier identifies the problem, but does not establish that variable or its threshold. My proposed underwriting test is a hypothesis, not evidence that Bayworks has passed it.

The strongest evidence is distribution velocity. Bayworks reports reaching 80+ dealerships in 110 days, including an unnamed top-50 US dealer group. That deserves meaningful credit at pre-seed. However, the 4,000 daily repairs describe those dealerships’ throughput; they do not establish 4,000 daily Bayworks interactions. The customer’s $3B+ revenue is also not startup revenue. These distinctions leave a promising deployment signal without establishing habitual usage or willingness to pay. The Android listing’s 0+ downloads does not resolve employer-provisioned adoption in either direction.

The product has more substance than transcription. Repair-order context, missing-information prompts, retrieval of previous repair stories, technician review, and return to the DMS form a recognizable operational loop. Better models could improve completion while Bayworks retains the integration and repair history. That earns architectural credit independently of the dealership count. But dealership ownership of operational data limits what I can assume about cross-customer learning. The relevant question is how much time and money a competitor needs to reproduce this workflow; the dossier establishes no reproduction barrier.

Tekion makes that question immediate because it already offers native technician voice documentation. The strongest bull case is that Bayworks can spread across existing dealership systems without requiring a platform replacement, then use technician adoption to distribute adjacent service products. Eighty dealerships in 110 days makes that possibility worth taking seriously. It fits my pattern of following software into physical-industry transitions. Yet cross-DMS distribution remains an unverified advantage, and the red flag is incumbent bundling, not simply better foundation models. WarrCloud’s reported 13.9% growth in dealership warranty revenue indicates expanding workload; it does not quantify Bayworks’s labor savings, reimbursement improvement, or pricing power.

The founders have shipped a relevant workflow. Goyal’s backend experience at Navi and internships at Scapia and Cisco support execution competence, but they do not document prior startup learning or costly personal commitment. I assign the missing-evidence default rather than infer motivation from credentials. The research-stage exception also does not apply: the dossier supplies neither frontier research nor scarce model-building capability that would justify underwriting this independently of customer economics.

I would pass today. Bayworks has stronger deployment evidence than economic underwriting evidence, which is a specific imbalance rather than evidence of a bad business. One verifiable flip condition would be an independently checked 90-day paid dealership cohort showing sustained usage and net labor-plus-warranty savings of at least three times subscription cost, including review and correction time. That would connect the promising distribution signal to the variable that determines whether this becomes a large company.
