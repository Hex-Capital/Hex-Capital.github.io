# 83 Sciences -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have earned the right to work on this problem. Ian Naccarella operated inside battery materials at Sila, Eric Riesel completed an MIT doctorate spanning experimental chemistry and materials models, and Yankang Yang deployed AI across a 30,000-person organization. More important than the résumés, the three have converted that knowledge into a working system, one reported novel material, and revised synthesis conditions that exceeded an anonymous battery lab’s prior capacity plateau. The evidence is company-reported and not yet independently validated, but it is action in the exact domain rather than borrowed enthusiasm.

The product attacks a real information bottleneck. Voice notes, failed runs, notebooks, and instrument exports are converted into a corpus that Dalton can query. A competitor can copy the interface and use MatterGen or OMat24, but it cannot automatically copy a partner’s unpublished experimental history. That is the potential durable asset. The unresolved question is whether 83 Sciences owns any transferable learning: no public contract terms establish exclusivity, cross-partner model-training rights, retention, or even permission to reuse derived knowledge. Proprietary data trapped inside separate customer silos is consulting leverage, not network leverage.

The strongest bull case is that the contract work is merely the ingestion mechanism. Dozens of partnerships are reportedly in progress, every engagement runs through the platform, and one battery collaboration produced improved conditions and a manuscript in under two months. If each deployment creates reusable experimental knowledge, the company could compound unavailable failed-run data while competitors train on published successes. But today each lab appears to require grants of up to $50,000, in-person onboarding, and direct discovery work. Users are not yet creating value or distribution for one another; company labor is connecting the graph.

The market also leaves insufficient room to maneuver as presently defined. Materials informatics is projected to grow from $170.4 million in 2025 to $410.4 million in 2030, which supports a useful company but not yet an obvious nonlinear outcome. The dossier does not identify the adjacent transaction layer—licensing discovered materials, owning foreground IP, operating autonomous labs, or selling into broader industrial R&D—that turns the initial data wedge into a much larger market. “Advanced materials” is a category, not a causal expansion path.

There is no disclosed price, revenue, take rate, or named payer. Discovery contracts suggest direct monetization may exist, but inference is not a transaction. The absence is acceptable at pre-seed; it simply cannot prove value capture. I also see none of the integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-instability red flags that would veto the option. The deeper contradiction is structural: my preferred systems allow outsiders to contribute value permissionlessly, while 83 Sciences currently acquires data through negotiated, partner-by-partner relationships.

My 36-month base case is a specialized materials R&D platform with roughly $1–3 million in annual contract revenue, valuable to several labs but still dependent on bespoke onboarding rather than a compounding discovery network. I am neutral today. One verifiable condition flips me to invest: standardized partner agreements that grant reusable cross-partner training rights, accompanied by held-out evidence that each additional laboratory’s data measurably improves predictions for other laboratories.
