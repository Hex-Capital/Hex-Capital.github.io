# Decawork -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Aman Raj built Barclays compliance tooling used by 240+ teams, but Decawork’s founders have disclosed no history of working together. That makes Decawork a schlep-heavy security company built by a promising but unproven pair. The gateway must hold credentials, mediate production actions, collect approvals, and survive audits. Most hackers would rather build the agent than become responsible for everything the agent might break. Decawork is therefore strongest where work becomes unpleasant and weakest where I would normally want evidence that two founders have already endured unpleasant work together.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung because compliance was not incidental to the product; accepting the compliance grind became part of the product. Decawork shares that mechanism. Decawork puts credentials outside agents, scopes permissions, gates sensitive actions, and records attributed audit logs. Those are chores until the first production incident, when they become infrastructure. Decawork diverges from Coinbase because the disclosed enterprise deployments belong to its prior autonomous-IT product, not the current control plane, and because Okta already brings 8,200 integrations. The schlep is real, but Decawork has not yet proved it owns the schlep.

The polished AI-wrapper pass fails because two founders assembled around a fashionable pitch are merely a pair on paper. Decawork has two founders, which is better than one, but Decawork provides no evidence that Sarthak Aggarwal and Aman Raj studied, worked, or built together before starting it. Stripe had two brothers; Reddit had dorm-room history; the relationship itself had already been tested. Here the configuration gets only modest credit. A pair facing credential custody, enterprise sales, and incident liability can split at exactly the moment the work stops being fun.

Decawork’s prior autonomous-IT product ran across multiple enterprises, while Aman Raj previously built a fintech serving 52,000 families and processing $1.5 million. Those are animal tracks, though not yet cereal boxes. Over the next 18 months, resourcefulness will compound if Decawork’s founders personally wire odd enterprise systems into the gateway, sit through security reviews, and recover failed agent runs at 3 a.m.; each ugly deployment then becomes a reusable integration and policy. Decawork’s pivot from human-employee incidents to agent control also shows willingness to discard the first framing. But no named customer, current-product revenue, or unscalable acquisition stunt appears in the dossier, so I cannot treat the prior deployments as Decawork’s Dropbox demo.

Sarthak Aggarwal worked on AI systems at NVIDIA and Ema, while Aman Raj built compliance tooling at Barclays. Decawork would move into the top tier on organicity if the founders could say: “We watched one of our own agents receive a dangerous credential, built this gateway for ourselves that night, and kept using it.” Decawork’s biography currently proves proximity to both halves of the problem, but not the collision that produced the idea. If the pivot came from repeated failures inside the prior autonomous-IT product, the idea is genuinely organic. If it came from observing Okta, Astrix, and Oasis announce an attractive category, it is market archaeology.

**Dropbox** (2007) had Drew Houston’s working demo, not merely a technical résumé. Sarthak Aggarwal’s Google Code-In win and work at NVIDIA and Ema give Decawork more hacker genealogy than the average enterprise-security pitch, while Aman Raj’s systems reached 240 Barclays teams and 100,000 monthly users in a later consumer-AI role. But Decawork has no attributable public repositories, technical paper, or current gateway artifact that outsiders can inspect. The founders have evidently shipped software; Decawork has not yet shown the piece of software that makes another hacker say, “I wish I had built that.”

Okta’s 8,200 integrations make the 36-month forecast unusually concrete: by July 2029, Decawork will be acquired by Okta after reaching roughly $3 million to $7 million in ARR as an agent-access gateway. Decawork’s credential mediation and audit machinery solve the sort of tedious problem an incumbent eventually needs, while Decawork’s unproven founder relationship and lack of a disclosed present-day moat make an independent category win less likely. The schlep can create a valuable product before it creates a durable company.

Astrix’s $85 million, Oasis Security’s $195 million, and Okta’s existing distribution form the strongest bear case against that forecast: Decawork may be a late feature bundle with no current-product traction, no disclosed security certification, and a gateway whose compromise could expose every connected agent. The bull mechanism that survives is founder-problem contact. Decawork combines Sarthak Aggarwal’s enterprise-agent experience with Aman Raj’s compliance systems used by 240+ teams, and the prior product already operated inside multiple enterprises. Animals can turn those relationships into the first control-plane deployments faster than a large vendor can ship a coherent product. Three named enterprises running Decawork’s current gateway in production by January 2027 would flip my verdict from Invest to Strong Invest; failure to produce even one would flip it to Pass.
