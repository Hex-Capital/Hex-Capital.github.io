# HERA -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the founders’ industrial-engineering degrees. It is what Meera Patel and Noelle So did around the problem: manufacturing work at Pfizer and TE Connectivity, a water-treatment facility built by So, more than 200 interviews with manufacturing practitioners, and a live demo in three production plants by May 2026. This is specific knowledge acquired through contact with factories. It is stronger than résumé alignment, although the dossier does not yet show that either founder previously owned drawing review, sold this exact product, or produced measurable shop-floor outcomes.

The problem is real and expensive. A review that prevents one $40,000 scrap event can justify enterprise pricing, and checking tolerance stacks, datums, assemblies, and company-specific rules is more consequential than generating plausible text. Keeping drawings on customer premises and attaching an evidence chain also fits the trust requirements of aerospace and medical-device manufacturing. But technical seriousness is not technical proof: the category benchmark’s 5.23% hallucination rate shows why an engineer must retain sign-off, while HERA provides no accuracy, recall, false-positive, or time-saved benchmark of its own.

The investment turns on what remains after a competitor copies the interface and the checking workflow. HERA’s best answer is the accumulating system of record: adjudicated violations, customer-specific standards, and engineer feedback could become scarce data and switching cost. Today that mechanism is asserted rather than demonstrated. CoLab, Axial, Redprint, and Glypad already cover overlapping checks, and Redprint prices reviews as low as $1.98–$4 per drawing. Four competing implementations mean code is not the asset. HERA must prove that each deployment creates a proprietary error corpus that makes the next review materially better.

The wedge has room to expand across aerospace, semiconductor tooling, medical devices, pressure vessels, and custom machinery because the shared capability is drawing interpretation against formal and internal rules. That is more promising than a single-purpose compliance check, but it is still a list of verticals rather than a demonstrated adjacency. No market-spend evidence, paid deployment, or published pricing establishes how much of engineering verification HERA can capture.

The deepest structural weakness is that customers do not presently create value for one another. On-premises deployment and confidential, company-specific rules may actually partition the learning. Every new plant appears to require another founder-led sale, NDA, configuration process, and review engagement. The opposite case is strong: three production plants and 200 interviews may be the beginning of a high-value proprietary dataset, and pre-seed enterprise adoption often appears before revenue. But the dossier does not show that lessons can cross customer boundaries, that accuracy compounds, or that the free 20-drawing review converts into recurring payments.

I would not invest yet. I see a credible industrial software company with unusually good early problem contact, but not yet a compounding venture asset. The verifiable flip condition is ten paying production plants showing, through a common held-out benchmark, that HERA’s adjudicated error corpus improves review accuracy as deployments accumulate. That single result would prove value capture and establish that the system learns rather than merely installs.
