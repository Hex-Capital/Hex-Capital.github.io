# HyperBench -- Paul Graham Evaluation

HyperBench has shown what its software can do. It has not yet shown what its founders do when something important refuses to work. Those are different tests. The dossier contains technical demonstrations, but no dated account of a blocked deployment, the founders’ workaround, and what changed afterward. I would leave resourcefulness at its missing-evidence default. Building this product requires ability. Inferring determination from the product alone would count the same evidence twice.

The technical evidence deserves real credit. On September 26, HyperBench reported running roughly one million model parameters at approximately 10,500 decode tokens per second on an AMD Kria KV260. Its October 8 debugging comparison reported 117.7 versus 237.2 seconds of solver time, with both attempts passing. Connecting repositories, component documentation, and physical testing could shorten an engineer’s investigation substantially. But the FPGA result does not establish reliable customer debugging, and one attempt per condition cannot establish a dependable speed advantage. These are inspectable claims from builders, with internal validation that warrants a discount.

The strongest reason to invest is that this team might understand a problem most software founders would avoid. Justin Leong worked on Anduril’s Roadrunner GNC firmware; Ivan Ge worked on software infrastructure at OpenAI. Getting code, toolchains, and physical devices to cooperate contains plenty of neglected work. Yet relevant employment does not establish that these founders personally encountered this particular problem and built HyperBench to solve it. Nor does the dossier document an observation that caused them to reject an earlier approach. I have evidence of technical competence, while problem origin and adaptability remain untested.

Who wants this badly enough to keep using the crude version? HyperBench offers private-beta enrollment, but the dossier identifies no returning engineering team, paid deployment, or customer insisting that another bench be connected. That matters to the business mechanism. Environment-specific integration could teach the founders something valuable on every installation, or consume their time in bespoke support. Repeated customer use would distinguish those possibilities. Nominal’s investigation agent and custom-agent interfaces make that distinction consequential: HyperBench needs to own a painful repair workflow strongly enough that customers seek it out. Undisclosed revenue and pricing are normal at this stage; they supply neither evidence for that outcome nor evidence against it.

I would pass today. HyperBench fits my interest in technical builders producing concrete proof, but the programmable-substrate pattern is not established until other engineers build or work on it repeatedly. There is no evidence here of partnership dependence, premature delegation, or an artificially assembled founding relationship; undisclosed history is not a red flag. A sufficiently compelling prototype or independently validated result can justify an exception, but these selected internal demonstrations do not replace the missing founder and user evidence. My flip condition is one auditable customer deployment: a named engineering team encounters a specific blocker, the founders ship a workaround within a week, and that team continues using HyperBench weekly for the following month. That would connect the promising machinery to the people and need I am betting on.
