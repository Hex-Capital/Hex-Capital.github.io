# Whitespace -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the plausible picture is easy to see: wholesale employees supervise exceptions while software handles orders, replenishment, customer questions, and planning across the ERP and inbox. Whitespace has found a small leak from that world—two named customers already running order-intake, customer-service, and inventory-planning workflows—but the dossier does not map the intervening transitions. It says agents are now capable; it does not explain how a two-person application company becomes the operating layer of record while incumbents, ERP vendors, Plato, Proton.ai, Canals, and Conexiom pursue the same destination.

The founders are the best evidence here. Alex Tung and Leon Yao both grew up in wholesale-distribution families, then independently accumulated the complementary operating tuition: Tung advised manufacturers and distributors at BCG and helped Ankar reach seven-figure ARR as its first non-engineering hire; Yao built logistics optimization at InstaDeep and previously shipped a product used by more than 1,000 people and three schools. Twelve years of friendship, prior experience living and working together, and a clearly identified CEO reduce—but do not eliminate—the governance question. This looks derived from repeated exposure, not a category chosen after AI agents became fashionable.

The mundane object that interests me is the six-month training period. Why does learning a distributor’s back office take that long? Because the real system is scattered across inboxes, spreadsheets, ERP fields, customer exceptions, and facts held in employees’ heads. Whitespace is attempting to capture that concealed operating knowledge, and the claimed 30–50% administrative burden gives the rent a measurable shape. But there is no artificial license, regulatory gatekeeper, or rent-taking institution here. The product returns time to staff; it does not yet demonstrate that it transfers economic control to a previously constrained person.

The product proof is real enough to matter. Kingfisher Direct and Warwick have deployed functioning workflows, with company-reported productivity gains above 80%, two-to-four-week implementations, and typical payback in three months. Customers are entrusting the system with orders, inventory, and customer responses—not merely joining a waitlist. Still, I cannot see the weird or costly repeated behavior beneath the headline: expansion from one workflow to several, operators voluntarily increasing autonomy, renewal, exception frequency, or what happens after the first incorrect replenishment recommendation. Those behaviors would tell me whether Whitespace is becoming part of the business or merely completing an attractive automation project.

The strongest bear case is architectural. Customer-specific knowledge bases, ERP mappings, and correction histories create some local switching cost, but Whitespace explicitly does not learn across customers. That privacy choice is sensible given access to pricing, accounting, and customer data, yet it weakens the compounding advantage while four heavily funded competitors cover overlapping workflows. Encryption and isolation address part of my privacy gate, but the dossier does not establish retention limits, employee access, auditability, or who bears responsibility when an agent acts incorrectly. This could become a services-heavy integration company sitting above closed models and legacy systems rather than an enabling layer it controls.

My 36-month expectation is a credible vertical software business at roughly $3–8 million ARR, concentrated among distributors willing to buy several workflows, but not yet the category’s operating system. The bull case stings: two customers and three-month payback at pre-seed may mean the founders have already solved the deployment problem their better-funded competitors missed. None of my exception patterns overrides the remaining gap, however: there is no singular technical bottleneck, extraordinary consumer ritual, or accidental artifact revealing a future others cannot yet see. I would not write the check today. My flip condition is verifiable: three named customers must renew and expand from one workflow to at least three while maintaining deployments under four weeks and publishing measured exception or correction rates.
