# horizontal. -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The secret worth testing at horizontal. would be that factories can delegate cross-department execution to software without first standardizing their fragmented systems. That proposition could be falsified by deployments that require persistent human coordination or extensive engineering for every new process. The dossier establishes the problem and describes the proposed solution; it supplies neither a disputed technical proposition nor an experiment testing it. I would leave the secret unproved rather than infer one from the ambition to run operations end-to-end.

Who owns the resulting market? Pelico already connects ERP and MES data to disruption-response agents; Oden automates investigations and workflows; Siemens announced governed industrial agent orchestration in June 2026. These are specific competitive claims against horizontal.’s proposed territory. Starting with one process limits implementation scope, but it does not identify a customer segment the company can dominate. Plant-specific integrations might eventually create switching costs. Today, the same integrations could simply create expensive obligations. The dossier demonstrates overlap before it demonstrates a monopoly mechanism.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ proximity to the problem. Niklas Marxer reports bringing agentic AI into more than 150 industrial companies before horizontal.; Jakob Hermeler has Stanford robotics research experience and coauthored work on autonomous quadrotor collision recovery. They met through research rather than a startup networking exercise. These facts make a technically serious industrial company plausible. They do not establish prior joint shipping, full-time status, ownership, or economic alignment, so the founding-structure score remains at its default. Marxer’s deployment history belongs primarily to the distribution case: it offers a plausible source of customer access, although no resulting acquisition channel is documented.

Horizontal.’s plant-manager invitation to a founder demo gives that distribution case an identifiable buyer and sales owner. The company also says it works with international manufacturers, which could conceal substantial early operating evidence. But the statement identifies no deployment, repeat usage, or paid contract. The missing distinction is whether factories are buying repeatable software or commissioning bespoke integration. No verified pricing or margin structure permits that distinction, and the adjacent $20.7 billion MES market cannot supply the missing revenue-engine arithmetic. These are unresolved questions at pre-seed, not evidence of commercial failure.

The company fits my less-public preference for making the physical economy work through software. That preference cannot substitute for control over the value created. Nor does the dossier establish the conditions behind my known exceptions: an independently demonstrated technical breakthrough, persistent customer behavior, or a validated deployment path that removes the central execution risk. The robotics paper is relevant technical background; it does not validate factory-operation agents. Conversely, there is no documented part-time founder, damaged capitalization, or revised technical claim. The decisive adverse evidence is competitive overlap without a demonstrated means of escaping it.

I would pass on horizontal. with my own money today. The founders may understand an important problem unusually well, but the evidence does not yet explain why solving it produces a company that keeps the profits. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: a customer-verified comparison showing horizontal. executing the same bounded production workflow at least ten times more efficiently than Pelico, with deployment and continuing human intervention included in the measurement. That would turn claimed execution breadth into evidence of proprietary advantage.
