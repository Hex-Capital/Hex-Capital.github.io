# Waddle Labs -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The attractive possibility is not better robot-programming software. It is collapsing the cost of adapting robots to new tasks so that physical automation reaches work conventional programming cannot economically serve. Waddle Labs has a plausible mechanism: an agent observes camera feeds, decomposes goals, writes editable control code, invokes VLA models, and replans after failure. But the dossier quantifies only a $1.671 billion programming-software market. It does not quantify engineering hours eliminated, robots newly economical, or the physical-work cost pool affected. The consequence could be large; today it is asserted rather than calculated.

The technical choice is more interesting than another VLA wrapper. Reusable programs could be more auditable and transferable than opaque end-to-end policies from Skild AI or Physical Intelligence. Six months of hardware operation, roughly 1,000 overnight LEGO repetitions, and a shared skill library show that Song and Ye built something beyond slides. But repetitions are not a benchmark. Waddle reports no task-success rate, adaptation time, intervention rate, safety result, or cost comparison against a named incumbent. CaP-X and ASPIRE already demonstrate similar execution-feedback loops, while performance still depends on third-party model capability and reasoning budgets. This is a technically credible hypothesis, not yet a 5–10x discontinuity.

The largest kill risk is reliable, economical generalization outside the founders’ laboratory. Waddle has not defined the cheapest decisive test, a pass/fail threshold, or a stop-or-pivot response. The LEGO work trained an ACT policy, but it does not show that the agent can safely produce reusable control programs across robots and workspaces. The absence of disclosed interlocks is particularly serious when generated code moves physical equipment. Capital spent expanding the preview API before settling this question would buy activity rather than risk reduction.

The bull case stings. Song has relevant program-induction and multimodal-model research; Ye has worked on diffusion robot policies; and two Harvard undergraduates have already accumulated more real-hardware experience than many larger AI teams. The move from teleoperated data collection in 2025 to agent control in 2026 may represent exactly the tactical flexibility I want. A tiny technical team could use normal robot operation to create a proprietary loop of skills, failures, and interventions. But the dossier does not document why that pivot occurred, what belief changed, why a prior co-founder disappeared, or whom the team has recruited to own safety and industrial deployment. The exception for a rapidly evolving small team requires either a giant demonstrated value pool or unusually strong external pull. Waddle currently shows neither.

Economics are the lowest-confidence gate. There is no pricing, paid deployment, cost per completed task, or model showing when agent inference becomes cheaper than human robot programming. Higher reasoning budgets reportedly improve performance, which may make the best demonstrations the least economical. My base case is that within 36 months Waddle Labs either pivots back toward robot-data and teleoperation infrastructure or remains below $1 million ARR because laboratory capability does not translate into safe, cost-effective deployment.

I pass today, but this is a testable pass rather than a dismissal of the category. One result would flip me: an independently reproducible benchmark showing at least a 5x reduction in engineer-hours per successfully deployed task versus Wandelbots across three materially different robot families, at equal safety and lower total operating cost. That would convert reusable code from an architectural preference into a discontinuity and begin establishing the cost-collapse case.
