# Async -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Two named law-firm deployments make **Async** a Wizard-of-Oz services business with potentially venture-shaped pricing trapped inside my least attractive category: consensus AI agents. **Async** scores highest on capital structure because workflow audits, outcome pricing, and separate expansion agreements can fund a disciplined regatta; **Async** scores lowest on sleeve fit because Anthropic already offers 15 small-business workflows and Gumloop has raised $70M to automate the same application layer. “AI agents are uninvestible” is the provocative version. The operational version is that Async needs service economics, not model capability, to be the asset.

**Fin** is the high rung on structure because manual operation was deliberately used to discover what deserved automation. **Async** shares that mechanism: Async audits a customer’s stack, scopes one or two workflows, installs approval logic, and accepts outcome responsibility rather than selling seats or tokens. The analogy matters because Clifton Law and the Law Office of Joshua Lowenthal show Async doing work before pretending software has generalized it. Async diverges from Fin because no public price, revenue, gross-margin, or labor-per-deployment figure demonstrates that the manual layer is shrinking; outcome pricing can be intelligent risk-sharing or consulting with uncapped downside.

The canonical “AI agent for an enterprise vertical” fails my lowest rung by combining commodity exposure, consensus language, and a five-round SaaS cap table. **Async** reproduces too much of that failure mechanism: Async has no published moat, depends on external models, and markets across law, healthcare, and real estate while Anthropic moves directly into overlapping small-business workflows. The unstated portfolio patterns do not rescue Async—there is no crypto activity, DTC brand, prescription rail, creator asset, employee-equity structure, or rollup. The one sleeve-adjacent feature is managed Wizard-of-Oz delivery, which keeps Async above a pure agent shell but not comfortably above it.

No public round size, valuation, competitive process, or passed investor is available for **Async**, so Async receives my price-discipline default rather than credit for being pre-seed. The score flips a tier only if Async is priced below the Summer 2026 batch median, has a credible tier-1 pass, or offers an instrument tied to workflow revenue rather than another generic SAFE. Relevance AI’s $37M and Gumloop’s $70M prove category heat, not Async’s entry price; without terms, I cannot claim the illegibility discount that generated the Solana outcome.

Ben Smith has moved from nonprofit fundraising agents to Skope billing, then legal agents, then **Async** across service industries. Over 18 months, those tacks either compound into proprietary knowledge about deploying accountable workflows or reveal that Async is chasing whichever AI wrapper currently clears the market. Smith’s resale background and Connor Park’s computer-science training fit consultative selling plus integration work, but Async has no founder essays, durable category thesis, or three-year obsession in the dossier. The red flag is not pivoting—Venmo took turns of the crank—the red flag is having no articulated missionary belief connecting the turns.

**John Fish / Bookshelved** entered with an audience that existed before the financing asset; **Async** has LinkedIn reach without equivalent owned distribution. Ben Smith’s 4K followers and Connor Park’s 1K are better than zero, but Async’s customer acquisition still begins with a contact form and consultative workflow audit, while the prior Skope launch reached only 132 Product Hunt points and 94 followers. Async therefore has founders who may sell, not founders whose community sits economically on the cap table.

Two law-firm customer stories lead me to predict that within 36 months **Async** will abandon the broad law-healthcare-real-estate framing, become a legal-workflow managed automation provider, and reach roughly $2M-$5M ARR without becoming a horizontal agent platform. Async gets there by repeating intake, conflict-check, engagement-letter, drafting, and email deployments until custom work becomes a bounded implementation playbook. That forecast follows the signature directly: the service structure can work even if Async’s category-level software thesis does not.

Anthropic’s 15 ready-to-run workflows are the strongest bear mechanism: falling model costs could erase **Async** before implementation knowledge compounds, while the conflicting SOC 2 claims are especially damaging in legal and healthcare sales. The Ro exception is the serious counterargument—capital-intensive operational complexity can become the moat when regulation and workflow integration make software insufficient—and Async’s two named law firms suggest that possibility. Async has not yet earned the exception because no retention, expansion, gross-margin, or compliance evidence shows that complexity is defensible rather than expensive. Twelve-month net revenue retention above 120% with deployment gross margin above 60% would flip my verdict to Invest; absent that, I would not write the personal check today.
