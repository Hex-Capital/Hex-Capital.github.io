# Trope -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence case is real but bounded. ERP implementations consume a $29.52 billion services pool and commonly take six to eighteen months; moving them to weeks at a fixed price could remove substantial consulting labor and implementation delay. But Trope has not quantified the dollars saved, companies reached, or downstream productivity released. This is potentially a 5–10x improvement in one enterprise workflow, not yet a 100x societal consequence.

The technical claim is better than a generic AI wrapper but remains unproven on the current product. Trope has an in-house harness, field-level Business Central APIs, release-wave testing, and agents spanning configuration, migration, reconciliation, testing, and documentation. Its earlier workflow product reportedly produced two-to-three-times faster completion and 90% fewer errors for paid pilots, including an organization with more than 400 staff. That evidence matters, but it is not a benchmark of the ERP system launched in July 2026. “Go live in weeks” against conventional six-to-eighteen-month partners is still a target, not an audited result.

The founders have enough learning velocity to keep my attention. Matthew Chow built production systems across Tesla, Zipline, Generac, and Schneider Electric; Victor Vannara worked on AI agents, MCP tooling, and distributed systems at Amazon. Their transition from workflow guidance to ERP delivery suggests tactical flexibility, and bringing in principal consultants credited with more than 200 implementations addresses domain risk. But the dossier does not document why they changed direction, what evidence overturned the prior plan, or which named recruit owns financial-process safety. Those omissions separate adaptability from mere repositioning.

Trope’s largest kill risks are obvious: an incorrect agent can corrupt financial records, and Microsoft can change the APIs, product, or commercial terms beneath the company. Human approval, retained run logs, release-wave retesting, and preventing unattended posting are sensible controls. They are not a falsifiable de-risking plan. I want a threshold for reconciliation accuracy, a shadow-mode deployment sample, its cost and timing, and an explicit stop or redesign rule. Spending on integrations or distribution before establishing that reliability curve would turn technical uncertainty into consulting labor rather than remove it.

The strongest bull case is the **Block** exception: a tiny team can begin with an apparently incremental tool, attack a very large existing value pool, and expand into infrastructure. Trope shares the small-team adaptability and large pool; its white-label route through systems integrators and Microsoft partners could accelerate adoption. The material difference is ownership. Block’s wedge created a direct customer relationship and room for a broader platform. Trope currently depends on Microsoft’s system of record, partner channels, and principal-consultant delivery. Campfire, Rillet, and DualEntry have each raised more than $100 million, while Microsoft already embeds Copilot. Without a proprietary learning loop proven across deployments, Trope risks becoming an efficient implementation firm rather than an uncapped software platform.

I expect that by August 2029 Trope will have moved toward managed finance agents or a broader AI-enabled implementation consultancy while remaining below $5 million in recurring software revenue. The fixed-fee model could still produce a good business, but no disclosed implementation price, agent operating cost, utilization assumption, or gross margin establishes software economics. I pass today. One verifiable flip condition would change that decision: ten production Business Central deployments showing at least 5x faster go-live, fewer than one material reconciliation error per 10,000 agent actions, and software-like gross margins above 70%.
