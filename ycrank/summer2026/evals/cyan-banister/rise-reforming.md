# Rise Reforming -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Small wastewater plants and dairy farms no longer treat biogas as an awkward byproduct requiring a pipeline-scale project; standardized boxes arrive, consume the gas where it is produced, and ship supply-secure chemicals out. The steps are legible: survive dirty feedstock, prove continuous conversion, secure host gas, contract chemical buyers, and finance a fleet. The leak from that future is not the $41.6 billion methanol TAM. It is a container already delivered to a Chicagoland wastewater plant, paired with one binding supply agreement and a conditional DME offtake.

The hidden system is attractive because the waste is economic, not merely environmental. Almost 90% of U.S. wastewater biogas reportedly remains flared or trapped in low-margin heat and power because conventional renewable-natural-gas projects need large plants and pipeline access. Rise Reforming proposes to pay smaller hosts for stranded gas, assume the equipment burden, and capture the spread through chemical sales. That returns an earning asset to facilities excluded by scale and infrastructure, although the absence of public pricing means I cannot yet tell whether Rise has removed the rent or quietly inherited it through capital costs and maintenance.

Three molecular-engineering students formed this during finals week in 2023, then built rather than narrated: Lucas Zubillaga led the bench reformer through more than 1,800 hours of stable syngas production, while the team raised over $1.7 million and recruited advisors in safety, permitting, biogas logistics, and process scale-up. Their prior work at Argonne, Avangrid, Mantel Capture, Nexamp, and an electrochemistry lab is relevant tuition. I see technical seriousness and recruiting momentum, but not yet the long pre-company trail of independent experiments or commercial command that would make founder obsession the decisive fact.

The sharpest bear case is sitting in the feed pipe. Rise’s operating record is syngas proof of concept, not continuous production from wet wastewater biogas carrying hydrogen sulfide, VOCs, and catalyst-poisoning siloxanes. The company pivoted from plastic waste to biogas in 2025, its field pilot will not operate until 2027, and M2X already has multiple deployments, $40 million in Series B capital, and a $10 million DOE award. A proprietary-process claim and an IP filing do not overcome that deployment gap. If cleanup or catalyst replacement destroys unit economics, the elegant distributed network never exists.

Still, this is beneath the visible climate story in the way I like. Rise is building a physical conversion layer that can turn one dispersed feedstock into DME, methanol, and potentially dimethyl carbonate; FuelEU Maritime and 350 methanol-capable vessel orders provide downstream pull without being the whole thesis. The controlled asset is currently the reforming process and modular operating design, but its defensibility must become field data across variable gas compositions and a repeatable portfolio of host and offtake contracts. Otherwise it is an expensive box in a market containing several other expensive boxes.

I would invest, but this is an engineering bet rather than a traction victory. My 36-month expectation is two to five operating modules and roughly $1 million to $4 million in annualized chemical sales by August 2029; failure to achieve 1,000 consecutive hours on live wastewater biogas within the disclosed 2027 pilot would flip me to a pass. That test collapses the central uncertainty: whether Rise has found a supply-secure chemical platform or merely demonstrated clean syngas under conditions the customer cannot provide.
