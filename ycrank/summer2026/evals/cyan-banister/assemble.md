# Assemble -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Aliyan Ishfaq is the named CEO, but Assemble’s three Stanford-trained co-founders have built a software agent for changing enterprise systems without showing that any founder ever lived inside a broken ERP implementation. That pairing is Assemble’s signature: governance is legible enough to lead the scorecard, while the capital-intensive, ugly-regulated swing is almost entirely absent. Assemble has Tiz—30 Salesforce Superbadges completed with a 93% first-attempt pass rate—but Biz remains unproven and Rizz is hidden behind matching Stanford biographies. I see capable visitors to enterprise IT, not yet founders *of* the place.

Anduril is the high rung for unambiguous authority because Palmer Luckey’s identity, product conviction, and operating control all point in one direction. Assemble shares only the surface mechanism: Aliyan Ishfaq alone carries the CEO title. Assemble diverges where the analogy becomes load-bearing—three co-founders, no disclosed equity split, no operating agreement, and no identified tiebreaker when Aliyan, Shaurnav Ghosh, and Shrish Janarthanan disagree. A title is helpful; a title is not a conflict protocol. This is a rebuttal, not a hard no, but my own-money check waits for the answer.

HandUp is the low rung on the capital-intensive axis: little hardware or technical-regulatory compounding, rescued in my portfolio by autobiographical mission alignment. Assemble is similarly software-led, except Assemble has no HandUp-like founder biography to override the weakness. Salesforce version control, deterministic rollback, and credential access are serious engineering, but Assemble’s own terms shift hallucination verification back to customers while SAP and Salesforce can embed native agents. Enterprise anxiety is not regulation, and API access is not a rocket factory.

Aliyan Ishfaq’s LangChain experience, ParaAI’s 100,000 users and $50K ARR, and Open SWE’s 10.3K GitHub stars show that Assemble’s founders can ship AI products. Over 18 months, however, founder-market fit compounds through the undocumented exceptions, political scar tissue, and administrator trust accumulated inside real enterprise deployments; Assemble currently offers Stanford AI Lab, AWS, Apple, and a KPMG internship instead. Assemble can learn that terrain, but the dossier contains no physical moment when Aliyan felt an ERP migration break beneath him. Technical adjacency earns more than the absent-evidence floor, not lived-problem credit.

Tribal has already raised $10 million, Nexus $4.3 million, Duvo $15 million, and Opkey $59 million around overlapping enterprise-agent workflows. Assemble therefore reads closer to a crowded AI-agent lane than to Crusoe burning stranded gas where polite investors would not go. The counterfactual that flips Assemble upward is specific: credible investors reject Assemble because reversible autonomous production changes look too dangerous, while CIOs repeatedly deploy the product anyway. The dossier instead shows 207 LinkedIn followers, no named customer, and a Gartner forecast saying agents will enter 40% of enterprise applications—consensus wearing an enterprise badge.

Niantic was observable magic: I could play Ingress, walk through the world, and feel the behavior before a spreadsheet explained it. Assemble offers a narrower artifact—recorded queries, mutations, shadow-environment validation, and deterministic rollback—so an enterprise administrator can observe Assemble’s work even if a normal human cannot touch it. Thirty Superbadges in 6.4 recorded hours prove Assemble can operate inside a deterministic Salesforce obstacle course; Assemble itself concedes that real environments contain incomplete requirements and dispersed logic. That is a useful laboratory specimen, not yet the Pokémon wandering down the street.

Thirty Salesforce Superbadges make my falsifiable 36-month prediction: Assemble will narrow into Salesforce release-management and testing, reach $1 million to $3 million ARR, and abandon the broader claim of autonomous agents spanning ERP, CRM, and HRIS. Assemble’s strongest asset is the reversible Salesforce execution layer, while native SAP and Salesforce agents compress the horizontal opportunity. I’m never playing the game I seem to be playing: Assemble looks like a general enterprise architect, but the evidence points toward a focused DevOps tool.

ParaAI’s 100,000 users and $50K ARR make the strongest bull case sting: Aliyan has shipped before, Open SWE has 10.3K stars, and DeepMind is my known exception proving that Stanford credentials can conceal real polymath compounding. Assemble could turn 629 badges, a 93% first-attempt pass rate, and deterministic rollback into the trust layer every enterprise agent needs. Assemble still loses that argument today because no customer, revenue, repeat production deployment, or founder-lived enterprise scar validates the leap from Trailhead to an actual system of record. Three named enterprises collectively paying Assemble at least $500,000 ARR after repeat production changes would flip my verdict; until then, I pass.
