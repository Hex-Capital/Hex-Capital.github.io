# Petrarch -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Industrial AI systems are trained on the private residue of real work—construction plans, factory failures, vehicle recordings, codebases—not merely on whatever survived the public internet. Getting there requires discovering those assets, proving chain of title, removing sensitive information, demonstrating model utility, and making suppliers return with more. Petrarch has found a real leak from that future: completed transactions across three dataset categories, including several thousand construction plans redacted and transferred within one week. The dossier proves movement through the pipe. It does not yet prove that the pipe improves a model or compounds.

The strangest and best evidence is behavioral. Someone permitted thousands of operational documents to leave a dormant silo, Petrarch processed them, and a buyer accepted the transfer. That is considerably more informative than a waitlist or an appraisal form. Transactions involving autonomous-driving recordings and codebases suggest the construction case was not a one-off category trick. But there are no named buyers, prices, repeat purchases, or measured performance gains, so I cannot distinguish a valuable data supply from an expensive procurement service.

The founders plausibly understand the hidden work. Samuel Hahn sold PII-redaction and compliance products, Ian Lee built training-data infrastructure and advised Scale AI on quality filtering, and Sudhish Swain worked on deployed computer vision and spatial-transcriptomic datasets. Dropping out of Harvard together shows mutual commitment and complementary recruitment. It does not establish pre-company obsession: the dossier gives me relevant employers and research, but no dated independent experiments or artifacts preceding Petrarch. The functioning marketplace work earns product credit; it cannot be recycled into a lifelong-founder story.

The broken system is still described too softly. “Dormant data lacks a monetization route” identifies an inconvenience, not the rule producing it. Who owns a bankrupt company’s recordings? Which contract prevents resale? Who bears the cost when consent or provenance is defective? Petrarch says it certifies legal clearance while its privacy policy places submission rights and legal-basis responsibility on providers. Redaction, DPAs, and the option to reject regulated datasets are useful controls, but that allocation leaves the ugliest tail risk precisely where suppliers may have the least institutional continuity.

The bull case stings: a restructuring network could make Petrarch the liquidation layer for stranded digital assets, while transaction history becomes proprietary pricing intelligence and exclusive supply compounds across construction, logistics, and manufacturing. The founders’ domain alignment and the one-week construction transfer make that plausible. Yet exclusivity is not documented, buyers and sellers can disintermediate the marketplace, and Troveo, Defined.ai, Human Native, and Datarade already cover adjacent sourcing and licensing functions. Petrarch sits beneath the AI application wave, but today it controls a workflow—not yet a scarce asset.

My 36-month base case is a useful data-acquisition and redaction business at roughly $1 million to $3 million ARR, not yet a defensible marketplace. I am neutral and would not write the check today. One verifiable condition flips me: three buyers each make a repeat purchase after Petrarch documents measurable model improvement under an exclusive, independently verified chain-of-title license. That would connect the present transaction artifact to the future infrastructure company—and close the privacy and disintermediation gaps in the same motion.
