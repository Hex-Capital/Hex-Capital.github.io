# Async -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Async has proved that small law firms will let a two-person startup automate consequential administrative work. Four named deployments and “thousands of hours” automated are meaningful pre-seed evidence. They do not yet establish a consequential outcome. The $7.63 billion agent-market estimate says nothing about the labor cost pool Async can eliminate, the number of businesses it can reach, or whether the result is a 10% convenience or a 10x collapse in operating cost.

The technical case is weaker. Async audits each customer, scopes workflows, integrates existing systems, and maintains agents built on Claude and other frontier models. SOC 2 Type I, tenant isolation, approval queues, and company memory make deployment safer; they do not constitute a 5–10x technical discontinuity. Lindy and Relevance AI already supply agent infrastructure, while Harvey and Luminai attack vertical workflows with far more capital. No benchmark shows Async beating a named alternative on accuracy, implementation time, cost, or throughput. Today the differentiation is largely skilled implementation labor—the AI pattern I am least willing to mistake for proprietary technology.

The strongest bull case is the founders’ willingness to revise the route. Smith and Park moved from outcome-based billing infrastructure to legal agents and then to Async, while preserving enough continuity to produce four legal customer stories. Outcome pricing also aligns payment with delivered value. This resembles the exception in **Block**: an initially unremarkable technical wedge can expand into infrastructure when an adaptive small team enters an enormous value pool. The material difference is that Async has neither quantified such a pool nor demonstrated a platform emerging beneath the customized deployments. Expanding simultaneously into healthcare, real estate, and childcare may increase implementation entropy faster than proprietary learning.

The largest kill risk is whether reliable automation can be replicated without founders repeatedly auditing, configuring, and maintaining every account. Approval queues address regulated-workflow errors, but Async provides no accuracy threshold, deployment-cost target, or experiment separating reusable product from consulting. SOC 2 removes a procurement objection; it does not test this existential question. Capital spent broadening vertical coverage before measuring repeatability would be the wrong risk-removal sequence.

The economics remain opaque. Outcome pricing could create attractive margins, but there are no disclosed prices, model costs, implementation hours, retention figures, or expansion rates. Managed audits and ongoing maintenance may simply substitute expensive startup labor for somewhat more expensive customer labor. My 36-month base case is that Async narrows back to legal operations and reaches $1–3 million ARR as a services-heavy automation provider, not a horizontal agent platform.

I pass. This is real execution and real customer usage, but the current evidence supports a useful implementation company rather than an asymmetric technical option. One condition would flip me: a verifiable cohort of 20 law firms running the same workflow at least 10x cheaper than human delivery, with no bespoke engineering after initial configuration. That would simultaneously demonstrate discontinuity, repeatability, and credible scale economics.
