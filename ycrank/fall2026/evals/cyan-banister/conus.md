# CONUS -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I want a surgical team to know which implant is available, which instructions apply to that exact variant, and where its own hospital’s protocol differs—without finding the one person who remembers. CONUS is assembling ingredients for that world: manufacturer documents, hospital standards, implant inventories, and answers linked to source pages. But connecting those ingredients reliably requires maintained device identities, current local information, and trustworthy verification. The dossier establishes document retrieval today; it does not establish the transitions into that future. I will not award the destination merely because I can imagine it.

Why does a surgeon have to telephone a manufacturer representative to find an instruction? CONUS identifies a real information failure: knowledge scattered across PDFs, portals, hospital protocols, and personal contacts. Returning that search time and independence to clinicians appeals to me. Yet the dossier does not measure the delay, identify an artificial restriction, or establish who profits from keeping the information fragmented. Manufacturer services finance operations while hospitals receive free access. That arrangement could fund useful independence, but it could also leave the supplier as the economically important customer. The institutional terms prohibiting preferential sorting and manufacturer access to hospital-specific usage data are meaningful boundaries.

The founders give me more reason to linger than the AI label. Paffrath began building in 2022 and recruited Becker and Schulz in 2024; the app appeared in January 2025, before Intelligence arrived in May 2026. This is a multiyear construction effort rather than evidence of a sudden category chase. Becker brings hardware and automation experience from Cleansort, Schulz developed biomedical electronics and embedded software at Miltenyi, and five chief-surgeon founders or advisors provide clinical access. Those are complementary capabilities and some first-believer proof. What remains missing is the pre-company obsession or specific operating wound that explains why this team will keep digging when maintaining device knowledge becomes tedious.

There is also a product people can actually touch. CONUS reports more than 20 live European hospitals, and its iOS app has 26 ratings averaging 5.0. That earns substantially more attention than a waitlist. But “live” does not tell me whether clinicians return before procedures, upload local knowledge, or abandon the old telephone routine. The six-month launch claim also lacks a clear starting point. My strongest objection to passing is that hospital adoption and named relationships with Johnson & Johnson, Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, and B. Braun could represent unusually valuable early access. My known exception for pre-revenue technical companies applies to the absence of revenue here; it does not supply the missing evidence of repeated behavior or independently derived founder obsession.

CONUS also touches an unstated pattern I like: an ordinary workflow repair can conceal infrastructure. Its reported 500,000 papers and 2,000 manufacturer documents are a starting corpus, but document count does not establish control over a scarce dependency. The valuable asset would be a maintained relationship between a particular device, its instructions, and a hospital’s actual inventory and standards. The dossier does not yet demonstrate that asset’s depth or exclusivity. C8 Health and Incision already organize adjacent clinical workflows. Nor can I assume implant selection supplies the expansion path when current terms exclude patient-specific selection. I see no documented governance shell game or severe privacy violation; the unresolved red flag is whether the knowledge layer has substance beyond retrieval that adjacent platforms can add.

I pass with my own money today, narrowly. CONUS has enough builder persistence and institutional access to become a useful business, but the enabling layer and six-year transition remain less established than the present workflow tool. One result would change my decision: an independently verified 90-day cohort showing five hospitals paying for and using CONUS weekly to resolve device-specific preparation questions from maintained local inventories and source documents, replacing manufacturer callbacks. That would show clinicians acquiring control, hospitals valuing it, and the underlying knowledge relationships surviving real work.
