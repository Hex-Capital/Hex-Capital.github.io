# Computable -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Computable’s standing buyback quote turns future H100 weeks into a regulated-looking financial instrument, while Computable’s two-person founding structure names no CEO and does not even publicly identify the second co-founder. That pairing is the signature: a magically weird, infrastructure-adjacent market whose governance is less legible than its order book. I can imagine Computable becoming important; I cannot yet identify the human with tiebreaker authority whom I would follow into the ugly part.

SpaceX made capital intensity itself an advantage because rockets could not be copied with a product sprint. Computable shares the wager that scarce physical infrastructure can support a new market layer: sealed bids cover specific calendar weeks, positions are redeemable for H100 nodes, and every position receives a standing buyback quote. Computable diverges where the analogy becomes load-bearing. SpaceX controlled the artifact; Computable has disclosed one cluster, no additional suppliers, and no liquidity reserve. Computable is selling exposure to the rocket without owning the rocket factory.

HQ Trivia exposed what happens when magnetic product pull temporarily conceals unresolved founder authority: Yusupov and Kroll eventually made the missing tiebreaker impossible to ignore. Computable repeats the dangerous opening configuration more starkly. Yuan Song is called a co-founder rather than CEO, the Coinbase co-founder is unnamed, and Computable publishes neither responsibilities nor a conflict protocol. Two founders are not automatically a disaster, but silence about who decides when the standing buyback drains cash is not a rebuttal. It is the beginning of a hard no.

Yuan Song’s inferred Flow Traders and Jump Trading history could move Computable out of the market-analysis bucket, but Computable has not shown the autobiographical moment when GPU-capacity risk became personal. The counterfactual is simple: show that Yuan or the Coinbase co-founder personally ran a compute book, lost money on stranded reservations, or built the relevant trading infrastructure before Computable existed, and the founder score moves into Invest territory. Today the evidence establishes adjacent professional competence, not the Anduril-like condition of being *of* the problem rather than visiting it.

Ornn’s $33 million compute-futures effort and SF Compute’s $55 million operated-cluster business mean Computable is weird, but not lonely. Over 18 months, Computable’s auction either compounds through repeated clearing prices, multiple suppliers, and credible buyback depth, or the mechanism runs backward: thin auctions create unreliable prices, unreliable prices repel buyers, and the standing quote becomes a balance-sheet liability. Computable’s first auction and 46-point Show HN launch are enough to make me watch. They are not enough to prove that Computable has found the non-consensus edge before consensus found it.

Computable’s first auction offers H100 nodes for specific weeks from August 2026 through January 2027, making Computable more observable than a generic B2B API but less tactile than Niantic. I could play Ingress and feel the world change; a Computable buyer can redeem capacity and run a workload, but an outsider can observe only a contract interface backed by one unnamed cluster. The artifact is real at redemption, yet the crucial liquidity promise remains an abstraction until someone sells a position during stress.

SF Compute’s thousands of GPUs and $55 million funding make a general-purpose exchange victory unlikely, so my 36-month forecast is that by July 2029 Computable will have abandoned universal instant liquidity for weekly GPU positions and pivoted into institutional GPU procurement and market-making, producing less than $3 million in annualized revenue. Computable’s strongest asset is regulated-market fluency; Computable’s weakest is the absence of visible founder authority needed to survive a liquidity crisis. That combination usually produces a narrower brokerage before it produces an exchange.

Jump Trading and Coinbase are the bull case that stings: Computable’s founders may understand market structure so deeply that the unnamed identities are privacy, not weakness, while the first H100 auction is merely the thin opening book of a future compute CME. Crusoe also began with an awkward mechanism and found the larger infrastructure game through a crisis. Computable loses that analogy today because no named CEO, supplier depth, reserve, transaction volume, or regulatory structure supports the standing quote, while SF Compute and Ornn already possess capital and overlapping products. I remain Neutral; I would flip to Invest if Computable names one CEO and publishes six consecutive monthly auctions involving at least three independent suppliers with every sellback honored.
