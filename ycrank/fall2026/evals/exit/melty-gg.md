# Melty -- Exit Evaluation

**Action:** Watch  
**Evidence confidence:** Low

### Company case

**Strategic acquisition of Melty Gym** is the strongest path. An AI lab or robotics simulation platform could pay to own Gym’s engine if control lets it build and modify evaluation tasks materially faster across multiple games than it could through internal development or a license. The memo documents demos of a simulated G1 robot driving in GTA V and interacting with server racks. Melty reports building custom tasks with unnamed teams. The demos show the kinds of tasks Gym can support. Setup time, cross-game reuse, and customer savings remain unmeasured in the memo. (The Idea, Defensibility, Market & Traction)

A paid production deployment that measures task setup time and cost across multiple games would test whether Gym’s engine creates a repeatable advantage. The demos and reported custom work make that test plausible at seed. The memo lists a $125,000 seed entry and gives no runway, burn, or test budget. Melty’s consumer terms reserve ownership of its prepared packages, catalog, and compatibility data. The memo gives no Gym-specific evidence about which game integrations and underlying rights an acquirer could take over. Consumer usage and fundraising supply no evidence of a separate route to cash for this holder. (Defensibility, Market & Traction, Key Risks, Key Facts)

The illustrative investment is a $500,000 check at a $10 million post-money valuation, with 5% ownership diluted to 3% and no follow-on checks. The eight-year, 5× objective requires $2.5 million in gross cash to the holder, or $83.3 million in distributable equity proceeds at 3%. The hurdle is $125 million at 2% ownership and $62.5 million at 4%. For both sale scenarios, assume zero net debt and fees, a simple pro rata waterfall with no preference adjustments, and all cash at closing. Stock, escrow, earnouts, rollover, and compensation are each assumed to be $0. The sale prices and timing are scenario assumptions. The memo supplies no usable Gym pricing, buyer economics, or transaction price evidence to support either sale price. (Market & Traction, Key Facts)

| Scenario | Timing | Enterprise value → distributable equity | Gross cash to holder |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Modest acquisition | Year 5 | $40M → $40M | $1.2M (2.4×) |
| Strong acquisition | Year 7 | $100M → $100M | $3.0M (6.0×) |
| Prolonged private | Through year 8 | Growth plateaus, buyers stay away, public scale remains out of reach, and cash is reinvested | $0 (0×), with shares retained |

Even the illustrative $100 million sale falls short at 2% ownership, yielding $2 million (4.0×). The valuation and transfer assumptions drive the low confidence.

### Countercase

A buyer could license Gym or build the narrower set of tasks it needs. Good Start Labs already builds curated game environments with verifiable rewards, supporting the narrower-build alternative. Publisher rules and permission requirements for Melty’s consumer mashups also raise the possibility that critical Gym integrations would need separate permissions. The memo leaves open whether those alternatives adequately substitute for Gym and whether any publisher restriction blocks a Gym sale. Those uncertainties support Watch. The evidence does not trigger a Pass override. (The Idea, Defensibility, Key Risks)

### Decisive diligence question

Can a paid production pilot with a prospective strategic buyer show that owning transferable Gym integrations would create enough value to justify a price near or above the proceeds hurdle? Buyer-validated setup savings across multiple games, documented transfer rights, and a quantified advantage over building or licensing would move the action to Reach out if they support that price. If the pilot shows a license captures the same value and caps acquisition economics materially below the hurdle, move to Pass.
