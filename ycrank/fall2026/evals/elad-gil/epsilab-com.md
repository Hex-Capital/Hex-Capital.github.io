# Epsilab -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Longer-running agents make realistic training environments more valuable; AI labs are the buyers, and Epsilab becomes a large company only if its environments improve real-world agent performance more cheaply than labs can reproduce internally. That is the controlling belief. The dossier establishes what the company supplies—executable workflows with tasks, rewards, and verifiers—but does not establish that economic advantage. I would pass on a personal check today.

The timing is credible. METR’s May 2026 report describes benchmark saturation and cheating failures, giving research teams a reason to seek harder environments with better success checks. Epsilab’s emphasis on long-horizon and multiplayer tasks addresses that need. But a broken benchmark does not automatically create an attractive supplier business. There is no measured training improvement, environment production cost, or licensing price here. The technology shift earns some credit; a demonstrated expansion in customer economics remains unproven.

Epsilab’s strongest product detail is the combination of versioned state, trajectory collection, and failure replay described on its company LinkedIn. Those components could place the product inside a recurring training-and-debugging loop. Better models could then increase demand for harder tasks while the environment preserves reproducibility. This is materially deeper than formatting model output. However, the description does not establish customer integrations, accumulated evaluation data, or an advantage in verifier reliability. I give the architecture intermediate credit without treating possible workflow ownership as an existing moat.

The market is already crystallizing around competing approaches. HUD announced a $16 million Series A in June 2026 and offers a supplier marketplace; Prime Intellect announced a $130 million Series A in July and combines environments with compute and training. Its reported annualized revenue above $100 million belongs to that integrated business, not necessarily to environments. Epsilab could sell through these platforms, but becoming a supplier might also leave distribution and pricing power elsewhere. My pattern of investing across a technology discontinuity supports investigating this layer; it does not establish that this particular supplier captures the value. Exclusive data and favorable delivery margins remain questions, rather than demonstrated advantages or established red flags.

The strongest argument against passing is the team’s prior startup learning. Hu and Peng previously built Shortbread together, and Peng’s biography reports $500,000 in revenue and two million episodes sold there. That is concrete experience shipping and monetizing a product, deserving substantial founder credit. It is not Epsilab traction. YC backing, 46 LinkedIn followers, and zero advertised jobs establish neither customer pull nor customer rejection, so demand receives the missing-evidence default. I also do not see the documented frontier research or scarce model-building capability needed for a research-stage or talent exception. Prior operating experience helps execution; it cannot substitute for the missing market mechanism.

One verifiable result would change my decision: a paid renewal from an independent AI lab after a controlled evaluation shows that Epsilab reduces the total cost of reaching a fixed level of held-out, real-workflow performance by at least 20%, including its fees. That is my proposed investment test, not a claimed company result. It would connect environment quality to a purchasing decision and explain why the lab keeps buying instead of rebuilding. Until then, Epsilab has a plausible product architecture and experienced founders, but insufficient evidence that the market will amplify their work into a durable business.
