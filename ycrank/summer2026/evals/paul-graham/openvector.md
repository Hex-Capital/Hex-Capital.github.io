# OpenVector -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The strongest fact is not that OpenVector uses AI. It is that Andrey Gizdov’s foveated-vision system reportedly processes about 3% of pixels, reduces bandwidth 5–10×, and appeared as a CVPR 2025 Spotlight. That is a measured artifact built before the company had much institutional permission. Combined with Vishal Urlam’s work on camera systems and network protocols, it suggests two technical founders who can make vision models survive contact with edge hardware rather than merely assemble an impressive demo.

But the dossier never shows what happened when something important broke. Long-horizon reasoning remains unreliable, false detections could trigger POS discounts or customer-profile changes, and the offline station will not ship until fall 2026. OpenVector has sensible mitigations—confidence thresholds, human review, foveated sampling—but no dated account of a failed deployment, the workaround the founders shipped, or the changed result. The product proves technical ability. It does not yet prove relentless resourcefulness under customer pressure. Those are different facts.

Demand is weaker still. “Live use cases” across security, retail, manufacturing, and logistics sounds broad when I want narrow. I cannot identify the user who needs the crude version now, how often that user returns, or whether anyone has paid beyond a refundable $100 reservation. The $449 monthly price is an economic proposition, not traction. Demo-led selling is appropriate, but converting a prospect’s stream during a 20-minute call only becomes evidence when the prospect keeps the workflow running after the founders leave.

The strongest bull case is that this is simply what a good pre-seed infrastructure company looks like before customer evidence becomes public: four people, unusually relevant technical backgrounds, peer-reviewed efficiency work, existing-camera compatibility, and a real edge appliance rather than a slide deck. If even a few of the reported deployments are recurring production usage, the current dossier understates the company substantially. Yet the breadth of those use cases may conceal the opposite problem. Security, retail, factories, and logistics have different error costs and buying processes, while Spot AI, Ambient.ai, and Voxel have already raised $93 million, more than $72 million, and $61 million respectively. A general platform must learn faster than specialists, and there is no usage evidence yet that it does.

There may be a good schlep-heavy wedge here. Legacy-camera integration, on-premise inference, reliability thresholds, and the consequences of autonomous actions are precisely the ugly work that can protect a company. Urlam’s embedded-systems experience makes the team more credible at that work. But the dossier does not show that either founder personally encountered this operational pain before choosing the market, nor does it document whether Foveal Vision became OpenVector because of customer evidence or merely a new positioning exercise. I will not infer an evidence-driven pivot from two live websites.

I would not write the check yet. The technical proof is unusually good, but it does not override the missing founder-under-resistance and user-pull evidence required by my decision gate. The verifiable flip condition is simple: one named operating niche running at least five paid deployments for eight consecutive weeks, accompanied by a concrete account of a detection or integration failure the founders corrected themselves within days. That would connect the impressive artifact to both resourcefulness and a narrow well of demand.
