# Spine -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Why does buying health insurance come with a second job? Spine’s useful insight is that founders already have software; they still need someone to chase the carrier, fix enrollment, and answer employee questions. Move the clock to 2032 and I can imagine a small company whose employment administration simply happens, with humans handling exceptions. Getting there requires reliable execution across platforms and explicit authority over sensitive records. Spine’s Slack-based specialists provide a present-day service, but the dossier does not yet show the technical artifact connecting that service to that future.

The money deserves a closer look. Carriers pay commissions, allowing Spine to remove separate administration fees while operating customers’ existing Rippling, Deel, Gusto, or ADP accounts. That returns time and preserves employers’ choice of software. But “free” still has a payer. Spine redirects insurance-distribution economics toward operational work; it has not demonstrated that it removes the underlying rent. The reported 15% healthcare savings would strengthen that argument, except the homepage also claims 25%. Those figures need reconciliation before I credit a quantified economic improvement.

There is real activity here: 30-plus startup customers and approximately 4.4-fold commission-revenue growth over six months through July 2026. Employers are entrusting consequential work to the team, which matters more than a waitlist. Yet the dossier provides little detail about repeated workflows, renewal cohorts, or customer effort displaced. The consumer app’s 582 ratings belong to a different product history. I will credit them as evidence that these founders shipped something, without treating those users as validation of the employer service.

Onur and Ece have also done the difficult work of building together before; Acadian describes Spine as their third company together, and Feed dates to 2021. Engineering, legal training, and repeated collaboration give this team more substance than an attractive benefits pitch. What I cannot establish is a pre-company obsession with this particular operating problem. My willingness to back a company before revenue depends on unusually strong alternative evidence, such as a long technical pursuit and a working artifact. Spine already has commercial activity; that exception does not supply the missing founder history or technical destination. The disclosed CEO and COO roles offer no affirmative evidence of a leadership shell game.

The strongest case against passing is that the apparently ordinary brokerage is financing something much more consequential. Commission income could pay for learning every ugly exception in startup employment administration, while the 50-plus-practitioner referral network supplies distribution. That fits my attraction to mundane businesses concealing a harder destination. But the proposed claims-data advantage remains an investor thesis, and the approximately 78% contribution margin is company-modeled. Existing brokers can add service, and the HR platforms control the systems Spine operates. I see a useful operator between institutions, with little demonstrated control over a scarce enabling asset. Health-data consent is stated in the privacy policy; severe privacy abuse is not established, although consent alone does not establish sound access architecture.

I would pass with my own money today. The service addresses an irritating, expensive responsibility, but the evidence supports a brokerage with operational support more strongly than the future infrastructure I want to own. One verifiable development would reopen the decision: a live demonstration of Spine-owned software completing an end-to-end benefits renewal across multiple HR systems, under explicit customer permissions, without specialist intervention. That would give me an artifact connecting today’s service to the future I can imagine.
