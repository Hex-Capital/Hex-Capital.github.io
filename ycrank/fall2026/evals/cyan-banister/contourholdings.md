# Contour -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I want an engineer building energy infrastructure to spend less time assembling paperwork and more time building. Contour proposes an appealing route: buy the environmental consulting practice, put engineers beside its staff, and automate the reports from inside. But faster document production must become reliable applications, and reliable applications must actually shorten the path to construction. The dossier establishes the proposed starting point; it supplies no operating artifact showing those transitions have begun. The April 2026 federal call for permitting automation expresses demand, not proof that Cortex can deliver that future.

Why does the consultant’s queue exist? Contour identifies repetitive manual reports, studies, and applications that constrain delivery capacity. That is a concrete operating problem, and the strongest part of this dossier. Yet I cannot tell how much delay comes from document preparation versus fieldwork, professional judgment, or agency review. Nor does the dossier establish a particular fee or artificial restriction that ownership and automation remove. The cited $27 billion environmental consulting market includes considerably more than permitting. Buying a share of those service revenues could make a business without returning much time or control to the builder.

The strongest argument against my pass is that acquiring the practice could solve several problems at once. Contour would gain an existing customer relationship, experienced practitioners, and a place to implement software without persuading an outside firm to change its workflow. Embedding engineers on-site also suggests respect for hidden operating complexity. This fits my preference for unglamorous workflow repair and potentially for infrastructure concealed inside an ordinary business. But the decisive word is “could”: no completed acquisition, controlled project dataset, or demonstrated performance advantage is documented. Paces and Transect already address adjacent workflows. Contour’s ownership strategy creates a possible learning advantage; the dossier does not yet establish possession of it.

The team has relevant pieces. Aarush Agrawal built enterprise search at Rezolve AI; Zack Lubar’s Lazard experience relates to acquisition execution. Louix Simpson is explicitly identified as CEO. What I cannot find is a dated encounter with environmental consulting that became an obsession: a prior experiment, a practitioner recruited through unusual conviction, or an acquisition already navigated. Employers establish exposure, not that first-believer act. I would not infer opportunism from missing biography, and four founders do not establish a governance problem. My founder score stays at the missing-evidence default.

Cortex is described as producing reports and applications, but the dossier contains no completed customer action through a demonstrated product. YC participation and zero job postings tell me nothing about whether a consultant repeatedly trusts its output. My pre-revenue exception requires affirmative prototype, customer-commitment, and founder evidence; those supports are absent here. The other exceptions involving extraordinary user behavior or a revealing technical artifact also lack a counterpart. There is no documented severe privacy violation, concealed leadership, or dependence on one closed model supplier to justify an adverse score. Acquiring a practice would still require establishing rights to use its client records; ownership alone does not settle that question.

I pass with my own money today. Contour has identified a plausible place to recover human time, but the acquisition thesis currently carries more weight than demonstrated founder obsession or technical control. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an acquired practice repeatedly delivering paid, regulator-accepted applications through Cortex with measured reductions in preparation hours and no increase in correction requests. That would connect the proposed ownership mechanism to something a builder can actually feel.
