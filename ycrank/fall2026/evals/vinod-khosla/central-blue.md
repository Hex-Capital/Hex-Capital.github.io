# Central Blue -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Central Blue’s consequential possibility is separating a spacecraft’s useful work from the power system it can carry. That could change satellite design: more payload operation, less onboard generation and storage. But how much changes? The dossier provides no baseline for displaced mass, spending, or lost payload hours, and no quantified expansion in capability. I can see the mechanism for an important company. I cannot yet calculate the consequence that makes a very unlikely success worth purchasing.

The engineering targets do not answer that question. HELION specifies **300 kWh of storage and 10 kW delivered for 4–5 hours**, all company targets rather than achieved performance ([company website](https://www.centralblue.ai/)). Those numbers need a measured transmission chain, receiver thermal limits, operating geometry, and system mass before they become an advantage over onboard solar and batteries. Nor does the dossier establish a technical discontinuity against Star Catcher, whose existing-array approach could avoid Central Blue’s dedicated-receiver integration burden. Star Catcher’s reported October 1, 2026 demonstration-payload launch makes that comparison concrete; its **$88 million raised** does not establish either company’s economics.

The founders are the strongest reason to keep looking. Brian Wu’s JPL navigation and robotics experience complements Jeewoo Choi’s mechanical engineering and Boeing thermal-testing background. Their prior planetary-robotics collaboration supplies more evidence than two prestigious résumés assembled around a fashionable market ([company biographies](https://www.centralblue.ai/company/)). Autonomy and thermal behavior are relevant disciplines here. That earns modest credit for complementary team formation. The shift from standalone CORE recovery software toward power infrastructure does not establish fast belief revision: the dossier does not explain what evidence caused it or what the founders abandoned.

The strongest objection to passing is that Central Blue fits my revealed willingness to fund difficult physical infrastructure before commercial validation. A nearly completed Seattle prototype and a planned demonstration with Wave Motion are credible places to begin; a two-person pre-seed team should not need flight heritage or revenue. But the dollar buys risk removal only when the experiment has a decisive result. The dossier supplies no power-transfer pass/fail threshold, test cost, or survival branch. The **2027 Blackwing CORE demonstration** tests software, not delivered power. My exceptions for an extraordinary instigator or an overwhelming quantified consequence do not resolve those gaps here. Neither is established in this record.

Economics could kill Central Blue even after the laser works. Customers must retain generation and batteries between deliveries because line of sight interrupts service. The relevant comparison therefore includes the power satellite, launch, replacement, receiver integration, and retained backup capacity against the customer’s original system. There is no disclosed model for that comparison, so economic parity receives the absent-evidence score. I see no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, premature organizational scaling, or founders rejecting contrary data. This is an unproven investment case, without an affirmative misconduct or execution red flag.

I pass with my own money today. My single flip condition is an **independently reviewed design-partner mission study, grounded in measured prototype performance, demonstrating at least 5× more usable payload energy per total system dollar than onboard generation and storage alone**. Include delivery interruptions and backup hardware in that denominator. That result would connect technical capability to customer consequence and give the next check a specific risk to retire.
