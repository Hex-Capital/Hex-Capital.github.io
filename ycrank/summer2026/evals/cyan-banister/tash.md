# tash -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. I can imagine collectors owning liquid portfolios of culturally important cards without researching, authenticating, storing, and reselling each object themselves. But tash has not mapped how we get there. The dossier gives me a $13.51 billion market, a retail-card boom, and a regulated wrapper; it does not identify the intervening unlocks or show why card indexes become a durable behavior rather than another product attached to today’s enthusiasm. A waitlist is not a leak from that future.

The founders are the strongest part of the case. They spent years buying at card shows and tracking prices, report more than $1 million in combined collectible transactions, and previously built an exchange that processed over $22,000 across 710 trades and 250 listed cards. That is real operating tuition. Four longtime friends and roommates also bring finance, data, and marketplace experience from UBS, Houlihan Lokey, J.P. Morgan, Capgemini, and Seso. This does not look like four people selecting a fashionable category from a spreadsheet.

The prior exchange elicited costly behavior: users listed inventory and completed hundreds of trades through a functioning product. But tash is now asking those users to perform a different action—buy shares in a managed, regulated portfolio—and that product has not launched. SEC qualification remains pending, while the privacy policy and terms still describe the former exchange. I make exceptions to missing revenue when the current prototype and institutional proof show that the future has arrived early. Here, the working artifact belongs to the previous model.

There is a genuine nuisance underneath the pitch. Collectors must source, price, authenticate, store, track, and resell assets individually. Yet tash does not quantify the rent embedded in those tasks, identify who captures it, or demonstrate that the investment vehicle returns meaningful control to users. In fact, it introduces Rialto Markets, North Capital, SEC qualification, custody, and uncertain secondary liquidity. Collectable halted secondary trading in 2024; wrapping illiquid cards into a portfolio does not automatically create liquidity.

The sharpest bull case is that these founders lived the problem, already persuaded users to commit cards and money, and understand enough finance to turn a fragmented hobby into boring investment infrastructure. That case stings. It is also undermined by the current architecture: no proprietary asset, structural barrier, fee schedule, or demonstrated liquidity loop; established competitors already provide fractionalization, custody, valuation, and trading. The CL50 chart’s interpolated intermediate points are especially concerning because performance credibility is the product, not decorative marketing.

My 36-month forecast is that tash pivots from regulated multi-card securities to card-portfolio analytics and marketplace tooling, where its founders’ domain knowledge matters without requiring them to manufacture secondary-market liquidity. I pass today. I would flip if tash closes one SEC-qualified multi-card offering with at least $1 million from 250 non-affiliated investors. Until then, the team has proven obsession with cards, but not that this particular wrapper is what’s coming.
