# Allia Health -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A three-person therapy practice no longer behaves like an isolated shop negotiating blind with insurers; it runs on a shared clinical operating system, contributes outcomes to a national group, and gains contracting leverage without surrendering ownership. The leak from that future is not the AI label. It is 8,000 clinicians across 1,000 practices using Allia Health daily, followed by 600 clinicians in 32 states joining one group under signed payer contracts. The causal chain—free system of record, aggregated practices, national contracts, outcome-based care—is unusually legible, although the last and most valuable step has not started producing care or revenue.

Why does a clinician need seven disconnected systems merely to treat someone and get paid? Because records, referrals, prescribing, billing, and payer relationships have accumulated as separate institutional gates. Allia reports returning six hours per clinician each week, while the clinical-group structure could return negotiating agency that small practices lack individually. That is a real operating wound. It is not yet a complete rent map: the dossier does not quantify denied claims, reimbursement improvement, or how much value Allia captures versus clinicians and payers. The $50 million figure is contracted annualized care capacity, not revenue.

The working artifact is the strongest evidence here. Nearly one million monthly visits and 300,000 patients represent repeated, consequential use inside clinical workflows—not a waitlist or an AI demo someone politely tried once. The 600 activated clinicians have also undertaken the administrative work required to join the group before care begins in September 2026. That behavior is costly enough to matter. It makes Allia look less like a fashionable mental-health wrapper and more like concealed coordination infrastructure beneath the visible EHR.

The founder evidence is thinner. Amie Leighton brings healthcare research and M&A exposure; Saroosh Khan brings technical work, including the reported ARC-AGI-2 result and population-health agent; clinical co-founders add practitioner context. But I cannot trace a dated, pre-company obsession through experiments, shipped artifacts, or an operating wound they personally endured. Nor does the dossier explain how the founders met, recruited one another, or divide ultimate authority. Those omissions do not prove opportunism or a governance problem, but they prevent me from underwriting exceptional founder inevitability.

The bear case stings: Headway can already give away an AI-assisted insurance-native EHR, has raised $327.5 million, and can attack the same acquisition wedge with far more capital. Allia is handling protected health information while SOC 2 and HITRUST certification were still in progress, and multi-state Part 2 compliance makes a mistake existential. Its longitudinal data moat remains inferred until claims actually flow. Massive reported usage could therefore become an expensive free-product funnel whose payer economics never clear.

I would still invest, but near the bottom of my invest range. My 36-month forecast is that by August 2029 Allia will generate $20 million–$40 million in annualized net revenue primarily through payer contracts covering at least 1,500 clinicians, rather than through EHR subscriptions. The one verifiable flip condition is claims conversion: if the 600 activated clinicians have not generated paid claims under Allia-held contracts by March 2027, I pass, because the clinical group is then positioning rather than infrastructure.
