# Native -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine a shop owner telling software what kind of business she wants to build, then spending her day with customers while the software finds the audience. Native’s interesting artifact is Norn: ranking ideas before generating them. If production becomes abundant, choosing what deserves to exist becomes more valuable. But that future is my extrapolation. The dossier establishes a selection mechanism; it does not yet establish the transitions from social-post ranking to dependable commercial judgment.

The founders have earned more belief than a newly assembled team chasing marketing AI. They built Wepost together, with NOK80,000 MRR reported in September 2025, before winding it down and starting Native in January 2026. Bekken’s content production, Hauga’s agency operations, and Gråsberg’s machine-learning work put complementary experience around the same operating problem. That is pre-company tuition and evidence they can build together. It does not establish lifelong technical obsession or exceptional recruiting power, but it is considerably more substantial than credentials alone.

People are already giving Native money and permission to speak for their businesses. That second expenditure matters: publishing something under your business’s name puts reputation into the machine. Native reports $2M ARR within six months, more than 2,200 paying customers, and 40,000 posts in an unspecified month. These remain company-reported figures, without retention cohorts, but they describe completed behavior through a working product. The Briggs on Homes comparison covers August 20–October 2, 2026; its engagement improvement is suggestive, while the observational research’s selection and survivorship biases prevent me from treating it as proof that Norn caused better results.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that this useful application could quietly manufacture its own enabling asset. Every approval, rejection, and published outcome could teach Native something about commercial taste that a generation model lacks. That fits my pattern of finding concealed infrastructure inside an ordinary beachhead. Here is where the argument catches: collecting feedback does not establish predictive advantage. Native documents aggregated, anonymized training signals and permits opt-outs, but demonstrates neither a scarce dependency nor superiority that other products must purchase. The Norn API is a waitlist, and Blaze already offers a comparable strategy-through-publishing workflow. A successful subscription product can remain a successful subscription product without becoming infrastructure.

At $79 a month, Native may return meaningful time and operating control to a small-business owner. Still, the dossier identifies no artificial restriction, quantified agency rent, or gatekeeper whose power it removes. My stronger system-repair criterion therefore stays at its missing-evidence default. Privacy deserves attention because brand context and connected accounts are sensitive; anonymization, training opt-outs, and approval controls are constructive evidence, with no documented severe violation here. Bekken is identified as CEO, while voting control is undisclosed. Neither missing governance detail nor reliance on frontier models establishes a red flag by itself.

I land at **Neutral**. Native’s paid usage is the strongest evidence; the weakest link is the claim that its learning layer becomes something downstream products need. My exceptions for pre-revenue technical conviction and extraordinary consumer rituals do not bridge that gap: missing revenue is plainly not Native’s problem, and publishing volume does not validate predictive judgment. One verifiable result would change my decision: a preregistered trial on previously unseen brands showing that Norn-ranked ideas produce at least 20% higher qualified-lead conversion than the same generation and publishing pipeline without Norn. Then I would have an artifact of the future worth underwriting.
