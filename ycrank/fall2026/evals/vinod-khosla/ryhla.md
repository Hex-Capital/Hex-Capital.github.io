# Ryhla -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Ryhla’s consequential opportunity is to make usable grid capacity discoverable before developers waste capital pursuing impossible connections. That could accelerate solar, storage, charging, and computing infrastructure. But screening out projects and connecting more megawatts are different outcomes. The reported **$160,000 in avoided fees across eight rejected battery-project candidates** establishes a concrete local benefit. It does not establish how much additional capacity gets connected, how much faster, or what fraction of infrastructure spending Ryhla could eliminate. I can underwrite that demonstrated benefit. I cannot yet price an option on a quantified transformation of the grid.

The strongest evidence is the reported **91% utility approval rate among model-approved 2025 applications**. These founders have put a model into contact with utility decisions. That matters more than another attractive energy dashboard. But 91% is conditional on which applications the model accepts. Without sample size, the approval rate under existing screening, and the viable sites incorrectly rejected, it cannot establish superior decision-making. Reject almost everything and precision can look excellent. Paces already offers interconnection reports and power-flow studies; Ryhla supplies no measured cost, speed, or accuracy advantage against that alternative. There is technical evidence here, but no demonstrated 5–10x discontinuity.

The bull case deserves attention. Both founders reportedly researched grid models at Stanford and initially deployed them with utilities; the Midwest developer deployment suggests that work can reach a commercial workflow. Proprietary distribution-grid data could turn each application outcome into better predictions for the next project. This fits my unstated preference for scientific workflows that become repeatable platforms compatible with installed infrastructure. Yet data rights and measurable learning effects remain unverified. Polytechnique, Stanford, and UCSF demonstrate relevant quantitative preparation; they do not demonstrate belief revision or recruiting complementary people against specific risks. My exceptions for an extraordinary instigator attacking a quantified giant value pool cannot be established from this record.

The largest kill risk is whether Ryhla’s predictions transfer economically across utilities with different data, engineering practices, and approval rules. An accurate model for one territory could still require expensive reconstruction for the next. The dossier contains no prospective test, numerical transfer threshold, experiment budget, or stop condition. The completed deployments earn technical credit; they do not separately prove disciplined risk removal per dollar. I would spend the next dollars testing transferability before expanding the interconnection-management operation.

The economic question is equally specific: how much engineering and application-management labor does each successful connection require? Customer fees avoided are not Ryhla revenue, gross profit, or proof of a scalable cost advantage. Pricing and delivery costs are undisclosed, so unsubsidized competitiveness remains unestablished. Enverus’s combination of energy data and acquired simulation software makes this consequential: a separate provider needs an advantage large enough to survive bundling. There is no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, exit-first behavior, or a generic model wrapper. Those red flags should not be invented.

**I pass with my own money today.** Ryhla has a credible technical foothold in consequential infrastructure, but the evidence currently supports project selection more strongly than infrastructure acceleration. My verifiable condition for reconsidering the pass is one independently audited, prospective trial across three previously unseen utility territories showing **at least 5x lower fully loaded diligence cost per ultimately approved project than existing practice, without reducing viable-project discovery**. Include engineering and service labor. That result would establish whether the model removes a bottleneck economically across territories.
