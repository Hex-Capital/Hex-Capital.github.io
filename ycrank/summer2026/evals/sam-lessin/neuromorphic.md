# Neuromorphic -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first three cells in my underwriting sheet are blank: proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely the first scarce check. YC’s $500,000 establishes financing, not price discipline. At pre-seed that silence is ordinary, but for my money it remains a gate: I cannot distinguish a cheap experiment from another fashionable robotics round whose doubt has already been bid away.

The team is the strongest evidence. Vatan has worked across PickNik, Rapyuta, KABAM, and Augmentus; Ege researches contact-rich manipulation and policy generalization; collectively, the founders report experience deploying more than 1,000 robots. More importantly, Neuromorphic crossed from robotics expertise into actual wet-lab operations: one biotechnology customer signed within five days of the strategy change and reached daily robot use within a week. That is a real outsider-insider bridge, not résumé inference.

The contract also gives Neuromorphic more than a demo. A customer allowed its workflow to be encoded through reusable navigation, manipulation, inspection, and tool-use skills and then operated the system daily. That is observed purchase behavior around an embedded workflow. But one deployment is not yet a proprietary dark pool. There are no disclosed intervention rates, safety results, cross-body benchmarks, or accumulated data advantage, while FieldAI, Skild AI, Physical Intelligence, and Generalist AI have raised more than $4 billion collectively. If the model layer commoditizes, the defensible asset must become customer-specific execution data and operational integration—not “embodiment agnostic” intelligence as a slogan.

The missing venture moment is more serious. The dossier never says what the next check will prove, by what date, or against what pass/fail metric. I can invent the obvious experiment—reproduce the one-week deployment across several wet labs—but that is my underwriting, not the founders’ capital plan. The prior $5,000–$10,000 monthly Robotics-as-a-Service pricing is useful price discovery, yet current revenue, hardware cost, monitoring burden, gross margin, burn, and runway are undisclosed. Delete the Series A and I cannot tell whether Neuromorphic becomes a profitable automation business or a deployment-engineering organization subsidizing every robot.

The strongest bull case is the speed of the pivot: a robotics-native team changed focus, signed a customer in five days, and put robots into daily operation within one week. That can be the beginning of the same mechanism that justified the AI exception in Ando—software becoming valuable because measurable enterprise operation already works. The material difference is that Ando supplied accuracy, conversion, retention, and a year of operating evidence; Neuromorphic supplies one contract and no reliability or cash metric. Physical AI errors compound in the customer’s laboratory, so this gap cannot be waved away as ordinary missing pre-seed history.

I am passing today, narrowly. My 36-month base case is that Neuromorphic abandons the general robot-brain positioning, concentrates on one or two wet-lab workflows, and reaches roughly $500,000–$2 million of recurring revenue as an integration-heavy automation business. One verifiable condition flips me: a sub-$10 million post-money round whose proceeds fund a six-month test in which two additional independent wet labs each pay at least $5,000 per robot per month for 90 days, with greater than 50% gross margin and no more than one human intervention per 100 workflow cycles. That would turn the round from an AI story into a finite, cheap truth-revealing experiment.
