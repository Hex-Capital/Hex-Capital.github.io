# GodHands -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most useful fact about GodHands is that someone left the founders’ previous employer and work broke for months. That is a plausible place to find a startup idea. Both founders worked at Graviton, so they encountered the problem from inside an organization rather than inventing it from a market map. But the departure explains why they care. It does not yet show what they did when blocked, how quickly they did it, or whether their workaround repaired the damage. That distinction matters when I am investing in the people.

There is some evidence of something built. GodHands describes a desktop application that observes work across applications and produces sourced playbooks. An anonymous support leader says it documented previously undocumented escalations within two weeks. I give the technical proof limited credit: this is a specific claimed result, but available through indexed website text, without an inspectable deployment or accuracy measurement. Aditya’s low-latency systems work and research implementation make technical competence credible. They do not establish that observing screen activity captures the reasoning behind an exception. The disagreement between agent-execution marketing and read-only security descriptions leaves the more ambitious capability unproven.

Support escalations could be the narrow well. A new employee who cannot resolve a customer’s problem needs the missing procedure immediately. The testimonial supplies modest evidence that someone used the product for this job; it does not establish repeated use, payment, or dependence. GodHands’ 504 LinkedIn followers establish none of those things. The strongest bull case is that these two technical founders have found an ugly, recurring problem and can sit beside support teams until the product becomes indispensable. Employee anxiety, sensitive information, and application boundaries supply plenty of work that competitors might prefer to avoid. That is a credible opportunity, although the dossier has not shown the founders winning those battles.

The change from engineering-agent memory in August 2026 to broader operations by October could represent useful learning. It could also represent a change in vocabulary. Without the observation that caused it, I cannot award evidence-driven adaptability. Similarly, their shared Graviton background reduces concern about cofounders assembled solely for an application, but does not establish a history of handling conflict together. The launch asks for demos and introductions; it does not disclose dependence on a major partner or premature delegation of sales. Those red flags are unestablished. My unstated preference for programmable infrastructure already appropriated by technical users is also unfulfilled: GodHands has described an agent knowledge layer, but has not documented developers building unexpected workflows on it.

Scribe’s reported $100 million ARR shows that organizations pay for adjacent workflow products. It is not evidence that they will choose GodHands. The founders need to discover which exception their product captures so much better that a team keeps using it despite an incumbent’s overlapping features. Neither the working-prototype nor independently validated-proof exceptions justify overriding my founder-action gate on this record, and the anonymous testimonial is too limited to establish unmistakable pull. I would pass at 38. This is missing evidence about the decisive behavior, rather than evidence of passive founders. One verifiable condition would reverse my decision: a dated customer deployment showing an employee-trust or workflow-capture blocker, the founders’ shipped workaround within weeks, and the same support team subsequently using the resulting playbooks every week for eight weeks.
