# Streamline Systems -- Elad Gil Evaluation

GPS interference creates a buyer for navigation that works without satellites; Streamline Systems becomes a large business only if reliable positioning is cheap enough to deploy across expendable aircraft. That is the controlling belief. The company reports satellite-independent autonomous flight and GPS handoff, which gives the technical premise some substance. But a successful flight does not establish the operating envelope: accuracy, drift, mission duration and delivered cost remain undisclosed. I give the demonstration partial credit without assuming it solves the economic problem.

The strongest bull case is the product’s insertion point. Streamline reports integrations with ArduPilot, INAV and PX4, plus operation without an RF signature. If those capabilities survive operational testing, a platform manufacturer could adopt the module without replacing its flight-control stack. That is a plausible distribution mechanism across multiple aircraft. The integrations earn architectural credit; qualification costs, accumulated flight data and durable customer dependence remain prospective. Better underlying sensing technology could strengthen this product, but the undisclosed architecture prevents assessing who captures that improvement.

The market need is clearer than the market expansion. The March 2026 EASA–EUROCONTROL action plan supports demand for interference-resilient avionics; it does not demonstrate a new cost curve. The dossier’s third-party estimate puts this category at $154.38 million in 2025 and forecasts $386.10 million in 2035. Those figures do not establish a large standalone outcome, and adjacent markets cannot simply be added. Advanced Navigation and ANELLO already commercialize alternative architectures. Streamline needs an economic advantage over those suppliers, rather than merely participation in the same adoption wave. Without pricing, manufacturing costs or comparative performance, the technology-shift dimension stays at its absent-evidence default.

Signed LOIs and scheduled government trials are useful pre-seed signals. They suggest someone wants to test the product, although the company-reported recruitment snippet supplies no counterparties, quantities or contractual commitments. I assign modest demand credit without treating scheduled trials as completed deployments. The strongest objection to passing is that this may be precisely when the opportunity is cheapest: flying hardware, existing controller integrations and prospective military users could precede a rapid qualification cycle. The unresolved issue is whether that cycle produces repeat purchases at attractive economics. The recruitment listing explicitly identifies hardening, productization and manufacturability as unfinished work.

Poraj’s reported Cambridge departure is evidence of commitment, and his prior agency establishes some entrepreneurial experience; neither establishes lessons from scaling a hardware company. Jessett’s RAF reserve affiliation supplies relevant exposure without proving procurement access. Their planned engineering hires have concrete development tasks, which fits funding proof before broad expansion. My physical-market exception nevertheless requires evidence that technology changes incumbent economics. The dossier also supplies neither the documented frontier research and rare technical team needed for a research-stage override nor evidence that incumbents have abandoned the category. Those exceptions do not resolve the central gap.

I would pass on a personal check today. Streamline Systems has a credible product insertion point and early customer interest, but its market thesis depends on an unmeasured performance-to-cost advantage. My single flip condition is a verified paid repeat production order from a platform manufacturer, supported by acceptance results showing mission-grade GPS-denied navigation at a fully loaded module cost compatible with that customer’s expendable aircraft. That would connect the working prototype to a repeatable business.
