# Hubble -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders know this problem through action. Prabha Dublish was Grow Therapy’s first product hire and worked across telehealth, integrations, and AI care; Aaron Leon built Amazon Care’s data platform and One Medical’s EHR and AI infrastructure for more than one million patients. Their decade-long working relationship also reduces ordinary two-founder coordination risk. Forty production tools across Epic, Cerner, athenahealth, UnitedHealthcare, and Aetna are shipped artifacts, not résumé claims. Founder-specific knowledge is Hubble’s strongest asset.

The product turns institutional fragmentation into one API by combining formal exchange networks with browser and voice agents. Source traceability, deterministic identifiers, and per-call audit logs make the system operationally credible. But copy the code and much of the advantage disappears. Hubble does not yet prove exclusive data, a proprietary model, contractual access, or retrieval histories that improve completion rates with each deployment. Particle Health, 1upHealth, Health Gorilla, and PicnicHealth have raised between $49 million and more than $100 million; they can finance adjacent retrieval methods. Forty integrations are useful infrastructure, but integration labor is not automatically a compounding asset.

The bull case is stronger than the financing evidence suggests. A dental group reportedly recovered $340,000 in denied claims in 90 days, another platform improved estimate accuracy from 50% to 94%, and a legal customer reduced paralegal work from hours to minutes. Those are concrete customer outcomes across healthcare operations and legal technology. The same retrieval capability can extend into care delivery, life sciences, claims, and patient-data products inside an interoperability market projected to grow from $3.4 billion in 2023 to $8.57 billion by 2030. This is a wedge with room to maneuver.

But value delivered is not value captured. The customers are anonymous, B2B pricing is unpublished, and the patient-facing Individual Access Service is free. Hubble has not disclosed revenue, renewals, retrieval volume, gross margins, or what proportion of difficult requests still requires human intervention. Browser and voice automation operating across portals, phone systems, consent rules, and release queues may become a scalable data layer—or a software façade over recurring operations.

The deeper weakness is that customers do not yet create value or distribution for one another. Every organization appears to arrive through direct demo outreach, and every retrieval depends on systems Hubble does not control. This is centralized orchestration rather than a permissionless network. I can override a missing network at pre-seed when a small team has created a distinctive product whose advantage compounds through taste or proprietary learning. The two-person team and 40 tools make that exception plausible, but the dossier does not show the compounding mechanism.

I am neutral today. The regulatory boundaries are manageable but real: protected records require separate authorizations, direct Individual Access Services are not legally subject to HIPAA, and even the published legal workflow took one week. There is no evidence of unfair founder conduct, artificial deadlines, or other integrity red flags; the problem is structural, not personal. My flip condition is verifiable evidence that retrieval history compounds—later cohorts achieving materially higher automated completion rates from proprietary workflow data, rather than additional integrations and human operations. Without that, Hubble can build a valuable healthcare workflow company but not yet the nonlinear asset I would fund with my own money.
