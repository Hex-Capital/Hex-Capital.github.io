# The Subvocal Company -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Silent input could remove a real constraint on computing: people cannot speak freely everywhere they need to work. The Subvocal Company proposes to remove that constraint through physiological decoding. That is a consequential technical possibility. But the dossier does not quantify how much work becomes possible, whose access changes, or what spending disappears. I cannot turn “talk to your computer without talking” into a 100x consequence by supplying my own market assumptions.

The strongest evidence is the company-reported continuous-speech prototype exceeding 200 words per minute. That makes this worth examining. It does not establish useful throughput: the earlier prototype reportedly achieved 45% accuracy, and the dossier supplies no current accuracy, correction burden, or evaluation protocol. Those figures describe different stages and cannot be combined into a performance claim. AlterEgo already pursues silent physiological speech decoding; Subtle offers whisper dictation. Subvocal needs to demonstrate an advantage under conditions where silence matters. Withholding the sensing architecture may protect intellectual property, but secrecy itself proves no technical advantage.

The immediate kill risk is whether decoding survives a different person, another day, and a shifted device. The founders explicitly identify those problems. Their proposed short calibration is a mitigation to test, not evidence that generalization works. Approximately $1.3 million in reported backing can buy meaningful experiments, but the public record gives no calibration threshold, test budget, deadline, or stop condition. Recruiting heads of ML, sensing, and hardware maps sensibly to the engineering work. Open positions do not establish that capital is removing the largest risk first, and they do not establish premature scaling either.

The bull case deserves weight. Yadav works on sensing hardware and firmware; Kumar has shipped a consumer application reaching more than 80,000 users. Yadav’s separate GreenSat venture reportedly exceeded $2 million in annual revenue. These are builders with evidence of execution, and the prototype could be the beginning of a genuinely new input modality. My pattern of backing difficult scientific workflows that become repeatable platforms is relevant here. However, the proprietary learning loop remains inferred, and neither founder’s record documents the belief revision or complementary recruits this particular problem demands. My exceptions for precommercial technology require an unusually compelling consequence or an established giant value pool paired with exceptional instigation. This dossier has not established that override.

Consumer hardware adds another independent uncertainty. Planned pre-orders establish neither willingness to pay nor a route to affordable production. There is no disclosed price, bill of materials, yield assumption, or support burden. That earns the absent-evidence economics score; there is no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence. Likewise, this is a substantive sensing problem, so the generic AI-wrapper red flag does not apply. The investment gap is specific: technical promise has not yet become measured capability, quantified consequence, or a cost model.

I pass on the evidence available. Failure risk alone would not deter me; an unpriced consequence and unmeasured capability do. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independently observed wearable trial achieving at least 150 correct words per minute at no more than 10% word error rate across previously unseen users and repeat sessions, with no more than five minutes of calibration per session. Those are proposed decision thresholds, not company results. That experiment would tell me whether Subvocal is purchasing a new computing capability with each engineering dollar.
