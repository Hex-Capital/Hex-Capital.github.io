# Silica -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Silica puts measurement where the robot touches the world. That could matter: motor current is an indirect estimate of contact force, and software cannot recover information the hardware never measures reliably. But the investment question is how much useful physical work becomes cheaper when that measurement improves. The dossier supplies an $18.62 billion actuator-market estimate, not a quantified reduction in robot cost, training requirements, or task failures. I cannot price an extraordinary outcome from a component-market forecast.

The technical question is sharper than “does output-side sensing work?” HEBI and Sensodrive already sell torque-sensing joints. Silica must establish what those products cannot deliver economically: sensitivity, bandwidth, durability, or cost at a materially different level. Its specifications remain design targets, and comparative prices are unavailable. The measurement location provides an intelligible engineering approach. It does not establish a 5–10x discontinuity. That distinction is the center of my pass.

The strongest argument against passing is Enrico Milletti himself. Materials engineering at Berkeley, mechanical engineering, manufacturing work at Apple, and founding-engineer experience at Artefact are relevant preparation for turning a difficult component into a manufacturable product. My actual investing pattern includes physical infrastructure and repeatable production platforms; Silica belongs in that search space. A cheap, dependable actuator could enable many robot builders rather than require Silica to build every robot. But manufacturing experience does not yet demonstrate rapid belief revision or recruiting against specific technical weaknesses. Nor does a plausible enabling role quantify the downstream consequence. The exceptional cases that justify overlooking early milestones require unusually strong evidence of consequence or an evolving platform opportunity. This dossier does not establish either override.

December 2026 prototypes followed by January 2027 production give me dates, but little information about risk removed per dollar. The first existential question is whether output sensing retains its advantage under temperature variation, gearbox friction, repeated loading, and wear. A production date supplies no acceptance threshold, experiment budget, or response to failure. The compressed schedule deserves examination; it does not prove the red flag of spending on scale before resolving technical risk. Preorders being open also establishes a sales channel, not orders or customer validation. I assign no penalty for having no disclosed revenue at this stage.

Economics could change the conclusion decisively. “Affordable” needs a bill of materials, calibration labor, yield assumptions, and a comparison with an equivalent commercial joint. None is disclosed, so unsubsidized parity remains unknown rather than disproven. My one verifiable flip condition is a customer-witnessed, repeatable comparison showing that Silica matches a specified HEBI joint’s torque-sensing performance and operating life at one-fifth the delivered cost, supported by pilot-build costs and yields. That would connect the engineering mechanism to an economic discontinuity. Until then, I pass with my own money: the physical bottleneck is credible, but the extraordinary option remains unsubstantiated.
