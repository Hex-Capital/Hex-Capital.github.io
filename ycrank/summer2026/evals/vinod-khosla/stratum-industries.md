# Stratum Industries -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Millions of permit, patent, benefit, and drug-approval applications sit in queues lasting months or years, making **Stratum Industries** consequence-rich but miracle-poor. At maximum ambition, **Stratum Industries** breaks the economic scarcity of government adjudicator attention: approvals become continuous computation rather than bureaucratic inventory. That is a meaningful world-state change. The analytical signature is unusually high consequence paired with unusually low technical risk—the mission resembles labor displacement, while the product currently resembles model orchestration.

**OpenAI** (2019) is the high rung on consequence because success meant redirecting civilization, not improving one workflow. **Stratum Industries** shares the load-bearing mechanism—replacing scarce cognitive labor with machines—and government decisions influence trillions of dollars in construction, medicine, intellectual property, and benefits. **Stratum Industries** diverges because the dossier identifies millions of delayed applications but no quantified economic consequence, no incumbent adjudication system that disappears, and no ambition beyond clearing queues. OpenAI threatened knowledge work itself. Stratum currently threatens waiting time.

**DoorDash** (2013) sits on the low rung for technical risk: no scientific miracle, no capital-hostile research program, and execution that any generalist could understand. **Stratum Industries** exhibits the same low-rung mechanism without DoorDash’s demonstrated consumer-distribution exception. Uploading an application and rubric, routing work among models, requesting missing information, and producing cited summaries are useful engineering, but ClearOS already describes nearly the same workflow and Salesforce Agentforce already carries FedRAMP-authorized distribution. **Stratum Industries** discloses no proprietary model, accuracy breakthrough, deployment architecture, or accumulated decision dataset. Human approval reduces liability; it does not create a moat.

Raj Jagiasi averaged 220% of quota at Deel, while Vihaan Jagiasi was founding engineer number three at Cadastral before its acquisition by Legora. Over the next 18 months, founder defiance compounds only if **Stratum Industries** converts outsider status into a thesis government experts reject—for example, proving that general-purpose agents can outperform category-specific adjudication systems without sacrificing due process. Otherwise, **Stratum Industries** compounds the opposite mechanism: no procurement experience means every security review, appeals process, and agency integration becomes newly discovered friction. Dropping out of Bath and Waterloo demonstrates risk tolerance. It does not demonstrate contradiction of documented expert consensus.

Vihaan Jagiasi generated more than 1,000 Perplexity Pro sign-ups at Waterloo within 48 hours, but distribution improvisation is not evidence of a prior thesis update. **Stratum Industries** would move up a full tier on learning rate if Raj and Vihaan documented that broad adjudication failed, explained why, and concentrated **Stratum Industries** on one category where case outcomes proved a different product architecture was necessary. Cadastral’s acquisition demonstrates that Vihaan can ship inside a successful trajectory; the dossier contains no pivot, rejected hypothesis, or “we were wrong” moment. I apply the absent-evidence default with a small credit for rapid execution.

**Commonwealth Fusion** (2019) required a $115M Series A because the technical roadmap itself demanded capital depth. **Stratum Industries** presents the inverse profile: two founders, no disclosed proprietary infrastructure, no certifications, no deployments, and an apparent dependency on external foundation models. **Stratum Industries** may eventually spend heavily on FedRAMP authorization, secure environments, integrations, and government sales, but those are commercialization costs rather than falsifiable scientific milestones. I am over-resourced for commodity SaaS economics. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself” does not mean make ordinary software expensive through procurement.

Salesforce’s $41.5B FY2026 revenue and existing Government Cloud distribution make my 36-month forecast blunt: by July 2029, **Stratum Industries** will have pivoted from broad government adjudication into private-sector insurance claims automation and will remain below $2M ARR. **Stratum Industries** has enough sales and engineering ability to find a narrower workflow, but insufficient technical differentiation to defeat Salesforce inside installed accounts or ClearOS in reviewer-side adjudication. Consequence without a proprietary mechanism usually retreats toward the customer who can buy fastest.

GovWell’s 130-plus government customers across 34 states prove agencies will buy modern workflow software, and the ninefold increase in reported federal generative-AI use cases from 2023 to 2024 is the strongest bull case. Raj’s 220% quota performance plus Vihaan’s acquired-startup experience could make **Stratum Industries** a DoorDash-style known exception: no science miracle, but exceptional execution turns an unglamorous operational wedge into essential infrastructure. The objection stings because government distribution can itself become a moat. The dossier still gives **Stratum Industries** no customer, pilot, security credential, processing volume, or differentiation from ClearOS, while Salesforce already owns authorized deployment rails. A signed multi-year federal or state contract processing at least 100,000 cases annually under a measurable accuracy SLA—and generating proprietary audited outcome data—would flip my verdict.
