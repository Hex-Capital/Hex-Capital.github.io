# Agent FM -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Agent FM exists because its founders encountered the problem and shipped through it. Aniket Deshpande brought software-engineering experience from Meta; the two-person team built passive detection for Claude Code and Codex, launched on macOS, and then extended it to Windows 10 and 11. That is action-revealed knowledge, not merely a fashionable claim about agents. But the dossier does not show repeat usage, customer outcomes, or prior work supervising large agent fleets. They have demonstrated proximity and execution, not yet exceptional specific knowledge.

The product’s strongest feature is also its structural weakness. Observing sessions without wrapping them reduces compatibility risk and makes adoption easier across existing tools. Yet if OpenAI copies the audio summary, Conductor adds ambient narration, or Omnara improves its voice controls, the code leaves behind no identified scarce asset. The proposed corpus of failures, decisions, and workflow data is still hypothetical. A future data moat is not a present moat; data compounds only when usage is proprietary, repeated, and converted into a better product.

There is a real expanding substrate underneath the wedge. OpenAI reported more than one million Codex developers and usage doubling from mid-December to February 2026, while the broader code-assistant market reached an estimated $8.51 billion in 2025. But “AI code assistants” is a parent market, not Agent FM’s causal expansion path. The company has not yet shown how an audio command center becomes the system of record for engineering work, production-agent operations, or enterprise governance. Without that path, this may remain a feature attached to somebody else’s platform.

The bull case is sharper than the current score: 809 LinkedIn followers, a self-serve free tier, cross-vendor support, and two desktop operating systems are meaningful output for a two-person pre-seed team. The strange interface may also create behavior that conventional dashboards miss—developers listening to agents while doing other work. But none of those users currently creates utility or distribution for the next user. This is a company producing summaries for customers, not yet a permissionless system accumulating value while it sleeps.

Pricing is legible but unvalidated. Starter at $48 annually and Pro at $144 annually establish a direct-value hypothesis, while Teams offers centralized billing, SSO/SAML, and privacy controls. The paid plans are waitlisted, however, and the dossier names no payer, completed transaction, or recurring revenue. Enterprise privacy requirements may create demand, but sending session context to Gemini or OpenAI can simultaneously block the buyer.

I see no founder-integrity, artificial-deadline, or reassurance-seeking red flag. The pass comes from the substrate, not the people. Early products can deserve an exception before monetization or network effects appear, but only when the prototype reveals an unusually durable product language or intense user behavior; public evidence shows availability, not obsession. My one flip condition is verifiable: I would reopen if Agent FM reaches 100 paying teams whose retained cross-session data measurably improves blocker detection or agent guidance beyond what Codex and Conductor can reproduce from their own telemetry.
