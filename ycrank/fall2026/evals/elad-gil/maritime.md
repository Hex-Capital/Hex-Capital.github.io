# Maritime -- Elad Gil Evaluation

OpenClaw and personal agents create a new buyer: startups that need a persistent computer for each customer’s agent. Maritime’s investment case turns on whether it can deliver that persistence at attractive margins while becoming the developer’s default operating layer. The pricing makes the question concrete: $100 per month buys 100 agent slots, subject to concurrency limits. A slot is not a continuously running computer. The controlling variable is the cost of serving a retained agent at its actual activity level. The dossier establishes the subscription structure, but provides no workload economics or evidence of a threshold where this becomes an attractive business.

Snapshot-based sleep/wake is a plausible mechanism for making that model work. Maritime also documents persistent storage, encrypted secrets, and dashboard, CLI, API, and SDK access. These are substantive product capabilities. Better models could make agents perform more useful work while Maritime continues to hold their files and execution state. That is the strongest part of this company: there is an operational layer beyond model output. But persistent infrastructure does not automatically confer ownership of the customer’s workflow, and documented functionality does not establish migration costs or a proprietary operating advantage.

The market is already crystallizing around well-funded suppliers. Docker announced managed microVM cloud sandboxes on September 24, 2026; Daytona announced a $60 million Series B on October 20. Maritime’s flat subscription is a different purchasing experience, but the dossier does not quantify lower customer costs, improved delivery speed, or better infrastructure margins. The OpenClaw catalyst establishes a reason to build now; it does not yet establish why this supplier captures the value. Competition alone does not invalidate the product, but it makes the missing winner mechanism consequential.

The strongest counterargument is that the founders encountered this exact problem while hosting hundreds of research agents at MIT and have built the persistent layer they needed. Maria Gorskikh and Aryaman Shrivastava’s NANDA work gives that argument substance; Open Instinct, built using Maritime, provides a concrete example of the product’s intended use. This fits my pattern of backing infrastructure across a technology discontinuity, and firsthand need earns more weight than their institutional affiliations. However, Open Instinct’s 416 stars and Product Hunt’s 253 followers do not demonstrate independent customers repeatedly choosing Maritime. The research-stage exception does not resolve that gap: the dossier establishes relevant engineering experience, without evidence of scarce frontier research or a technical breakthrough that would justify overriding commercial uncertainty.

I would pass with my own money today. Maritime has built something useful, but I cannot yet connect its strongest asset—persistent execution state—to durable distribution and attractive economics. This is not an allegation of failed adoption, a vanity project, or an unsupported proprietary-data claim; those red flags are not established. My single flip condition is a verifiable 90-day cohort of independent paying customers whose persistent agent fleets expand while producing positive contribution margins after compute, storage, and support. That result would connect the product architecture to both customer pull and the economic variable that matters.
