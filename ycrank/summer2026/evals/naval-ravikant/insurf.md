# Insurf -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The useful signal is not that Chang Lu is in Brown’s BS/MD program or that Bryan Chung has two IEEE publications. It is that two students turned clinical and technical knowledge into Inveto, an operating workflow now being piloted by specialty practices. The product preserves policy snapshots, submitted text, physician attestations, payer responses, and payment evidence. That is specific knowledge expressed through action. The missing piece is proof that either founder previously endured prior authorization from inside a practice or has produced measurable pilot outcomes. They have entered the problem; they have not yet demonstrated mastery of it.

Insurf’s product is more complete than a generic letter-writing agent. Deterministic matching, source-cited drafting, physician review, staff-controlled submission, and outcome tracking address the administrative failures documented in the 2026 study of LLM-generated authorization letters. But completeness is not durability. A competitor copying the workflow still has access to the same insurer channels and improving models. The audit trail could become proprietary payer-policy and outcome data, but selected, non-random pilots with undisclosed case volume do not yet establish a compounding asset. Code records data; scale and exclusive learning make the data a moat.

The market is real but the expansion path is under-specified. Providers average roughly 40 authorization requests and 13 staff-hours each week, denial-management software was valued at $1.32 billion in 2025, and CMS requirements create a transition toward electronic workflows through January 2027. Yet a growing niche is not automatically a huge market. Insurf needs to show how the same decision infrastructure expands from authorization and appeals into a larger provider revenue cycle or payer transaction layer. Otherwise, it enters a defined category against Silna, Anterior, Waystar, and Cohere, which already processes more than 12 million requests annually.

The strongest bull case is the capital-efficient-prototype exception: a two-person team has built a careful product, secured early pilots, and designed around physician responsibility before production PHI is enabled. The bear case still wins today. SOC 2 is unfinished, BAAs and launch gates block production PHI, CoverMyMeds and athenaOne control important interfaces, and direct pilot sales create no user-to-user distribution loop. Every new practice appears to require another integration, sale, and compliance relationship. That is software-plus-service leverage, not a permissionless network.

There is no disclosed revenue, customer count, price, or completed recovery economics. Contractual fee credits and managed reports suggest someone may pay, so this is slightly better than a purely hypothetical business model, but an inferred contract structure is not direct value capture. The absence of revenue is normal at pre-seed; the absence of a demonstrated causal loop from submission to recovered dollars is still the central commercial uncertainty.

I see no evidence of the behavioral red flags that would veto the founders—no unfair transaction, manufactured deadline, or reassurance-seeking. I also see none of the unstated patterns that would override the weak moat: Insurf does not yet turn fragmented participants into price-discoverable supply, let outsiders contribute useful work, or let each customer improve the product for the next in a measurable way. I pass now. One verifiable condition flips the decision: twelve months of payer-level outcome data showing at least 20 production practices, recurring paid usage, and materially improving appeal success or staff-hours saved as the case corpus grows.
