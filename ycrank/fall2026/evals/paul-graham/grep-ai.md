# Grep AI -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most revealing fact about Grep AI is that its agents became expensive enough to hurt. Asver describes nearly $80,000 in monthly AI spending and loss-making runs, followed by a harness redesign that reduced costs. That is a specific obstacle followed by an engineering response. It is the right kind of founder evidence. But the account comes through a search snippet, without elapsed time or measured savings. I give it meaningful, provisional credit. I cannot yet tell how quickly these founders move when something breaks.

Their reason for encountering the problem is much clearer. Asver and Ríos worked together at Brex beginning in 2020, and their experience included automating risk and compliance operations. The backlog of 2,000 businesses awaiting manual verification gives the problem a concrete origin. These founders have spent years inside the work they propose to automate. Encoding review policies, handling exceptions, and obtaining governance approval are precisely the unpleasant tasks that make an apparently obvious product difficult to build. This is Grep AI’s strongest evidence.

There is also a functioning system. IG’s deployment incorporated its review criteria and underwent months of shadow testing against live cases before approval. That earns technical credit independently of Grep’s claim that agents improve themselves. The customer results supply a separate demand signal: automated screening rose from 10% to 30%, and flagged-customer activation fell from two or three days to under ten minutes. Those changes explain why an operations team would keep using the product. The reported accuracy above 99% remains a customer testimonial, rather than an independently reproduced benchmark, but this is substantial evidence for an early company.

The difficult question is why that experience led to a broader agent platform. Parcha began with compliance in 2023; Grep opened its research preview in December 2025 and attracted hundreds of users within two weeks. The January explanation points to applying coding-agent techniques outside programming. That establishes a change of direction. It does not establish which customer observation required it. I see no rejected hypothesis followed by a measured improvement from the new route. My forecast is that, in three years, financial-services compliance will still generate most of Grep AI’s revenue. Its demonstrated advantage lies in understanding that difficult work.

The strongest objection to my hesitation is that the founders have already done what most agent companies merely promise. They have deployed into regulated operations, report customers including Airwallex and Worldpay, and have an established working relationship. The working-product exception deserves consideration. But the evidence does not yet justify overriding my founder-action threshold. My recurring preference for programmable infrastructure also needs actual developer use; API access and AgentRun’s 411 stars establish availability and interest, not habitual dependence. There is no demonstrated dependence on a ceremonial launch or one indispensable partner. The sharper risk is that expansion puts Grep alongside n8n and Gumloop before it has shown that its narrow advantage transfers.

I would hold off on writing a personal check at **63/100**. Grep AI has unusually credible problem origins and real deployment evidence. What remains unresolved is whether its founders can repeatedly turn expensive exceptions into economical procedures. The $200 and $500 subscription plans do not answer that question, and current margins are undisclosed. One verifiable result would flip me to Invest: a dated deployment record showing the founders taking a loss-making customer workflow to positive contribution margin within 30 days while preserving its accuracy and continued customer use.
