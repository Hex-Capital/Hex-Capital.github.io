# 83 Sciences -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely the first scarce check. YC affiliation and three active executive-level hires could indicate a financing process already organized around momentum, but the dossier supplies no terms or auction evidence. I therefore cannot tell whether I am buying an unpopular materials thesis cheaply or paying a consensus AI price for it. That is not administrative missing data; it is the first underwriting gate.

The proposed venture moment is also undefined. There is a potentially sharp experiment here—whether unpublished failed runs can predict synthesis conditions that outperform a lab’s known plateau—but 83 Sciences does not connect round proceeds to a dated number of replications, a named performance threshold, or a pass/fail decision. Hiring an AI engineer, a sales leader, and a full-stack engineer while offering partners grants of up to $50,000 funds several simultaneous goals. That looks closer to building a capital-consuming organization than flipping one truth-revealing card.

The dark pool is the real argument. An anonymous battery lab supplied historical experiments; Dalton proposed revised synthesis conditions that exceeded the lab’s prior capacity plateau and produced a manuscript in under two months. That is materially better than follower counts or “dozens of partnerships in progress”: proprietary workflow data caused an observed technical result. But the access may belong to each laboratory rather than to 83 Sciences. Without cross-partner data rights, retention, or foreground-IP terms, every engagement can reset into expensive bespoke ingestion. The company then owns a services workflow, not a compounding corpus.

The team is unusually well assembled for that translation problem. Eric Riesel brings experimental chemistry and materials-modeling work, Ian Naccarella has operated around battery-materials commercialization at Sila, and Yankang Yang led enterprise AI across a 30,000-person firm. Together they span laboratories and enterprise deployment, although the dossier names no prior product, exit, or scaled materials system they shipped end to end. Ian’s CEO title partially answers the equal-founder committee problem; it does not establish who has final authority when scientific publication, customer IP, and platform learning conflict.

The strongest bull case is that the battery result is an early version of the exception that justified Ando: AI sitting on a functioning enterprise workflow rather than hoping model quality becomes a business. The shared mechanism is measurable improvement inside customer operations. The material difference is that Ando had greater than 90% forecast accuracy plus complete pilot-to-paid conversion and retention; 83 Sciences has one anonymous result, no named customer, no revenue, and no repeat-use evidence. Meanwhile MatterGen, OMat24, Citrine, and heavily financed competitors commoditize the public model layer. The unpublished-data advantage must therefore appear in contracts and cash, not merely in technical possibility.

My base case is that within 36 months 83 Sciences becomes a services-heavy materials-data integration vendor with less than $2 million ARR, rather than a discovery platform with a cross-customer learning advantage. There is no disclosed price, gross margin, burn, or route to break-even without another round, so I am passing at present. One verifiable condition flips me: a named industrial laboratory signs and renews a paid contract whose disclosed data-rights terms let 83 Sciences reuse learned representations across customers and whose contribution margin is positive before subsidies.
