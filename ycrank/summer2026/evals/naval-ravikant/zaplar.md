# Zaplar -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is what the founders learned by doing. Douglas built event-booking software for his family’s hotel, Axel worked in hospitality, and the team interviewed more than 100 hotels before committing to the product. By June 2026, pilots were operating in several countries. This is specific knowledge expressed through action, although undisclosed customer counts and revenue prevent me from knowing whether Zaplar has sold a repeatable system or merely delivered promising prototypes.

The property graph is more interesting than the word “agentic.” Mapping rooms, resources, rules, and workflows into a constrained operating model produced a company-reported 20% reduction in estimated housekeeping walking distance across tens of schedules. That is technical proof, but not yet durable leverage. If Mews or Apaleo copies the interface and agent workflows, Zaplar’s defense must be accumulated operational context that materially improves decisions. The dossier describes that asset as a future possibility, while Mews already has 15,000 customers and $300 million of new capital.

The market has room, but not unlimited room. A unified architecture can expand from housekeeping into bookings, payments, messaging, revenue management, and vendor coordination within a $4.2 billion market projected to reach $7 billion by 2033. The same breadth also creates the failure mode: replacing as many as 15 systems requires migration, retraining, integrations, security, and production reliability across financially consequential workflows. A larger product surface creates option value only when one shared capability unlocks it; otherwise it creates fifteen ways to fail.

Zaplar does not yet compound permissionlessly. Founder-led hotel visits may be the correct early sales motion, but each property currently appears to require another relationship, migration, and implementation. No evidence shows one hotel creating utility or distribution for another. There is also no published price, realized revenue, take rate, or recurring contract, so multinational pilots establish use—not direct value capture.

The strongest bull case is that the difficult migration is itself the moat: once Zaplar becomes the operational system of record, property layouts, rules, and workflow histories could produce high switching costs and proprietary data. The pre-network exception for a technically coherent, capital-efficient prototype deserves consideration, especially after the SEK 3 million pre-YC raise. It does not yet override the evidence because institutional procurement prevents effortless adoption, agent mistakes touch bookings and payments, and no retention or deployment-time data proves the architecture becomes easier to sell as it grows.

I see no disclosed integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-stability red flag. The problem is structural rather than behavioral: strong action-derived knowledge is paired with weak autonomous distribution. My base case is that within 36 months Zaplar reaches roughly $1–3 million ARR through a limited number of implementation-heavy hotel contracts, unless the property graph turns deployments into a repeatable data advantage. I would not invest today. One condition flips the decision: evidence that at least 25 hotel groups went live through a standardized deployment taking under 30 days.
