# Ample Computer -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Coding agents are creating applications faster; Ample Computer wants to capture the infrastructure spending that follows. The controlling variable is whether an agent-created deployment becomes a durable, human-claimed production account. If that happens repeatedly, the initial deployment becomes distribution for databases, storage, and additional applications. The dossier describes the acquisition mechanism, but provides no conversion rate or evidence that customers keep their production workloads there. I can identify the hypothesis; I cannot yet underwrite it.

The strongest bull case is that Ample already controls something consequential. Its MCP connector and CLI provision infrastructure, execute code in isolated microVMs, and return an HTTPS endpoint with diagnostic logs. Better coding models could increase demand for those operations while Ample retains responsibility for running the application. That is meaningful workflow ownership. Backing infrastructure across an AI discontinuity fits my pattern of investing across multiple layers of a new market. The Anthropic-verified connector, added in October 2026, also gives this two-person team a credible route into an existing agent ecosystem.

The difficulty is where the market has already crystallized. Railway and Render both expose deployment operations to agents, and Vercel combines hosting with its own application-creation product. Ample’s deploy-first, claim-later flow could remove an important onboarding bottleneck, but the dossier establishes a workflow difference without establishing an economic advantage. Vercel’s reported 28-fold annual increase in agent-triggered deployments supports the adoption wave; it does not establish Ample’s share of that wave. Per-second metering with a one-minute minimum describes billing mechanics, not superior gross margins, lower customer costs, or a demonstrated reduction in deployment time. The interface could become standard while existing infrastructure providers capture the spending.

Six showcased applications, including Yard Bros and Saltless Surf, make the product more tangible, but their ownership and payment status are undisclosed. The Hermes plugin and company-reported October 17 protocol deployment demonstrate shipping activity; they do not establish repeat customer demand. I would give absent pull its pre-seed default rather than treat it as failed adoption. Ravi Mishra’s prior startups and company-reported 2012 acquisition provide some evidence of second-time learning, although the dossier does not document the lessons or costly commitment behind Ample. That strengthens execution confidence without resolving the market question. The research-stage exception also does not apply: the evidence concerns deployed infrastructure integrations, without documented frontier research or scarce model-building capability that would justify overriding absent pull.

I would pass today. The real counterargument is that agent distribution could establish Ample’s default position before customers ever compare hosting providers, and its operational workflow gives that argument substance. But connector availability has not yet become demonstrated distribution, and managed infrastructure has not yet become demonstrated customer dependence. My one verifiable flip condition is a cohort of at least ten independent customers that claim agent-created deployments, remain paying after 90 days, and deploy a second application through Ample. That would directly test whether the first product creates a reusable customer channel.
