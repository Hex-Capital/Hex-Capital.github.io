# Audun -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have credible general ability, but the decisive knowledge is missing. Peter Flo has worked at Midjourney and researched AI; Jacob Hambro and Magnus Njålstad Vonen bring McKinsey and operating experience; Henrik Brun Fevang has practiced data science. None has publicly shipped, sold, regulated, or collected debt. Credentials establish intelligence. They do not establish the specific knowledge acquired by negotiating with distressed debtors, integrating creditor systems, or operating inside collection law. I assign the uncertainty prior rather than pretending adjacent experience is exact experience.

Audun’s product is legible: automate reminders, negotiate payment plans, preserve an audit history, and send disputes or vulnerability cases to humans. The code is also visibly reproducible. AgentCollect, Altur, and Domu already overlap across calls, texts, disputes, negotiations, and payment plans. Audun has disclosed no proprietary model, exclusive data, telephony infrastructure, workflow lock-in, or case volume. If a competitor copied the interface tomorrow, the dossier identifies nothing Audun would retain except four capable people.

The strongest bull case is that regulated workflow becomes the substrate. Norway’s new May 2026 Debt Collection Act, licensing requirements, GDPR constraints, human escalation, and interaction records could turn compliance integrations and outcome data into a durable system. The $5.98 billion global software market is large enough to reward that transition. But this is potential energy, not an asset: licence status is unverified, the debtor portal is still “coming soon,” and no deployed cases show that Audun learns faster with volume. A future data moat earns no present moat credit.

The market is real but the expansion logic is thin. The dossier supports a $2.5 billion European collection-software segment growing 8.6%, yet it does not show how the creditor wedge unlocks a larger adjacent transaction layer. Direct sales through “book a walkthrough” can build a respectable software company, but each creditor currently appears to be another enterprise sale. Debtors do not contribute reusable supply or distribution for other debtors, and creditors do not make the system more useful to one another on the evidence available. There is no permissionless network, fragmented supply aggregation, or participant-created value loop.

Economics remain unproven. Platform and recovery-based fees are inferred; there is no named payer, price, contract, completed recovery, or revenue. Pre-seed status excuses limited history, but it does not convert a walkthrough into direct value capture. The relevant pre-launch exception would require unusually strong prototype evidence or demonstrated capital efficiency; neither appears here. Conversely, I find no evidence of unfair founder dealings, artificial financing pressure, or founders requiring reassurance. Integrity is unknown, not impaired.

I pass. My 36-month base case is that Audun becomes a sub-€3 million ARR Nordic collections vendor or is absorbed by an incumbent such as Intrum, rather than becoming a compounding global platform. The option becomes interesting if Audun can verify that aggregate deployment data materially improves recovery outcomes for later creditors—turning each customer into proprietary leverage for the next—while operating under the required licence. That single mechanism would change both durability and distribution; without it, Audun is selling copyable automation into a regulated market.
