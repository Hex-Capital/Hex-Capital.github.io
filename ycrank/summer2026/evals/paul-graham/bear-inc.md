# Bear -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Bear has done something more interesting than putting a chatbot in front of accounting software. It has built the ledger and taken responsibility for delivering the accounting. That exposes the founders to all the unpleasant details an interface company can leave to someone else. But my first question remains unanswered: what specifically broke, what did Sami and Riki do about it, and how long did that take? Owning the difficult parts creates opportunities to demonstrate resourcefulness. It does not establish that they have demonstrated it.

There is a real artifact here. Bear reported processing more than $80M for dozens of Finnish companies by July 2026, which puts its proprietary ledger beyond an architectural proposal ([company blog](https://lastaccountingcompany.com/news/interfaceable-general-ledger)). This fits a pattern I like: technical founders building the underlying system before announcing themselves as a platform. The limitation is what the number measures. Transaction volume establishes that the system has handled accounting data. It does not establish classification accuracy, autonomous completion, or how much correction the accountants perform. I give the deployed product meaningful credit, while discounting the unverified technical claims.

The strongest argument for investing is simpler: more than ten customers reportedly pay Bear to do their accounting ([founder website](https://samilaa.com/)). These are customers entrusting a necessary business function to a small company. That is substantially better evidence than a waitlist. Finnish businesses also give the team a sufficiently narrow place to start. The fixed-price bundle could make every eliminated manual task economically valuable. But the same bundle lets human effort conceal software deficiencies. The decisive question is whether successive closes require less intervention while customers keep paying. The dossier supplies neither that progression nor retention, and its customer count is undated.

Sami and Riki previously worked together at Token Terminal, and their financial-data infrastructure experience makes the ledger credible. The reported childhood relationships also reduce the concern that the founding team was assembled for an application. Neither fact establishes that the founders personally encountered the accounting problem, however. Nor does the dossier show a product hypothesis rejected after customer feedback. I leave those dimensions at their missing-evidence defaults. Sami’s dropout status supplies no independent signal either way. Bear’s working product and paying customers are the kinds of evidence that can justify exceptions to an uncertain founder prior, but they do not yet demonstrate unmistakable pull or independently validated technical performance sufficient to override my resourcefulness gate.

I would hold off on a check. Bear has earned technical and commercial interest; it has not yet shown the founder behavior that would make this my bet. Balance already combines agents with accountant signoff, so the service architecture alone cannot explain why Bear wins ([Balance](https://getbalance.ai/about)). The proprietary ledger might let this team fix problems faster, but it also makes them responsible for more problems. My flip condition is one verifiable customer case documenting a specific accounting blocker, a founder-built fix shipped within weeks, and that customer successfully completing the next close using it. That would connect the promising machinery to the people operating it.
