# Ordo Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

The little thing I want to pick up and turn over is Ordo Labs’ release-specific change notice. Someone has thought about what happens *after* the software changes and a laboratory must defend its results. Move the clock to 2032: scientists could supervise experiments while software carries the evidence from instrument output through an approved result. Getting there requires reliable interpretation, qualified workflows, and acceptance of the resulting records. Ordo supplies pieces of that machinery today. The dossier does not yet establish a path from its reporting wedge to that larger future, so I will not give the imagined destination the same weight as the shipped documentation.

Why is a scientist doing this work manually? Because someone must check results against SOPs, trace reported values, and approve the outcome. Those obligations protect people; they are not an artificial restriction to sweep away. Ordo’s opportunity is to remove the clerical work surrounding accountability while preserving accountability itself. File ingestion avoids an initial instrument-integration project, and source-linked values give reviewers something concrete to inspect. That returns scientists some control over their time. But the dossier identifies neither a gatekeeper collecting an avoidable rent nor the cost of qualification. The reported savings therefore do not establish that Ordo changes the underlying economics of running a regulated laboratory.

The strongest argument against my hesitation is that laboratories in the US and Europe already pay for pilots. These customers are spending money on a product in a setting where mistakes matter. Ordo reports more than 95% faster first-pass QC and 82% faster report completion and review. Those are substantial claims, although the dossier supplies no customer-level cohorts, repeat-use history, or independently measured results. This fits my less glamorous investing pattern: concealed operating machinery can matter enormously. The validation package is a real artifact. What remains unproven is whether it becomes a dependency customers build around. Sapio already offers governed AI reporting, so better document preparation alone gives Ordo limited room to become indispensable.

The founders have relevant ingredients. Adam Tovatt brings more than ten years of software development, chemistry education, and prior company-building; Kristina Hoang previously co-founded a pharmaceutical-authentication company; Sebastian Apelgren contributes quantitative research and software experience. Those facts deserve credit beyond their degrees. They do not yet show a dated sequence of experiments or a prior operating wound that explains an unusual commitment to this particular problem. My exceptions for companies without revenue do apply in principle: a working artifact and exceptional founder evidence can justify an early check. Here, revenue is already present; exceptional evidence of founder obsession is the missing part. I also see no documented leadership shell game, equal-control deadlock, or dependence on one closed model supplier. Unknowns are not violations. Human review and electronic signatures are useful controls, though they do not establish data-custody protections.

I pass with my own money today. Ordo Labs has a credible workflow business taking shape, but its strongest evidence concerns present-day utility, while my primary conviction would need to concern what that utility enables next. One verifiable milestone would flip my decision: three unrelated regulated laboratories qualifying Ordo as their shared evidence-and-approval layer across multiple instrument workflows and using it in production for six months. That would demonstrate the infrastructure role I can currently only imagine.
