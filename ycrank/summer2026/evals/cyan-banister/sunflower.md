# Sunflower -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years from now, addiction care should not begin with somebody finding an available clinician, affording a $125 hour, and remaining functional until the appointment. It should begin the moment a person asks for help, with software maintaining continuity and humans entering when judgment, medication, or crisis care requires them. Sunflower has a remarkable leak from that future: 500,000 users, 100,000 MAU, 21.5 million sober days, and 7 million AI-sponsor messages. But “sobriety for the future of superintelligence” is still a slogan, not a map. The dossier does not explain how today’s tracker becomes that six-year institution, which clinical decisions remain human, or which technical and regulatory transitions make the destination safe.

The strongest evidence is behavioral. Ten thousand people pay, users have recorded millions of sober days, and the app carries 4.9 stars across 5,700 iOS ratings and 4.7 stars across 5,560 Android reviews. This is not waitlist theater. People repeatedly disclose painful information, return daily, and entrust the product with behavior that can determine whether they relapse. The 37,500 clinic requests are especially important because they show the consumer ritual pulling users toward costly real-world care rather than merely generating chatbot engagement.

Koby Conrad also paid tuition before this became a fashionable AI category. His own recovery led to Quirk, then health-tech growth work at Rupa Health, and now Sunflower. Reaching $1 million ARR in ten months, hiring a sobriety creator into the company, and turning one creator relationship into 50,000–100,000 monthly clicks demonstrate both product obsession and commercial command. The dossier identifies one accountable CEO, so I do not see a governance red flag merely because the co-founder history is absent.

The broken system is concrete, although Sunflower has not fully decoded its economics. Scarce clinicians, $125 therapy sessions, $350 quarterly medication costs, state licensing, Medicaid approvals, and county contracts create delay and fragmentation. The $20 assessment and $89 monthly medication offering return immediate access to the patient. What remains unclear is who captures each layer of rent and whether Sunflower can lower it after acquiring licensed clinical operations in dozens of states. A clinic can return agency, or it can become another expensive gatekeeper wearing a friendlier interface.

Here is the case that stings: recovery conversations are extraordinarily sensitive, the outcome claims come from company surveys rather than a published Sunflower trial, and the Android listing acknowledges broad personal and financial data collection. HIPAA controls, encryption, role-based access, and 42 CFR Part 2 procedures are necessary, but they do not answer whether seven million sponsor messages touch an external model or become training material. Meanwhile, Reframe, Monument, and Pelago already cover adjacent care, and Conrad himself says consumer apps are rapidly cloneable. The interaction corpus, Pennsylvania and Michigan licenses, and app-to-clinic funnel could become a concealed operating layer, but today they are an emerging advantage rather than a controlled bottleneck.

My 36-month bet is that Sunflower reaches $8–15 million ARR primarily as a recovery subscription and telehealth company, not yet as a general “sobriety platform for superintelligence.” I would invest because the working artifact, founder history, and user behavior are unusually strong, while treating the $60 million post-money valuation as requiring disciplined ownership. My one verifiable flip condition is a third-party privacy audit confirming that sponsor conversations are not used to train external models and can be deleted by the user; failure turns this into a pass, regardless of growth.
