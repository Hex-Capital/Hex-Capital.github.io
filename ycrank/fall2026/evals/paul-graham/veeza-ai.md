# Veeza AI -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Moursy has shipped something people pay for. What I cannot find is what he did when something broke. Veeza AI operates among scarce appointments and changing government portals, but those are descriptions of the terrain. The dossier contains no specific episode in which a portal failed, Moursy built a workaround, and applications started moving again. That leaves my most important founder test unanswered. His engineering background and previous product roles cannot answer it for him.

The product deserves credit independently. Veeza went from closed beta on April 19 to public beta on June 7, and offers document checking, form completion, and appointment booking with human review. That is more than a proposed agent architecture. But the dossier does not establish how much of an application the software completes, how often it makes mistakes, or how much work the humans perform. My preference for concrete technical proof applies here: a functioning service provides intermediate evidence; it does not yet establish that the team has solved the difficult automation.

The strongest case for writing a check is the narrowness of the need. Egyptian passport holders already endure this process and pay brokers to help. Veeza reports paying customers, roughly 400 WhatsApp community members, and 10,000 feedback messages. Its EGP 1,500 Schengen and EGP 4,000 UK/US service fees attach a price to a specific job. These are considerably better signals than launch views. The reported increase from 1,000 to 7,000 users strengthens that case, although an undefined user count cannot tell me how many applications were completed or customers referred. A working product and unmistakable demand can justify an exception to incomplete founder evidence. Here the product exists, but the strength of the demand remains insufficiently resolved to justify that exception.

Moursy’s own Egyptian-passport travel experience makes this an organically encountered problem. The unattractive work—documents, appointments, exceptions, and anxious applicants—is precisely where a useful company could begin. I give that origin some credit, discounted because the account is retrospective rather than supported by a dated personal artifact. Human review is sensible at this stage. The question is whether each application teaches Veeza to handle the next one better. Atlys and iVisa/HelloGov already combine assistance with overlapping services, so merely adding automation does not establish that learning advantage. Travel-agency introductions could help distribution, but the existing WhatsApp and founder-content channels mean I would not infer dependence on a large partner.

I would hold off on a personal check. There is no documented product revision tied to contrary evidence, so adaptability remains unknown rather than poor. Likewise, the second team member’s undisclosed history does not establish a hastily assembled cofounder relationship. Veeza has a credible beginning and actual customers; my uncertainty concerns the founders’ response to resistance. My flip condition is one documented portal disruption that the team fixes within a week, with before-and-after records showing that the fix restores completed, paid applications. That would supply the missing founder evidence and clarify what the software actually does.
