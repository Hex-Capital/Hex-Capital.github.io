# Induction Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Photon-1 is a 106B-A5B model trained on 575 million frames, yet nobody outside Induction Labs can buy it, install it, or independently test its headline result. That pairing is Induction Labs’s signature: unusually authentic technical founders building an unusually intangible artifact. Jonathan Li worked on reasoning and reinforcement-learning infrastructure at Cohere before Induction Labs; David Li brings software-engineering and machine-learning experience. The founders look more real than the product feels. I can see the tiz. I cannot yet touch the magic.

Anduril is the high rung because Palmer Luckey built the adjacent future before asking anyone to believe the defense story. Jonathan Li’s Cohere work, ACL 2023 paper, and progression from Axiom 1 in August 2025 to Photon-1 in July 2026 give Induction Labs a smaller version of that mechanism: technical continuity predates the current launch. Induction Labs diverges because the dossier supplies no autobiographical moment when Jonathan or David Li personally felt the computer-use problem, and no evidence that Axiom reached users. They are of the technology, but the evidence does not yet show that they are of the problem.

The consensus YC AI-agent darling fails my tactile test because its value exists inside a benchmark and a fundraising story. That failure mechanism appears inside Induction Labs: the Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite comparison is internal, access terms are absent, and the public evidence consists of demonstrations and research claims rather than a product I can hand to a friend. Induction Labs has an observable artifact—Photon-1 simulating desktops, checkers, and billiard physics—but observing a video is not using the machine. HQ Trivia once taught me that overwhelming product pull can override governance anxiety; Induction Labs has roughly 4,000 X followers after launch, not HQ-like evidence of compulsive use.

World Labs has raised $1.23 billion, General Intuition $454 million, and Runway $860 million, so Induction Labs is operating in a consensus capital race wearing a magically weird technical costume. Over the next 18 months, Induction Labs’s unlabeled-video method either compounds into a proprietary corpus and verified rollout loop, or its 2-billion-video index becomes expensive raw material available to every well-funded model lab. The claimed 30-times compute advantage and three-times lower serving cost could make Induction Labs the strange little machine everyone underestimated. Without independent validation, the same claims read like model-layer differentiation that a capitalized competitor can erase—the red flag where the model, rather than the founders, owns the moat.

Jonathan Li is explicitly CEO while David Li is listed as Founder, which gives Induction Labs more clarity than a shared-title partnership. Induction Labs still consists of two brothers “building a company together again,” with no disclosed equity split, vesting terms, or conflict protocol. The counterfactual is clean: documented final operating authority for Jonathan, plus founder vesting and a deadlock procedure, would move governance into the top tier. Until then, “CEO” is a title rather than proof of the tiebreaker conversation, and I have seen that movie end badly.

Crusoe turned capital intensity into an operating moat because Chase Lochmiller could walk onto oilfields, deploy physical infrastructure, and survive a commodity collapse. Induction Labs also accepts real technical and capital risk: approximately 30,000 H200 GPU-hours, a 106B-A5B architecture, and 575 million curated frames are not an AI wrapper assembled over a weekend. Induction Labs lacks Crusoe’s physical territory, regulatory friction, and social license, however. The compute bill is hard; the category is fashionable. I reward the frontier-model swing without pretending that expensive software is automatically the kind of ugly moat other investors refuse to enter.

Approximately 30,000 H200 GPU-hours produced impressive research but no disclosed pricing, customers, or revenue. My 36-month forecast is that by July 2029 Cohere acquires Induction Labs for less than $150 million, with Induction Labs below $5 million ARR, primarily to absorb Jonathan Li, the video-pretraining corpus, and Photon-1’s compression architecture rather than to acquire a durable standalone business. Strong founder-domain continuity plus weak physical distribution creates valuable laboratory IP before it creates an independent institution.

Photon-1 reportedly beats Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite with 30-times less pretraining compute, and that is the bull case that stings: Induction Labs may be DeepMind again, where cross-domain technical compounding overrides my preference for lived biography and tactile products. Induction Labs already moved from Axiom 1 to a radically different observational-learning architecture, filtered roughly 2 million screen recordings, and shipped a model large enough to attract 4,000 X followers; perhaps the polymaths are revealing themselves through output. The acquisition forecast still wins because every decisive performance claim remains internal, data rights are undisclosed, and no deployment feedback loop exists. Three named paying agent platforms, independently reproduced benchmark leadership, and at least $1 million ARR would flip my verdict to Invest.
