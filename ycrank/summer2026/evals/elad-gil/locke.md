# Locke -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: rising legislative volume and increasingly strategic government procurement are pushing early-stage defense, healthcare, and GovTech companies to buy government-affairs execution earlier; the controlling variable is whether Locke can increase revenue per policy professional enough to turn lobbying from a headcount-constrained service into a scalable product business.

The strongest evidence is demand. Locke reports an annual revenue run-rate above $500K after six weeks, with clients spanning three regulated sectors. Even discounted because the customers are unnamed and the figure is company-reported, this is unusually informative pre-seed product-market pull. Customers are apparently paying for an unfinished hybrid operation rather than waiting for a mature platform. The unresolved issue is what they are buying: repeatable agent-assisted execution or scarce access to Parth Badhwar and the initial lobbyists.

Locke has chosen the correct workflow boundary. Its agents analyze legislation, monitor government signals in Slack, track stakeholder engagement, and draft proposals and compliance briefs, while its lobbyists carry work through policymaker meetings and funded contracts. Better models should improve several of those tasks. The product therefore has more potential durability than a policy-summary wrapper. But Quorum already serves more than 2,000 organizations with policy data, AI assistance, stakeholder management, advocacy tools, and professional services. Locke has disclosed no proprietary workflow state, outcome dataset, accuracy results, or integration that a capitalized incumbent could not reproduce.

The technology shift is real but the economic shift remains unproven. State legislatures introduced more than 135,500 bills in 2025, approximately 55% more than in 2024, which makes automated monitoring more valuable. That does not establish software economics. Locke is simultaneously recruiting policy and technical staff, with policy compensation reaching $200K, so the current evidence is equally consistent with a fast-growing consultancy using AI to improve employee productivity. The red flag is not services themselves; it is claiming scalable economics while delivery remains proportional to expensive policy headcount.

The strongest bull case is that $500K-plus run-rate in six weeks already demonstrates the wedge: underserved startups want one accountable provider spanning policy intelligence, lobbying, procurement, and proposals, and the resulting client channel can support adjacent products without reacquiring the buyer. Badhwar’s White House, Deloitte policy, campaign, and Clipbook GTM experience gives him unusually direct access to this market. I still cannot use the frontier-team or physical-economics exceptions here. There is no documented scarce technology, structural barrier, or quantified margin reconstruction that overrides the service-throughput concern.

My 36-month base case is a credible $4M–$8M ARR government-affairs firm whose growth remains materially tied to policy headcount, rather than a category-defining software company. I would monitor rather than invest my own money today. The verifiable flip condition is a cohort demonstrating at least 70% gross margin and twelve-month retention while revenue per policy employee rises as client volume grows; that would show the agents are scaling institutional work rather than decorating a conventional firm.
