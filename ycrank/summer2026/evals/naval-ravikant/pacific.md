# Pacific -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Pacific’s strongest evidence is not the proposed data center. It is that Harper Dammann Smith has already sold a technical product to roughly £10K MRR in one month, while Ralph Heward-Mills has worked as an Airbus thermal engineer. One founder has demonstrated commercial motion; the other has relevant knowledge acquired inside a physical system where heat and reliability are constraints, not presentation slides. But neither has publicly manufactured or operated a data center, and revenue from the digital-twin product does not transfer to the new pod merely because the founders transferred.

The market gives them room to maneuver. JLL expects modular-system and micro-data-center sales to rise from $11 billion in 2025 to $48 billion in 2030, while 57% of data-center projects experienced delays of at least three months. Starting with powered but stalled sites is a coherent wedge into defense deployments, relocatable compute, and eventually harsher environments. The same standardized power-and-cooling architecture can address adjacent demand. Space is optionality; it is not yet evidence.

This is also the right substrate and the wrong proof level. Diamond-wafer dry cooling, integrated transformers, switchgear, and power distribution would survive someone copying Pacific’s software. Physical systems can compound through production learning, supplier control, reliability data, and deployed-fleet experience. None of those assets is documented today: no patent, benchmark, certification, production unit, or field result. Armada already has deployed modular units, and Crusoe has both capital and an integrated energy-to-cloud stack. A claimed hardware moat before measured hardware exists is still a claim.

The bull case stings because Pacific fits the recent infrastructure pattern unusually well. Compute demand is colliding with power, water, and construction constraints; the team has aerospace and thermal knowledge; YC and NVIDIA Inception reduce some early coordination friction; and unnamed large aerospace companies are discussing managed pods. The earlier £10K MRR also suggests the founders can ship and sell rather than remain trapped in research. Pre-seed investors are paid to recognize that combination before certifications and fleets make it obvious.

But Pacific has no compounding participant loop. Each installation presently appears to require more equipment, capital, siting work, permitting, and founder-led sales. Hosted GPU hours provide direct monetization in theory, but the company publishes no price, contract, current-pod revenue, or completed transaction. The prior digital-twin revenue proves founder execution, not demand for this product. There is no known-exception override because the dossier shows neither a functioning pod nor adoption strong enough to substitute for monetization and network evidence.

My base case is that by August 2029 Pacific has pivoted from operating hosted compute to supplying thermal or power modules to larger infrastructure companies and remains below $1 million ARR. I would not reject the founders; I would reject the present evidence. The single flip condition is a third-party-verified, revenue-producing pod operating continuously at a disclosed power density and cooling efficiency. That would convert the diamond-wafer system from an engineering story into a durable asset and make this an option worth owning.
