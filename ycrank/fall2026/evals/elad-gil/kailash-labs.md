# Kailash Labs -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Kailash Labs is making two different bets: that specialized models can understand factory video, and that this capability can make robot stations economical for manufacturers. The second determines whether I invest. Better robot policies could expand automation into previously uneconomic tasks; the controlling variable is the fully loaded cost per successfully completed task, including equipment financing, integration, supervision and downtime. The dossier provides no measurement of that variable. Offering customers zero capex changes who finances the equipment; it does not establish better economics.

The current product has a more interesting ownership boundary than a standalone video model. Kailash describes integrating the arm, gripper, vision and safety into a station for assembly, inspection, packing or kitting. That gives it a plausible place to absorb improving models while retaining responsibility for the physical result. I give some credit for that integration scope. I cannot yet credit a learning advantage: deployment data, operating feedback loops and evidence that subsequent installations become cheaper are all unestablished. Formic already packages equipment, deployment, maintenance and financing, so the service structure itself is available elsewhere.

The strongest case for investing now is that the founders can connect model research to deployment before factory automation fully crystallizes. Sharma has KAIST robot-learning experience, Tsinghua offline-RL research and a NeurIPS 2022 spotlight publication. Marlin is a released artifact, not a proposed research program. Its 934 monthly downloads and nine Hugging Face Spaces show some developer experimentation, although neither establishes repeat industrial usage. Mete’s manipulation research would strengthen this case if his current involvement were confirmed. These are meaningful technical signals, but the biography does not establish prior startup learning or costly personal commitment.

That bull case still needs an economic bridge. Physical Intelligence’s April 2025 generalization demonstration supports the possibility of more adaptable robots; it does not measure Kailash’s deployment cost or reliability. Marlin’s company-reported benchmark position also cannot establish production performance, particularly given the authors’ own annotation concerns. My pattern of investing across several layers of a technology transition makes both models and applications interesting. It does not make success in one layer evidence of attractive margins in another. The research-stage exception requires a sufficiently rare technical position and a credible capital path; the disclosed research credentials and YC funding do not establish that combination for a financed robot fleet.

I would pass with my own money today. This is an evidence gap rather than proof of poor economics, and I would not treat a two-person team or absent revenue as adverse facts. The unresolved issue is whether Kailash can turn technical capability into repeatable delivery without retaining the cost structure of a custom automation integrator. My flip condition is one independently verified, paid production deployment sustaining a fully loaded cost per successful task below 70% of the customer’s displaced labor cost for 90 days, at a price that leaves Kailash a positive station contribution margin. That would connect the model, the workflow and the market in one falsifiable result.
