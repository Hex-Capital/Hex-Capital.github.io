# Chromie -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. The compelling version of Chromie is not “AI helps contractors read solicitations.” It is a government market in which every small contractor has a persistent institutional memory, notices opportunities before publication, and can compete without maintaining a capture bureaucracy. But the dossier does not work backward from that world. It names the May 2025 FAR overhaul and today’s portal fragmentation, yet supplies no sequence showing how procurement behavior, agency processes, or contractor economics change. Snovation’s missed RFI is a useful little leak, but it proves better monitoring—not that the future has arrived early.

The founders are the strongest part of this bet. Ananth built an acquired product reportedly used by more than 40,000 people, conducted AI research at UMIACS, and persuaded a high-school classmate with strong systems credentials to build alongside him. Then they bought one-way passage to Washington and visited 50 GovCon offices. That is real first-believer behavior. The trouble is the direction of travel: during 2026 Chromie moved from Chrome-extension creation to browser agents, insurance workflows, and finally government contracting. I see technical appetite and sales energy; I do not yet see a pre-company obsession with this particular system.

Why does government capture consume so much labor? Chromie answers with portals, long solicitations, spreadsheets, and email, but stops before identifying the rule and rent. Which procurement requirement creates the waste? Who benefits from the opacity? Does Chromie return pricing power to small contractors, or merely help them perform the same expensive ritual faster? The product could broaden access, but the dossier has not established that transfer of agency. An $8.1 billion software category growing 9.2% annually describes an existing market, not a broken rule being removed.

The strongest case against my pass is concrete: three named customer logos are already visible, and Snovation says Chromie found an RFI that its existing pipeline missed. Combine that functioning artifact with Ananth’s prior 40,000-user product and the founders’ 50-office field trip, and this may be a young team rapidly discovering its real wound rather than aimlessly pivoting. I will make exceptions to missing revenue when a prototype, institutional commitments, and deep founder obsession reinforce one another. Here the exact customer count, repeat usage, contracts, and revenue remain undisclosed, so one missed RFI cannot yet tell me whether users are rearranging their work around Chromie or simply testing another dashboard.

The hidden-infrastructure possibility is the Company Brain: capability records, past performance, pursuit history, and outcomes could become a proprietary substrate for qualification. Today that is only an inferred future moat. Chromie depends on SAM.gov and SLED ingestion, model-generated extraction, and customer documents while controlling no disclosed dataset or lower-stack bottleneck. Four funded competitors already overlap heavily; two report nearly 200 and more than 300 customers. More seriously, Chromie accepts sensitive internal context without a public security certification while competitors disclose federal controls. Source tracing and replayable audit logs help with correctness, but they do not resolve consent, custody, or access architecture.

My 36-month expectation is that Chromie pivots again into a broader regulated-document workflow before the current GovCon product exceeds $1 million ARR. The founders look capable of building and selling; I simply do not yet believe this application owns the system beneath the wave. I pass with my own money. One verifiable condition flips me: a 90-day cohort of ten paying contractors in which at least eight renew and three submit bids on opportunities Chromie surfaced before their incumbent tools.
