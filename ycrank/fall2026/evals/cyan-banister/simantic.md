# Simantic -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: an engineer asks an agent to change a robot’s behavior, and the agent returns firmware plus evidence that it survives the relevant hardware conditions. Simantic is working on a necessary step between those two things. Generating code faster means little if testing still waits for a board. Its API/MCP access is a small artifact from that possible future. But the dossier establishes the first connection—generated firmware needs virtual validation—without establishing the remaining transitions to trustworthy, broadly modeled hardware. I can imagine that world more clearly than Simantic has demonstrated its route there.

Why does the engineer wait? Physical prototypes take weeks or months, according to the founders’ account in Waterloo’s July 2026 coverage. Removing that dependency could give a small engineering team control over its own iteration schedule. That is concrete agency returned. The distinction matters, though: this is a physical constraint, with no identified gatekeeper collecting an artificial rent. Simantic must reproduce enough of the hardware’s behavior to remove the wait safely. A quick simulation that produces false confidence simply moves the cost downstream.

Hong and Shahriar have worked where that mistake matters: Tesla, robotics companies, and Neuralink’s implant and charger software. Hong started the venture in September 2025; Shahriar joined in early 2026 after they knew each other through Waterloo’s engineering community. That gives me relevant operating experience and a complementary first believer. It does not yet give me a documented history of independent experiments or enduring obsession before the company. The CEO and CTO roles identify accountable leaders; they do not establish the control arrangement either way.

The strongest attraction is beneath the visible AI activity. Privately developed engines, peripheral models, and board tooling could become infrastructure used across robotics, electronics, and other embedded systems. That fits my pattern of backing the concealed enabling layer behind a fashionable application. Here is the uncomfortable counterargument: the public SDK defaults to Renode, which already supports peripheral modeling and CI. Simantic’s Rust backend and model work earn technical credit, but the dossier does not demonstrate superior fidelity or coverage. A convenient agent interface could attract users while the hardest accumulated value remains elsewhere.

The product exists; costly user behavior remains unproven. BoardRepo describes planned testing before manufacture, and Embedder and WATFlight provide additional partnership signals. None establishes repeated completed testing or payment. The $100 monthly Developer price is an offer, not revenue, and the SDK explicitly asks users to defer production pipelines. I would make the pre-revenue exception for unusually persuasive technical and founder evidence, but the dossier lacks signed industrial proofs of concept and a documented long pre-company building record. Its firmware-retention controls and private hosting also give the confidentiality concern a limiting architecture; transmission of PR data to Anthropic still makes customer consent worth inspecting.

I would keep my own checkbook closed today, while wanting to try Simantic on a real board’s firmware. My flip condition is one independently auditable, recurring paid deployment where Simantic catches silicon-confirmed peripheral bugs that the customer’s existing Renode setup misses before board manufacture. That would connect the controlled technical asset to behavior costly enough to believe. Today, I see a plausible enabling company with relevant founders, but insufficient evidence that its particular simulation layer earns enduring dependence.
