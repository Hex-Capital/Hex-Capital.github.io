# Deploy -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important question about Deploy’s pen-packing demonstration is what happened when it failed. The dossier reports failures and describes remote intervention, but does not connect a particular failure to a founder-built correction and a subsequent result. That distinction matters. Designing a system to accept human help is sensible engineering. It does not yet tell me how these founders behave when their original plan breaks.

There is a real artifact here. Deploy reports packing 800+ pens at DELIVER America in October 2026 using approximately 30 minutes of fine-tuning data. That earns technical credit. The result is company-reported, and the dossier supplies neither throughput nor intervention frequency, so I would give it intermediate credit rather than treat it as a demonstrated labor replacement. Packing 800 objects can be impressive while still requiring an uneconomic amount of supervision.

The strongest argument for investing is the combination of these particular founders and that artifact. Chada researched vision-language-action models at Honda. Polson deployed autonomous airport vehicles at AeroVect. They were laboratory partners before becoming cofounders. Deploy therefore fits my unstated preference for technical teams that produce something concrete before asking investors to believe a large claim. Their experience also makes the proposed intervention-to-training loop plausible. But plausibility is not an accumulated proprietary dataset, and relevant employment does not establish that this business began with a specific problem they encountered themselves. There is also no documented product revision driven by contrary evidence; I cannot infer either adaptability or rigidity.

The business turns on whether human help becomes less necessary with use. Deploy advertises monthly station coverage below replacement labor cost, with hardware and integration included. That gives the founders a reason to do the ugly installation and servicing work themselves. It also makes them responsible for every expensive exception. A narrow warehouse task could supply both revenue and useful training data, provided customers keep using it and intervention costs fall. The dossier identifies returns, kitting, and packing operators, but supplies no customer repeatedly using a station. Manifold already offers overlapping robotic labor services, so customers have another way to buy the outcome.

I checked the red flags and the exceptions. There is no evidence of founders waiting for a partner, delegating the hard work, or refusing contrary feedback. An exhibition is not evidence of those failures. Nor do missing pre-seed revenue figures establish rejection. But the exceptions that would justify overriding my founder gate require an unusually convincing working product, unmistakable user pull, or independently validated technical proof. Deploy’s demonstration is encouraging without establishing any of those strongly enough. I would pass on the evidence available. One verifiable flip condition would be a paid warehouse station renewed after eight weeks, with logs showing a named operational failure, the founders’ shipped correction within that period, and sustained throughput at a positive contribution margin including teleoperation and servicing. That would connect founder behavior to an outcome a customer actually wants.
