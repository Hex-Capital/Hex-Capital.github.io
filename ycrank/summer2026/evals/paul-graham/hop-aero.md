# Hop Aero -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important missing fact is what broke. Matija Milenovic and Jacob Balaj have formidable résumés, but the dossier does not name a Hop Aero obstacle, the workaround they built, or how quickly it changed the result. Milenovic previously scaled loitering-munition production from 10 to more than 100 units monthly, and Balaj has integrated vehicles at SpaceX, Virgin Orbit, Masten, and SpinLaunch. Those facts show competence under difficult conditions. They do not yet show how these founders behave when Rook itself refuses to work. I cannot infer resourcefulness from aerospace prestige.

They have nevertheless built more than a presentation. Hop Aero completed a tethered small-scale prototype test and an engine-and-igniter hot-fire using hopOS. These are functioning artifacts, not diagrams. But the intended product must carry 250 kilograms for 750 kilometers, re-enter at hypersonic speed, and land on an unprepared surface. A tether and a hot-fire remove two uncertainties from a long chain; they do not establish the chain. Rook has not completed an untethered point-to-point flight, much less crossed a measured payload, range, or landing threshold.

The Air Force is a real customer niche, and its $1,249,909 Phase II SBIR award is much stronger than a waitlist or an anonymous expression of interest. It proves that a named buyer will pay Hop Aero to pursue rapid logistics. It does not prove that military units repeatedly use the crude product, because there is not yet a usable delivery vehicle. The economics also remain obscure. The dossier supplies no mission price, launch cadence, or comparison between a Rook delivery and the cost of tolerating the delayed part. Government R&D can finance learning without proving procurement demand.

The founders encountered most of this system from the inside. One built propulsion, supersonic UAVs, and munitions; the other worked on Marine Corps avionics, launch integration, lunar landers, and reusable boosters. Licensing, range safety, propulsion, re-entry, payload integration, and landing are exactly the ugly work that keeps casual competitors away. This is genuine schlep. But the dossier does not identify the incident that caused either founder to need Rook, and it shows no rejected product hypothesis followed by an evidence-driven route change. The mission may be organic; the causal origin and running-back behavior remain unproven.

The strongest bull case is the known exception for young technical founders who replace missing conventional evidence with numerical physical proof. Hop Aero shares the direct domain exposure and willingness to build hardware seen in Bindwell. The material difference is that Bindwell’s laboratory result tested the central biological claim, whereas Hop Aero’s tethered test and hot-fire validate subsystems several steps short of the promised delivery mission. The company also partly matches the pattern of unusually concrete pre-round proof: it has an artifact and a paid federal program. But social followers, press coverage, and $3 million of financing add little, while dependence on one federal award and competition from SpaceX, Rocket Lab, Inversion, and Outpost make each missing technical step expensive.

My base case is that by August 2029 Hop Aero will have demonstrated progressively larger flights but will not operate a 250-kilogram, 750-kilometer cargo service, and will narrow toward propulsion, landing, or shorter-range defense systems. I would not invest my own money today. The operational gate fails because I have no company-specific example of a blocker turned into a shipped workaround, and neither the technical proof nor customer usage is strong enough to override that absence. One event would flip me: an Air Force-witnessed untethered point-to-point flight with a representative payload, produced by a documented founder-built workaround to a named flight blocker.
