# Evergrove -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Workers’ compensation care coordination is unquestionably wasteful, but Evergrove has not quantified a consequence large enough to make modest success irrelevant. Phone, fax, email, holds, and retries delay treatment; automating them may improve rehabilitation and provider throughput. Yet the dossier gives no spending baseline, affected-worker count, TAM, or evidence that this wedge can alter healthcare costs by an order of magnitude. This is a useful workflow with an unproven societal multiplier.

The strongest evidence is operational. Two founders have put agents through 350,000 production calls, support more than 500,000 calls annually, integrate in three weeks, and claim 40% higher completion, dramatically shorter cycles, and over 60% lower cost. Those are exceptional pre-seed deployment signals. They show that regulated voice automation can survive IVRs, holds, transfers, PHI controls, and system updates. They do not establish a technical discontinuity: the results lack cohort definitions, measurement methods, and a direct benchmark against Liberate or human contact-center economics.

Sid Unnithan and Jacob Chia are credible doers. Both built insurance automation at Newfront; Jacob worked on authorization, file infrastructure, and automated placements, while Sid led AI products after Microsoft’s VS Code and Azure ML teams. This is real founder-market fit, not generic AI résumé assembly. But the evidence does not show a material belief revised after data, a complementary expert recruited to own compliance or clinical risk, or an explicit decision to attack the company’s largest uncertainty.

That uncertainty is not whether an agent can make calls. Production volume has substantially answered it. The kill risk is whether Evergrove’s specialization creates a proprietary learning system before a well-funded horizontal competitor absorbs the workflow. Liberate has raised $72 million and already offers insurance-native voice agents; CLARA and Gradient AI sell into overlapping workers’ compensation buyers. Evergrove discloses integrations, audit trails, and configurable controls, but not retention, revenue, facility-level performance improvement, or a data advantage that compounds with every call. Capital should now buy evidence against commoditization, not merely more call volume.

The bull case stings: 350,000 production calls from a two-person company may be the platform-shaped wedge that the dossier has not yet articulated. Workflow configurations, contact patterns, and outcomes could accumulate into an insurance operations layer, while 60% lower customer cost supplies a plausible route to unsubsidized adoption. That resembles the exception made for Block: an initially ordinary interface into a giant financial workflow could expand into infrastructure. The material difference is that Evergrove has neither quantified the underlying value pool nor demonstrated that its data compounds into capability competitors cannot reproduce.

I pass today. This may become a good vertical software company, but the current evidence supports cheaper execution of an existing task, not an option on a radically different cost curve. One result would flip the decision: an independently customer-verified cohort showing that Evergrove sustains at least 60% lower all-in cost while materially improving injured-worker recovery time—and that the advantage widens with accumulated proprietary call data rather than merely matching a general voice model.
