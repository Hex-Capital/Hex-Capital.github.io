# Exosat -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I cannot underwrite the deal before I know the deal. Exosat discloses neither valuation nor competing-term-sheet count, and Orange Collective’s published memo establishes investor attention, not that this remains a cheap, scarce first check. Financing silence gets no anti-consensus credit. The sovereign-connectivity thesis may be unfashionable relative to consumer broadband, but a contrarian story bid to an unknown price is not yet a contrarian investment.

There is a real venture moment hiding inside the broad ambition: the booked April 2027 launch can test whether a roughly $300,000 satellite built from automotive-grade, non-ITAR/EAR components actually operates in low orbit and communicates through standard NTN equipment. A successful flight would retire meaningful technical and supply-chain risk. But the dossier never says that this round funds that experiment, defines its pass metric, or carries the company through it. Exosat is simultaneously proposing four frequency bands, capacity leases, turnkey constellations, satellite IoT, direct-to-device service, and an 11,000-satellite network. That is too many cards when scarce capital should flip one.

The best current-company signal is the reported $400 million-plus of LOIs across six nations, reinforced by early pilots and a published ITU filing. This fits the translation-layer pattern: Exosat could connect governments and operators seeking sovereign control with a globally manufactured, exportable satellite platform. The filing may become proprietary access, and the LOIs represent more customer action than generic market interest. But they remain company-reported, unnamed, and unconverted. An LOI headline and an 11,000-satellite filing can become the space-industry version of MAU theater unless one buyer commits cash to one deployable configuration.

Edward Ge is the reason not to dismiss this as pure theater. He has already co-founded two space companies, launched two Aethero satellites, generated millions of dollars of prior-company revenue, sold to Blue Origin, Booz Allen Hamilton, and AFRL, and participated in a $109,740 Air Force SBIR award. He has shipped on the satellite side and operated across the government and commercial buyer side. The solo-founder structure also makes accountability unusually clear. The strongest bull case is that this operating history, the booked launch, the spectrum position, and multinational LOIs are precisely the kind of hard evidence that can override missing deal terms. The objection stings, but the receipts mostly belong to the founder’s prior company; they do not establish that Exosat’s present customers will fund this architecture.

The capital model is the fatal gap. At the claimed $300,000 per satellite, 11,000 satellites imply $3.3 billion of spacecraft cost before launches, gateways, spectrum coordination, replacements, and network operations. Commercial service is targeted only by the end of 2028, while Exosat reports no revenue, pricing, gross margin, burn, runway, or customer prepayments. Turnkey sovereign sales could shift deployment financing to governments and operators, but that is only an inferred business model today. On the disclosed evidence, this looks less like one-and-done capital and more like a capital factory competing with Starlink, AST SpaceMobile, Lynk, and Sateliot.

My base case is that by August 2029 Exosat has flown one or a handful of satellites but has pivoted from operating a sovereign Starlink-scale network to selling satellite buses, spectrum coordination, and bespoke pilot constellations, with less than $5 million in recurring revenue. I would not write the check today. One verifiable condition flips me: a named customer signs a binding, prepaid contract large enough to fund the April 2027 on-orbit demonstration through an explicit acceptance test without another equity round. That would turn the LOI stack into owned demand and make the first launch a financed truth-revealing experiment rather than the opening installment of an enormous funding obligation.
