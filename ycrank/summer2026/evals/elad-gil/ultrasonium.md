# Ultrasonium -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: domestic manufacturers need geometries that machining and casting cannot produce, acoustic control may eliminate the powder, laser, and post-processing burden of metal additive manufacturing, and Ultrasonium becomes large only if it can repeatedly make production-qualified parts at materially lower fully loaded cost. The governing variable is therefore not whether acoustic levitation works in a laboratory. It is whether the system achieves greater than 99% density, submillimeter accuracy, and the claimed 15x throughput and 75% cost reduction across commercially relevant alloys and geometries.

The dossier identifies those thresholds, but it does not show that Ultrasonium has crossed them. The $305,000 NSF Phase I award describes density, finish, and deposition speed as anticipated results, beginning with low-temperature alloys before aluminum and copper. The 15x and 75% figures are company-reported without test methods, comparison equipment, part geometries, or production volumes. The issued patent covering acoustic and thermal modulation protects a real technical approach; it does not establish repeatability, qualification yield, machine uptime, or cost per accepted part. This leaves the primary market variable measurable but presently unvalidated.

The bull case is unusually credible for a pre-seed industrial company. Removing powders and high-powered lasers could alter both equipment cost and operating economics rather than merely attach software to commodity manufacturing. Jack Qiu brings control-hardware research, Chris Carter has metallurgy and melt-pool simulation experience, Hunter Brown has worked on ITER and CERN-related systems, and Alexander Urbanski has both founded a DOE-funded hardware company and worked at manufacturing-monitoring company Phase3D. This is a rare technical team attacking the actual physics, supported by a patent and federal research funding.

The objection that most changes my view is capitalization. Seurat has raised approximately $178 million and VulcanForms $575 million, while Ultrasonium already has 17 people and advertises 14 openings before the dossier documents a qualified part or paying customer. Frontier manufacturing can justify aggressive investment, but capital should follow a named prototype or a working scale channel. Here the proposed beachhead is small machinist shops, while the launch emphasizes aerospace, defense, energy, and compute—buyers with different materials, qualification regimes, sales cycles, and machine requirements. Broad hiring and four end markets risk funding several proofs simultaneously.

There is also no product-market pull yet. The NSF award and TigerLaunch win validate technical interest, not customer demand; no revenue, named LOI, repeat prototype use, or customer capacity constraint is reported. Nor is the future process-data moat established: the feedback models and metallurgical parameters remain Phase I objectives, and Fluent Metal can pursue similar customer economics through a different liquid-metal architecture. The relevant defensibility will come from qualified recipes, yield data, installed machines, and service history—not the general proposition that acoustic control is novel.

I would not write the check today because Ultrasonium narrowly misses both market gates: the controlling variable is clear, but the cost and performance step change remains a forecast. The known frontier-technology exception does not yet apply; this is a strong specialized team, but the dossier does not show a uniquely scarce research position plus a credible capital path sufficient to override absent demand. One verifiable flip condition would change the decision: an independent customer producing aluminum or copper parts at greater than 99% density, with documented accepted-part cost at least 50% below a named incumbent process and a paid follow-on order.
