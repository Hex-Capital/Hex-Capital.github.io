# Superserve -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Superserve could make persistent computers cheap enough that autonomous agents work for weeks without people managing their infrastructure. That is a useful enabling layer. But the dossier does not quantify how much computation, human labor, or spending disappears if it succeeds. Modal’s reported $300 million sandbox run rate establishes an existing demand pool; it does not establish Superserve’s consequence of success. I can lose my entire check on an improbable breakthrough. First I need evidence that the breakthrough changes something large enough to justify buying that option.

The technical question is whether persistence changes the economics of useful agent work. Superserve documents memory-and-disk snapshots, persistent forks, and credential brokering outside the guest, alongside claimed startup below 50 milliseconds. Those are concrete capabilities. Yet E2B already preserves memory across pauses, and its 24-hour continuous-runtime limit is different from losing state after 24 hours. Unlimited sessions could remove a binding customer constraint, but the dossier provides no matched workload showing a 5–10x advantage. I give the implementation some credit; I cannot turn a session-duration distinction into a measured technical discontinuity.

The strongest argument against passing is already running: a three-person team reports more than 100,000 machines weekly, names Lindy and YC as users, and has attracted 471 repository stars and 53 forks. This fits my pattern of taking small infrastructure teams with early technical communities seriously. Bhalla’s Dosu agent-engineering experience and LTSE platform background also make this a credible team to build the product. But machine starts do not establish retained paid workloads, and repository interest does not establish a committed developer community. The RayAI-to-Superserve transition suggests tactical flexibility; without evidence explaining what prompted it, I cannot count it as demonstrated belief revision or risk-directed recruiting.

Superserve’s largest investment uncertainty is whether customers obtain a durable economic advantage that competing sandbox providers cannot readily absorb. The documented boot/resume failures and transition conflicts identify things to measure, rather than proving the product unreliable. What is missing is a test connecting those failures, recovery costs, and long-running workload completion to a customer’s purchasing decision. The dossier supplies no ranked kill risk, experiment budget, pass/fail threshold, or stop condition. Weekly usage is encouraging, but it does not tell me how many dollars bought removal of that uncertainty.

The published prices—$0.0504 per vCPU-hour and $0.0162 per GiB-hour—make the offer inspectable. They do not reveal underlying compute costs, snapshot storage, utilization, retry overhead, or sustainable margins. Introductory credits are ordinary onboarding, not evidence of permanent subsidy dependence. Equally, inexpensive-looking compute is not proof of cost parity per completed task. My exceptions for an evolving infrastructure wedge or an initially uneconomic breakthrough require a documented instigator attacking a giant value pool, or evidence of extraordinary capability progress. This dossier establishes neither override. It also does not establish the adverse claim that Superserve is merely an AI wrapper or is scaling recklessly; my pass rests on missing affirmative evidence, not invented red flags.

I would pass with my own money today. Superserve has more evidence of a functioning infrastructure product than of a discontinuity capable of changing its customers’ economics. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independently reproducible benchmark on a paying customer’s week-long agent workload showing at least 10x lower all-in cost per successfully completed task than E2B, at matched completion quality and reliability, including storage and recovery overhead. That would convert the persistence claim into an economic mechanism worth underwriting.
