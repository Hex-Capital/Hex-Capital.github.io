# ORO AI -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine giving a shopping agent a budget and a messy instruction—find something my father will actually use—and trusting it to spend my money. Getting there requires agents that understand unstated preferences, then evaluations that distinguish that understanding from memorizing a catalog. ORO AI is working on the second step. Its replaceable environments, hidden needs, and changing inventory address a real obstacle along that path. The company’s June 2026 research reports Qwen3-4B improving from 18.0% to 42.7% on a guarded held-out partition. That is an experimental result worth following. It does not yet establish that the improvement survives contact with actual shoppers.

The behavior that catches my attention is builders doing work. ORO reported more than 345 competing agents across four completed competitions and $85,000 distributed by April 16. People wrote agents, submitted them, and competed through a functioning system. That is substantially more informative than a waitlist. But follow the money: ORO’s competition rewards builders; the dossier does not show customers paying for what those builders produce. The 4,891,831 scored trajectories are distinct agent-version/task pairs, not that many independent purchasing decisions. I want to preserve the strange competitive behavior while being precise about what it proves.

Why does this competition need to exist? Synthetic traces inherit their teacher’s biases, and production logs often lack reliable supervision. Those are specific technical failures. ORO has not yet identified an institutional toll, its beneficiary, or a measurable transfer of control to an individual. Developers might gain access to better training material, but the dossier does not establish what access currently costs or whose gatekeeping disappears. My enthusiasm for returning agency cannot do the work of evidence here.

Bansal’s distributed-training evaluation research and founding-engineer experience at Crucible Labs give him relevant context beyond a recent attraction to shopping agents. Schilbe brings AWS ECS and Cloud Map experience plus a prior marketplace venture. Their shared Cloud Map background makes the technical pairing credible. I still cannot reconstruct a dated, independently pursued obsession or see how either founder recruited the first believers. Their operating experience earns credit; the more exceptional founder story remains unproven.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that ORO fits my recurring pattern of finding infrastructure beneath a visible wave. It controls the development of its validator and evaluation environments, and it has already encountered—and responded to—agents exploiting public tasks without shopping. My exception for pre-revenue technical companies matters here: missing revenue would not stop me if the enabling asset were convincing enough. But ORO’s reported practice-to-hidden score gap, 0.71 versus 0.18, exposes how much of that asset remains unfinished. Open code can help recruit contributors, yet Prime Intellect already offers environments and reinforcement-learning infrastructure. ORO must make its continuing environment development indispensable. A corpus alone does not establish that.

I would hold my personal check today. Bittensor rewards are governed outside the company, so the participation loop depends partly on machinery the founders cannot command. That is a concrete dependency; the dossier does not establish the separate red flags of severe privacy abuse, concealed leadership, or an equal-control deadlock. My concern is whether ORO can turn rewarded competition into something another business repeatedly needs. **My flip condition is one independent shopping-agent developer paying for a second evaluation cycle after documenting improvement on its own previously unseen tasks.** That would connect the builders’ effort to durable external value and materially change my conviction.
