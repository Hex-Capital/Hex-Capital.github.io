# OpenTag -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is the team, not the product. Tony Kam, Shelden Shi, and Wilson Nguyen previously operated together at Lilac Labs, and their work across enterprise software, infrastructure, security, and regulated systems is relevant to deploying agents inside company workspaces. They have shipped together and returned to the arena. But the dossier does not show that they previously built or sold this exact workflow, nor does it identify customer outcomes from Lilac Labs or Gini. Specific knowledge is visible, but only partially corroborated.

The decisive weakness is what remains after a competitor copies the code. Anthropic already offers the same tag-an-agent interaction through Claude Tag; Dust and Lindy connect shared agents to Slack and company tools. OpenTag adds Microsoft Teams, more than 80 models, pooled credits, and workspace memory. Those are useful features, but they are not yet durable assets. The retained wiki pages, learned formats, and permissions could become switching costs, yet ten early-access teams and no retention data prove resemblance to a moat, not the moat itself.

The early adoption is real enough to keep watching: ten teams arrived in two weeks, and six organizations are displayed as doing work with the product. The economics are also legible—a $50 monthly Team plan, 5,000 pooled credits, paid top-ups, and custom enterprise pricing. But a posted price is not value capture. The dossier identifies no paying workspace, recurring revenue, completed transaction, or unit economics. OpenTag has demonstrated interest, not willingness to pay.

The market claim is similarly broad without being causal. A forecast that AI agents grow from $7.6 billion in 2025 to $182.9 billion in 2033 does not explain how a chat coworker expands into adjacent transactions. The product could become an operating layer for approvals, knowledge, and recurring workflows, but that path remains inferred. More importantly, isolated workspaces do not create the permissionless contribution loop I look for: no cohort, referral, contribution, or usage evidence shows that one user makes OpenTag more valuable or easier to distribute to the next.

The strongest bull case is the known exception represented by Notion: a cohesive, capital-efficient team can deserve backing before monetization or a visible network loop. The shared mechanism is an early collaborative tool whose accumulated user context may deepen its utility. The material difference is timing. Notion developed a strange product language before its category became legible; OpenTag entered approximately two months after Anthropic validated essentially the same interface. Ten teams may still reveal an unexpected behavior, but today they do not overcome direct incumbent substitution.

I find no documented founder-integrity problem, artificial deadline, or morale dependency, so this is not a character pass. It is a structural pass. OpenTag does not yet fit the deeper pattern of a permissionless system, fragmented supply network, or tool whose users generate compounding value for one another. My 36-month expectation is that the team retires the general Slack-and-Teams coworker and pivots toward enterprise agent permissions, approvals, and audit infrastructure, remaining below $2 million ARR by August 2029. I would reopen the investment if a six-month cohort of at least 25 paying workspaces showed over 100% net credit retention and at least 30% of runs originating from reusable workflows created by another employee.
