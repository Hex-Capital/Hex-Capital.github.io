# Fleety -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Fleety’s most consequential claim is the $500,000 annual revenue gap between a shop’s best and average technicians. Can better assignment recover that gap, or does the gap reflect skill, salesmanship, and job mix that dispatch cannot change? That distinction determines what I am buying. Shops handling 100+ jobs daily offer a real allocation problem. But redistributing lucrative jobs among technicians does not automatically increase total productive capacity. Fleety identifies an economic opportunity; it has not yet quantified an orders-of-magnitude change in the cost or availability of skilled home services.

The product attacks a sensible bottleneck: extract requirements from calls, reconcile equipment records, and send the right technician. The difficulty is that [ServiceTitan Dispatch Pro](https://help.servicetitan.com/commercial/docs/dispatch-pro-home) already assigns work using predicted job value and technician performance. Compatibility with ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, and Jobber gives Fleety a broader integration surface, but establishes no technical discontinuity. I cannot tell whether its advantage is better inference, better optimization, or founders doing difficult configuration work onsite. Those produce very different businesses. The dossier supplies neither a comparative benchmark nor evidence that accumulated outcomes improve the system.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ operating knowledge. Ben Zhou built a roofing business and Timrep; Ariyaman Molanorouzi previously automated operations across 3,000+ property units. Both worked as dispatchers. Weeks spent inside customer shops, references from Albert Nahman, Ace Plumbing, and Apex Plumbing, and reported activity across California and Florida give this team a credible route to discovering constraints that software incumbents overlook. My actual investing patterns include execution-led businesses; scientific novelty is not an absolute requirement. But these references establish access and engagement, not measured customer gains. The record also contains no documented belief revision or recruitment against a named technical weakness. I credit the operating experience without inventing those separate capabilities.

Fleety’s largest kill risk is whether it produces enough incremental economic value over native dispatch to support another vendor, its integration dependencies, and its support burden. Dispatcher review mitigates mistakes, but could also preserve much of the labor the product intends to remove. No completed experiment establishes the balance. Likewise, advance subscription billing describes collection terms, not unit economics: public pricing, onboarding hours, continuing intervention, and cost per completed job are undisclosed. These are missing evidence, not proof of a bad business. They leave the risk-removal and cost-parity dimensions at their stated defaults.

I would pass with my own money. The exception for a modest initial product opening into enormous infrastructure deserves consideration here: cross-platform dispatch could become a broader operating layer for trades. However, the dossier does not establish that expansion mechanism or a proprietary learning advantage. Nor does it establish the model-wrapper red flag outright; Fleety’s architecture is undisclosed. My objection is narrower: the demonstrated wedge overlaps an incumbent capability, while the extraordinary consequence remains unproven. One result would reopen the decision: an independently auditable, 90-day deployment across multiple shops showing a fivefold reduction in fully loaded dispatch cost per successfully completed job against the best available native workflow, including onboarding and human intervention, without reducing contribution per technician-hour or service quality. That would turn workflow promise into evidence of a cost discontinuity.
