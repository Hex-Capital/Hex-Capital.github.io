# Stratum Industries -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A permit, patent, benefit, or drug application arrives; software assembles the relevant evidence, tests it against policy, requests omissions, and gives an officer a cited decision packet. The human handles ambiguity instead of moving documents. That future is coherent. Stratum Industries has not yet mapped the road to it. The April and May 2025 federal directives explain why agencies may buy AI now, but they do not show which adjudication class becomes automatable first, which institutional constraint falls next, or what present artifact proves this future has begun.

The broken system is much more legible. Applicants lose months or years to paper, legacy software, and fragmented review, while officers repeat work that machines could prepare. Stratum preserves human authority and proposes auditable steps, which is directionally right for returning time without quietly transferring legal power to a model. But the dossier does not quantify the rent, identify who benefits from the delay, or show that faster processing survives the appeals, testing, and impact-assessment requirements governing high-impact federal AI. “The government backlog” is a symptom spanning many institutions, not yet one decoded economic mechanism.

The brothers bring an unusually useful combination for a two-person company. Raj reports 220% of quota over two years and a top-five ranking among more than 1,000 Deel representatives. Vihaan was founding engineer number three at Cadastral before its June 2026 acquisition and previously generated more than 1,000 Perplexity signups at Waterloo in 48 hours. I can see recruiting energy, technical-agent experience, and commercial ability. I cannot yet see pre-company obsession with government adjudication: neither founder has documented procurement experience, agency work, or a dated attempt to fix this particular system. Their relationship may create unusual trust, but ownership, ultimate decision authority, and a contractual tiebreaker remain diligence gates rather than facts I will assume.

The sharpest bull case is that those gaps are normal at pre-seed. The policy opening is real, competitors have validated agency demand, Raj can sell, and Vihaan can build document-oriented agents. Routing steps among different models also avoids the most naive single-model wrapper. A first agency could generate reviewed cases, rubric infrastructure, and integrations that compound into a horizontal adjudication engine.

But Stratum has not disclosed that first agency, a pilot, a proprietary case set, or even repeat shadow-mode use. GovWell already serves more than 150 agencies across 5,000 processes, while Polimorphic and Clariti each have $28 million behind adjacent workflows. Cross-domain breadth therefore increases the burden of proof: patents, disability benefits, drug approvals, and municipal permits do not merely use different forms; they encode different legal standards, error costs, appeal rights, and data-custody obligations. Human final approval and cited summaries are good promises, but they are not evidence that the machine reduces work without increasing reversals or surveillance risk.

I pass today. This looks like a real institutional wound attached to a capable team, but the strongest evidence is still the problem rather than the artifact. The verifiable flip condition is one named agency paying for a production or shadow deployment that materially shortens case-processing time while publishing audited accuracy and appeal-or-reversal rates no worse than the human baseline. That would be the small leak from the future I need.
