# Prized -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most revealing thing Prized has done is not its YC launch. It is the strange outbound experiment: the founders built target-specific tools, packed ten physical toolboxes with QR codes and fortune cookies, and delivered them around San Francisco. This is founder behavior rather than delegated marketing, and it suggests they will bend the route to reach users. But the dossier does not name the rejection or bottleneck that prompted the campaign, how quickly they responded, or whether anyone used the tool. It is an ingenious throw whose result has not been observed.

The product is stronger evidence. Prized has already assembled per-tool database roles, brokered credentials, restricted outbound traffic, audit logs, company sign-in, and approval gates for risky writes. Those are concrete pieces of working infrastructure, not a diagram of what will be built after funding. Griffith and Eliades also shipped together at Gander before its acquisition, so this is not an application-assembled team. Yet the technical claim remains self-reported: there is no measured security result, published connector count, independent test, or production deployment. This looks like a pair of fierce nerds, but the dossier proves the software more clearly than the fierceness under resistance.

The missing fact is who wants the crude product now. Two hundred twenty-three LinkedIn followers, a product video, and ten delivered boxes do not establish use. There is no named operations team repeatedly building tools, no administrator who expanded access after a pilot, and no revenue. This matters more because Prized sits directly beside Superblocks, which already offers natural-language generation, permissions, auditing, and more than 50 integrations. Retool and Lovable further prove that the category can be large, but competitor revenue is not Prized’s demand. A $58.2 billion low-code forecast is almost irrelevant until one narrow group pulls this particular product back.

The strongest bull case is that the founders have built the ugly part first. Generated software touching Salesforce, Postgres, and Zendesk creates real credential, authorization, and audit problems, and the team has treated those as infrastructure rather than disclaimers. That matches my observed preference for programmable interfaces with a difficult substrate already in place. It also avoids several red flags: distribution does not depend on a large partner, the founders are personally selling, and they have not hired salespeople to discover the customer for them. But the other recurring pattern is absent: there is no sharp pre-round proof spike, unexpected workflow, or group of technical users appropriating the primitive. The known-exception route therefore does not apply. A working product can override uncertainty when its use or independent proof is unmistakable; here neither is yet unmistakable.

Nor does the dossier establish that the idea grew from a problem the founders personally suffered. Their work at Gander and Suno makes them capable builders of agentic software, but it does not document a collision with enterprise permissions or shadow IT. There is also no before-and-after product decision showing that evidence changed the route. My base case is that within 36 months Prized has pivoted from a horizontal non-engineer app builder toward governed connectors and execution infrastructure for enterprise agents, because its strongest evidence is the control layer while its weakest is user pull for the present interface.

I would not write the check today. This is a credible product and team, not yet a logical proof of a startup. The verifiable flip condition is five named workspaces where non-engineering employees use Prized-built tools weekly and administrators subsequently broaden connector or write permissions. That would show both sides of the multi-stakeholder system pulling the product instead of the founders pushing boxes toward them.
