# Erinys -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The proposed secret is that plaintiff law firms should be rebuilt as AI-native operating companies, not improved through software sold to incumbent firms. What would prove it wrong? If capable lawyers prefer Eve or Supio while retaining ownership and control, or if Erinys-operated firms cannot acquire and resolve cases more profitably than conventional practices, the “network” is merely a services wrapper around commodity legal AI. Erinys has named the opposing model, but it has not yet reported an operating firm, completed matter, or comparative result. This is a testable secret, but still an assertion rather than a discovery.

The founders provide the strongest evidence. Zyuzin and Guo built a working investigation prototype querying 22 government databases in April 2026, and all three share a University of Maryland connection. This is more coherent than a team assembled around a fashionable market slide. Their security, OSINT, finance, and software backgrounds could produce an unusual information system for finding and prosecuting claims. Yet the company changed from False Claims Act investigation software to a general plaintiff-firm network within four months. No founder is known to be a lawyer, and the dossier does not establish that all three are full-time, retain meaningful ownership, or have operated a legal practice together. Shared history is affirmative; complete foundational alignment remains unproved.

The beachhead is intelligible: lawyers starting or converting plaintiff-side practices. Erinys would provide intake, matter flow, staff, billing, collections, compliance, capital, and software. If multiple firms generate proprietary case-selection and workflow data, the system could improve from inside the institution rather than from outside it. That resembles an unstated pattern I favor: creating the operating company and its infrastructure together. But resemblance is not a moat. Eve already serves more than 1,000 plaintiff firms and 200,000 cases, while Supio attacks the same workflow without asking lawyers to surrender operational independence. Erinys has no disclosed member firm, case volume, exclusive dataset, or measured learning advantage. Today this is a bounded market with a hypothesized monopoly mechanism, not a monopoly forming.

The economic design is less definite. Supplying working capital while participating in affiliated-firm economics could create a large revenue engine because plaintiff litigation combines high case values with fragmented operators. It could also create a coordination trap: Erinys must recruit lawyers, finance matters, supervise AI, manage collections, comply with confidentiality duties, and navigate state-by-state ownership and fee-sharing rules before proving one repeatable unit. The $396.8 billion legal-services TAM does not answer whether Erinys may legally capture the economics it claims to improve. No price, fee structure, gross margin, case acquisition cost, resolution time, or revenue has been disclosed.

Distribution is real but embryonic. The “Start a Firm” funnel and referral solicitation identify a buyer and channel, which is better than saying “enterprise sales.” They do not establish conversion, sales-cycle length, implementation burden, or whether one recruited lawyer produces enough cases to repay Erinys’s capital and operating costs. The red flag is not regulation alone; it is that regulation, financing, service delivery, and an untested channel must currently work simultaneously.

The strongest bull case is that the OSINT prototype becomes a proprietary claims-discovery engine, attracting entrepreneurial lawyers with matters already attached. That would turn distribution into production: Erinys would not merely recruit firms but manufacture advantaged firms around information others cannot see. It would also fit the pattern of adversarial information systems making contested reality legible. But the dossier never connects the 22-database prototype to current case sourcing, and none of the known-exception logic applies yet: there is no persistent transaction demand, independently verified technical discontinuity, or validated operating unit sufficient to override the regulatory and coordination stack.

I pass today. Erinys may have found a more important company than another legal copilot, but it has not shown whether it is building a last mover or an especially complicated first customer. One verifiable condition would flip me: three licensed affiliated firms operating for six months with disclosed case cohorts showing superior acquisition cost, resolution economics, and contribution margin, under a documented ownership and fee-sharing structure. Until then, the company is a secret under examination, not a monopoly under construction.
