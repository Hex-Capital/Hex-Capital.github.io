# Tsenta -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Agnay Srivastava and Pulkit Gupta submitted more than 3,000 applications themselves, then built Tsenta in their dorm and grew it from roughly 1,100 to nearly 8,000 users in two months. Tsenta is an organic-idea company with thin hacker genealogy: the founders plainly live inside the problem, but their public technical trails do not yet resemble the trails hackers usually leave. That pairing matters. Tsenta looks less like a new algorithm than two frustrated users behaving like animals around an unusually large schlep.

**Dropbox** is the high rung for organic ideas: Drew Houston kept losing his USB stick and built the thing he wanted. Tsenta has the same load-bearing mechanism because Srivastava and Gupta endured 3,000-plus applications as international students before automating the workflow across 15-plus ATS platforms. Tsenta diverges from Dropbox because the pain is obvious to thousands of competing job seekers and has already produced Jobright, Simplify, and Teal; Houston’s pain yielded a product whose simplicity concealed a harder technical problem.

The polished AI-wrapper from my pass pattern fails because credentials and generated code substitute for hacker history. Tsenta partly exhibits that failure mechanism: Srivastava’s public Unicycle repository and Gupta’s Gmail animation each have zero stars, while Srivastava says Claude Code generated the entire three-month MVP. But Tsenta is not merely a deck wrapped around an API. Tsenta monitors 50,000-plus career pages, operates on web, mobile, messaging, Chrome, and MCP/CLI surfaces, and survived a rewrite after users said the local-browser version looked like a virus. The GitHub genealogy is weak; the shipped artifact is real.

Nearly 8,000 users after two months can compound Tsenta’s resourcefulness if every broken ATS form becomes another manual repair the founders turn into software. Tsenta already responded to a trust failure by replacing the local-browser architecture with cloud execution, which is more informative than an internship résumé. Over 18 months, the animal test will be whether Tsenta keeps repairing Workday-shaped potholes, chasing application failures, and onboarding users after LinkedIn or Indeed blocks a route. The current evidence is well above the absent-evidence default, though it is not yet an Airbnb cereal-box stunt because no paid-user count or verified outcome cohort is disclosed.

Rose-Hulman gave Tsenta a two-founder configuration with shared context, complementary CEO/CTO roles, and enough prior relationship to build together during internships and launch from a dorm. Tsenta would move into the top rung if Srivastava and Gupta had already built together for several years or could name a prior non-trivial artifact they jointly shipped before Tsenta. It would fall a tier if the student-network dinner was effectively founder dating and one founder proves incidental; the dossier presently shows more shared work than that, but not a Stripe-like lifetime collaboration.

**Coinbase** is the useful parallel for Tsenta’s schlep. Coinbase became valuable partly because Brian Armstrong accepted compliance work other hackers unconsciously avoided; Tsenta accepts the brittle, repetitive work of maintaining submissions across 50,000 career pages and 15-plus ATS platforms. Tsenta’s version is less defensible because LinkedIn and Indeed can prohibit automation rather than merely make it unpleasant, and Jobright already performs end-to-end applications. Still, Tsenta’s sensitive-data handling, ATS breakage, sponsorship filtering, and hostile platform policies are genuine schleps, not imagined “execution risk.”

The 50,000-page integration footprint and weak public hacker trail imply that within 36 months Tsenta will pivot from indiscriminate auto-application volume to outcomes-linked placement for international students and reach $3 million to $7 million in annual recurring revenue. Tsenta’s organic founder pain should keep acquisition and product instincts alive, while the commoditization of form-filling—and Tsenta’s inability to observe employment outcomes directly—will force the product toward verified interviews, sponsorship eligibility, and recruiter relationships.

Jobright’s $1 million of 2024 revenue, Tsenta’s Claude Code origin, and LinkedIn’s prohibition on automated extensions form the strongest bear case: Tsenta may be a rapidly copied wrapper whose apparent usage reflects desperate applicants consuming 25 free submissions rather than customers receiving value. Airbnb is the uncomfortable exception to that argument because an initially dubious product can be rescued by unusually resourceful founders, and Tsenta’s 1,100-to-8,000 growth plus the virus-triggered rewrite are real animal tracks. Tsenta wins my check because those tracks outweigh the weak genealogy today. I would flip to Pass if a disclosed paid cohort of at least 1,000 users retains fewer than 25% after three months.
