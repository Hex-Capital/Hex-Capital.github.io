# Peer -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: production-grade voice and text agents now let freight brokers automate coordination, shippers buy a managed quote-through-delivery service, and the controlling variable is whether automation can reduce labor per load without degrading exception resolution. Peer has not yet supplied the evidence needed to test that variable. It reports neither human touches per shipment nor contribution margin, service reliability, shipment volume, or even a target threshold. In a $21.28 billion U.S. brokerage market, market size is not the constraint; proving structurally better brokerage economics is.

The timing is plausible but no longer undiscovered. FleetWorks launched an always-on dispatcher in October 2025, Cartage already automates phone, email, tracking, and escalation, and more than 40% of brokers reportedly plan AI or machine-learning deployments in 2026. These facts establish a real technology shift. They do not establish that Peer benefits disproportionately from it. FleetWorks already reports 10,000 carriers, relationships with more than 15 of the top 100 brokers, and over $1 million in annual revenue. Peer has disclosed no shipper channel, carrier network, or workflow difference capable of overcoming those distribution advantages.

The strongest bull case is that Aaron Zhang built trucking voice agents at Waylens, Felix Peng shipped internal AI tools used by more than 250 LADWP staff, and together they are attempting the entire brokerage loop—quoting, carrier selection, tracking, and exception handling—rather than selling a narrow assistant. Better models could improve each step while completed loads generate lane-pricing and carrier-reliability data. Zhang taking leave from Harvey Mudd is also real commitment. A two-person technical team with some domain contact can discover the right wedge unusually quickly.

But the current evidence supports capability, not an owned workflow. Peer was still described as screen-recorded workflow automation in May 2026 and had pivoted to freight brokerage by August. There is no public evidence of shipments, repeat usage, integrations, operating authority, the required $75,000 bond, or performance on in-transit exceptions. The proposed data moat is circular: proprietary operating data becomes valuable after Peer wins volume, while volume is precisely where established carrier networks already confer an advantage. A stronger base model also helps every broker and automation vendor; without proprietary workflow state or distribution, it commoditizes Peer as readily as it improves it.

No known exception rescues the case today. This is not frontier research supported by a uniquely scarce technical team, nor a physical-market reconstruction with documented product-style margins. It is a recent pivot into a crystallizing category where competitors already demonstrate the same technical direction. My 36-month base case is another pivot—most plausibly from principal freight brokerage into agent software sold to existing brokers—rather than a standalone brokerage reaching meaningful scale.

I would pass now. The verifiable flip condition is 100 completed loads for at least three repeat shippers, accompanied by evidence that Peer resolves exceptions at equal or better service levels with at least 50% fewer human touches per load than a conventional broker. That would simultaneously validate the governing economic variable, early product-market pull, and the claimed end-to-end workflow.
