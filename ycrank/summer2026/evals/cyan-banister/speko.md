# Speko -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Voice software is multilingual, ambient, and embedded in ordinary businesses; developers do not manually rebake three vendors whenever a model changes. Speko can plausibly become the control plane for that world. But the dossier gives me the destination without the road: no account of which institutions adopt voice first, how routing requirements compound, or why an independent router captures the value rather than becoming a feature inside LiveKit, Vapi, or OpenRouter. Tracking 23 speech-to-text models and finding four different winners across nine languages is a real leak from the future. It is not yet a map of it.

Beknazar Abdikamalov is the strongest part of this company. He has worked on voice technology since 2019 and spent four years manually comparing providers in prior products before building Speko. That is operating pain becoming product specification, not a founder discovering conversational AI during a fashionable fundraising cycle. His Amazon engineering experience, prior Hupo CTO role, and four-person team support technical and recruiting competence. What I cannot yet see is the full Rizz-Tizz-Biz loop: who followed him before the opportunity became legible, what he built outside his assigned jobs, and whether he understands how a 5% routing surcharge becomes a durable business.

The irritating system is clear but the rent is slippery. Voice developers repeatedly test speech recognition, language models, and synthesis vendors; Speko converts that work into one API, public language-specific benchmarks, and pre-response failover. It returns deployment control and engineering time. Yet no gatekeeper is imposing this waste, and the dossier does not quantify the testing burden or production losses. If provider switching is merely annoying, LiveKit and OpenRouter can absorb it. If bad routing causes measurable failed calls, regulatory exposure, or lost revenue, then Speko is repairing infrastructure rather than selling convenience.

There is a working artifact: open SDKs, LiveKit and Pipecat adapters, a hosted MCP server, first voice-agent partners, and one commercial pilot. The public benchmarks also expose a genuinely non-obvious behavior—model quality changes by language. But two GitHub stars on each principal SDK, 12 Launch YC votes, and no disclosed repeat usage or paid consumption do not show developers entrusting production traffic to the router. The commercial pilot matters; it does not yet demonstrate costly or weird user behavior.

The bull case stings because language fragmentation may outrun every bundled platform. A neutral routing layer could continuously arbitrate latency, accuracy, cost, and availability while applications proliferate, and Abdikamalov encountered that need years before founding Speko. The bear case is more structural: Speko controls neither the models nor the network, publishes its benchmark data, and does not train on customer traffic. LiveKit already routes providers, OpenRouter already handles speech and transcription, and routed audio inherits each provider’s privacy, retention, and compliance terms. That last point is a gate for me. “We do not train on it” is not a consent and custody architecture.

I am neutral and would not write the check today. The founder has earned another meeting, but the company has not shown that it owns the bottleneck beneath voice AI rather than a useful comparison layer inside someone else’s stack. My verifiable flip condition is three production customers each routing at least 100,000 minutes per month for six consecutive months, with documented workload-specific performance gains and enforceable provider-level data-retention controls. That would prove both dependency and trust.
