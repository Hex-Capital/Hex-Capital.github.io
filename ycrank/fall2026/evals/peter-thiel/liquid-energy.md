# Liquid Energy -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Liquid Energy’s possible secret is that useful inference capacity can be delivered faster by bringing a complete solar-powered system to the customer than by bringing electricity to a conventional data center. Its advertised eight-week contract-to-first-token timeline makes part of that proposition testable. But bypassing the grid is already an industry response to scarcity: JLL describes “bring your own power” mandates, and Rune attaches compute to existing solar generation. The unanswered question is what Liquid Energy knows about carrying the solar array that those alternatives have missed. The dossier establishes a hypothesis, without identifying a rejected consensus or an experiment that validates it. I assign the secret criterion its absent-evidence default.

Who would choose NanoDC even if competing inference capacity were available? “Companies of every size” does not identify an initial market the company can dominate. An unfolding array distinguishes the package from Rune’s approach, but a different configuration does not establish proprietary technology, network effects, or scale economies. The claimed modules and cooling technology lack independent performance measurements. Crusoe supplies another substitution route through managed inference and modular infrastructure. These offerings do not prove NanoDC is interchangeable; they make ownership of a narrow market the central unresolved investment question.

The strongest opposing case is that Liquid Energy fits the shift toward owning the physical AI production stack. Four-year grid waits create an unusually expensive constraint, and factory completion could remove considerable deployment coordination. **Panthalassa** provides a relevant exception to my skepticism about integrated energy-and-compute systems: both combine physical infrastructure to escape power scarcity. The material difference is the specific pilot-factory and deployment path supporting that exception. Liquid Energy’s dossier provides no comparable operating result, while its terms explicitly leave advertised specifications subject to development. That is neither a disproved technical claim nor sufficient evidence to override the coordination risk.

The founders have relevant pieces of experience, but those pieces answer different questions. Ravikumar’s software background supports compute execution; Khosa’s three brokerage signings at AJMER show prior selling experience. Neither establishes the founders’ shared working history, full-time status, ownership, or control of core engineering, so founding alignment receives the stated default. Likewise, an inquiry email identifies a sales contact without demonstrating a repeatable acquisition route. No public contract pricing, utilization, gross margins, or customer expansion establishes how one inference-capacity engine could produce a power-law outcome. YC participation and NVIDIA Inception affiliation cannot supply that arithmetic.

I pass on writing a check from this dossier. Missing revenue at pre-seed is ordinary; the decisive uncertainty is whether the integrated hardware creates an economic advantage that Liquid Energy can retain. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently audited, customer-paid NanoDC deployment that meets pre-agreed availability and cost-per-token requirements after delivery within the advertised eight weeks. That result would turn the central claim into operating evidence and justify investigating whether a monopoly can follow.
