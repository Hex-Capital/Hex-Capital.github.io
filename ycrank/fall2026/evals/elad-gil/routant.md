# Routant -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Routant’s market turns on one variable: whether a reusable authorization layer lets agent vendors deploy into customer systems materially faster than negotiating access separately for every deployment. Vendors integrate once; customers retain control over resources, approvals, and audits. That is a coherent mechanism for turning enterprise security from a deployment bottleneck into infrastructure. The dossier supports the bottleneck: 69% of 150 decision-makers in an Okta-commissioned January 2026 survey said security concerns slowed agent adoption. It does not establish how much friction Routant removes or whether customers accept its trust model.

The strongest bull case is that authorization becomes a distribution channel. An agent vendor could bring Routant into successive customers, and each customer could reuse its policies across successive agents. If that happens, better models increase the volume of work passing through the product. My pattern of backing infrastructure across a technology discontinuity supports looking here. But this distribution loop remains a hypothesis: the dossier provides no deployed integrations, customer reuse, or verified enforcement architecture. Stated approvals and audit trails do not yet establish ownership of the operational loop.

The competitive question is already specific. Keycard documents policy-bound credentials and delegation chains; Arcade documents action-level authorization, approvals, and audits. Keycard disclosed $38 million of financing, while Arcade disclosed $72 million before its September 2026 extension. Those figures establish financed alternatives, not winning products. They nevertheless mean Routant needs to explain why vendor-to-customer deployment is a distinct buying decision those alternatives cannot serve efficiently. The category is crystallizing, and the public evidence does not yet identify Routant’s wedge.

There is also a difference between proving demand for security and proving an economic step change from this product. The survey establishes the former. Routant has no verified deployment-time reduction, implementation cost, pricing, or gross-margin evidence establishing the latter. The adjacent market estimate—$210 million in 2026, projected to reach $1.02 billion in 2031—cannot substitute for an addressable revenue model. I would give the company neither a software-economics premium nor a services penalty without that evidence.

No public traction or founder information is available, so those dimensions receive the stated missing-evidence defaults. The reported team size of zero and inaccessible website are research limitations, not evidence of an empty company or weak commitment. I also cannot establish an exception based on rare frontier research, scarce model-building talent, or demonstrated reconstruction of physical-world economics. Conversely, nothing here establishes the frozen-model, invented-data-moat, or founder-liquidity red flags. The concern is competitive value capture, rather than documented misconduct or technical failure.

I would pass on a personal check from this dossier. The access bottleneck is credible, but Routant has not connected its proposed mechanism to a measurable advantage over available substitutes. My single flip condition is a customer-verified production deployment showing that Routant’s reusable authorization integration materially shortens launch time versus a named alternative and is subsequently reused for another agent deployment. That would turn the central market belief into evidence and give me a reason to revisit the company.
