# Veeza AI -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Aly Moursy has done something more informative than describe a painful visa application: he shipped a product other people use. Veeza AI opened its closed beta on April 19 and public beta on June 7, then reported growing from 1,000 to 7,000+ users. His Egyptian-passport experience supplies context; the working product supplies evidence of specific knowledge. The 400-person WhatsApp community and 10,000+ feedback messages show sustained contact with the problem ([SceneNow](https://scenenow.com/Business/Veeza-AI-Wants-to-Make-Global-Mobility-Easier-for-the-Global-South)). That earns substantial founder credit. His earlier product roles add little without evidence that they produced an asset transferable to this business.

The investment question is what Veeza keeps when another team reproduces the portal automation. Document checking, form completion, appointment booking, and human review already overlap with competitors’ capabilities. Stored passports offer convenience, but the dossier establishes neither meaningful switching costs nor exclusive learning from applications. Route-specific exception handling could become an asset; today it is a hypothesis. Portal changes and appointment shortages remain constraints imposed by systems Veeza does not control. Automation can reduce work without creating ownership of anything scarce.

Veeza resembles one pattern I like: replacing fragmented offline intermediaries with software that gives individuals more agency. But it has not demonstrated the compounding mechanism behind that pattern. Seven thousand applicants are seven thousand potential customers; the dossier does not show that applicant 7,001 receives a better service because the others participated. Feedback sent to founders is useful product research, but there is no measured contribution, referral, or matching loop. Required human review also leaves open how much labor accompanies each additional application. I assign no network credit beyond the absent-evidence default.

The strongest argument for investing is that this team is already getting paid to discover the difficult exceptions. Reported paying customers and published fees of EGP 1,500 for several Schengen destinations and EGP 4,000 for UK/US applications establish direct value capture ([pricing](https://www.veeza.ai/en)). They do not establish margins, particularly after review, retries, acquisition, and refunds. The expansion path is credible enough to matter: the same application workflow can serve additional passport-and-destination routes and travel agencies. Visa2Fly’s existing agent channel and Atlys’s reported operating revenue establish spending beyond Veeza’s initial Egyptian consumer wedge. They also establish competition. The broader market estimate alone would earn little credit.

I would consider an exception to the missing network if extraordinary founder execution or an unusually original product supplied the asymmetry. Veeza’s dated launches and adoption make that objection serious. Yet the dossier shows neither exceptional capital efficiency nor a differentiated technical asset sufficient to override the durability gap. Arabic/English assistance and local knowledge may help win customers, but there is no evidence yet that they become harder to reproduce with scale. I see no documented integrity or artificial financing-pressure veto; the reservation concerns the business structure.

At **51/100, I am neutral**. Veeza AI has demonstrated more founder knowledge and willingness to pay than its defensibility score captures, but I would not write a personal check on the assumption that application volume inevitably becomes a moat. My flip condition is a measured proprietary exception system that cuts human review minutes per completed application by at least half across three successive cohorts, without worsening application accuracy. That would show customer activity producing an accumulating asset rather than simply another queue of work.
