# Omanta -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The decisive fact is not that Omanta is trying something difficult. It is that the dossier contains no episode in which difficulty met a founder. I cannot identify who encountered the problem, what broke, what the four-person team built in response, or how quickly anything changed. The website was inaccessible during research, and the domain appeared in a July 2026 sale listing. That does not prove the company is inactive, but it leaves the resourcefulness test unanswered. At this stage, founder adjectives would not help. I need the obstacle and the workaround.

The proposed service has real schlep in it. Coordinating tissue, laboratories, medical records, multiomic analysis, specialists, and a living clinician roadmap is precisely the sort of administratively ugly work that software founders often avoid. But a description of ugly work is not evidence that Omanta has performed it. There is no completed patient case, functioning clinician interface, laboratory partner, validation study, CLIA-qualified workflow, or independently measured result. The FDA classification question and genomic-data obligations make a working proof more important, not less.

The bull case is that “a research lab for one” could be a narrow well disguised as a service business. Patients with recurrence, no diagnosis, or exhausted standard options may have unusually urgent demand and tolerate a crude, labor-intensive product. Falling sequencing costs make individualized analysis more feasible, and the adjacent markets are large: precision oncology was estimated at $115.8 billion in 2024 and rare-disease diagnostics at $21.6 billion. If even a few families repeatedly paid for case coordination and treating clinicians acted on the resulting roadmap, the manual work could reveal the software system Omanta should become.

But the dossier names no patient, clinician, payment, LOI, repeat use, referral, or outcome. Nor does it disclose pricing or whether the customer is a family, provider, laboratory, or institution. This matters because Tempus already generated $1.2718 billion in 2025 while covering molecular testing and AI insights, and xCures and Massive Bio occupy adjacent record-structuring and trial-access workflows. Omanta’s broader scope is differentiated only as prose. Without patient pull or a measured artifact, breadth can mean merely that the company has combined several hard businesses before learning which one users need.

The same absence defeats the exception case. There is no founder-originated collision with disease, no numerical biological result, and no evidence that the team has revised its route after a failed hypothesis. Pre-seed companies need not have revenue or publicity, but they do need some inspectable fact that could not have been produced by writing a category description. Omanta currently offers no such fact. My 36-month expectation would be a pivot toward a narrower clinician-facing case-research or records-coordination service, if the company survives, because the full patient-specific program combines too many regulated and operational steps before demand has selected the valuable one.

I would pass now. The one verifiable condition that flips me is five completed patient programs in which named treating clinicians use Omanta’s roadmap, with at least three families or institutions paying and one documented diagnostic or therapeutic decision changing because of the work. That single result would simultaneously reveal real users, a functioning product, and founders willing to cross the schlep rather than describe it.
