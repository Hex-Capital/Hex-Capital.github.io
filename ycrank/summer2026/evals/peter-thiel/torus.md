# Torus -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Torus’s contested truth is that the costly failures in physical engineering are not primarily failures to retrieve documents, but failures to model every parameter and dependency across the entire project. The falsifier is straightforward: deploy on live engineering packages and show that its project-wide map does not catch consequential inconsistencies beyond what existing review processes and document-intelligence tools find. Torus has built more than 50 categories of checks and is deploying with Fortune 500 companies and major EPCs, but the dossier reports neither independently verified errors caught nor avoided rework. This is a plausible secret, not yet a proved one.

The narrow wedge—consistency checking for data-center and energy-infrastructure projects—is real. The monopoly mechanism is not. Trunk Tools already structures and proactively analyzes construction documents, while Transcend, Augmenta, and SWAPP automate adjacent design and documentation workflows after collectively raising more than $147 million. Torus discloses no exclusive data, measured technical advantage, retention, or dominant share. Customer-specific parameter maps and approval histories could eventually create switching costs, but a possible future moat is not a present moat. Today this is differentiated software inside a competitive category.

The strongest evidence is distribution. Torus asks an EPC or project owner for five or six current-project documents, produces a demonstration in 15 minutes, and then offers managed-cloud or sovereign deployment. That route matches the security and implementation burdens of large engineering customers better than an abstract “PLG” strategy. Multiple Fortune 500 deployments suggest the founders have crossed an unusually difficult pre-seed access barrier. But without customer names, contract values, deployment counts, renewal evidence, or sales-cycle length, I cannot distinguish repeatable enterprise distribution from several labor-intensive design partnerships.

Marcus Lima brings energy-systems training and experience founding the capital-intensive direct-air-capture company Heimdal; Rahul Thayil previously built and financed the AI risk-intelligence company Orion. Both now publicly identify with Torus, and no part-time founder, outsourced core team, excessive founder liquidity, or investor-heavy capitalization is documented. Those are meaningful positives. Yet there is no public evidence of a long working relationship, founder ownership, compensation, or how the five-person team divides core engineering. Two relevant repeat founders are better than a convenience-matched team, but missing foundational evidence is not proof of alignment.

The power-law case remains indefinite. The broader AEC software market may reach $24.3 billion by 2032, but the cited capital-project-management segment reaches only $4.24 billion by 2033, and Torus provides no pricing, gross margins, expansion arithmetic, or evidence that one product can become the system of record. My 36-month base case is $3 million–$8 million of ARR concentrated in customized enterprise deployments, with useful workflow software but no demonstrated category control. The bull case stings: if early Fortune 500 customers are already entrusting live infrastructure packages to Torus despite accuracy, security, and adoption friction, the company may be accumulating the proprietary project graph before competitors recognize that this—not document chat—is the valuable layer.

I still pass today. The customer access is informative, but it does not override the failed monopoly gate because no disclosed deployment proves either a zero-to-one technical result or compounding customer behavior. One verifiable event would flip the decision: a paid Fortune 500 customer renewing and expanding Torus from one project to at least three after independently documenting more than $1 million of avoided rework attributable to cross-document inconsistencies competitors missed. That would convert the secret from assertion to proof and show the beginnings of a proprietary data loop. Until then: valuable product, plausible secret, commodity risk.
