# Grip -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Grip’s wet-organics robot makes Grip a schlep-rich but cofounder-evidence-poor bet: the work is unusually unpleasant, while only Jonas Gruetter is identified despite a four-person headcount. **Coinbase** is the high rung for this kind of schlep because compliance improved through contact with reality. Grip has the analogous loop in physical form: every successful and failed grasp can improve its operating dataset. But Grip diverges where Coinbase already had Armstrong’s working wallet. Grip discloses no deployed fleet, throughput, accuracy, or uptime. The schlep is real; Grip has not yet proved that it can survive the schlep.

Grip’s four-person headcount risks the polished-AI-wrapper failure mechanism from my pass case: people assembled around an idea are not necessarily cofounders who compound under pressure. Grip names Jonas Gruetter but provides no cofounder identities, prior working history, or evidence that two or three founders have built together for years. This is not evidence of bad chemistry. It is no evidence—so I apply the absent-evidence default. Grip may have a strong pair or trio hidden behind the sparse dossier, but headcount alone does not establish one.

Jonas Gruetter helped field-test reinforcement-learning control on a 12-ton excavator that achieved 70% boulder-removal success. That is an artifact, not a credential, and it moves Grip well above founders whose biographies merely say ETH Zürich. Over the next 18 months, this resourcefulness compounds only if Grip converts research competence into dirty facility work: clogged grippers, changing feedstock, retrofit delays, and operators who do not care how elegant the model is. Grip has disclosed founder-led outreach but no installation or unscalable customer stunt. Gruetter looks like an animal of a roboticist; Grip has not yet shown the animal salesperson or operator.

Jonas Gruetter’s background in irregular-object manipulation makes Grip adjacent to his own work, but the dossier never says he personally encountered plastic contamination in compost or built Grip for a workflow he suffered through. Grip’s organic-idea score would jump a tier if Gruetter found the problem while operating machinery at Gravis Robotics or Ursa Mining and built the first wet-waste picker before anyone requested it. Without that missing causal link, Grip resembles a technically informed opportunity more than Drew Houston forgetting his USB stick. Domain fit is present. Founder-pain fit is not yet established.

**Stripe** had hacker genealogy visible before the company: the Collisons had already built Auctomatic and Patrick’s Lisp trail was unmistakable. Grip has a credible industrial version of that signal in Gruetter’s sparse-LiDAR, reinforcement-learning, and heavy-machinery research. A 12-ton excavator working in the field is more persuasive than a polished GitHub profile. Still, Grip’s organization has one follower and no public repositories, and no Grip-specific demonstration is reported. Grip sits closer to Stripe’s artifact-first pattern than to a credential-only robotics pitch, but the lineage currently belongs to Gruetter’s prior research rather than to the product.

Zero disclosed deployments lead to a deliberately bounded forecast: by July 2029, Grip will remain independent with three to eight paid wet-organics installations and roughly $500,000 to $1.5 million in annual recurring service and software revenue. Grip will win a small number of facilities because wet feedstock creates enough contamination, maintenance, and end-effector pain to deter generic sorting vendors. Grip will grow slowly because every installation will behave partly like a robotics project. This is schlep blindness producing a foothold, not yet a default platform.

ZenRobotics has operated since 2007, Recycleye has raised $24.6 million including government funding, and EverestLabs already sells monitored multi-arm cells. The strongest bear case is that Grip has mistaken an unserved segment for an unattractive one: wet waste may destroy grippers, ruin vision, and support too little equipment spending to justify bespoke hardware. Grip’s inaccessible website, empty public GitHub organization, and missing performance data make that objection sting. The evidence that keeps me from passing is Gruetter’s real field robotics artifact and Grip’s choice of the filthiest stream rather than the easiest demo. Airbnb and Loopt show that founder evidence can override an initially weak-looking market, but Grip has not produced their override signal yet. If Grip cannot show one paid facility pilot with measured uptime and pick-rate data by July 2027, I would flip to Pass.
