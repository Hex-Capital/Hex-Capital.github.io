# Osmaura -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely the first scarce check. Osmaura may be cheap and ignored, but the dossier does not establish either fact. YC attention—1,144 reactions and 131 comments on Woldemichael’s announcement—is not an auction, yet it is certainly not evidence of non-consensus pricing. I cannot underwrite scarcity from silence.

The product has a clean underlying mechanism. Law-firm partners reportedly spend at least ten hours weekly hunting through relationships without knowing which companies are nearing a legal need. Osmaura watches public events, combines them with firm context, and proposes timely outreach. Reported daily customer logins suggest the workflow causes repeat behavior, which is the company’s strongest evidence. But usage is not yet the same as economic causality: no customer count, outreach-to-meeting conversion, matter attribution, revenue, or proprietary feedback corpus is disclosed. LinkedIn attention is rented distribution, not a dark pool.

The bull case is real and stings. Two technical founders have built something lawyers reportedly use daily in a notoriously trust-sensitive workflow; months of partner interviews may have found a wedge that Nexl and Intapp overlooked. If every accepted recommendation produces labeled data linking a corporate event, partner relationship, outreach, and eventual matter, Osmaura could accumulate a compact dataset incumbents cannot reconstruct from public signals alone. That could override ordinary AI-wrapper skepticism just as documented conversion can override weak initial gate evidence.

The current evidence does not earn that exception. Nexl already serves more than 150 law firms and combines CRM data, relationships, and business-development workflows; Intapp owns another natural product surface. Both can add public-event monitoring. Osmaura therefore needs to prove that its private outcome loop—not the agent interface or public-data ingestion—causes purchases. The disclosed two-month negotiation with a major firm that Osmaura ultimately abandoned shows how easily enterprise activity becomes deal-count theater rather than cash.

The capital plan is also undefined. There is no named hypothesis, deadline, pass/fail metric, or explanation of what this round uniquely funds. “Customers log in daily” could support a finite experiment, but it is not one: the card to flip is whether surfaced opportunities create attributable, paid matters at a repeatable rate. Economics are similarly absent—no contract value, gross margin, burn, runway, or route to break-even—so deleting the next financing from the model leaves nothing measurable.

Abeje and Woldemichael bring credible ML and software training from MIT, AWS, Meta, Jane Street, and research, but neither has documented responsibility for selling legal services, running law-firm business development, or shipping a prior commercial system. Interviews provide access; they do not prove an outsider-insider translation advantage. My base case is that within 36 months Osmaura either becomes a narrow signal feature inside a legal CRM or is acquired by Nexl for less than $25 million, rather than reaching $10 million ARR independently. I would flip to investing upon one verifiable result: at least five law firms paying for the product and documenting that Osmaura-sourced outreach produced attributable new matters, with the conversion measured from signal to collected revenue.
