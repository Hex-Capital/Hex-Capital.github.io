# Waddle Labs -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is: stronger coding agents can now generate and repair robot programs, robotics developers may buy an adaptation layer instead of collecting new data for every task, and the controlling variable is whether Waddle can reduce the cost and time of deploying a reliable task across heterogeneous robots by an order of magnitude. The dossier identifies the mechanism but not the threshold. Six months of internal operation and roughly 1,000 overnight LEGO repetitions show that the loop runs; they do not establish that it replaces engineering work under production conditions.

The product architecture is the strongest part of the company. Waddle inspects camera feeds, decomposes goals, writes control code, invokes VLA models, tests execution, replans after failure, and preserves an editable program. This owns more workflow than a model wrapper, while the accumulated skill library and intervention traces could improve with deployment. Better foundation models should expand the tasks it can complete. The weakness is value capture: code-as-policy research is public, CaP-X and ASPIRE already use execution-feedback loops, and a well-funded competitor could reproduce six months of internal traces without extraordinary cost.

The strongest bull case is a frontier-technology exception: Song has program-induction and multimodal-model experience, Ye has robot-policy research, and competing approaches have attracted more than $3 billion across Skild AI and Physical Intelligence. This resembles the technical discontinuity behind the research-stage **Anthropic** exception, but the material difference is that Waddle does not control a scarce foundation layer or document an exceptional capital path. It currently sits above third-party models in a $1.671 billion robot-programming software market, so rare technical talent alone does not override the market gate.

There is no external product-market pull yet. The preview API, 971 X followers, and internal robot repetitions demonstrate availability and technical persistence, not customer adoption. No revenue, named deployment, repeat external usage, pricing, or independently observed capacity constraint appears. Nor does the dossier quantify the economic step change: Waddle may reduce retraining and redevelopment, but it does not show hours saved, deployment cost, success rate, intervention frequency, or gross-margin consequences. Autonomous code safety and unspecified guardrails further separate a laboratory demonstration from an industrial purchase.

The founders are unusually relevant technically for their age, but the biography does not document desperation, a prior startup lesson, or costly commitment beyond sustained development. The shift from 2025 teleoperation and data-collection infrastructure to the current agent-control product may indicate learning, but the unexplained co-founder and product discontinuity prevent affirmative credit. My unstated pattern of buying broad coverage across a discontinuity helps the category, while the red flags cut the other way: dependence on third-party reasoning budgets, an inexpensive-to-reproduce data claim, and no evidence that capital would scale a proven system rather than fund broad exploration.

I would not invest today. My 36-month base case is that Waddle pivots back toward robot data collection and evaluation infrastructure or becomes an acqui-hire rather than reaching $5 million ARR as a general control layer. One result would flip the decision: three external industrial customers renewing paid deployments after six months while reporting at least a 10× reduction in task-deployment time across multiple robot types. That would validate the controlling variable, establish pull, and turn the execution traces into a potentially compounding asset.
