# STASIS -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The proposition worth testing is that measuring an individual’s ocular state enables automatic adjustments that reduce eye strain beyond what ordinary brightness controls, filtering glasses, and break reminders achieve. That would give STASIS a reason to exist. A controlled comparison showing no incremental benefit would falsify it. But this is my reconstruction of the opportunity: the dossier supplies neither a named prevailing belief the founders reject nor an independent test of their opposing claim. The founder’s 30-day wear test demonstrates personal experimentation. It does not establish the secret.

The founding relationship is the strongest evidence here. Soham Chakraborty and Neil Goradia began working together during freshman year, and Berkeley independently identifies both on GlaucoGlasses. They have collaborated on an actual ocular-device project; this is more informative than two impressive biographies assembled around an application. Their bioengineering and EECS backgrounds make the integrated product credible enough to investigate. Full-time status, ownership, and economic exposure remain unknown. Student status does not establish part-time commitment, and there is no documented convenience matching or investor-dominated capitalization.

What would STASIS own if the product worked? “Screen users” describes an enormous population without identifying a small market the company could dominate. The claimed sensor patents have no verified identifiers or scope. Longitudinal measurements might become proprietary infrastructure, but the dossier supplies no evidence that accumulated data improves predictions. IXI and Emteq establish adjacent technical activity, not that STASIS is interchangeable with their products. I therefore give the monopoly mechanism its absent-evidence default rather than treating competition as a demonstrated fatal flaw. Still, combining sensing, tinting, and screen controls establishes an integration task; it does not yet establish an enduring advantage.

The strongest argument against passing is that these founders have already chosen and worked inside a difficult physical problem. Their shared device-development history and sustained personal testing could precede a useful invention that public research has barely captured. My actual investing patterns include consumer products and physical technology; neither category is a reason to reject STASIS. But the exceptions that justify financing complex systems require some decisive technical result, deployed customer behavior, or validated production path. None is documented here. The seven distributor LOIs totaling $168,000 concern GlaucoGlasses and cannot provide that override for STASIS. Nor does the dossier establish a revised foundational claim or regulatory prohibition; the concern is unverified performance and delivery, not demonstrated misconduct or technical failure.

The commercial structure also remains provisional. A hardware sale followed by optional $15 monthly tracking is a coherent engine, but conflicting $95 and $240 preorder prices, unknown margins, and unknown subscription attachment prevent credible outcome arithmetic. Website preorder solicitation and LinkedIn demonstrations provide a concrete acquisition route, earning modest distribution credit; conversations with Kaiser Optometry leadership do not establish a repeatable channel. I would pass on writing a personal check today. One verifiable event would reopen the decision: an independently run controlled trial showing sustained, meaningful strain reduction against ordinary settings and reminders. That would establish that STASIS has discovered something worth building a company around.
