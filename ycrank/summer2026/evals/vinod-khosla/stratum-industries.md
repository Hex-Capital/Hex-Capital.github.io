# Stratum Industries -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

If Stratum Industries works, millions of permit, patent, drug-approval, grant, and benefits applications could move in days rather than months or years. That is a consequential expert-service bottleneck. But the dossier never converts “millions” into adjudicator hours, annual spending, affected applicants, or a measured compression ratio. The $34.4 billion smart-government TAM is a category statistic, not the consequence of this mechanism. I can imagine a 100x outcome; Stratum has not quantified one.

The product reads applications, applies agency rubrics, routes steps among models, requests missing information, and produces cited summaries for human officers. Useful, but where is the discontinuity? There is no benchmark against human adjudication or a named incumbent system—no throughput, accuracy, appeal rate, cost per case, or completed-case evidence. GovWell already serves more than 150 agencies and 5,000 processes, while Polimorphic and Clariti sell overlapping AI workflows. With no deployed customer, proprietary dataset, or demonstrated technical moat, this currently looks more like systems integration around prevailing models than a new capability.

The strongest bull case is real. Government already bears an enormous, unavoidable workflow burden; April and May 2025 federal policies explicitly accelerated AI procurement and permitting modernization. Vihaan brings document-agent experience from Cadastral, which was acquired by Legora in June 2026. Raj brings exceptional enterprise selling evidence—220% of quota over two years and a top-five ranking among more than 1,000 representatives. A technical builder plus an unusually effective seller can discover the right narrow wedge faster than domain incumbents expect. This is the plausible exception: a small, adaptable team entering a large existing value pool before its initial product looks technically profound.

But an exception requires evidence of adaptability, not merely biographies compatible with it. Neither brother has documented government procurement or adjudication experience, a material belief revision, or a recruited regulatory owner. The company’s cross-domain ambition compounds the problem: patents, disability benefits, and drug approvals have different error costs, rubrics, evidence standards, and appeal pathways. The largest kill risk is not whether an agent can summarize a file. It is whether one bounded workflow can achieve materially higher throughput without increasing erroneous or discriminatory decisions. Stratum names human review and auditability, but supplies no experiment, threshold, budget, or stop condition aimed at that risk.

The economics are equally unwritten. Software should be capable of beating human-delivered adjudication costs, but there is no price, cost per processed application, integration burden, model-inference assumption, or procurement-cycle model. Direct agency sales may produce contracts, yet bespoke rubric configuration and compliance work could turn apparent software leverage into services labor. That is not Chindia-price scalability; it is an unanswered question.

I pass with my own money today. My 36-month base case is that Stratum narrows from cross-government adjudication to municipal permitting or licensing and reaches $1 million–$3 million ARR by August 2029, becoming a competent vertical workflow company rather than a government-wide technical platform. One result flips me: a paid agency deployment processing at least 1,000 cases with at least 5x adjudicator throughput and no deterioration in error, appeal, or reversal rates. That would simultaneously establish the discontinuity, attack the kill risk, and begin quantifying the consequence.
