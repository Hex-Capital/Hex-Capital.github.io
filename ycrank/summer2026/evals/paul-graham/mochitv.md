# MOCHI.TV -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when something broke. Users reported incomplete series, authentication failures, and ad-playback problems between April and August 2026. The dossier says MOCHI.TV shipped multiple updates and continued releasing episodes, but not who diagnosed the failures, how quickly the founders acted, or whether the fixes changed retention. So I cannot infer resourcefulness from activity. This is unusually important here because a consumer-content startup will encounter new breakage every week.

The technical proof is much stronger. Three people have shipped iOS and Android apps, more than 100 original series, daily episodes, and Mochi Lab’s scene-by-scene creation system. This is a functioning production-and-distribution machine, not a proposed AI workflow. Producing a 30-episode series in one day is also the sort of concrete pre-round proof spike that has predicted some of my investments. But it demonstrates output, not yet a difficult proprietary threshold. Competing platforms can access similar models, and the dossier identifies neither exclusive IP nor a technical result they cannot readily reproduce.

Users are less hypothetical than the technology. MOCHI.TV reports 300 million organic views, 900,000 social followers, and 100,000 users; Google Play independently shows more than 10,000 downloads and 1,180 reviews. Those numbers are exceptional for pre-seed. The strongest bull case is that the founders have already solved the expensive part of this market—attention—without paying the acquisition costs that can consume 90% of a microdrama budget. Robert Qiu has also previously reported scaling a game to $200,000 in monthly revenue, while Michael Ninh has managed hundreds of video creatives. This could be a team repeating a known consumer-growth trick in a new medium.

But views are not the same as users pulling a product back. The reported 200–300 million views cannot yet be reconciled with 100,000 users or 10,000-plus Android downloads, and there is no cohort retention, paid conversion, repeat creation, or revenue figure despite live subscriptions, season passes, coins, and gems. The ratings prove real consumption; they do not prove a narrow well of urgent demand. The operational gate therefore remains unresolved rather than failed outright: a working product and unmistakable pull can override missing founder-obstacle evidence, but social reach alone is not that override.

The origin is plausible but too polished. Ninh has watched more than 400 anime series, and the founders understand consumer games, machine learning, and video distribution. Yet the dossier never shows the personal frustration that caused them to build this product or a neglected burden they chose to endure. Content rights could become such a burden, but the absence of disclosed training-data licenses or a review process is presently risk, not schlep earned. Meanwhile AniShort, Dashtoon, My Drama, and GammaTime have collectively raised substantial capital around overlapping production systems. MOCHI.TV must become a compounding creator-audience network, not merely the fastest operator of commodity models.

None of the explicit founder red flags is established: there is no dependence on a ceremonial launch, large partner, premature sales hire, or delegated product function, and absent cofounder history is not evidence of a bad relationship. My base case, however, is that within 36 months MOCHI.TV pivots its center of gravity from the streaming feed to paid creator-production tooling and remains below $3 million ARR. I am neutral today. One condition would flip me: a cohort of at least 1,000 users retaining above 30% at day 30 after a founder-documented fix to the incomplete-series and reliability problems. That would connect obstacle, action, and user pull into one logical proof.
