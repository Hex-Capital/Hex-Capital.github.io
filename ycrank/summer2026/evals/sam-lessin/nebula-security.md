# Nebula Security -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I first write down the deal: valuation unknown, competing-term-sheet count unknown, first-check scarcity unknown. That earns no benefit of the doubt. YC and a crowded 2026 offensive-security market—XBOW has raised more than $270 million, Depthfirst $120 million, RunSybil $40 million—make this look more auctionable than discarded. Without actual terms, I cannot tell whether I am buying a non-consensus technical team or paying consensus prices for an AI-security label.

The technical receipts are exceptional. Nebula reports 1,393 validated findings, 98 public CVEs, eight Chrome zero-days, and more than $400,000 in team bug bounties. Eten Zou has shipped security work inside Microsoft and kernel research outside it; the other founders bring program analysis, Zellic experience, Black Hat presentations, and DEF CON finals. This is not résumé theater. They understand both institutional security software and the adversarial research culture Vega is attempting to translate into a repeatable product.

But technical discovery is not yet a dark pool that causes purchases. The 1.3K-star exploit repository, 4K X followers, and enterprise private beta establish attention and capability, not paid conversion, retention, or proprietary workflow capture. Public CVEs are deliberately public. OpenAI Codex Security and Microsoft MDASH have access to models, distribution, and customer repositories; Nebula therefore needs evidence that exploit reproduction and remediation feedback compound into something those platforms cannot simply bundle.

The strongest bull case is the known exception for a security product whose extraordinary external adoption outweighed an expensive, crowded round. Nebula has one part of that mechanism: unusually hard technical validation. It does not yet have the material override—thousands of users, named enterprise customers, or a rejected acquisition offer. An enterprise beta without paid conversion cannot carry the same underwriting burden.

The capital plan is the deeper problem. Metered access, per-repository enterprise pricing, and fixed-quote audits describe three possible businesses, but the dossier names no finite experiment funded by this round, no deadline, and no pass/fail metric. Nor does it provide revenue, gross margin, burn, or a route from audit labor to self-financing software. Delete the Series A and I cannot tell whether Vega becomes a profitable security product, a capable consultancy, or an expensive agent whose economics deteriorate when validation still requires researchers.

My 36-month base case is that Nebula remains a technically respected audit-and-tooling business below $3 million ARR rather than becoming the default continuous-security platform. I pass today. One verifiable condition flips me: twelve months of cohort data showing at least ten paying enterprise repositories, greater than 90% renewal, and positive contribution margin after human validation—paired with a round price that still reflects a first scarce check.
