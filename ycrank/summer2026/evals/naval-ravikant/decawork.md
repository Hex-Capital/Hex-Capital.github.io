# Decawork -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the founders’ credentials; it is what they have already put into production. Aman Raj built AI-compliance tooling used by more than 240 Barclays teams, while Sarthak Aggarwal worked on enterprise AI at NVIDIA and Ema. Decawork’s prior autonomous-IT product also reached multiple enterprises in India and the United States. This is costly, relevant learning: the team has encountered enterprise buyers, operational constraints, and compliance requirements rather than merely discovering agent governance through a market report. The evidence is adjacent rather than exact—the disclosed deployments predate the current control plane—but it establishes real specific knowledge.

The weakness is structural. Per-agent identities, scoped credentials, approval gates, gateway logs, and retirement workflows make a coherent product, but a competitor copying those features does not destroy a scarce asset. Okta already offered agent registration, credentials, ownership, and access governance by April 2026. Astrix, Noma Security, WitnessAI, and Zenity have each raised more than $55 million around overlapping infrastructure. Decawork’s reported SOC 2 status improves its ability to sell; it does not create compounding defensibility. The hypothetical dataset of agent actions and failures could become proprietary, but a future feedback loop is not a present moat.

The bull case is that deployment and maintenance—not identity alone—becomes the real control point. A gateway that sees every tool call could accumulate operational context, and the founders may understand this workflow more deeply than identity incumbents approaching agents as another credential type. The adjacent market is large: non-human-identity access management was estimated at $11.14 billion in 2025, while AI governance was projected to grow 36% annually through 2033. If enterprises standardize agent launch, monitoring, remediation, and retirement through Decawork, the product could expand from governance into the operating layer for enterprise agents.

But the system currently compounds through company labor, not customer participation. There is no evidence that one enterprise improves utility or distribution for another, no disclosed proprietary model, and no measured advantage from pooled telemetry. This misses the permissionless pattern: every new account appears to require another enterprise sale and integration. It also leaves the gateway as both Decawork’s best observation point and a concentrated availability risk. The recent shift toward infrastructure is directionally attractive, but this remains a software control plane exposed to incumbent bundling rather than a demonstrated substrate competitors cannot reproduce.

There are no disclosed integrity problems, artificial financing deadlines, founder instability, or other behavioral red flags. Nor does a known exception clearly override the structural weakness: the team has stronger domain evidence than the typical pre-seed company, but Decawork has shown neither a self-reinforcing user behavior nor an unusually hard technical artifact that would justify ignoring copyability. No current-product customers, revenue, pricing, or usage have been disclosed, so the prior product’s deployments cannot be treated as validation of the new one.

I would pass today while keeping the relationship open. The option becomes attractive if Decawork can verify one flip condition: several production enterprises using the control plane must generate proprietary cross-deployment policy or anomaly-detection performance that measurably beats Okta and the funded specialists. Without that, the likely 36-month outcome is a pivot toward broader IT-operations orchestration or a sub-$3 million ARR product compressed by identity-platform bundling. The founders have earned another look; the product has not yet earned durability.
