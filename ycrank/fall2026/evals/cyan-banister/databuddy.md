# Databuddy -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine a tiny software team waking up to a diagnosed conversion problem, the deployment that caused it, and a fix ready for review. Nobody spent Tuesday dragging filters around. Databuddy has shipped a small piece of that world: persistent investigations, introduced in July 2026, connect analytics with GitHub deployments and deliver findings through Slack. But getting from “here is a plausible explanation” to “you can safely act on this” requires reliable diagnosis and permission boundaries. The dossier shows those transitions are unfinished. My imagined future cannot substitute for evidence that Databuddy can reach it.

Why does the founder still have to become a part-time analyst? Because collecting events and deciding what happened are different jobs. Databuddy attacks that gap directly, returning investigation time to teams without dedicated analysts. Its $49.99 Pro plan makes the economics accessible. Yet the dossier identifies neither a quantified institutional rent nor an artificial restriction that the product removes. This is useful operating relief, with less evidence of the deeper transfer of control that makes me reach for my own checkbook.

Issa Nassar gives me a reason to keep looking. His analytics-integration responsibility at Confinity preceded Databuddy, and the product was documented as privacy-focused analytics in October 2025, before persistent AI investigations arrived. That chronology matters: there was an operating problem before there was an agent pitch. His separate Keypal library, with 206 GitHub stars, adds evidence of independent building. I see technical initiative and relevant experience; I do not yet see a long record of obsession or demonstrated recruitment of complementary talent. The open founding-engineer role establishes a need, not recruiting success.

The strongest argument against my hesitation is wonderfully ordinary: people are already paying. Stripe-connected figures show $3,125 MRR, 35 active subscriptions, and $11,087 cumulative revenue as of October 27. These are stronger evidence than the company-reported 3,000 businesses or approximately 1,200 repository stars. Databuddy fits my pattern of backing unglamorous workflow repair, and the analytics business could finance a more ambitious investigation engine. But those payments cover the whole platform, including the earlier offering. They establish willingness to buy Databuddy, without establishing repeated reliance on its AI conclusions. My exceptions for early technical bets depend on unusually strong founder history and concrete external validation; that combination is not established here.

The distinction becomes consequential when the product touches sensitive data. The changelog records fixes for invented root causes, double-counted Stripe revenue, protected-column access through SQL aliases, and retrieved instructions deleting memories. Publishing and fixing these defects shows operating work. It does not establish that the privacy gate is satisfied, and the dossier does not establish an ongoing breach either. Meanwhile, Amplitude already delivers funnel-monitoring agents through Slack, and PostHog overlaps with investigations. Databuddy controls instrumentation and investigation history, but the dossier demonstrates no scarce enabling constraint that those competitors must obtain from it. Owning useful plumbing is a start; becoming indispensable infrastructure requires more.

I am **Neutral**. There is enough independent building and paid demand to reject a casual “AI wrapper” dismissal, but insufficient evidence that the investigation layer becomes dependable infrastructure. One verifiable flip condition would move me toward investing: a six-month production cohort showing customers repeatedly pay for and act on Databuddy investigations, with independently tested access controls protecting the connected data. That would establish the behavior change—and the trust required to sustain it—that the current subscription totals cannot resolve.
