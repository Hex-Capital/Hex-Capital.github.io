# Veeza AI -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Veeza AI can remove paperwork. It cannot manufacture embassy appointments or change who receives a visa. That distinction sets the ceiling on the consequence-of-success case presented here. The dossier identifies a $4.05 billion tourist-visa services market and a real burden on Egyptian travelers, but does not quantify how much spending or applicant time its automation eliminates. Making scarce expertise affordable could matter enormously. The evidence currently establishes a useful service addressing an existing expense, with no demonstrated orders-of-magnitude change in access.

The technical question is whether Veeza can complete difficult applications reliably with radically less human work. Computer-use agents navigate portals, check documents, and prepare forms; humans review before submission. That is a sensible product design. It is not yet a measured discontinuity. Atlys and iVisa/HelloGov offer overlapping assistance, while Veeza’s disclosed differentiation centers on MENA travelers and Arabic/English support. There is no comparative accuracy, completion-time, or intervention-rate benchmark. I will leave room for undisclosed engineering rather than declare this a commodity wrapper, but regional positioning does not establish technical superiority.

The strongest argument against passing is that this team may already have found the distribution and trust needed to build a substantial consumer business. Company-reported users increased from 1,000 to 7,000+, paying customers exist, and a roughly 400-person WhatsApp community generated 10,000+ feedback messages. Moursy combines personal Egyptian-passport experience with engineering studies and product work at Bell and Efreshli. Those are meaningful early signals. My actual investing pattern includes execution-led consumer businesses; scientific novelty is not an absolute requirement. But user growth does not establish completed applications, and feedback volume does not document a consequential belief revision. The added team member’s responsibilities and complementary capabilities are undisclosed. Founder learning and recruiting therefore receive the absent-evidence score.

Veeza’s largest unresolved risk is whether reliable completion survives changing portals without human labor consuming the service fee. Its terms acknowledge portal interruptions and unavailable appointments. Human review and applicant inspection are useful safeguards, but the dossier supplies no decisive experiment, threshold, budget, or stop condition. Beta launches demonstrate shipping; they do not establish how efficiently capital removes this risk. Likewise, EGP 1,500 for Schengen assistance and EGP 4,000 for UK/US applications establish selling prices, not economic advantage. Without reviewer minutes, inference costs, retries, and refunds per completed case, unsubsidized cost parity remains unproven. Both dimensions receive their specified defaults.

I would pass with my own money. The exceptions that justify overriding my usual gates require a documented exceptional instigator addressing a giant value pool, or a consequence so large that a small probability is enough. Veeza’s lived problem, paying customers, and early adoption make that possibility worth investigating, but do not establish it. My one flip condition is an independently auditable, consecutive 100-application trial across two existing routes showing at least 10x lower human handling time than conventional agents, no higher material-error rate, and positive contribution margin at the published fees. That would turn the central claim into evidence: affordable expertise delivered through automation that survives contact with real applications.
