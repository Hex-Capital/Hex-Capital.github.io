# HERA -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: manufacturing drawings are continuously checked against geometry, assemblies, internal rules, and external codes before a human releases anything to the floor. HERA could occupy that world, but the dossier does not map how we get there. The cited 2025 extraction benchmark explains why automated review may work now; it does not establish which intervening steps turn HERA into the system manufacturers trust six years from now. That missing chain matters. This is presently a useful application with a possible future, not yet a future leaking through one unmistakable artifact.

The system wound is wonderfully concrete. Senior engineers spend days checking ASME, ISO, GD&T, tolerance stacks, and contradictory title blocks, while one miss can scrap a $40,000 part. HERA returns traceable violations in minutes and leaves sign-off with the engineer. That is better than generic “manufacturing is inefficient” language. But the dossier identifies internal waste rather than an artificial gatekeeper collecting rent, and the agency returned is engineering time—not a new ability for an excluded person to own work, set prices, or enter an occupation.

The founders are the strongest part of this company. Meera Patel completed a six-month Pfizer water-system project, worked on manufacturing projects at Pfizer and TE Connectivity, and conducted healthcare-ML research. Noelle So built a water-treatment facility adding 40 L/s at nineteen and automated workflows at Wyze Labs. Together they interviewed more than 200 manufacturing engineers, quality engineers, and machinists before launch. That is real industrial tuition. I do not yet see a long-running, independently derived obsession, unusual recruiting proof, or evidence that either founder understands the commercial machinery of enterprise manufacturing, so I stop short of treating biography as destiny.

A working product is live in three production plants, which is much more informative than a rendering or waitlist. The missing behavior is what users did next. The public offer remains a free, NDA-covered review of twenty drawings; there are no named paid deployments, repeat cohorts, or examples of engineers trusting HERA with increasingly consequential work. My pre-revenue exception only applies when prototype proof converges with signed industrial commitments and extraordinary founder-history evidence. HERA has part of that pattern, not the full mechanism.

The bull case is that every adjudicated violation and company-specific rule makes HERA harder to remove, eventually turning drawing review into infrastructure beneath aerospace, medical devices, semiconductor tooling, and custom machinery. The bear case stings more today: CoLab, Axial, Redprint, and Glypad already advertise overlapping checks, Redprint charges as little as $1.98 per drawing, and HERA discloses neither dataset scale nor an accuracy benchmark proving control of the bottleneck. Keeping drawings on customer premises is a thoughtful answer to my privacy red flag. The unresolved red flag is governance: two best friends founded the company, but the dossier does not identify the ultimately accountable leader or a contractual tiebreaker.

I am a pass for now, though not a dismissive one. The portfolio pattern I want is a mundane artifact revealing that a larger technical layer has arrived early; 200 interviews demonstrate diligence, while three plant demos demonstrate access, but neither yet shows costly or weird customer commitment. One condition flips me: at least two of those three plants converting to paid annual deployments after a blinded audit establishes a sub-1% critical-error miss rate. That would connect the founders’ industrial fluency, the $40,000-error economics, and the claimed system of record into something I would fund with my own money.
