# Praxis -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a two-lawyer firm accepting clients, assembling documents, and collecting payment without either lawyer spending the evening chasing email. But work backward: first the software must execute reliably across disconnected systems; then firms must delegate enough authority that supervision stops consuming the time automation saves. Praxis describes the first transition. The dossier does not yet show the second beginning. “Live with law firms” establishes a foothold, but it does not tell me which consequential actions those firms trust the agents to complete.

Why does a lawyer spend 1.5 hours a day setting up clients and preparing engagement letters? That unnamed partner’s account is the most interesting object in this dossier. The work falls between systems, and the lawyer becomes the person carrying information between them. Praxis could return occupational control by handling that coordination. The reported $350,000 annual loss from slow responses suggests the cost might extend beyond wasted hours, although it is another unnamed firm’s estimate. There is a specific operating failure here; there is less evidence about who captures its economics or how much control customers actually recover.

Diana’s reported experience taking Gorgias’s AI Agent to more than 1,000 customers and $8 million ARR in under a year makes the strongest case against passing. She has encountered the commercial work of getting businesses to adopt agents, and her McKinsey work included law firms. Maxence has built multimodal systems at SightCall and worked in data engineering at Uber and BlaBlaCar. Those are useful, complementary capabilities. They establish more than enthusiasm for a category. They do not establish a pre-company obsession with this particular operating wound, dated independent experiments, or evidence of recruiting first believers around it.

The bull case is that Praxis becomes the dependable operator between systems while incumbents remain bounded by their own products. Mundane workflow repair fits a recurring pattern I like, and deployment inside real firms deserves credit before revenue is disclosed. But Lawmatics already qualifies inquiries and triggers follow-ups; Clio advertises complete workflow execution. Praxis needs something customers cannot obtain simply by turning on another feature. Firm-specific configurations might eventually provide that, but the dossier demonstrates no controlled bottleneck. Nor does it document repeated delegation, payment, or measured time savings. The exceptions that would let me overlook commercial proof require unusually strong founder obsession or a revealing working artifact; neither is established here.

Confidentiality could stop this investment, but the evidence does not establish a severe privacy violation: Praxis states encryption, firm separation, zero-retention AI providers, and human review. Diana is identified as CEO; unclear founder voting rights are an unanswered question rather than evidence of dysfunctional control. My pass rests on the investment mechanism: a credible team is repairing a costly workflow, while the evidence connecting that repair to a distinct future remains thin. **One verifiable flip condition:** a documented 90-day cohort of firms repeatedly delegating complete inquiry-to-paid-invoice workflows across existing systems, with materially fewer lawyer intervention minutes and no material confidentiality or execution failures. That would show the small independent firm gaining an operating capability it previously could not afford. Until then, I would keep my own money in my pocket.
