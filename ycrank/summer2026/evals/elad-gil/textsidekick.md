# Sidekick -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Multimodal models now let plant workers query procedures and initiate maintenance through text; plant managers buy when no-account access captures activity that conventional software misses, and Sidekick becomes large only if that activity compounds into an operational system faster than incumbent CMMS vendors can copy the interface.

The controlling variable is the percentage of real frontline work captured through SMS rather than merely enabled by it. Sidekick has a credible causal mechanism: remove account and app friction, answer from plant documents, route work orders, escalate exceptions, and preserve resolutions. But the dossier does not establish a threshold—weekly active workers, work orders initiated, downtime avoided, or retention—at which that wedge becomes a system of record. The market thesis is therefore plausible but not yet falsifiably specified.

The strongest evidence is 20-plus plants and 600-plus frontline workers in July 2026. For a solo pre-seed founder, that is meaningful product pull. It is weaker than the headline suggests because these are company-reported deployments without paid conversion, repeat usage, expansion, or cohort retention. I cannot distinguish 600 active workers tolerating an unfinished product from 600 provisioned phone numbers across pilots.

The product is more substantial than document chat. Creating and routing work orders, escalating unanswered questions, and retaining verified resolutions could give Sidekick workflow state that improves as models improve. The missing technical proof is execution depth: PLC and SCADA synchronization is promised, but no supported-system catalog or deployed integration is documented. The terms also leave workers responsible for verifying generated answers, limiting how much operational responsibility the agent presently owns.

The economic discontinuity is much weaker. Better document, image, and voice models lowered the cost of building Sidekick, but they lowered it for MaintainX and Augmentir as well. MaintainX already combines manuals, voice notes, work orders, asset history, and enterprise integrations; its expected $135 million-plus 2026 ARR and approximately $3.6 billion Autodesk acquisition validate the category while showing that it is crystallizing around an installed incumbent. Sidekick’s plant history is not yet a demonstrated moat because the dossier never estimates what it would cost that incumbent to reproduce it from its broader asset and maintenance data. This activates the data-moat red flag, not the discontinuity pattern I normally fund.

Justin So has genuine context: a computer-science master’s degree, summers on his family’s factory floor, and exposure to a third-generation manufacturing business. That supports founder-market fit, but it does not document second-time learning, costly commitment, or the desperation this criterion measures. Nor does a known exception rescue the case: Sidekick is neither scarce frontier research nor a reconstruction of industrial unit economics.

The bull case is real and uncomfortable: one founder reaching 20 plants may mean SMS is not a feature but the missing distribution layer for workers whom app-based systems never capture. If engagement is recurring, each conversation could deepen plant history while work-order execution expands the product beyond retrieval. Today, however, the evidence cannot separate that outcome from a highly copyable engagement interface sitting above incumbent systems. I pass for now. My flip condition is a 90-day cohort of at least 10 paying plants where more than half of enrolled workers use Sidekick weekly and at least 20% of maintenance work orders originate through it.
