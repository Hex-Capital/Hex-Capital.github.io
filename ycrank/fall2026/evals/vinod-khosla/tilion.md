# Tilion -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Tilion has demonstrated that browser access is an engineering problem worth attacking. It has not demonstrated how much consequential work becomes radically cheaper when access works. That distinction determines whether I buy this option. Millions of daily agent operations could represent valuable automation or inexpensive retries. The dossier supplies neither the underlying service-cost baseline nor the human work displaced. I cannot turn operation counts into a societal consequence by multiplying them by enthusiasm for agents.

Fortress contains real technical work. Native Chromium C++ patches and coherent device identities go beyond JavaScript camouflage. Published test tooling, 86.4% successful loads across 92 protected sites, and BrowserArena’s October 22 result of 156 ms median lifecycle latency with 99/100 successful runs make the product testable. But these measurements answer different questions: lifecycle speed does not establish protected-site reliability, and neither establishes a 5–10x advantage against Browserbase or Camoufox under equivalent conditions. Separately supplied network egress remains a dependency. If IP reputation determines success, improving browser fingerprints solves only part of the binding constraint.

The strongest argument against my pass is substantial: four founders have shipped browser infrastructure that reportedly attracted 100,000 installs and engineers at 170 companies. A tiny team assembling a technical community around a platform wedge fits an unstated pattern I take seriously. Arham maintains Fortress releases; Hoque brings AWS experience; the founders have jointly authored research. This is more informative than fashionable AI credentials. Nevertheless, those facts do not document belief revision or recruiting complementary people to own specific weaknesses. Nor does the community establish a proprietary learning loop: detection reports are public, competing engine-patching implementations exist, and earlier BSD releases remain available to forks.

Tilion’s economic and experimental evidence leaves the investment case unfinished. A $2,000 monthly organization license and announced $0.03 browser-hour price establish offers, not paid adoption or cost parity. I need cost per successful completed workflow, including egress, retries, maintenance, and human recovery. The dossier also provides no budgeted experiment with a pass/fail threshold for the largest kill risk: maintaining reliable access as defenses change. These are missing facts, not evidence that the founders spend irresponsibly. There is likewise no documented advertising business, acquisition-first ambition, or refusal to learn that would justify an active-mismatch verdict.

I would pass with my own money. My known exceptions permit incomplete milestones or modest initial technical novelty when a documented instigator and an enormous existing value pool justify the option. Tilion’s adoption makes that possibility credible, but does not establish it. Native engineering also clears the simplest wrapper objection; it does not yet prove an enduring discontinuity. One result would reopen my decision: a customer-verified, 90-day production deployment showing at least 10x lower fully loaded cost per successful workflow than Browserbase, including egress and human recovery, within a quantified consequential service market. That would connect the browser achievement to the economic consequence I am underwriting.
