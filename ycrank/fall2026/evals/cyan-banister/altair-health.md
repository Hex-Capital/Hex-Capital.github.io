# Altair Health -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, an independent clinician should be able to treat someone without also operating a small insurance-negotiation department. Altair Health’s theorem prover is the interesting object here: software proposes a billing action, checks it against encoded insurer rules, and hands exceptions to a human. Getting from that architecture to the future requires two difficult transitions—reliably translating changing policies, then reducing human intervention across different practices. The dossier establishes the proposed architecture; it does not yet establish either transition. I can imagine the destination. Imagination does not earn the company evidence points.

Why does a practice need this machinery to collect money it has already earned? Insurers control authorization and reimbursement requirements; mismatches in documentation and coding leave the practice carrying the administrative work and payment delay. Altair attacks that specific loss of control through an outsourced operation that works with existing systems. That is a meaningful agency story. But the dossier does not quantify avoidable denials, days of payment delay, or the fees Altair retains. I see the gatekeeper and the mechanism, with less visibility into how much economic control actually returns to the clinician.

The strongest argument for writing a check is the unnamed substance-use-treatment practice. In an October 2026 founder report, Altair says it collects several hundred thousand dollars monthly for that client. Those are **client receipts, not Altair revenue**, but entrusting a company with reimbursement is consequential behavior. This is more informative than an EHR waitlist. The reported year between initial conversation and full deployment also matters: someone stayed through the plumbing. The limitation is attribution. We cannot tell how much of that result comes from verified automation versus capable human billers.

Aouss brings enterprise selling experience from Grafana Labs and AppDynamics; Jad brings healthcare-ML research and founding engineering experience at Helical AI. Aouss’s Texas clinic visits show willingness to go find the problem in person. These are useful, complementary capabilities, although the biography provides little evidence of a billing obsession predating Altair. I would not reject this team for being pre-revenue or lacking a long company history. The exception I make for an early technical team rests on demonstrated technical function and unusually deep founder commitment; the dossier only partially establishes those supports.

Altair fits my pattern of finding opportunity in unglamorous operating wounds. The more ambitious possibility is a reusable policy-verification layer underneath healthcare transactions. Today, that remains a possibility: there is no independently validated coverage or exception rate, and Candid Health and Anomaly already occupy adjacent claims and payer-intelligence workflows. A theorem can be correct while the encoded policy is wrong. Human escalation acknowledges that complexity, but could also keep the business dependent on labor. Patient-data custody deserves the same precision: reported BAAs, encryption, access controls, and logging are affirmative controls, with no documented severe privacy violation. Likewise, being brothers establishes neither equal voting control nor a governance defect; the CEO and CTO responsibilities are identified.

I am **Neutral at 47/100**. Altair Health has a credible way to restore some independence to practices, but I cannot yet underwrite the leap from operating a billing service to controlling infrastructure that many practices depend on. My flip condition is one independently audited deployment study across three unrelated practices showing that the same policy-verification system adapts to payer-rule changes, shortens collection times, and reduces human work per claim without increasing errors. That would make the small technical object in this dossier a much more convincing leak from the future.
