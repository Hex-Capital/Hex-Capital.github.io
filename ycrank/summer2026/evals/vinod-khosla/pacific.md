# Pacific -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The attractive fact is not “data centers in space.” It is the terrestrial bottleneck: 57% of data-center projects suffered delays of at least three months in 2025, and Pacific claims those delays cost the United States $1 billion weekly. An assembly-line pod placed on an existing grid connection could attack a real, expensive constraint in a modular-systems market projected to grow from $11 billion in 2025 to $48 billion in 2030. But Pacific never quantifies how much time, capital, water, or compute cost its system removes. This is a consequential demand pool, not yet a 100x consequence.

The technical thesis rests on diamond-wafer dry cooling and vertical integration of transformers, switchgear, and power distribution. Neither is benchmarked. There is no disclosed thermal capacity, rack density, PUE, reliability result, certification, production unit, or measured deployment advantage. Armada already has deployed modular units, while Crusoe is investing in prepackaged systems. Without a measured delta, Pacific may be combining expensive components into another container rather than creating a 5–10x discontinuity.

The more serious problem is risk sequencing. Thermal feasibility is the first kill risk, yet no field test or pass/fail threshold is public. Component availability is another: industry equipment lead times average 33 weeks, while Pacific proposes to internalize several difficult categories simultaneously. Existing grid access also leaves permitting intact in a market where local support is only 35%. Hiring and broad vertical integration before killing the cooling hypothesis is the wrong money-versus-risk graph. Technical risk is welcome; five unranked technical and operational risks are not.

The founders provide the strongest signal. Ralph Heward-Mills brings Airbus thermal-engineering and ESA experience; Harper Dammann Smith combines aerospace training with prior AI deployments. More importantly, they abandoned a digital-twin product that reportedly reached 10K MRR in May 2026 and moved into physical compute infrastructure. That is observed tactical flexibility, not merely a claim of adaptability. It is still unclear whether evidence drove the pivot, and the team has no disclosed data-center manufacturing operator or record of recruiting owners for supply-chain, certification, and deployment risk.

The relevant exception is Block: a tiny team can begin without a scientific discontinuity when it enters an enormous existing value pool through a wedge capable of evolving. Pacific shares the small team, large pool, and mutable route. The material difference is that Block had a narrow initial product; Pacific currently proposes cooling innovation, electrical-equipment manufacturing, site deployment, cloud operations, defense systems, and eventually orbit. The bull case—unnamed aerospace work becoming a high-value managed-pod wedge—stings, but it does not justify underwriting the entire stack without one working pod.

Pacific fits my unstated preference for industrial technology compatible with installed infrastructure, but it has not yet earned the manufacturing-learning or proprietary operational-data moat that would make that pattern investable. My base case is that by August 2029 the company has pivoted into managed thermal-compute systems for defense and aerospace, remains below $5 million ARR, and is not operating a meaningful terrestrial GPU fleet. I pass today. One condition flips the decision: an independently validated pod operating at a stalled interconnect with comparable GPU uptime, at least 5x faster deployment, and no higher all-in cost per GPU-hour than Crusoe.
