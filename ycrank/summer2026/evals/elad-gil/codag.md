# Codag -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Production debugging is shifting from humans searching logs to agents consuming them, and Codag becomes large only if context compression becomes a separately purchased control point rather than a feature bundled into observability platforms. That is the governing belief. The product offers a measurable mechanism: group repetitive lines, rank relevant patterns, preserve source evidence, and reduce agent token consumption by 5.9–7.5× while answering 98–99% of benchmark incidents. This clears the first market gate, but only narrowly because the 51 incidents were synthetic and internally evaluated rather than drawn from customer production systems.

The technology shift is real. Logs that were previously a human-search problem are becoming a model-context and inference-cost problem. Codag’s CLI, API, and MCP interfaces put compression directly in the agent path, while plans from $19 per month to $499 per month plus usage provide a plausible way to monetize that new constraint. What is not established is the economic magnitude: the dossier does not show that log tokens are a meaningful share of agent-debugging cost, that compression improves incident resolution time, or that customers will maintain a separate budget for it.

The strongest bull case is distribution. A no-account install, MIT-licensed repositories, and Michael Zhou’s prior 655-star developer tool could let Codag become the default preprocessing layer before observability markets crystallize around agents. His infrastructure work at Okta and Shopify is relevant, and five pivots suggest persistence. But current-product evidence remains 45 stars for `codag-drain` and 20 for `codag-cli`, with no revenue, repeat production use, or named customer. The prior visualizer’s adoption does not transfer automatically to this product.

Durability is the larger problem. Codag preserves nearby context and line numbers, which is better than a pure summarizer, but it owns no telemetry system, incident state, permissions, remediation action, or feedback loop. Its deterministic core adapts Drain3 and is MIT-licensed. A stronger model with a larger context window could reduce the need for compression, while Dash0, Honeycomb, Sentry, or Cribl could bundle equivalent preprocessing into systems that already possess the logs and customer relationship. This is a useful component, not yet an owned workflow.

The known-exception test does not rescue the investment. Zhou has credible domain alignment and open-source execution, but the dossier does not show a scarce frontier technology team or a technical asset that funded incumbents cannot reproduce. Nor is there evidence that submitted logs have created a defensible dataset; the proposed data flywheel is inferred, with no retained volume or measured improvement. I would not underwrite a proprietary-data claim until Codag can quantify the cost and time required to recreate it.

I would not invest today. The single verifiable flip condition is ten unrelated companies paying the $499 Team plan and using Codag weekly on production incidents for three consecutive months. That would demonstrate both product pull and that compression can remain an independent control point. Without it, the most likely outcome is a pivot into broader agent observability or absorption of the feature by an incumbent.
