# Topos -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Topos leaves the first underwriting line blank: post-money valuation, competing offers, and whether my check buys an experiment that otherwise cannot happen. All unknown. YC Fall 2026 participation does not establish an auction, and adjacent companies raising hundreds of millions does not establish Topos’s price. But I cannot call this cheap, scarce capital either. I would pass on the evidence available.

The product question is sharper than “can agents verify one another?” What independently determines that they are right? Topos describes agents communicating, formalizing biological problems, and checking reasoning, but supplies no checker specification or benchmark. A swarm could make a valid deduction from a false biological premise. The investable experiment would establish a useful boundary between what computation can certify and what still needs a laboratory. That could reveal something valuable. The dossier does not establish that round proceeds fund such a test, with a metric and deadline; I cannot award credit for designing the experiment myself.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ actual overlap. Ryan Min June Shin brings mathematics and Dana-Farber experience; Sohum Sukhatankar brings mathematical computing and seizure-localization research at Boston Children’s Hospital. They have collaborated on Felt and a computational prime-progression project credited to both in September 2026. There is more here than two prestigious biographies. This could become the translation layer between formal methods and biological research that neither ecosystem builds alone. Those projects earn some founder credit, but they do not yet demonstrate a deployed bridge into a laboratory’s operating workflow.

That distinction determines who captures the value. Topos could accumulate biological formalizations that become proprietary infrastructure, but the dossier labels that possibility as inferred. It identifies no owned dataset, committed laboratory, repeat usage, or customer action caused by privileged access. Meanwhile, Edison already occupies scientific research workflows, and Harmonic and Axiom operate in adjacent verification markets. A useful verification capability could therefore become a feature inside somebody else’s customer relationship. The unstated pattern I want—ownership of the connection between two systems—is plausible here, but unproved.

Delete the next financing from the model and the answer remains unknown: no business model, burn, margin, or seed-funded break-even plan is disclosed. That earns the stated missing-evidence default, not an assumption that Topos is uneconomic. Nor does the dossier establish the red flags of an auction, confused founder control, or margins premised on disappearing agent errors. I checked the exceptions: there is no documented paid conversion, retained enterprise demand, prior exit, or alternative financing structure strong enough to override the unresolved gates. One verifiable event would reopen this for me: a laboratory pays for and completes a fixed-budget, 90-day deployment that passes a pre-agreed independent correctness test and reduces experimental rework against its existing process. That would connect the technical claim to an owned workflow and an economic action. Today, the founders are the strongest evidence; the investable transaction remains undefined.
