# ClaimKit -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

ClaimKit has evidence that customers want the service. I still cannot price the investment: proposed post-money, competing offers, and whether my money is actually scarce are all undisclosed. YC membership does not establish an auction, so I will not invent one. But neither does it establish a cheap first check. The other primary gate is equally unresolved: the dossier names no capital-funded experiment, deadline, or pass/fail result. Connecting engineering records to tax claims describes the product; it does not explain what truth my check buys.

The strongest evidence is Edexia switching from accountants mid-project and reporting a faster, cheaper engagement. That is an actual purchasing decision around a workflow, which matters more than ClaimKit’s 206 LinkedIn followers or the Airwallex event announcement. The company also reports 80-plus customers within six months. There is something here. Retained claim records could make subsequent engagements easier and give the firm useful proprietary context. But the dossier does not yet establish that those records cause renewals, referrals, or an acquisition advantage competitors cannot buy. I give credit for observed customer action, with limited credit for ownership of the access behind it.

Huang has performed the manual R&D work at KPMG, and the founders have shipped a product joining engineering evidence with accounting records and specialist review. This fits my preference for people who can translate between systems with different operating languages. Mahon’s Google, Akuna, and IMC experience supports technical competence, although those titles do not establish a prior independently shipped business. Retiring the Grants module on April 22, 2026 also concentrates the current operation around one service. The team understands a concrete problem; that is stronger evidence than an abstract claim that AI will transform tax.

The upfront software fee and minimum-or-percentage service fee provide a plausible way to collect money. They do not establish profitable delivery. Specialist review, documentation rework, and later regulatory questions all consume labor; invoicing at lodgement leaves collection timing important too. The reported 100% claim success rate supplies neither a denominator nor evidence of audit durability. Without margins, burn, or a funded break-even plan, I cannot delete the next financing from the model. That is missing underwriting evidence, not proof that the business is uneconomic.

The objection to passing is substantial: four people have reportedly attracted more than 80 customers, and one named buyer has already displaced an incumbent accountant. ClaimKit could become a valuable tax firm even if AI drafting becomes cheap. That is the strongest route around my usual skepticism of AI applications. But the exceptions I would make require demonstrated conversion and retention economics, unusually strong external validation, or a financing structure that removes repeated fundraising. This dossier does not supply those overrides. Neo.Tax’s ONESOURCE distribution also means ClaimKit’s potential workflow advantage faces an established route into the buyer. I pass on the investment as documented. My one verifiable flip condition is a signed financing package below $10 million post-money that funds a dated cash-break-even milestone, explicitly costing specialist review and rework without requiring another round.
