# Cova -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Michigan licensure is Cova’s strongest signal; the absence of a named incumbent failure mode is Cova’s weakest. Cova is a regulated-services operator using AI to compress administration, not yet a zero-to-one technology company. The analytical signature is a real institutional wedge without a demonstrated secret: Cova has entered the Medicaid machinery, but has not shown why Honor, Aidaly, Entyre, or Michigan’s directly enrolled caregivers structurally cannot follow.

Anduril turned regulation into monopoly because Lockheed and Raytheon were trapped by cost-plus contracting. Cova shares the regulated-customer mechanism superficially: Michigan provider licensure, Home Help enrollment knowledge, and electronic-visit-verification compliance can reward operational depth. Cova diverges where the analogy becomes load-bearing. Michigan expressly permits caregivers to enroll directly or associate with another agency, and the dossier identifies no exclusive contract, certification bottleneck, or capex barrier. Anduril used regulation to restrict competition; Cova currently uses regulation to participate in it.

Asana exposed what happens when a consensus product lacks a contrarian secret: founder lineage must carry the bet. Cova’s “AI-native home care agency” framing risks the same mimetic failure, because AI handles intake, benefits coordination, enrollment, insurance, and billing without a named architectural defect that Honor, Aidaly, or Entyre cannot repair. Cova’s provider pivot may conceal a real secret—that owning care delivery is the only way to automate regulated workflows—but Cova has not stated why incumbents are institutionally unable to integrate the same software. Asana had Dustin Moskovitz. Cova does not have that exception.

CMS requires at least 80% of certain Medicaid HCBS payments to compensate direct-care workers, leaving Cova’s automation to fight over a narrow administrative remainder. Over 18 months, Cova’s workflow data could compound through faster enrollment, fewer denied claims, and lower scheduling overhead; those advantages matter only if case density creates switching costs or meaningfully superior margins. Without published case volume, reimbursement rates, or cost performance, Cova’s data loop looks like an operational efficiency available to every scaled agency. Monopoly is the condition of success; Cova currently has a hypothesis about efficiency, not pricing power.

Jordan Ibe and Ansh Tandon spent the preceding year building AI for home-care agencies and hospitals before Cova became a provider. Cova’s definite-plan score would flip upward if that pivot represented a committed five-year architecture—Michigan density, measured claims automation, replication into named states, and a regulatory-data standard Cova would not abandon. The disclosed record instead moves from selling software to operating care, with no named 2027–2031 milestones. Cova may have learned something important, but learning followed by a pivot is still evidence of iteration until the founders state what will no longer change.

Jordan Ibe brings VA medical-center exposure, while Ansh Tandon studied bioengineering and data science at Penn and has a two-star GitHub project. Cova therefore has credible domain proximity, but the relevant parallel is Affirm: Max Levchin had already shipped PayPal at scale before the second check. Cova’s founders have one year of adjacent building and no verified exit, scaled operating role, or exceptional technical achievement. Cova’s willingness to assume provider liability is more interesting than its credentials, but it does not trigger the operator-pedigree exception that rescued Asana or Quora.

Cova will, by July 2029, have pivoted back toward selling Medicaid enrollment, EVV, and billing software to home-care agencies at less than $2 million ARR rather than operating a scaled multistate care provider. Cova’s regulated operating experience should reveal valuable workflow pain, while the 80% direct-care compensation rule, caregiver-capacity constraints, and weak agency-level pricing power make software the easier adjacent business. The high-regulation, low-secret signature produces a vertical-software pivot: knowledge accumulates, but monopoly does not.

Honor operates across more than 1,000 communities and reported $2.1 billion in combined home-care-services revenue, which supplies the strongest case against that forecast: Cova could use AI overhead reduction, family-caregiver supply, and mixed Medicaid, LTCI, and private-pay reimbursement to build a denser and more profitable provider. Cova is also licensed and live at pre-seed, so the regulated-sector unstated pattern is genuinely present. The objection fails today because Cova discloses no patients, revenue, unit economics, or measured automation advantage, while direct enrollment and Aidaly already weaken exclusivity. Neither the Asana nor Quora exception applies because Cova lacks a Moskovitz- or D’Angelo-grade operator. I would flip to Invest if Cova demonstrates, across at least 500 active cases, agency contribution margins at least ten percentage points above comparable Michigan providers while expanding into a second state without proportional administrative hiring.
