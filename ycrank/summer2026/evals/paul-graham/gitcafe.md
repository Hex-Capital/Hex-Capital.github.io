# GitCafe -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important fact is missing. GitCafe has built a surprising amount, but the dossier never names something that broke, what either founder did next, or how quickly the response changed the outcome. A custom git engine, kiln runtime, native binding, forge, CLI, and stacked diffs show formidable output. They do not by themselves show resourcefulness under resistance. Since that is my first gate, I cannot infer it from the sophistication of the artifact.

The artifact is nevertheless the strongest evidence here. This is not a mockup of a “modern GitHub.” It is a deployed, vertically integrated system with self-service accounts, an npm-installed CLI, private repositories, and a public pull-request demonstration. Building through the git implementation instead of wrapping the standard binary is exactly the sort of impatient technical choice a fierce nerd makes before asking permission. The discount is that GitCafe reports no benchmark, reliability threshold, repository count, or independently verified result. The software works; its advantage is not yet measured.

The eight named organizations are better than anonymous interest. Engineers at Corgi, alphaXiv, AthenaHQ, OpenRelay, Cerenovus, Miso Labs, Marengo, and harmont apparently use the product. But “presented as users” does not tell me whether they migrated active repositories, returned after a week, opened stacked pull requests repeatedly, or merely created accounts. Free private repositories during beta further blur the signal. GitCafe has found a plausible niche—technical teams willing to try an independent forge—but has not yet shown that the crude product gets pulled back by urgent use.

The bull case is sharp. GitCafe fits a pattern I care about: technical founders assembling a programmable substrate before its category has settled, now extended to coding agents through first-party skills. Veronica’s prior work as a founding engineer at Corgi and her TypeScript, Rust, and platform-binding experience make the stack credible. The planned migration tooling and CI compatibility are also genuine schlep. If the eight organizations are running important private repositories rather than experiments, the company could be much further along than its public evidence suggests.

But the same facts support the bear case. GitHub has more than 180 million developers and 630 million repositories; GitLab exceeds $1 billion in ARR. Against those network effects, GitCafe still lacks one-command migration, GitHub Actions or GitLab CI compatibility, paid pricing, and a service commitment. Those are not peripheral features. They determine whether a team can move. Nor does the dossier say how the first users were recruited individually. A working prototype can override many conventional weaknesses, but it cannot substitute indefinitely for evidence that the founders respond rapidly when adoption hits a wall.

My 36-month expectation is that GitCafe remains below $1 million ARR and narrows from a general-purpose GitHub replacement into agent-native code-review and stacked-diff infrastructure. I would flip to Invest if the founders document one named organization whose migration was blocked, ship the workaround within two weeks, and then show five teams using migrated repositories weekly for eight consecutive weeks. That single sequence would prove the missing mechanism: obstacle, founder action, and user pull.
