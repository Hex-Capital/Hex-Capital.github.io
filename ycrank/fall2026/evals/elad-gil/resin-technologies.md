# Resin Technologies -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Resin Technologies is selling engineering time back to hardware teams as AI begins to execute multistep electronics design. The controlling variable is total engineer time per functioning board, including review and fabrication rework. Generating a schematic faster creates little economic value if checking it consumes the savings or an error adds another manufacturing cycle. The dossier identifies that problem, but supplies no measured result establishing that Resin changes this variable. That is where my underwriting stops.

The $6.1 billion PCB design software estimate establishes an existing spending category. It does not establish how much Resin can capture or whether automation expands that spending. At $199 per month for Pro and $149 per user for Team, the product could be inexpensive relative to engineering labor. Those are asking prices, however; there is no quantified time saving, delivery-cost comparison, or evidence of willingness to pay. Flux already offers an integrated editor and AI assistance and announced $37 million in financing in February 2026. The market is crystallizing around competing workflows. Resin needs a specific economic advantage within that market.

The strongest argument for investing is the product architecture. Resin combines editable schematics, datasheet-to-footprint generation, design rules, and placement and routing in its own editor. Better models could improve task completion while the editor retains the design state and the engineer’s corrections. This is substantive alignment with my preference for owning the operational loop. But workspace libraries and design history are only a proposed source of switching costs; editable exports make lock-in less automatic. The company-described proprietary routing engine also lacks comparative validation. I would give credit for the workflow already described without underwriting an established moat.

The founders make that bull case more credible. Koosha Rezaiezadeh’s electronics and robotics experience complements Anmol Jawandha’s enterprise AI deployment background, and they met studying Engineering Physics. That combination fits my pattern of backing software tools for large physical-industry transitions. Their biographies do not document prior startup learning or costly personal commitment, so I would not infer those qualities from employer names. Nor does this dossier support an exception for scarce frontier research talent or technology that demonstrably reconstructs physical-world economics. Those exceptions require affirmative evidence beyond a plausible technical team.

Resin’s October 2026 launch is recent enough that absent commercial history is unsurprising. Its 176 LinkedIn followers and published subscription plans nevertheless provide no evidence of repeated use or payment. I would pass on a personal check today: the strongest evidence concerns workflow ownership, while the market’s controlling economic proposition remains untested. There is no documented frozen-model dependency or fundraising misconduct driving that decision. One result would change my stance to active diligence: an independently checked, matched-board benchmark showing at least a 50% reduction in total engineering time, including review and rework, with unchanged first-pass manufacturing yield. That would establish whether Resin’s integrated workflow delivers the economic step change the investment needs.
