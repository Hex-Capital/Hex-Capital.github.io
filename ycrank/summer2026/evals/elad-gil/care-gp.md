# Care GP -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Agentic systems can now complete document and voice tasks inside practice software, Australian GP clinics are buying labor replacement rather than another clinician dashboard, and the controlling variable is whether Care GP can expand from one useful agent into the operating layer for each clinic before competitors collapse the product into a feature.

The demand evidence is unusually strong. Care GP reached US$200,000 ARR after 3.5 months, converted 85% of pilots, reported zero churn and profitability within six months, and subsequently grew to more than 400 clinic operators and three million processed documents. Yarrawonga Medical Clinic reduced document allocation from six hours to under 30 minutes. These are not waitlist metrics: customers are paying roughly US$5,000 ACV and routing recurring, consequential work through an unfinished product. Inbound referrals and threefold growth over 90 days further suggest product-market pull rather than sales-driven experimentation.

The technology changes workflow economics, although the evidence is stronger on customer labor than on Care GP’s own margins. A greater than 90% reduction in document-processing time can make automation an obvious purchase amid staff shortages. Identifier cross-checking, refusal to allocate mismatched records, ISO 27001 certification, Australian data residency, and Bp Premier integration make the product operational rather than merely conversational. Better models should improve extraction and call handling while Care GP retains permissions, corrections, practice-system state, and responsibility for task completion.

The bear case is market structure. Facere already offers overlapping document and voice automation inside Bp Premier, while incumbent practice-management vendors control distribution. Care GP’s three million documents are not automatically a moat: the dossier does not quantify how much time or money a funded competitor would need to reproduce the relevant correction data. A 5%–20% human edit rate also means the hardest records still require labor. Most importantly, 7,000 Australian clinics at the reported US$5,000 ACV imply only about US$35 million of domestic ARR before allowing for incomplete penetration. A profitable document product can therefore be real without becoming a large company.

The resolution is expansion through the installed workflow, not the initial document classifier. Care GP has already added voice and describes email, billing, referrals, records transfer, and secure exchange as adjacent agents. If the first product lowers acquisition costs for those products, revenue per clinic can compound while integration depth raises switching costs. Today, however, only one agent’s early commercialization is documented; the broader operating layer remains a credible proposition rather than demonstrated fact.

Chen brings prior company-building and commercial experience, but the public record shows neither clinical experience nor a concrete founder sacrifice, and the unexplained departure or changed status of two previously named co-founders deserves diligence. I would still invest because the market evidence carries the team: paid adoption, repeat operational usage, conversion, and workflow expansion are already more informative than founder polish. My 36-month base case is US$8 million–US$15 million ARR by August 2029, with at least 30% coming from clinics buying two or more agents. I would reverse the decision if, by August 2027, fewer than 25% of retained clinics adopt a second paid workflow; that would show Care GP is a strong point solution in a bounded market rather than a distribution platform.
