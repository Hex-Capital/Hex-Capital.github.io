# Volaren Inc. -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Volaren Inc. has built something, but the dossier doesn't show what happened when building it became difficult. That is the missing fact I care about most. Connecting brokerage accounts, interpreting holdings, and placing orders must produce obstacles. I want one specific example: what broke, what Akshat Mittal or Martin Pestana did, and how quickly it worked. Their experience at Goldman Sachs, AQR, and Low Tide makes them credible people to investigate this problem. It doesn't establish resourcefulness. I give that dimension its missing-evidence default, rather than treating their résumés as a substitute.

The product deserves some credit independently of the founders' backgrounds. Volaren describes account connections, text approvals, spending limits, and controls for pausing agents. Its published limitations—some brokers support viewing without trading, and prediction-market trading remains confined to practice accounts—give the description useful specificity. But these are company-reported capabilities, without a measured execution result or independently demonstrated reliability. This is partial technical proof. It also falls short of my unstated preference for a programmable tool that users are already bending into unexpected workflows: the dossier shows neither developer adoption nor such appropriation.

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders understand quantitative finance, knew each other at UChicago, and have put a product in front of a claimed 900 funds and retail traders. That could conceal a small group who badly need it. A four-person team charging $12 a month could learn very quickly from such users. But “funds and retail traders” combines customers with quite different needs, and the count has no activity or payment definition. I cannot tell whether anyone returns when there isn't an exciting market move. The referral program and YC launch provide ways to recruit users; neither proves that recruitment has produced a narrow well of demand. The approximately $1 million raised doesn't answer that question either.

There is potentially valuable ugly work here. Existing-broker execution means handling permissions, connection failures, and unexpected fills. Yet the founders' financial work history doesn't document a personal encounter that led them to this particular product, so the origin criterion remains at its default. Nor is there a documented rejected hypothesis and subsequent product change; adaptability remains unknown. Volaren's statement that it is neither an investment adviser nor a broker-dealer leaves its regulatory positioning unresolved. That is a question to investigate, not evidence of misconduct or credit for having solved the schlep. Robinhood's own agents make the distinction consequential: Volaren needs a user problem it solves substantially better than the broker already holding the account.

I would pass at **35/100**. This is missing evidence at the center of my framework, rather than an active founder red flag. The founders have prior acquaintance, and there is no evidence of waiting for a partner, delegating the product before understanding it, or dismissing contrary feedback. I checked the possible exceptions: the reported product supplies some support, but there is no independently validated technical result or unmistakable user pull strong enough to override the failed resourcefulness gate. My flip condition is one documented customer episode: a specific brokerage obstacle, a founder-built workaround shipped within two weeks, and that customer subsequently paying and using it weekly for eight weeks. That would connect founder behavior to something a user actually wants.
