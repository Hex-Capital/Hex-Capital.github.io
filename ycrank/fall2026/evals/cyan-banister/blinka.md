# Blinka -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

A bank closes your business account and mails you a check. Where are you supposed to deposit it? That little absurdity is the most interesting thing in Blinka’s dossier. Juan Pablo experienced it while operating his US LLC abroad. The product addresses a specific loss of control: a business can be legally incorporated in one country yet lose financial access because its operator lives elsewhere. I care about giving that control back. Whether Blinka can actually keep it matters more than how many financial features fit inside its interface.

Move the clock to 2032. A founder runs a US company from Bogotá, pays people locally, and changes banking providers without interrupting the business. Getting there requires eligibility that survives compliance review and financial connections that survive a partner’s withdrawal. Blinka’s bundle makes that future imaginable, but the dossier does not establish either transition. Its dependence on Portage Bank, Third National, and Mural Pay leaves the underlying permission elsewhere. Worse, advertising access for non-US entities while publishing terms requiring a US entity or US-operating sole proprietor makes the boundary of the promised freedom unclear.

The founders have earned more attention than their credentials alone would buy. Their collaboration on business-credit software predates this banking product; Pygma dates its first SAFE to April 2024. Sebastián’s self-taught engineering and Juan Pablo’s legal experience form a useful pairing for this particular mess. The earlier mortgage, lending, and real-estate products suggest operating tuition, although they do not establish a lifelong obsession or unusually strong recruiting ability. I would not call those pivots opportunism. Nor would I turn “brothers” into an invented governance problem: the dossier identifies a CEO and CPO, but says nothing about voting control.

The strongest argument against my hesitation is that customers have already entrusted Blinka with consequential work. Company-reported volume exceeded $10 million in its first two months, and one customer reportedly manages more than 200 entities through the portal. That is a much better artifact than a waitlist. The 25 pre-launch clients also matter; the more than 150 account-opening applicants are a different, weaker signal. These reports lack retention and transaction-concentration detail, but moving money and administering entities require real effort and trust. Missing public revenue does not erase that behavior, and payment volume is not revenue.

This could become the unglamorous workflow business I like: enter through an irritating operating wound, then become difficult to remove. That pattern is genuinely present. The competing possibility is that Blinka organizes access without controlling it, while Slash already offers much of the surrounding bundle. The exception I would make for a compelling prototype and exceptional founder history does not resolve that dependency here. There is no documented severe privacy abuse or concealed leadership switch, and external providers are normal in banking. But normal dependence still limits the claim that this is an enabling layer with durable power over its own service.

I land at **Neutral** with my own money. Blinka has meaningful early customer behavior and a concrete agency problem; its weakest evidence concerns the path from convenient banking interface to dependable cross-border access. My one verifiable flip condition is a documented partner withdrawal or eligibility change that Blinka handles by moving affected customers to an independent provider while preserving compliant account access and payments. Show me that, and the product begins to return the control its founder originally lost.
