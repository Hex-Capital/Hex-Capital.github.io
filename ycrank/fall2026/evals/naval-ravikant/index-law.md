# Index Law -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Pativada has begun acquiring the specific knowledge Index Law needs. Assess shipped on July 14; Mike launched in November. Those are concrete encounters with legal workflows. His experience buying slow legal services supplies the original irritation. His prior education venture’s reported $20M+ booked revenue establishes neither legal expertise nor demand for Mike. I give him credit for building into the problem, with less conviction about how much he has learned: the dossier supplies no repeat-use evidence or independently examined legal outcomes.

The decisive question is what Index Law owns after a competitor reproduces its software. Persistent company memory could become valuable, but storing context does not establish that customers cannot move it. Agent harnesses and matter-level evaluations are useful engineering; no disclosed benchmark, exclusive dataset, or demonstrated switching cost establishes a durable asset. External model infrastructure, including OpenRouter, makes ownership of the surrounding workflow more consequential. Today, the dossier does not establish that ownership.

The strongest argument for investing is that Mike could earn it through use. A business repeatedly explaining itself to different lawyers has a genuine coordination problem. Remembering its contracts, preferences, and unresolved matters could make the next transaction easier. Index Law reports turnaround below 60 minutes and quotes up to 80% below traditional firms. If those outcomes hold across recurring matters, the product could become the place a business keeps its legal context. That is a substantial bull case. The reported outcomes lack named customers and sample sizes, however, and the retention mechanism remains a hypothesis.

There is room beyond the first contract. Mike’s move from matter assessment into contracts and corporate work, together with its proposed Teams and Enterprise offerings, describes a plausible expansion through the same business context. Harvey’s reported $400M-plus ARR and Legora’s $200M-plus ARR establish spending on legal software, although their professional buyers do not establish Mike’s addressable demand. The proposed $149 and $499 monthly plans identify a payer and a direct charging mechanism. They remain forthcoming during a free preview; neither realized revenue nor the economics after specialist involvement is established.

Index Law partially matches my pattern of organizing fragmented professional supply: independent lawyers provide judgment when the software escalates. But recruitment alone does not produce a network effect. Nothing disclosed shows that adding one business improves another business’s experience, or that adding lawyers measurably improves matching. The 169 templates are company-supplied content; 191,088 launch-post views measure attention. Neither demonstrates customer-created distribution. I see no documented integrity or financing-pressure veto, and the company’s explicit acknowledgment of supervision and accountability is sensible. My concern is structural compounding, rather than founder conduct.

I pass at this evidence level. I can accept missing revenue when a network is already forming, or missing distribution when an exceptional product and capital efficiency are demonstrated. Index Law has not yet supplied either exception strongly enough to overcome its uncertain durable advantage. My verifiable flip condition is a six-month customer cohort showing that accumulated matter history drives expanding paid use and materially better legal outcomes than the same system without that history. That would turn persistent memory from a feature claim into an asset.
