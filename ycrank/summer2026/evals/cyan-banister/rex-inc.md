# Rex -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Enterprise finance teams no longer organize people around inboxes, procurement portals, and reconciliation queues; they supervise procedures that act continuously across those systems and surface only consequential exceptions. Rex has a convincing leak from that world: 22,000 autonomous actions in four months, including retrieving more than $40 million in purchase orders across 30–40 customer portals. This is not a narrated agent demo. Synthesia entrusted a working product with repetitive, financially consequential labor and absorbed twice the inbound AR email without adding headcount.

The future chain is less complete than the artifact. Tool-using models make cross-system execution possible, editable procedures provide guardrails, and accumulated exception histories could create institutional memory. But Rex has not shown why that memory becomes proprietary rather than portable, or how it escapes competition from HighRadius, Tabs, and Kolleno. The company currently operates above other vendors’ ERPs, inboxes, portals, and models; it does not control a scarce underlying resource. If the procedures remain customer-specific configuration wrapped around closed models, this becomes excellent implementation work with software margins rather than a defining enabling layer.

Kafka and Blackwood have paid unusually relevant operating tuition. They spent four years together at Sequence, where their product and engineering work touched billions in receivables, more than 50 ERP integrations, and hundreds of finance teams. Blackwood was also an early GoCardless engineer and previously founded Personably. That is more than résumé adjacency: the wound, workflow, and complementary working relationship all predate Rex. What is still missing is evidence of a longer independent obsession—experiments or artifacts they built before January 2026 because they could not leave this problem alone. I see strong Biz and credible Tizz; the dossier gives less proof of unusual recruiting magnetism or first-believer behavior.

The broken system is concrete but not fully decoded. Finance teams move among spreadsheets, email, ERPs, offshore operators, BPO firms, and procurement portals, while a missing purchase order can hold up payment for weeks. Rex returns time and operational control to the finance team by turning those practices into editable procedures with approval gates. Yet the dossier does not quantify the rent captured by BPO providers, identify an artificial rule preserving the fragmentation, or show whose occupational agency expands beyond “fewer manual tasks.” This looks like a costly workflow failure, not yet a broken institutional rule.

The bear case stings because the strongest evidence and the weakest moat are the same fact. A two-person team can automate 22,000 actions precisely because general-purpose models and existing systems now expose enough surface area; funded competitors can use those same ingredients. Only one implementation is described in detail, SOC 2 Type II is unfinished, and Rex sends sensitive financial context through third-party cloud and AI providers. Customer-specific histories may compound, but “could become an asset” is not evidence that customers cannot export them or competitors cannot reproduce the procedures. I also need the governance documents to establish a contractual tiebreaker; naming Kafka CEO is helpful, not sufficient.

I would invest a modest personal check because users have already delegated costly, reputation-bearing work to the artifact, and the founders understand the hidden operations well enough to deploy in under three weeks. My conviction flips to a pass if, within the next two named enterprise deployments, Rex cannot show six-month repeat usage with declining human intervention and reusable procedure infrastructure rather than bespoke founder labor. The company has earned belief in the product. It has not yet earned belief in the moat.
