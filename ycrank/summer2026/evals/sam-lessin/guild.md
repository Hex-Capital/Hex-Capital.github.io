# GUILD -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first three numbers are missing: proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and outside capital beyond YC’s standard $500,000. YC acceptance proves financing, not scarcity. I cannot tell whether this is a cheap first check into a neglected thesis or another defense-AI company being auctioned during a fashionable cycle. With no round or competition evidence, this gate stays at its low default.

GUILD’s operating model is more interesting than its AI label. It assumes prime-contractor responsibility, translates fragmented technical packages into production plans, and coordinates qualified factories through testing and delivery. That is a real translation layer between government procurement and a shrinking small-manufacturer base. But the round’s venture moment is unnamed: there is no specified component, contract, deadline, capital requirement, or pass/fail result. “Build the system and win more contracts” funds an organization; it does not identify the single truth this check will reveal.

The best evidence is that GUILD says it already wins and executes government manufacturing contracts. Those transactions could seed a proprietary record of requirements, quotations, supplier performance, awards, and delivery outcomes—the compact execution memory that becomes more valuable than the public AI thesis. Still, contract count, value, repeat purchase, and delivery history are undisclosed. The supplier portal demonstrates supply recruitment, while 197 LinkedIn followers demonstrate nothing economically causal. I give partial credit for an operating workflow associated with purchases, but not full credit for a data flywheel that has yet to disclose its first turn.

The bull case stings. Small-business participation in the defense industrial base fell more than 40% in the decade preceding 2023, and the February 2026 arms-transfer strategy explicitly calls for more domestic capacity and nontraditional suppliers. An asset-light coordinator could mobilize capacity faster than Hadrian, Machina Labs, or Divergent can build factories. Physical complexity is acceptable when software reorganizes a neglected operating system, and GUILD fits that pattern unusually well.

The problem is who presently owns the hard-won access. Kaya Celebi has credible ML receipts, including AI leadership at Hartree and a public repository with 183 stars; Erim Gurlemis brings finance, client operations, and AI-implementation experience. Neither biography shows a defense contract delivered, a factory operated, or aerospace compliance managed. GUILD is accepting pricing, CMMC, testing, logistics, and delivery liability without documented shipping experience on the manufacturing side. The ontology is therefore a proposed consequence of execution, not a moat already earned. Nor does an inferred gross-margin model establish a profitable path: there is no revenue, pricing, contribution margin, burn, runway, or working-capital evidence.

My base case is that by August 2029 GUILD will have retreated from prime-contract liability and repositioned Forge as quoting and compliance workflow software for defense manufacturers, with less than $3 million in ARR. I am passing because none of the exception mechanisms is present: no measured conversion or retention, no disclosed acquisition interest, no prior exit, and no extraordinary technical adoption overriding the missing deal and operating evidence. One result would flip me: a second paid order from the same government buyer after an on-time first delivery, at a disclosed contribution margin above 20%. That would show the workflow causes repeat purchasing and that the team can carry the liability without requiring a capital factory.
