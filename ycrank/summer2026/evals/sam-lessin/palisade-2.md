# Palisade -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Fnu Prince has practiced offensive and defensive security since age 16, but Palisade has only 79 LinkedIn followers and five YC launch votes. That combination is Palisade’s signature: unusually credible missionary conviction trapped inside a business with almost no owned distribution. Palisade may contain a serious kernel-security operator, yet the current asset is still an illegible technical capability rather than a venture-shaped distribution machine.

**Drop.io** is the high rung on founder conviction: I kept building a file-sharing primitive against free incumbents because I had a non-obvious belief and the missionary temperament to survive the turns. Prince’s Caltech departure, professional security work, and demonstrations against CVE-2024-1086, CVE-2025-6019, and CVE-2025-32463 put Palisade meaningfully toward that rung. Palisade diverges because Prince has no discoverable public writing, Twitter account, or GitHub repositories showing how he thinks about kernel policy, automated rollback, or why Cisco’s Tetragon has framed the problem incorrectly. The obsession is credible; the contrarian thesis is not yet inspectable.

**Fin** exposed the low-rung distribution failure mechanism: a technically ambitious Wizard-of-Oz operation can produce insight without producing owned demand. Palisade has 79 LinkedIn followers, five launch votes, no accessible website, and no disclosed customers, while founder-led requests for introductions remain its only published go-to-market motion. Palisade therefore has neither a John Fish-style audience nor a community that existed before the product. Enterprise security does not require celebrity, but without practitioners adopting rules, publishing integrations, or contributing detections, Palisade must rent trust one security lead at a time.

No disclosed customers, revenue, pricing, or rollback measurements make Palisade’s capital structure look like generic enterprise SaaS rather than a venture moment. Over the next 18 months, Palisade must build cross-distribution kernel support, prove that automated rules do not interrupt legitimate processes, and survive long security-procurement cycles before subscription revenue compounds. Each requirement adds another regatta tack, while Cisco, Aqua Security, and Sysdig can subsidize adjacent capabilities from existing platforms. Palisade could be bootstrappable as a focused Linux security tool, but Palisade becomes founder-drainage if a standard SAFE forces broad endpoint coverage and enterprise sales before one narrow workflow is default-alive.

No public round price, investor list, or financing instrument exists for Palisade, so I apply the absent-evidence default on mispricing rather than awarding non-consensus points merely because the product is early. Palisade would flip up a tier if the entry were demonstrably below the Summer 2026 batch median after established security funds passed because kernel remediation looked too narrow or dangerous. Palisade would flip down if “AI that fixes vulnerabilities” is raising a hot YC-priced round: AI plus cybersecurity plus SaaS is precisely the consensus label stack where access masquerades as underwriting.

**Sublime Security** is the useful single-anchor parallel: both Sublime and Palisade enter enterprise security through a narrower technical wedge before the next platform consolidation. Palisade earns real sleeve credit because kernel-level enforcement is more substantive than an LLM wrapper, and Prince has demonstrated three named CVEs rather than a slide-deck agent. Palisade is nevertheless closer to an AI-overlay pitch than Sublime was at entry because Tetragon already supplies open-source eBPF observability and enforcement, while Tracee and Falco cover adjacent telemetry. “AI security is investible” is the overstatement; the operational point is that Palisade must prove its generated mitigation policies constitute a new control plane rather than a feature incumbents can absorb.

Three named CVE demonstrations lead me to predict that within 36 months Palisade will pivot from general endpoint-fleet remediation to Linux cloud-workload policy generation, remain below $1 million ARR, and be acquihired by Aqua Security for less than $25 million. Palisade’s founder conviction should produce technically useful kernel-policy IP, but Palisade’s absent distribution and the safety burden of fleet-wide automatic enforcement make an independent enterprise-sales regatta unlikely. The likely asset is Prince plus the policy engine, not a durable standalone endpoint company.

Cisco’s Tetragon is also the strongest bull argument because an incumbent open-source primitive can validate the category while leaving the high-value remediation workflow unowned. Prince’s security work since age 16, three working CVE demonstrations, and solo-founder speed could make Palisade another Sublime-style pre-consensus security check; the Pinterest exception says exceptional missionary temperament can override a missing conventional signal. The objection fails today because Palisade discloses no paid fleet, detection benchmark, false-positive rate, rollback result, or proprietary dataset, while Tetragon already performs in-kernel enforcement. Three paid production fleets exceeding 10,000 endpoints each, with published rollback and availability measurements over six months, would verify that Palisade owns a control plane rather than a demo and would flip my verdict.
