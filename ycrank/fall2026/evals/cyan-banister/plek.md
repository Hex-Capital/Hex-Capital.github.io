# Plek -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine a worker handing routine tasks to software while keeping control over how the job changes. Plek currently gives executives a map of employees’ tasks, costs, and AI exposure. The distance between those worlds matters. Accurate company-specific maps must become reliable automation, and employees must have a reason to participate honestly. The dossier establishes neither transition. An organizational chart becoming a task map is a useful starting point, but it does not yet reveal who gains control when the map becomes actionable.

Pierre Bouquet is the strongest reason to keep looking. His March 2026 task-content research and May 2026 AI-exposure paper address the actual measurement problem underneath Plek. YC also reports that his previous startup, Biot, sold in 2022. Those are dated intellectual and operating artifacts, substantially better than a founder discovering workforce automation during fundraising. I see domain commitment and some commercial tuition. I do not yet see the recruiting evidence or extended history of independent experiments that would put him at the very top of my founder range.

Why does a company need consultants and interviews to discover what its own employees do? That is an excellent systems question. Plek identifies the cumbersome workflow, but the dossier does not establish its cost, who captures an avoidable rent, or how the product returns autonomy to employees. The founder-reported assessment of 250 people, 735 tasks, and 36 FTEs doing AI-addressable work offers concrete output. It does not establish that employees repeatedly contributed, that a customer paid, or that an automation worked. The larger website examples cannot fill that gap: Plek’s terms explicitly describe case studies as illustrative and based on example or historical data.

The strongest argument against passing is that Bouquet could turn this mundane assessment into the task infrastructure that many automation products need. Unglamorous workflow repair belongs in my portfolio, and including implementation could teach Plek where its estimates fail. Its claimed proprietary ontology gives that possibility some substance, although control over a scarce dependency remains unproved. Workhelix already overlaps in opportunity assessment, while Skan observes actual workflows. My willingness to back a pre-revenue technical founder applies here; the stronger exception requires a functioning artifact and credible commitments that bridge the technical thesis to deployment. This dossier supplies a reported assessment, without that bridge.

The privacy architecture makes the distinction consequential. Plek says free-text employee responses reach AI providers without scanning or redaction; instructions to respondents carry much of the protective burden. Those interviews are supposed to correct the organizational estimates, so candid participation sits inside the product’s accuracy mechanism. Conflicting SOC 2 disclosures further weaken my confidence in the custody promises. I would pass with my own money today. My flip condition is one independently referenceable paid deployment in which employees knowingly participate under documented consent and data-handling controls, and validated task maps lead to measured automation savings. That would demonstrate that Plek can earn the trust its measurement system requires.
