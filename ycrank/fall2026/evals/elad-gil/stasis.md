# STASIS -- Elad Gil Evaluation

STASIS needs to establish that automatic eye-strain relief creates a recurring purchase. Sensor-equipped eyewear may be becoming more familiar, but the relevant buyer is a screen user who experiences enough improvement to keep wearing—and paying for—these glasses. The controlling variable is sustained willingness to pay for the intervention. Ocular measurements matter commercially only if they cause adjustments that make the user feel materially better. The dossier does not yet establish that causal link or an adoption threshold.

The proposed sensing-to-action loop is the interesting part: inward-facing sensors drive lens tinting and connected-screen adjustments. That could be more useful than another wellness dashboard. However, the described architecture does not establish a working, validated loop, and the founder’s reported 30-day wear test establishes neither measurement accuracy nor symptom relief. Better analytics could strengthen personalization, but there is no documented feedback loop demonstrating that accumulated measurements improve outcomes. Claimed patents without identifiers or claim scope cannot carry that argument.

The economics remain equally open. Conflicting $95 and $240 preorder prices imply very different room for manufacturing, returns, support, and acquisition costs. The optional $15 monthly subscription begins after year one, so the recurring-revenue thesis depends on value persisting well beyond initial hardware curiosity. There is no quantified technical change showing that STASIS can deliver this benefit at attractive margins. My physical-market pattern requires technology to change the economics; adding sensors and a subscription does not establish that change. NIQ’s reported 158% sales-value growth for Ray-Ban Meta in tracked European markets demonstrates adoption of another eyewear proposition, not demand for ocular monitoring.

The strongest objection to passing is the founders’ relevant building history. Chakraborty and Goradia previously developed GlaucoGlasses together; bioengineering, EECS, and ocular-device experience give this team a more credible starting point than a generic consumer-hardware team. That earns limited credit for prior product-building learning, without inferring desperation from university or research affiliations. Their experience could shorten the path to a useful intervention. But GlaucoNova’s reported 348 physician intent forms and seven distributor LOIs totaling $168,000 belong to a different product. STASIS has founder testing and conversations with optical organizations, with no established customer pull.

The research-stage exception does not resolve this gap: the dossier contains neither published causal validation nor evidence of a field-defining technical capability and a credible capital path. Nor does it establish the economic reconstruction that would override my caution about physical products. JINS MEME’s discontinuation is a relevant warning about category durability, although it does not prove poor retention or falsify STASIS. My concern is therefore an unproven mechanism, rather than demonstrated customer rejection. The claimed longitudinal-data moat also remains unearned: neither predictive improvement nor reproduction cost is established.

I would pass with my own money today. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independently run, eight-week paid trial showing a prespecified meaningful reduction in eye-strain symptoms versus sham adjustments, with at least 70% of participants subsequently choosing the $15 monthly service. That would connect the intervention to a purchasing decision. Until then, STASIS has relevant device-building experience, but the market mechanism, economic step change, customer pull, and durable workflow each remain at their absent-evidence defaults.
