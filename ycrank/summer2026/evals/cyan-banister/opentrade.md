# OpenTrade -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Ojas Kandhare shipped CruiseAI and Tario You researched quantitative finance, but OpenTrade’s analytical signature is an observable consumer artifact without an observable captain. OpenTrade’s web drills, friend challenges, ChatGPT Arena, and iOS briefs can be touched within minutes; neither founder is identified as CEO or tiebreaker. I see product energy, but I do not yet see who wins the argument when Ojas wants another game and Tario wants regulated execution. That pairing—a playable object with ambiguous authority—is more dangerous than a merely unfinished product.

Niantic is the high rung because I played Ingress before investing; direct use revealed John Hanke’s location-world obsession without a spreadsheet translating it for me. OpenTrade shares that load-bearing mechanism: I can open the iOS app, challenge a friend, and watch OpenTrade turn market judgment into behavior. OpenTrade diverges where Niantic was already an expression of Hanke’s decade inside Keyhole and Google Earth: the app launched July 1, 2026 with too few ratings for a summary, while the fixed Market IQ scorecard shows only a 50% hit rate and a nine-point calibration gap. The artifact exists, but user love is not yet visible in the artifact.

HQ Trivia is the low-rung warning: tactile virality temporarily overrode unresolved co-founder governance, then the Yusupov-Kroll conflict helped consume the product from inside. OpenTrade names Ojas and Tario only as “Founder,” provides no CEO, and provides no operating or equity tiebreaker. OpenTrade also shifted in one month from AI briefs and prospective execution toward fantasy games and stock drills. That may be healthy discovery, but under two equal-looking founders it can also be the first visible seam of two product theses pulling against each other. HQ Trivia’s known exception requires explosive pull; 5x users in five days without a baseline and an iOS app without enough reviews do not qualify.

Tario You’s 2025 LLM-and-portfolio-optimization paper and Ojas Kandhare’s 26 public GitHub repositories give OpenTrade technical contact with the domain, not a lived founder origin. Over 18 months, that distinction compounds: a founder who has personally lost money, taught novice investors, or operated a brokerage keeps discovering painful details, while research credentials tend to produce increasingly polished features around the original abstraction. OpenTrade’s campus ambassadors and beta-tester recruiting may create proximity to young investors, but OpenTrade’s dossier contains no physical moment when either founder felt the investing problem before deciding to build it. Biz and Tiz are plausible; the autobiographical Rizz is missing.

StockGro’s $112.01 million in disclosed funding makes OpenTrade look less magically weird than strategically crowded. For OpenTrade to flip this dimension into my invest range, credible investors would need to reject OpenTrade because short-form market games appear unserious or because the regulatory boundary appears ugly—and OpenTrade would need retention showing that those investors misread play as learning infrastructure. The current evidence points elsewhere: Invstr already combines fantasy investing with brokerage, Alinea has $15.9 million of equity funding plus a $22.5 million acquisition facility, and retail trading reached $5.4 trillion in 2025. OpenTrade may still be underpriced, but “AI plus investing plus games” is not yet a lonely idea.

Vigil Labs is the useful single-anchor parallel because regulated finance can become an unsexy moat when the founder walks toward the compliance burden. OpenTrade currently walks around it: OpenTrade says real-money functionality is unavailable, advisory requires onboarding, agreements, registration, and compliance approval, and the iOS app disclaims broker and adviser status. That is prudent, but prudence is not a regulatory asset. Until OpenTrade secures a registration, regulated partner, or proprietary execution path, the product remains software sitting beside the hard part rather than compounding through it.

OpenTrade’s July 2026 shift from AI briefs toward fantasy drills drives my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, OpenTrade will have abandoned brokerage or advisory ambitions, pivoted into campus-oriented financial-literacy games, and remain below $1 million ARR. OpenTrade has enough tactile product instinct to survive as an experience, but no disclosed moat, no monetization, funded feature-complete competitors, and no named CEO to keep successive pivots pointed at one destination. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing; here, unfortunately, the game may remain the product rather than the wrapper around valuable financial infrastructure.

OpenTrade’s reported 5x user growth in five days, 3x WAU growth in three days, 1,941 LinkedIn followers, and 100K-view video form the strongest bull case: OpenTrade could be the HQ Trivia exception, where visible play produces a social graph before conventional metrics can see it. Friend leagues and referrals could turn financial identity into distribution, and Ojas’s shipping history plus Tario’s quantitative research could supply the rare Biz-Tiz pairing. The objection stings because consumer magic often looks statistically unserious at birth. The evidence that wins today is the missing absolute baseline, insufficient App Store ratings, 50% historical hit rate, and July product discontinuity. I would flip to Invest if OpenTrade names one CEO with explicit tiebreaker authority and demonstrates at least 100,000 weekly active users with 30% eight-week cohort retention.
