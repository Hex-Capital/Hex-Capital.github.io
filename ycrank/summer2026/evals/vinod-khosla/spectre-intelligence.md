# Spectre Intelligence -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

A $5.6 trillion hedge-fund market is a large pool of money, not a quantified societal consequence. Spectre Intelligence could make additional trading pods less dependent on human analysts, but the dossier supplies no baseline research cost, productivity multiplier, affected population, or expert service made 10–100x cheaper. The upside may be financially large. It is not yet consequential enough to justify buying a 90%-likely failure as a world-changing option.

The technical mechanism is more credible than a generic AI wrapper. Two semiconductor and biotech pods trade real capital, and every decision can produce a proprietary trace linked to ground-truth P&L. That is a potentially useful learning loop. But Spectre provides no return series, drawdown, AUM, or benchmark against human analysts, quantitative funds, Abundance, Nof1, or Numerai. The company itself says LLM traders remain ineffective and successful strategies will be copied. Live execution proves that the machinery runs; it does not prove a 5–10x discontinuity or durable alpha.

The team is at least spending effort near the principal technical risk. Simulated and live trades can test memory, long-running robustness, and learning, while the two active pods create evidence rather than demos. Yet there is no pre-registered threshold, experiment cost, time horizon, or stop condition. “Already profitable” without profit, capital, volatility, or benchmark figures removes almost no risk. A favorable trade can be luck; even a favorable month can be regime exposure. The decisive experiment is an out-of-sample, net-of-cost comparison against sector benchmarks and a fixed quantitative baseline across enough trades to distinguish learning from noise.

Michelle Li brings biology research and personal trading exposure; Joshua Zyzak brings reinforcement learning, AI engineering, and an Optiver program. That combination is directionally appropriate for a biotech agent pod. It does not document rapid belief revision, nor has the team recruited owners for semiconductor domain risk, portfolio construction, compliance, or statistical validation. The compensated expert network supplies inputs, but purchasing expert calls is not magnet recruiting. At pre-seed I do not penalize the missing operating history; I also do not manufacture evidence that is absent.

The strongest bull case is real: a two-person team is already trading, claims profitability, and may be creating a proprietary dataset that compounds with every decision. My broader pattern of backing execution-heavy fintech means I would not reject Spectre merely because it is not a science company. The known exception for a tiny team attacking an enormous financial value pool also applies in form. It fails in substance today: the $5.6 trillion is capital managed, not value demonstrably displaced, while well-funded competitors and incumbent funds can access similar models, market data, and execution systems. Spectre’s own admission that strategies are copied makes the proposed moat decay precisely when it becomes valuable. This is the prevailing-model red flag in a more thoughtful architecture, not yet proof that the red flag has been escaped.

I pass. My 36-month base case is a pivot from proprietary trading toward a financial-model evaluation or labeled-data platform for funds, reaching $0–$2 million ARR, because the instrumentation appears more defensible than the alpha. One result flips me: an audited 18-month live record across both pods showing at least 8% annualized net alpha over their sector benchmarks, lower maximum drawdown, and more than $25 million of deployed capital. That would convert an interesting experiment into evidence that the learning loop creates an economically meaningful discontinuity.
