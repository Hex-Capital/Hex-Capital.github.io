# Rex -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Rex’s candidate secret is that enterprise order-to-cash work is not a collection of irreducible human exceptions; it is a learnable operating system that an agent can execute safely across email, ERPs, and procurement portals. The proposition is falsifiable: if autonomous-action rates plateau, exception handling remains labor-intensive, or financial-error costs rise faster than managed receivables, the secret is false. The dossier never identifies the prevailing belief Rex rejects, so part of this thesis must be reconstructed. Yet 22,000 autonomous actions in four months, more than $40 million in purchase orders retrieved across 30–40 portals, and follow-up cycles compressed from weeks to hours constitute a shipped experiment, not merely another claim about AI transforming finance.

The foundation is unusually strong. Merlin Kafka and Lewis Blackwood worked together for four years at Sequence, where their roles covered product and engineering for the same financial workflows Rex now automates. Blackwood was a founding Sequence engineer; Kafka was its CPO. They then founded Rex together in January 2026 and appear to perform the essential product work inside a two-person company. This is prior alignment around the problem, not a team assembled around an accelerator application. Founder ownership, compensation, and secondary liquidity remain undisclosed, preventing the highest score, but there is no affirmative organizational red flag.

The difficult question is not whether the product works. It is whether working creates ownership. Rex has a plausible narrow entry point in enterprise collections and portal operations, but HighRadius, Kolleno, and Tabs already overlap important parts of the workflow. HighRadius has more than 600 clients and has marketed autonomous finance since 2019; Kolleno offers agents for collections, reconciliation, and disputes. Customer-specific procedures, decision histories, and audit records may accumulate into switching costs, but one documented implementation does not yet prove proprietary data, a network effect, scale economics, or a tenfold technical advantage. Today this is a useful system operating inside a competitive category, not an observable monopoly mechanism.

The strongest bull case is that the procedure layer itself becomes the system of record for financial judgment. Synthesia onboarded in under three weeks, doubled inbound AR email without adding headcount, and entrusted Rex with more than $500 million in Fortune 100 receivables. If each deployment compounds customer-specific operating context, the apparent services burden could instead become proprietary institutional memory. But the opposite mechanism is equally plausible: every customer requires bespoke procedures while competing vendors gain access to the same models, integrations, and approval patterns. The evidence currently proves customer value, not that Rex alone can retain it.

Distribution is more definite than the moat but remains incompletely measured. Rex has a named enterprise route—finance-leader outreach, demonstrations, case studies, and outcome- or SLA-based contracts—and reports conversations with more than 50 finance leaders. The three-week Synthesia deployment shows that delivery need not collapse under implementation complexity. Still, the dossier provides no contract value, sales-cycle length, conversion rate, renewal evidence, or gross-margin structure. Managing $500 million of receivables is operational volume, not revenue. The $4.8 billion AR-automation market permits a large company, but neither TAM nor processing volume demonstrates that one revenue engine can produce a power-law outcome.

My 36-month base case is that Rex reaches roughly $8–15 million in ARR through disciplined enterprise execution but remains one of several agentic order-to-cash vendors rather than controlling the category. I would not write the check yet. This is a founder-rich, proof-bearing company with a monopoly-poor dossier: definite execution, indefinite value capture. One condition flips the decision—three independent enterprise customers must each show at least twelve months of retention while accumulated procedures produce a measurable autonomy or accuracy advantage that competing O2C platforms cannot match.
