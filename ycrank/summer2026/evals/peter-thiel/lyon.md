# Lyon -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Lyon’s secret is precise: a customer-specific foundation model trained on transaction histories can replace separate feature-engineered models across many financial decisions. It is wrong if the reported 4x precision on premium-card conversion does not transfer to credit, fraud, collections, or another institution. Training on 28 billion transactions and beating production rules is a real experiment, not an AI slogan. But credit is only being deployed, and no result exists for the other promised tasks. The secret has survived one test; it has not yet demonstrated generality.

The essential asymmetry is technical proof without proven ownership. Lyon has experience operating inside customer VPCs, multi-task adapter tooling, SOC 2 Type 1 compliance, and 35 monitored controls. These capabilities may shorten future deployments. Yet customers retain both their data and trained intelligence, preventing an evidenced pooled-data flywheel. Published transaction-model research and funded substitutes—including Taktile, Feedzai, Pecan, and H2O.ai—also make the underlying method contestable. A 4x result can establish value creation; it does not establish value capture. Today this is proprietary implementation expertise, not yet a monopoly mechanism.

The founding structure remains an unanswered gate. Gabriel Noya has credible model-systems ability from Stanford EE/CS, NVIDIA, and his TreeHacks optimization work. The dossier, however, identifies only one active founder while reporting a team of two and three LinkedIn profiles. It supplies no co-founder history, ownership information, or confirmation that every core contributor is full-time and that the essential model work remains in-house. This is missing evidence rather than evidence of a defective foundation, so I assign the stated default. But missing evidence cannot become founder conviction.

The proposed revenue engine is coherent: negotiated enterprise contracts for private model training, deployment, and additional task adapters. The fintech’s move from card conversion into credit suggests account expansion, while work with an insurer suggests some cross-institution demand. Distribution is nevertheless inferred rather than measured. Lyon names no buyer, contract value, paid deployment, sales cycle, or implementation cost. Customer-specific training inside each VPC could produce high contract values—or turn the company into an expensive succession of bespoke projects. An $82.3 billion analytics forecast cannot decide between those outcomes.

The strongest bull case is that the 28-billion-transaction deployment is an early technical discontinuity whose importance precedes clean commercial evidence. The known OpenAI exception shows how genuine zero-to-one proof can outweigh an uncertain monopoly; the shared mechanism is a working technical result, but the material difference is that Lyon has shown one customer-controlled benchmark on one task, not a broadly observable capability that competitors must reorganize around. Lyon also fits two revealed preferences better than the typical AI application: it sits in financial infrastructure and builds model-stack technology rather than a shallow copilot. There is no evidence of the explicit part-time-founder, outsourced-engineering, or investor-control red flags. The remaining danger is coordination: cross-task validation, private-cloud deployment, security procurement, and enterprise sales must all become repeatable before incumbents absorb the technique.

My base case is a technically credible but concentrated enterprise business—roughly $3–7 million ARR from fewer than eight institutions within 36 months—without evidence yet that it becomes the last mover. I am Neutral rather than passing because the experiment is unusually substantive for pre-seed. I would move to Invest upon one verifiable event: the existing fintech signs a contract worth at least $500,000 annually after two additional task adapters each beat its production baseline by at least 2x at fixed recall. That would connect secret, repeatability, pricing, and distribution in one customer decision.
