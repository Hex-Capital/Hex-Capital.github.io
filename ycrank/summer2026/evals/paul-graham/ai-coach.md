# COACH -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The useful fact is not that these founders discovered “AI sales coaching.” It is that the Perez brothers had already operated a 44-rep field-sales business and encountered the absurd mechanics of coaching it: managers traveling as far as 100 kilometers to observe one meeting. They responded by shipping a mobile recorder and coaching system rather than waiting for better models, a larger team, or permission from enterprise buyers. Reaching a reported €1 million ARR within eight months shows speed and consequence. But the dossier does not date the original obstacle, the first workaround, and the resulting change tightly enough for full resourcefulness credit.

The technical proof is real but not deep. COACH has a functioning application that has processed 74,473 meetings across 200 companies and turns conversations into one-skill-at-a-time coaching. That is considerably more persuasive than a diagram or planned milestone. Yet Anthropic supplies the underlying AI and transcription, while COACH publishes no independent benchmark showing that its analysis, recommendations, or shared “sales brain” outperform simpler prompting. The corpus is an artifact. Whether it is a compounding technical advantage remains an assertion.

The strongest evidence is user behavior. Thousands of reps at five large French field-sales companies reportedly use the product; pilots convert at 100%, churn is 0%, and revenue grew 120% month over month to €500,000 ARR before reaching the later €1 million claim. Even after discounting these founder-reported figures, they describe a narrow well: managers with enough field reps that physical ride-alongs have become impossible. The 27% versus 32% conversion-lift discrepancy and the absence of cohort methodology matter, as does the surprisingly small public count of 50-plus Google Play downloads. But enterprise distribution can make app-store counts misleading, while customers paying and continuing to use the product cannot be dismissed so easily.

The origin is unusually good. The founders did not begin with a sales-enablement TAM report; they lived inside the work, employed 44 field reps, and then accepted the recording-consent, deployment, pilot, and customer-success burdens required to instrument face-to-face selling. Those schleps are part of the wedge. Closing Lucy before starting COACH also suggests willingness to abandon a product, but the dossier never identifies what Lucy was trying to accomplish, what evidence falsified it, or whether the underlying goal persisted. I cannot turn an unexplained shutdown into proof that this team changes routes intelligently.

The painful objection is that Rilla and Siro already sell substantially the same workflow and have each raised roughly $75 million. If COACH’s €1 million ARR is annualized pilot revenue, its 200-company meeting corpus includes mostly unpaid trials, and its uplift claims cannot survive cohort analysis, this becomes a lightly differentiated interface over commodity speech and language models. The pre-round proof spike argues against that interpretation, but does not eliminate it. Nor does COACH need a special exception to my normal rules: the founders know the buyers, sell through forward-deployed pilots rather than a ceremonial launch or unnamed partner, and already have a working product with pull.

I would invest, though not at strong-invest conviction. The company looks like a fast revenue machine whose ability to alter its route has not yet been tested. My expectation is that by August 2029 COACH reaches €8–15 million ARR, remains strongest in European field-sales organizations, and has not yet broken out against Rilla or Siro in the United States. One result would flip me to a pass: independently verifiable twelve-month net revenue retention below 90% across the customers included in the current ARR claim.
