# Lyon -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Lyon has disclosed a model trained on 28 billion transactions, but neither the price of its equity nor what this round buys. Those are different underwriting questions. Proposed post-money: unknown. Competing term sheets: unknown. Whether my check supplies genuinely scarce capital: unknown. Stanford and YC do not establish an auction, and I will not invent one. But a technically interesting company does not automatically offer an interesting investment. With my own money, I pass on the documented deal.

The strongest argument against passing is substantial: an unnamed fintech serving tens of millions of active users let Lyon train on its transaction history, and Lyon reports four times greater precision identifying premium-card converters than existing rules. That is meaningful access and a measured application result for a two-person pre-seed company. It earns credit for a customer-specific workflow producing useful action, although the [YC account](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/lyon) leaves payment, production usage, and benchmark methodology unresolved. The 28 billion transactions matter because somebody opened a difficult door; they are not revenue or evidence that Lyon owns what sits behind it.

That ownership distinction determines the upside. Lyon trains customer-owned models inside customer VPCs. This fits the pattern I like of connecting mistrustful systems: financial institutions can use modern prediction infrastructure without surrendering their data. It also places the proprietary dark pool primarily with the buyer. Lyon needs to capture durable economics from maintaining and extending that capability. Reusable embeddings could accomplish this, but switching costs remain inferred. SAP’s acquisition of Prior Labs supplies a concrete distribution threat, even though equivalent event-model performance is unproven. The AI red flag is therefore a question of who captures the value as prediction technology spreads; the dossier does not establish that Lyon depends on disappearing errors or temporarily scarce models.

The founders have begun building that bridge. Noya’s NVIDIA and financial-sector affiliations suggest access to both ecosystems; Höhn’s mathematics and machine-learning background and separate shipped sign-language project supply technical evidence beyond a résumé. Still, affiliations do not document responsibility for a deployed financial system. More importantly, the credit deployment underway does not specify a capital-funded experiment, deadline, or acceptance threshold. Nor does the dossier disclose revenue, training costs, burn, or a route to supporting these installations without another financing. Those omissions receive the stated defaults. I am not treating them as evidence of failed conversion or bad economics.

The known exceptions make this a harder pass: unusually strong customer conversion and retention can justify an AI bet, and demonstrated enterprise demand can outweigh an unattractive financing setup. Lyon’s reported precision improvement is the beginning of that argument, not its completion. It does not establish paid retention, repeatable delivery economics, or a reason to waive both financing gates. My single verifiable flip condition is a signed, prepaid customer expansion contract whose documented delivery budget leaves enough cash to reach operating break-even without another raise. That would show the customer-owned model architecture can finance Lyon itself—and give me a concrete business against which to negotiate a price.
