# RouterPlus -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Frequent model releases and fragmented serving capacity create a buyer: production AI teams that want someone else to procure reliable inference. RouterPlus becomes a large company only if pooling that demand produces a persistent procurement advantage after its own costs. The controlling variable is retained gross profit per customer at equivalent model quality and service levels. Forty-plus provider relationships and competitive bidding offer a mechanism, but supplier access without exclusivity does not establish that advantage. I would pass on writing a personal check today.

The strongest counterargument is that RouterPlus may already be handling a problem large enough to force customers toward it. Its largest customer reportedly runs 3 million agent conversations daily. That is meaningful pre-seed evidence, although the dossier does not establish how much traffic RouterPlus serves or whether the customer pays. Separately, the reported dedicated deployment reduced P95 latency from 4.8 seconds to under 4 seconds—an improvement exceeding 16.7%. Capacity procurement can therefore create value beyond choosing a cheaper model. The economic question is how much of that value the intermediary keeps. Prepaid token sales establish a charging mechanism; free BYOK inference and separately negotiated capacity contracts leave the actual margin structure unresolved.

Market crystallization makes that distinction urgent. OpenRouter already offers workload-specific evaluations and dedicated deployments, alongside substantial financing and distribution. Its estimated $160 million annualized revenue indicates category demand, but says little about RouterPlus’s attainable gross profit. The bull case is that pooled procurement compounds: additional demand attracts better bids, better bids attract more demand, and the first deployment creates distribution for subsequent capacity purchases. That fits my pattern of investing across a technology discontinuity. The dossier establishes supplier breadth, however, without demonstrating preferential pricing or a distribution advantage that survives a well-funded competitor matching the product.

RouterPlus does own more of the operational loop than a model-selection recommendation. Held-out evaluations, customer approval before deployment, a unified API, peak-capacity testing, and backup providers connect selection to execution. Better models could make this loop more valuable by creating additional substitution opportunities. But the proprietary procurement-data advantage remains hypothetical, and the intake terms exclude provider-performance guarantees. The product therefore has credible workflow substance without yet establishing durable control over the delivered result. Supplier operations and ongoing intervention could also consume the apparent savings; the dossier provides no evidence that this has happened, so I treat it as the economic test rather than an established failure.

The founders have relevant operating experience: Bishoyi worked on edge-AI deployment, and Narayana built Samsung GPU infrastructure and served as Trupeer’s early CTO. Those credentials improve execution plausibility, but do not document costly commitment or specific lessons from a prior startup, so the founder criterion stays at its absent-evidence default. The exceptional research-team override does not apply to the infrastructure integration described here; nor is there evidence of a physical-economic transformation that warrants overriding the market gate. My single flip condition is a verified six-month cohort of paying capacity customers showing expanding usage and positive contribution profit after provider and support costs, with sustained savings at equivalent quality and reliability against OpenRouter. That would turn a plausible procurement mechanism into evidence of a business worth owning.
