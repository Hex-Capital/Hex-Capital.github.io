# ObsessionDB -- Exit Evaluation

**Action:** Watch  
**Evidence confidence:** Low

### Company case

Strategic acquisition is the strongest path. A managed database provider such as Aiven could buy ObsessionDB’s ClickHouse-compatible storage engine and cache to improve the economics of its managed service and bring over production workloads. Osmosis reported that its annual operating cost fell from about $60,000 to $30,000 after migrating from Tinybird, while serving 13 million API requests in 30 days. That testimonial measures customer savings. The buyer’s potential margin gain and the value of owning the engine rather than licensing it remain inferences. EnterpriseDB’s acquisition of Altinity shows that a buyer has acquired an adjacent ClickHouse business. The memo gives no price for that deal or evidence of interest in ObsessionDB. *(The Idea, Defensibility, Market & Traction)*

The operating milestone is to reproduce a material cost advantage on paid production workloads against current shared-storage alternatives while meeting tail-latency targets during cache misses and concurrent ingestion. ObsessionDB reports a deployment serving APIs over 50 TB with hundreds of thousands of events ingested per minute, which makes production testing plausible. Runway, burn, and the capital required to repeat those deployments are unknown. The documented proprietary engine and annual customer-cloud license support a plausible acquisition structure. IP assignments for work originating at Numia and customer change-of-control terms remain unverified. *(The Idea, Defensibility, Market & Traction, Founders & Team, Key Risks)*

Under the shared scenario, a $500,000 check at a $10 million post-money valuation buys 5%, diluted to 3% with no follow-ons. The $2.5 million gross cash target within eight years requires about $83.3 million of distributable equity at 3%, $125 million at 2%, or $62.5 million at 4%. With illustrative net debt of $5 million, a $25 million enterprise-value sale at year eight returns $600,000 gross (1.2×), and a $100 million sale returns $2.85 million gross (5.7×). These are pro rata, pre-tax scenarios. Actual ownership, debt, fees, preferences, and a realizable sale price are unknown. In a scenario where growth plateaus, buyers stay away, public scale remains out of reach, and cash is reinvested through year eight, the holder realizes $0 and retains the shares. A wind-down recovery depends on the residual after obligations and preferences, which the memo does not quantify. *(Market & Traction, Key Facts)*

The memo reports paying customers and pricing. It supplies no cash-generation evidence for a sponsor buyout, eligible demand for a secondary or tender, operating scale or readiness for an IPO, or excess cash and willingness to fund dividends or buybacks. *(The Idea, Market & Traction, Key Facts)*

### Countercase

ClickHouse Cloud already offers isolated compute over shared storage, customer-cloud deployment, and its own agent service. Altinity’s shared-storage extension gives EnterpriseDB an alternative it already owns. The published ClickHouse benchmark uses warm-cache runs, and the architecture documents added latency on cache misses. Osmosis’s savings against Tinybird leave the comparison with these closer substitutes open. This weakens the case for paying to own the engine, but the memo establishes neither a structural closing barrier nor a supported price range that leaves the holder materially short. The Pass gate does not apply. *(Defensibility, Market & Traction, Key Risks)*

### Decisive diligence question

Can repeatable production results show that owning ObsessionDB creates enough value for a managed database provider to justify an acquisition near or above the illustrative $88.3 million enterprise-value hurdle? Move to Reach out when paid deployments demonstrate durable savings at comparable tail latency and a buyer economic model supports that price after accounting for integration costs and the alternatives of building or licensing.
