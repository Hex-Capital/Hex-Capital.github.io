# Verdant -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important question is not how many officials the founders interviewed. It is what broke, what they did next, and whether the workaround changed anything. Verdant interviewed more than 200 local-government employees and discovered that incomplete permit applications create repeated exchanges before review can begin. That is unusually diligent problem selection. But the dossier names no rejected deployment, failed ordinance import, procurement barrier, or inaccurate review followed by a founder-built correction. I cannot infer resourcefulness from effort alone.

The founders are plausibly close to the problem. Aidan Ng worked across DOJ, USDA, Capitol Hill, and Deloitte’s government practice; Jason Yi built government HR and legislative-tracking software and researched public-benefits automation. More important, they chose a genuinely ugly wedge. Every jurisdiction brings its own ordinances, forms, GIS data, hearings, and approval process. Verdant must translate that mess into configured intake and review workflows while keeping decisions with government staff. The schlep is real. What remains unproved is whether permitting arose from a specific collision the founders experienced or from systematic interviews around a broad govtech thesis.

The product description sounds like software, but it is not yet technical proof. Verdant says it checks drawings against local rules, flags omissions, exposes ordinance and GIS sources, and drafts reports. Yet the only dated shipment is marketing site v0.1 on July 3, 2026. There is no live jurisdiction, accuracy result, processed-permit count, or independently inspectable artifact. The 200 interviews identify a narrow user type and an urgent job, so they are better than a TAM slide. They do not show that planners repeatedly use the crude product, that applicants complete submissions more successfully, or that anyone pays.

The strongest case for investing is that government software often looks weakest just before implementation knowledge begins compounding. Two founders with relevant government and technical experience have manually interviewed the buyers, chosen modular deployments, and found a workflow painful enough that GovWell and Clariti have raised substantial capital to attack it. A first jurisdiction could turn ordinance configuration from apparent services work into the proprietary substrate. The exception does not apply yet, however: there is neither a working prototype strong enough to replace missing founder evidence nor unmistakable user pull. Competitor funding validates demand but does not validate Verdant.

Nor do Verdant’s public signals match the concrete proof spikes I tend to fund. LinkedIn followers, a summit sponsorship, demo requests, and a YC launch are investor-legible activity, not usage. The distribution plan is at least direct rather than dependent on an unnamed partner, and there is no evidence of premature hiring or delegated founder sales. But the cofounders’ shared-execution history is vague, no route change has been tied to contrary evidence, and the reported $307 million global permit-management market is too narrow to establish a large outcome without expansion into adjacent government workflows.

I would pass now. The single condition that would flip me is a named jurisdiction using Verdant repeatedly after the founders encounter a specific configuration or procurement blocker and ship a measurable workaround within weeks—for example, reducing incomplete resubmissions or staff review time on actual permits. That one episode would test the founders, the artifact, and the user pull simultaneously. Until then, Verdant is a sensible thesis with unusually good discovery work, not yet a logical proof.
