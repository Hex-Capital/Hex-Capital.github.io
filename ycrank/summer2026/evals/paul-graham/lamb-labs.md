# Lamb Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The 20,000-token-per-second chip target makes Lamb Labs a high-schlep, low-animal company. Designing architecture-specific silicon is work most software founders unconsciously route around, but Lamb Labs has supplied no tape-out, independent benchmark, customer, or pre-funding stunt showing that Niki Kotecha and Thomas Lanning are animals rather than unusually clever researchers. The unpleasantness of the problem is ahead of the evidence that the founders can survive it.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness: Brian Armstrong built the wallet, then ran toward the compliance machinery everyone else avoided. Lamb Labs similarly chose the painful version of AI inference—model conversion, compiler work, sub-1W hardware, manufacturing, and OEM integration—rather than another cloud wrapper. But Lamb Labs has only claimed 2× GPU inference and described 20,000+ tokens per second and 63× intelligence per watt as future chip targets. Coinbase had already entered the schlep; Lamb Labs has drawn a technically interesting map of it. That puts Lamb Labs above ordinary software startups on this dimension, but well below Coinbase.

**Kiko** (2005) is the low rung for the animal test because a failed product sold on eBay was not itself proof that the founders had found a way through; the important evidence appeared later when Kan and Shear returned with Justin.tv. Lamb Labs shows the same evidentiary gap before the redemptive second act: no fabricated chip, no independent reproduction of the 2× claim, no revenue improvisation, and no named design partner. Niki Kotecha’s £150K-plus scholarship and Thomas Lanning’s top-of-class record prove intellectual horsepower, not relentless resourcefulness. Lamb Labs may contain animals, but animals leave tracks, and Lamb Labs has not yet left enough.

Niki Kotecha’s graph-neural-network research and Thomas Lanning’s CERN-data ML work give Lamb Labs an intellectually organic route into model optimization, but the dossier never says either founder personally needed sub-1W inference or built Lamb Labs to solve a problem in a prior robot, phone, or wearable. Over 18 months that distinction compounds. A founder solving personal pain knows which compromises users will tolerate; a founder pursuing an elegant technical opportunity can spend 18 months optimizing the wrong boundary. Lamb Labs could close the loop through direct work with robotics and industrial manufacturers, but the current outreach list is a collection of customer categories, not evidence of lived pain.

Thomas Lanning has public GitHub repositories and reproduced the Higgs-boson result with CERN data, while Niki Kotecha published work on graph neural networks and multi-agent reinforcement learning. That puts Lamb Labs above credential-only technical founders, but below hacker genealogy that produces an undeniable artifact before the startup exists. The score would flip upward if Lamb Labs released a working converter, reproducible model-quality results, or a compiler demonstration that outside hackers could test. Until then, Lamb Labs has academic traces rather than the kind of hacker trail Drew Houston left with the **Dropbox** (2007) demo.

**Stripe** (2010) is the useful parallel for co-founder configuration: two technically formidable founders attacking infrastructure together. Lamb Labs also has a pair, and Kotecha’s AI research complements Lanning’s mathematical-physics background. But the Collisons had years of shared history and had already built Auctomatic; Lamb Labs discloses no prior working relationship at all. Two names are better than one, but Lamb Labs has not shown whether the pair behaves like brothers debugging at 3 a.m. or two impressive résumés placed beside each other.

The achieved 2× GPU claim and the absence of a tape-out make my 36-month forecast precise: by July 2029, Lamb Labs will have pivoted from selling custom edge silicon to licensing its diffusion-conversion software or compiler, with less than $2 million in annual recurring revenue and no Lamb Labs chip in mass production. Hardware schlep becomes valuable only when founders repeatedly turn physical obstacles into artifacts. Lamb Labs currently has the schlep without that sequence of artifacts, so the software layer is the likeliest surviving piece.

Hailo’s $340 million, Axelera AI’s more than $450 million, and Etched’s $800 million make the strongest bull case for Lamb Labs: inference efficiency is important enough to fund, and Lamb Labs may have found an architectural discontinuity those incumbents cannot copy without abandoning their existing designs. **Loopt** (2005) is the known exception—the infrastructure can be early when founder force overwhelms bad timing—and Lamb Labs has two mathematically serious founders plus a claimed 2× result. But Loopt’s exception was Sam Altman passing the animal test; Lamb Labs has no equivalent evidence, and its 20,000-token and 63× targets remain unverified. An independently reproduced sub-1W benchmark from fabricated silicon plus a named OEM design partner would flip my verdict to Invest. Without both, Lamb Labs is a research bet wearing a chip company’s capital requirements.
