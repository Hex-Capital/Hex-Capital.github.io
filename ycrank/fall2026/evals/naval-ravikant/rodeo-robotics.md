# Rodeo Robotics -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Rodeo Robotics is trying to turn a technician’s work into a machine’s accumulated knowledge. That interests me. Michael Hazard spent four years at Applied Intuition; Colby Duke led neural simulation there, where the founders met. These are relevant encounters with vehicle complexity, according to their YC profiles. But simulation expertise does not establish that either founder has built or operated an automated maintenance system. I give their experience credit without silently converting it into demonstrated knowledge of servicing vehicles.

The decisive question is what remains after another robotics team copies the software. Rodeo describes collecting demonstrations through teleoperation and instrumented hand tools. That is a concrete acquisition mechanism for physical task data. It could make human assistance productive twice: completing today’s oil change and teaching tomorrow’s robot. But the dossier establishes neither an accumulated proprietary dataset nor measured learning gains. Hardware creates contact with reality; ownership of hardware alone does not create defensibility. Rodeo must show that its experience becomes increasingly expensive for a competitor to reproduce.

The strongest case against passing is that the initial service can finance the learning. Centralized fleet depots offer repeated tasks, and human assistance permits a useful product before full autonomy. This fits my observed preference for businesses attacking physical bottlenecks. It also creates the central danger: every deployment may purchase more technician hours rather than produce reusable capability. Nothing establishes permanent dependence on human labor, so I will not assume it. Equally, no evidence shows that adding one customer improves another customer’s experience. A proposed learning loop has not yet earned the credit of an operating one.

Oil changes provide a clear initial buyer and task. Expansion into other depot maintenance would require additional manipulation capabilities; the shared customer relationship does not make those capabilities automatic. The forecast $599.7 billion US aftermarket includes substantial spending Rodeo cannot presently address. PitPro’s tire automation and UVeye’s inspections show adjacent activity, but neither their products nor their financing validate Rodeo’s economics. Public pricing, completed paid maintenance, and intervention costs remain unknown. The reported $500,000 YC financing and October 13 launch coverage establish a beginning, not direct value capture.

I pass on the present evidence. My exceptions for outstanding founders before commercial clarity, or unusual prototypes before distribution, deserve consideration here. The dossier supplies relevant experience but no comparably decisive founder action or demonstrated prototype performance to justify that override. There is also no evidence of an integrity violation, manufactured fundraising urgency, or the other behavioral red flags that would make this an active rejection. My one verifiable condition for reopening is an auditable fleet-depot deployment showing human minutes per successful oil change falling across successive vehicle cohorts while completion quality holds constant. That would begin to distinguish a compounding maintenance system from a service business carrying robots.
