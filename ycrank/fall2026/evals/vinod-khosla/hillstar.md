# HillStar -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Nine minutes to evaluate 19,800 pages is interesting. The investment question is what becomes possible after those nine minutes. Does HillStar materially reduce the cost or lead time of building electrical infrastructure, or does it accelerate one administrative step while equipment shortages, engineering, and approvals determine delivery? The dossier establishes a procurement workflow. It does not quantify the connection to cheaper power, additional grid capacity, or a radically larger supply of procurement expertise. I cannot price an enormous consequence merely because the customers are utilities.

There is useful product evidence here. An unnamed utility reportedly processed 22 submissions with an 11-person expert team; another case identified missing mandatory certifications in two of 14 bids, including the cheapest. Source-linked findings and committee review address a real constraint: speed has little value if an award cannot survive scrutiny. But the nine-minute claim uses a baseline drawn from comparable procurements, and the dossier does not establish total human review time or error rates. It also supplies no controlled comparison with Keelvar or Fairmarkit. HillStar has evidence of functioning workflow automation, with technical superiority still unresolved ([case study](https://www.hillstar.ai/case-studies/engineering-services-rfp)).

The founders are the strongest objection to passing. Nooshin Behroyan built Paxon in utility services and reportedly exited in 2025. Kenny Stone co-founded PlanGrid, acquired for $875 million in 2018, and subsequently worked in geothermal software. That combination puts customer knowledge beside experience building software for difficult physical-industry workflows. HillStar fits the execution-heavy enterprise businesses that my broader investing pattern accommodates. Yet these accomplishments do not document rapid belief revision or the recruitment of specific people to retire named risks. The advisors’ utility credentials establish access; they do not establish who owns correctness, deployment economics, or the learning system.

The relevant exception is Block: an ordinary-looking workflow wedge can become consequential infrastructure when exceptional builders expand its scope. HillStar shares the possibility of entering through an underserved operational problem. The material difference is that this dossier does not establish how procurement assistance expands into control over a much larger value pool. Retaining award rationales and supplier outcomes could help, but collecting records alone does not prove a proprietary learning advantage. I would need evidence that those records improve subsequent decisions in a way competing sourcing platforms cannot readily reproduce.

My first experiment would attack evaluation correctness. On previously unseen utility procurements, can HillStar preserve mandatory-requirement detection while sharply reducing **total** expert labor? The certification example is encouraging product evidence, but it does not establish a repeatable acceptance threshold, test budget, or stop condition. Those planning details are absent. Economics are similarly undisclosed: the platform-plus-professionals offering provides no labor allocation, pricing, or delivered-cost model. I see no demonstrated subsidy dependence or premature scaling behavior. I also cannot assume inexpensive software economics while professionals remain part of delivery.

I pass with my own money. HillStar could become a valuable procurement company; the evidence does not yet establish the magnitude and mechanism I require. **One verifiable flip condition:** an independently audited deployment across multiple utilities showing at least a 10x reduction in total evaluation cost, no deterioration in mandatory-requirement detection, and a measured reduction in the procurement bottleneck delaying infrastructure delivery. That would connect its document-processing capability to consequential, economically repeatable change.
