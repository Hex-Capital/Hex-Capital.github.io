# Skymerse -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

**3,500+ registered Notamify users** make Skymerse a fast-learning vertical software company, while the absence of a capital-intensive roadmap makes Skymerse a poor match for my money. The analytical signature is high learning rate and low required-capital fit. Damian Szumski has moved from a narrow NOTAM product processing three million notices into an operational layer spanning schedules, weather, aircraft limits, manuals, and cockpit support. That is intelligent adaptation. It is not yet a world-state change. The economic mechanism Skymerse currently breaks is dispatcher workflow fragmentation, not aviation labor itself.

**Slash (2024)** is the high rung on learning rate because its documented Kanye West pivot exposed a founder willing to discard the original thesis when reality supplied a better one. Skymerse shares the mechanism: Notamify became a wedge, and Skymerse expanded the thesis from interpreting notices to maintaining common operational context across each flight. The divergence is decisive. Slash had a public pivot narrative tied to market feedback; Skymerse reports that thousands use Notamify daily, but the dossier attributes no comparable adoption to the broader Skymerse system. Learning is demonstrated, but the updated thesis remains unvalidated.

**DoorDash (2013)** exposes the low-rung capital-fit mechanism: a capital-light business can become enormous, but capital scarcity is not the moat and my check size is not the advantage. Skymerse sells Notamify Pro for $24.90 per month and provides no roadmap requiring $50 million before a commercial milestone or $200 million to scale. Skymerse may need expensive airline integrations and safety assurance, but those are enterprise-software costs, not the capital-hostile science of Commonwealth Fusion. DoorDash escaped this objection by reshaping a massive commerce flow. Skymerse has not yet earned that exception.

**Three hours per flight** is enough waste for Skymerse to build a meaningful business, but consequence compounds only if Skymerse progresses from presenting alerts to displacing material dispatch labor and autonomously resolving operational constraints. Over the next 18 months, every operator manual and correction could improve Skymerse’s flight-specific evaluation layer. The compounding stops where Notamify’s terms require outputs to be checked against official sources and prohibit their use as the sole basis for safety-critical decisions. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself.” A great outcome here requires eliminating the reconciliation loop, not placing a better screen inside it.

**Three million processed NOTAMs** provide Skymerse with useful domain infrastructure, but they do not yet constitute the technical risk that repels consensus capital. Skymerse has a custom aeronautical model and AIP extraction agents; Jeppesen already has Airflow, while Flightkeys and Air Space Intelligence have adjacent operational software. The score flips a tier only if Skymerse demonstrates a falsifiable reliability breakthrough: automated interpretation of operator-specific manuals and live constraints at safety-critical accuracy that an incumbent cannot reproduce from the same public data. Until then, Skymerse is stack-aware software, not a science miracle.

**Glean** is the single-anchor parallel for founder orthodoxy. Arvind Jain’s ex-Google search background was expert continuity, but Glean overcame it by attacking Fortune 500 information asymmetry at enormous scale. Damian Szumski is similarly continuous with his field: licensed dispatcher, LOT and FLYR operator, Lufthansa Systems aeronautical-chart experience, then Flightradar24 API product owner. Skymerse benefits from exceptional founder-market fit, but Skymerse offers no documented instance where Szumski publicly contradicted aviation consensus. Experts often extrapolate the past; Jeppesen’s overlapping launch suggests the industry is already extrapolating in the same direction.

**Jeppesen’s 2026 Airflow launch** makes my 36-month forecast specific: by July 2029, Skymerse will have narrowed back toward dispatcher decision support and NOTAM monitoring and will be acquired by Boeing’s Jeppesen for less than $100 million, rather than becoming an autonomous flight-operations platform. Skymerse’s learning rate should keep Skymerse commercially relevant; its low capital requirements, shared data sources, incumbent integration disadvantage, and non-authoritative outputs should pull it toward strategic absorption.

**LOT Polish Airlines** is the strongest objection to that forecast. Skymerse already has a production airline reference, thousands of daily Notamify users, three million processed notices, and a founder who has lived inside dispatch, airline software, and aeronautical APIs. The DoorDash exception says capital-light businesses can still reshape an economic flow, and operator-specific corrections could become a proprietary decision dataset Jeppesen cannot buy off the shelf. The objection stings. It loses today because Skymerse’s public traction belongs primarily to Notamify, while Skymerse’s terms preserve independent verification and therefore preserve the dispatcher’s core responsibility. One signed airline contract designating Skymerse as the authoritative system for automated dispatch release—without mandatory source-by-source human verification—would flip my verdict.
