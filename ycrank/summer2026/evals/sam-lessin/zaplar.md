# Zaplar -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Zaplar has raised SEK 3 million from Nyman Holding and Michael Ingelög, but I still cannot write down the proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or whether the YC round is genuinely scarce. The obscure syndicate is mildly better evidence than a prestige auction; it is not evidence that I am buying cheaply. Price matters especially here because “agentic operating system” is already an investable meme, Mews has raised $811.6 million, and Apaleo already markets an AI-powered PMS. I would not pay a consensus price for Zaplar’s architectural ambition.

The real signal is narrower than the pitch. Zaplar has pilots operating in several countries, conducted more than 100 hotel interviews, and reports a 20% reduction in estimated walking distance across tens of housekeeping schedules. Spatially mapping a hotel may create proprietary operational context that generic agents lack. Founder-led property visits also fit a procurement market where workflows are learned on-site rather than acquired through rented digital distribution. But the dossier does not establish that this context caused a purchase, renewal, or expansion. Pilots are evidence of access; 897 LinkedIn followers and association memberships are not.

The team plausibly understands both computation and hotel operations, but the receipts are uneven. Axel Andersson Lingbert worked in hotels and restaurants, Douglas Solberg has family-hotel exposure, Jon Milles brings cybersecurity and autonomous-systems experience, and Oscar Karlströmer has managed B2B projects involving accounts above A$1 million. Collectively, they can speak to both sides of the bridge. None has yet been shown shipping a production hotel system, operating a hotel at scale, or completing an enterprise deployment across both environments. Four co-founders and eight employees also make accountability important: the dossier names CEO, CTO, COO, and CFO titles but does not establish who has final authority when product scope, sales commitments, and operational safety collide.

My central objection is that Zaplar has confused a promising wedge with an operating-system mandate. Replacing 5–15 systems means synchronizing bookings, payments, housekeeping, messaging, revenue management, and vendor coordination while surviving migration and retraining. That is several reliability problems, not one venture moment. The dossier gives no dated milestone, pass/fail metric, or use-of-funds plan, so I cannot tell what one scarce check reveals. The 20% housekeeping result could support a finite experiment; “become the agentic hotel OS” cannot.

The bull case stings: incumbents may be trapped by old architectures and integration commitments, while Zaplar’s property graph could accumulate layouts, rules, and workflow histories that become a genuine dark pool. Pilots across several countries suggest the concept travels, and targeting hotels already planning system replacements concentrates sales effort around real buying windows. But there is no disclosed revenue, customer count, retention, gross margin, burn, or route to break-even. Delete the next financing and the current plan still appears to require simultaneously completing a core system, supporting migrations, and funding enterprise sales against vendors with thousands of customers.

I am passing at this evidence level. The verifiable flip condition is five named hotels paying for the housekeeping wedge, with at least three renewing or expanding after six months and documented labor savings exceeding the subscription price. That would show owned workflow causing purchases, isolate a truth-revealing experiment, and provide a controllable cash metric. Without it, my 36-month expectation is that Zaplar narrows into housekeeping and property-operations optimization rather than successfully replacing the full hotel software stack.
