# Lyon -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A bank should not maintain separate little prediction machines for fraud, credit, collections, churn, and cross-sell; one longitudinal representation of customer behavior should adapt across all of them. Lyon has found a leak from that world: a model trained on 28 billion transactions delivered four times the precision of existing premium-card-conversion rules. But the dossier does not map the road from that result to the 2032 institution. It names recent foundation-model research, not the intermediate technical, regulatory, and organizational transitions required to make Lyon the durable layer beneath financial decisions.

The strongest case for investing is that this is not a narrated demo. A fintech serving tens of millions of users supplied consequential data, accepted a customer-cloud deployment, and is extending the model from card conversion into credit. An insurer is also working with the company. Those are costly enterprise behaviors: security review, engineering time, data preparation, and reputational exposure. At pre-seed, that is far more informative than a pile of lightweight pilots, and it makes the working artifact Lyon’s clearest signal.

Now I ask why the old system has its present shape. Lyon says financial institutions endure separate rules and months of feature engineering, but it does not quantify that rent, identify who captures it, or show whose agency is restored. Today the product makes incumbents better at underwriting and cross-selling; it does not yet transfer control to consumers, operators, or independent practitioners. Without a sharper institutional wound, four-times precision can become a valuable capability that an existing decision platform bundles rather than the foundation of a standalone company.

Gabriel Noya has credible model-systems aptitude: Stanford EE/CS, NVIDIA experience, and a 2025 Edge AI competition win for optimizing `torch.conv2d` on a phone. What is missing is the pre-company trail showing that transaction intelligence was an obsession rather than a newly available application of strong technical skills. There is also no documented banking operating experience, recruiting story, or co-founder history. YC lists one active founder and a team of two while LinkedIn shows three employees; Gabriel is identified as CEO, but I would verify roles, ownership, and the contractual decision-maker before investing my own money.

The lower-stack shape does fit me. Lyon trains inside the customer’s VPC, produces reusable embeddings, and attaches LoRA adapters for several downstream tasks rather than wrapping one closed model API. SOC 2 Type 1, GDPR compliance, 35 monitored controls, and Type 2 work in progress are meaningful responses to the privacy gate. The weakness is control: customers retain the data and trained intelligence, no pooled-data flywheel is evidenced, and Taktile, Pecan, Feedzai, and H2O.ai all possess funded routes into adjacent functionality. Reusable schemas, deployment tooling, and security reviews could compound, but the dossier currently describes those advantages as possibilities rather than demonstrated assets.

My 36-month base case is a technically respected business reaching $3–8 million ARR across fewer than ten financial institutions, with pressure to become part of a broader decision platform. I am neutral today because the artifact is unusually real while the six-year causal map and independent bottleneck are not. One result flips me: three paid institutions using the same core Lyon representation for at least three materially different tasks, each outperforming its incumbent system, with deployment time shrinking from one customer to the next.
