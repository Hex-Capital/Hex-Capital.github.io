# Neuromorphic -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence case is potentially large but presently unquantified. Neuromorphic could make programmable physical labor available wherever specialized integration engineers keep robots uneconomic. Yet the dossier establishes only weeks-long onboarding, a $24.23 billion robot-software market, and a $2.4 billion laboratory-robotics segment. It never translates those figures into labor displaced, experiments accelerated, laboratories reached, or a 10x cost collapse. “Embodiment agnostic robot brains” describes breadth, not consequence.

The technical case is weaker. An onboard LLM composes reusable navigation, manipulation, inspection, and tool-use skills, but Neuromorphic reports no task-success rate, human-intervention rate, safety result, cross-body transfer benchmark, or comparison against conventional integration. Packaging existing capabilities around wet-lab workflows may be commercially useful; it is not yet evidence of a 5–10x discontinuity. FieldAI, Skild AI, Physical Intelligence, and Generalist AI have collectively raised more than $4 billion to pursue overlapping intelligence. Their financing proves nothing about who wins, but it makes an unmeasured technical claim especially inadequate.

The founders are the reason not to dismiss the company. They have worked together since high-school robotics competitions; Ege Doğanay researches contact-rich manipulation and policy generalization; Vatan Aksoy Tezer has navigation and deployment experience across PickNik, Rapyuta, KABAM, and Augmentus; and the team reportedly participated in deploying more than 1,000 robots. More importantly, they changed strategy during YC, signed a contract within five days, and brought one biotechnology customer to daily operation within a week. That is real belief revision and unusually fast execution, not résumé theater.

The strongest bull case is the known exception in which an initially modest product becomes a platform because a tiny, adaptive team enters a large existing value pool. Neuromorphic has the adaptive team, an expensive workflow, and an early customer. The material difference is that the dossier does not yet show platform-shaped proprietary learning: one deployment cannot establish that execution data transfers across robot bodies, nor that a reusable skill library compounds faster than competitors’ foundation models. Today the business may be a capable Robotics-as-a-Service integrator charging the prior $5,000–$10,000 per robot per month, with software language wrapped around substantial deployment labor.

Capital is also not yet aimed at a falsifiable kill risk. The decisive uncertainty is autonomous reliability across customers and embodiments, but there is no threshold, experiment cost, intervention ceiling, or stop condition. There is likewise no bottom-up path from hardware, monitoring, support, and deployment-engineer expense to an unsubsidized gross margin. My 36-month base case is $1–3 million ARR from wet-lab robotics integration and managed RaaS, with Neuromorphic no longer credibly positioned as a general robot-brain platform.

I pass today. The single flip condition is an independently reproducible deployment benchmark showing the same workflow onboarded on three materially different robot bodies in under one day, completing at least 99.5% of runs without human intervention. That result would convert the current service story into a technical discontinuity and make the execution-data loop worth buying as a one-sided option.
