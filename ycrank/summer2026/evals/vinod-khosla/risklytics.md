# Risklytics -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Risklytics abandoned its Ember disaster-scoring beta for a frontier-tech brokerage before securing producer licensure. That makes Risklytics a high-learning-rate, low-required-capital profile: an agile option searching for a wedge, not a consequence-scale bet. The economic mechanism does not break. Existing brokers still intermediate carriers and insureds; Risklytics merely routes submissions around exclusions. YC pedigree and a rapid pivot match my unstated appetite for adaptable fintech founders, but pending licensure and dependence on carrier permission reinforce my red flag against businesses built around regulation rather than a proprietary miracle.

**Slash** (2024) is the high rung for learning rate because its Kanye West pivot was specific, explained, validated, and eventually supported a $100M Series C. Risklytics shares the willingness to discard a product: Ember reached beta in July 2026 before Risklytics repositioned as a brokerage. The analogy stops at the decision, however. Risklytics provides no evidence about what Ember falsified, what customer observation caused the pivot, or whether brokerage prospects repeatedly requested coverage. A pivot without a documented thesis update can be speed or randomness. Risklytics earns credit for motion, not yet for learning.

**DoorDash** (2013) sits at the low rung on check-size fit because ordinary startup capital made my balance sheet unnecessary; DoorDash escaped that screen only through ambition and a category outcome ultimately worth $72B. Risklytics has the same mismatch without the demonstrated override. Risklytics is a two-person, commission-based brokerage whose immediate milestone is producer licensure, not a $50M technical program or a $200M deployment roadmap. Capital cannot accelerate carrier appetite or manufacture proprietary loss history. My money adds little beyond what YC’s initial financing already supplies.

The **$125.36 billion** global brokerage market could support an excellent business, but Risklytics currently proposes a narrower distribution path inside that market, not its destruction. To flip consequence magnitude into an invest-level score, Risklytics would need to make affirmative AI coverage the indispensable infrastructure through which robotics, energy, construction, and autonomous systems become commercially deployable—and control that infrastructure rather than refer risks to admitted and E&S carriers. Today Risklytics leaves Newfront, Vouch, and Founder Shield intact. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself” does not mean attaching a large TAM to an unchanged economic regime.

**Newfront’s $3.1 billion in annual premiums placed** exposes how the technical-risk dimension compounds against Risklytics over the next 18 months. Risklytics cannot accumulate carrier-appetite data, renewal history, or policy-language intelligence until it becomes licensed and binds policies. During that interval, Newfront can add a physical-AI questionnaire using existing licenses and carrier relationships. Risklytics reports no proprietary model, claims dataset, underwriting engine, or technical breakthrough. The only required miracles are market-access miracles, and those are neither scientific nor defensible.

**Glean** is the single useful founder parallel because Arvind Jain’s domain-expert continuity was acceptable only after capital scale and enterprise-information consequence supplied the override. Risklytics presents the inverse profile: Samuel Gold and Alexander Risio are Harvard outsiders with computer-science, research, and investment-group experience but no documented insurance, underwriting, robotics, or producer-licensing history. Risklytics may be contradicting insurance orthodoxy, but the dossier records no public position, technical thesis, or expert rejection. Ignorance of an industry is not defiance of its consensus. No evidence—applying the absent-evidence default.

Risklytics’s pending producer license and demonstrated willingness to abandon Ember produce a falsifiable forecast: by July 2029, Risklytics will have pivoted again from independent brokerage into policy-language monitoring software for established brokers and carriers, generating $1 million to $3 million in ARR rather than placing most policies itself. Risklytics can iterate cheaply, while carrier access and incumbent distribution make software the easier adjacent market. That is a reasonable outcome. It is not an **OpenAI** (2019) distribution of outcomes.

The **January 2026 AI exclusions** create the strongest bull case: renewal deadlines force action, physical-AI liability grows with robotics deployment, and every placement could compound proprietary policy and carrier data. Risklytics also has the **Slash** exception available—YC pedigree plus a rapid pivot can reveal learning faster than credentials do. The case still loses because Risklytics has zero disclosed placements, no license, no named carrier appetite, and no moat against Newfront’s nationwide channel. One condition would flip my verdict: Risklytics binding 100 physical-AI policies across at least five named carriers by July 2027. Until then, consequence over probability means passing on modest success, not financing it.
