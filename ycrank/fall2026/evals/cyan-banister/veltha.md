# Veltha -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, an injured worker should not have to become a records courier to get a claim resolved. Veltha’s document classification, missing-record requests, and evidence-linked determinations suggest pieces of that world. Getting there requires reliable rule application, institutional acceptance, and faster resolution reaching the worker. The dossier establishes none of those transitions yet. Ten-minute file preparation could lead somewhere wonderful; it could also make today’s administrator more profitable while leaving the claimant’s experience untouched.

The founders give me the strongest reason to keep looking. Roland’s Palantir work on mortgage underwriting automation is relevant operating tuition: applying rules to consequential financial decisions is different from making a chatbot sound confident. Edin’s government AI deployment experience adds familiarity with institutional constraints. His July 2026 conference account also shows commercial learning—changing outreach until response rates moved from 0% to 5%, collecting more than 50 leads. Those are prospects, but the willingness to learn the buying process counts. What I cannot establish is a pre-company obsession with claims or a personally experienced operating wound that makes this particular problem unavoidable for them.

Why does a file take six hours? Veltha identifies actual machinery: scattered documents, missing records, and requests traveling through email, phone, and fax. That is more useful than saying insurance is broken. But who captures the savings? Contract prices are unavailable, and the inferred administration-fee model does not tell me whether faster work benefits employers, adjusters, injured workers, or principally Veltha. The dossier demonstrates administrative friction without establishing an artificial restriction being removed or new control being handed to an individual.

The October launch’s claims of ten-minute preparation and four times the caseload deserve a product session. They do not yet demonstrate the behavior I underwrite. There is no identified adjuster repeatedly entrusting live files to the system, no dated usage cohort, and no methodology behind the improvement claims. Source-linked determinations and licensed signoff are sensible product choices, but neither establishes correct decisions. I would want to follow an awkward claim through the whole workflow, including the moment the evidence disagrees with the proposed answer.

The strongest case against my hesitation is that Veltha can own the operation rather than sell optional software. That fits my recurring attraction to unglamorous workflow repair, and the move from Co-Adjuster toward a TPA could expose the team to the corrections needed to improve the product. But the concealed-infrastructure pattern is still hypothetical: data rights and learning advantages are unverified, while Sedgwick’s Omni and Reserv already combine automation with claims operations. My pre-revenue exception needs a demonstrated technical artifact and unusually strong founder obsession; revenue itself is not the missing ingredient here. Medical-record custody also remains an unanswered privacy gate. Human approval addresses decision accountability, not consent or data access, though the dossier provides no evidence of an actual privacy violation.

I pass with my own money today. Veltha has relevant builders and a specific operating problem, but I cannot yet distinguish a future claims infrastructure company from an efficient new administrator. One verifiable result would flip my decision: an independently audited, 90-day live claims cohort showing materially faster claimant resolution without worse decision accuracy, operating under documented consent and data-custody controls. That would connect the machine’s saved minutes to a person getting their life back.
