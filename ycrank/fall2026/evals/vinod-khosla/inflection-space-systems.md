# Inflection Space Systems -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Ten thousand spacecraft a year matters if it changes what humanity can afford to do in orbit. Inflection Space Systems identifies a manufacturing bottleneck, but the dossier does not connect its 2031 production target to an orders-of-magnitude expansion in affordable communications, observation, or another consequential service. The $3.79 billion satellite-bus market establishes an existing spending pool. It does not establish the consequence of removing this bottleneck. I can see the factory ambition; I cannot yet price the option on its consequences.

The technical work gives me a reason to keep looking. Inflection reports building more than half its internally designed stack, including flight and ground software. Combining terrestrial components, one standardized platform, and robotic manufacturing is a coherent engineering approach. But Apex also pursues productized, vertically integrated manufacturing. Exponent’s 100 kg payload capacity and 1.5 kW payload power define its operating envelope; they do not demonstrate superior production economics. There is enough disclosed implementation to earn modest technical credit, but no measured throughput, labor-hour, or reliability comparison establishing a 5–10x discontinuity.

The strongest objection to passing is the founders. Alex Lisso has worked across spacecraft design, manufacturing, testing, and operations; Matt Hanley automated satellite integration and testing at Loft Orbital and worked on Lunar Photon at Rocket Lab. These are builders addressing a constraint they have encountered directly. Inflection fits my revealed willingness to finance difficult physical systems and manufacturing qualification. A standardized bus could also sacrifice customization to unlock much better factory utilization. That is a serious bull case. However, the dossier documents relevant experience, without an observed belief revision or a difficult recruit assigned to a specific weakness. I will credit the experience without inventing the learning behavior.

The economic question remains decisive. Published prices fall from $1.5 million for one spacecraft to $700,000 each for one hundred—a roughly 53% volume discount. Those are selling prices. The dossier supplies no bill of materials, assembly hours, qualification expense, yield, or utilization assumptions showing that the lower price supports a durable business. The booked Q1 2028 launch is concrete scheduling progress, but it does not disclose a costed experiment with a pass/fail threshold against the largest manufacturing uncertainty. Missing revenue is ordinary at this stage. Missing evidence of the proposed cost collapse leaves the central investment mechanism unproven.

I pass on Inflection Space Systems today. The exceptions I make for an extraordinary instigator attacking a quantified, enormous consequence do not yet override these gaps. Nor does the dossier establish the adverse behaviors I avoid: a business-development opening alone does not prove premature scaling, and there is no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence or founders rejecting contrary data. My flip condition is one independently auditable production demonstration: ten consecutive flight-qualified Exponent units meeting the published specifications, with fully burdened cost supporting a positive gross margin at $700,000 and at least 5x fewer assembly-and-test labor hours than a comparable conventional bus. That would turn factory ambition into evidence worth purchasing.
