# Perit.AI -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine asking a voice agent to fix a disputed payment and trusting it to catch my interruption before moving money. Perit.AI is working on an ordinary detail that could determine whether that world functions: speech models miss transaction-critical information that clean recordings and general raters fail to expose. But imagining the destination is my contribution. The dossier does not yet establish the intervening steps from better speech datasets to dependable delegated transactions, or show that Perit owns the asset those steps will require.

The working business deserves attention. Perit reports six major lab customers, “multiple millions in ARR,” and more than 4,000 delivered audio hours across nine locales. Those are substantial claims at pre-seed, although customer identities and the revenue methodology remain undisclosed. The public artifact is smaller and more inspectable: 45 conversations, 5.10 hours, and 350 monthly downloads. Someone can actually use this material. Downloads do not establish repeated expenditure or production dependence, and the scripted, studio-clean English sample cannot validate the broader deployment promise. I give the product credit without treating the revenue announcement as independently demonstrated customer devotion.

Kshitij and Poorva have built things before. ZeoAuto’s reported profitable $2 million ARR business suggests commercial command; SalaryBook and Poorva’s engineering experience suggest more than a team assembled around an attractive market. That prior-company revenue belongs in my assessment of the founder, never in Perit’s sales column. What I cannot find is the earlier speech experiment, stubborn technical pursuit, or firsthand operating wound that explains why these founders needed to build this particular company. Their history earns meaningful credit, but it does not establish the pre-company obsession that makes me comfortable financing a long, uncertain journey.

Perit’s strongest fit is underneath the visible application wave. Foundry, calibration gates, practitioner recruitment, and live caller environments are operating machinery that multiple labs could need. This matches my pattern of backing concealed infrastructure and mundane workflow repair. Yet the controlled asset currently appears to be the production operation: access to contributors is not demonstrably exclusive, and non-exclusive licensing is the default. Mercor and Turing could recruit and calibrate competing teams. Nor does the dossier identify a particular fee, artificial restriction, or gatekeeper whose removal returns occupational control to contributors. Itemizing compensation makes the economics more legible; it does not establish that transfer of agency.

The strongest argument against my hesitation is that Perit may already be earning its infrastructure position through delivery. Fifty audio hours per working day, if sustained, could teach this team which model failures matter and how to reproduce them economically. That accumulated judgment could become harder to replace than the contributor roster. But the dossier labels the durable rubric and reviewer-history advantage as inferred. My willingness to back an early prototype before revenue does not resolve that gap: the corresponding exception requires unusually convincing founder obsession and technical proof. Here, the commercial claims are stronger than the evidence of an enduring bottleneck.

I would pass with my own money today. This is a promising supplier whose next-wave ownership remains unproven. Recorded consent and regional processing address my privacy concern enough to avoid presuming a severe violation; retention, reuse, and customer-data access still need examination. The CEO and CTO are identified, with no affirmative evidence of a leadership shell game or a governance breach. One verifiable result could flip my decision: an independently documented lab renewal showing that Perit’s accumulated evaluation assets materially improved spontaneous, multilingual transaction completion. That would connect the present operation to the future I want to inhabit.
