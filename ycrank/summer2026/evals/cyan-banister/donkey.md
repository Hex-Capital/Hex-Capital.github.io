# Donkey -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A small American distributor photographs a product, learns which factory actually made it, receives a landed price, and buys without assembling its own sourcing, inspection, financing, and customs operation. Donkey has one artifact leaking from that future: customs-data entity resolution already produces a delivered-duty-paid quote within 72 hours. But the dossier does not map the intervening steps from today’s seven-province inspection network to that default purchasing behavior. I can imagine the future; Donkey has not yet shown me the bridge.

The present system is wonderfully specific. Trading companies conceal factories, capture 15–30% container markups, and can increase duties because customs sees the intermediary’s invoice rather than the factory price. Donkey found the same factory and quoted one restaurant-supply buyer 15% below its existing supplier. More importantly, the team understands why the middleman exists: somebody must take title, inspect goods, finance payment, clear customs, and absorb delivery failures. They are rebuilding that machinery rather than claiming two engineers can make it disappear. The tension is that Donkey’s own margin is also embedded in an opaque delivered price. It may remove one rent collector only to become a more efficient version of one.

Benjamin Martindale is the strongest evidence here. Before this pivot, Prelude OS acquired 31 paying Chinese factories in 39 days and became cash-flow positive. Mandarin fluency, work with Sany and Sinoboom, and driving from Vermont to San Francisco to visit independent distributors look like operating behavior, not accelerator cosplay. James Tan supplies the complementary customs-filing classification work needed to separate factories from shells and forwarders. The two are high-school best friends, but the dossier does not disclose ownership or a contractual tiebreaker. I see no leadership shell game; I would still require one clearly accountable operator before signing.

The product evidence is much thinner than the founder evidence. Donkey quoted $110,000 in its first sales week, but “signing now” is pipeline, not costly customer behavior. There is no settled order, repeat purchase, inspection claim, financing loss, or importer that has entrusted a second container to the system. The 31 Prelude customers prove that this team can sell into Chinese manufacturing; they do not prove that US importers will repeatedly let Donkey own the transaction.

The bull case stings because every completed order could add factory pricing, defect, lead-time, and reliability data while title-taking and net-45 terms make the operation harder to copy than a sourcing interface. That could become concealed infrastructure beneath thousands of importers. Today, however, the dataset’s depth and exclusivity are unestablished, while Sourcify, Flexport, Inspectorio, and Zetwerk already occupy adjacent pieces of the stack. Capital exposure may become the moat, or it may simply magnify classification mistakes and delivery claims. The stale website calculation using an expired 10% Section 122 surcharge is a small but revealing operational error for a company selling landed-cost certainty.

I would not write the check today. My 36-month forecast is that Donkey reaches $15–40 million in annualized merchandise volume by August 2029 but remains a services-heavy trading company with less than $4 million in net revenue, rather than becoming the default procurement infrastructure it implies. One result flips me: a six-month cohort of at least ten independent importers completing second orders at positive contribution margin after financing, inspection, freight, duties, and claims. Repetition would show that the future is arriving through behavior rather than through a very good first quote.
