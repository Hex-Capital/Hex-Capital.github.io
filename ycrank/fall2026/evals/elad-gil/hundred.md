# Hundred -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Hundred’s market turns on the cost of inspecting every incoming part at an acceptable error rate: controlled imaging could let electronics manufacturers replace sampling with complete inspection, provided each station saves more in labor and downstream defects than it costs to deploy and operate. That is a falsifiable market thesis. The dossier establishes the proposed inspection mechanism, but supplies no production throughput, error rates, customer loss baseline, or station economics with which to test it. The broader $25.3 billion machine-vision market does not resolve that question.

The product has more substance than a model demonstration. Hundred describes ambient-light exclusion, controlled illumination, whole-tray imaging, and three complementary comparison methods targeting cosmetic defects below 100 micrometers. Owning image acquisition and the inspection decision gives the company a recurring operational task that better vision models could improve. That earns some workflow credit. It does not establish ownership of factory acceptance decisions, downstream investigations, or a proprietary feedback loop. Elementary’s camera retrofit also creates a concrete competitive test: Hundred’s dedicated optics must produce enough additional inspection value to justify installing another system.

The strongest bull case is that calibration from one acceptable part makes previously uneconomic inspection practical across many component types. Erica Diaz’s five-and-a-half years in consumer hardware, including supplier troubleshooting in China and Mexico, give that product choice credibility. Gosserand brings production-software experience, and Liao brings quantitative and ML research. This fits my pattern of backing physical-industry transitions where relevant expertise can change the economics. But one-part calibration is currently a company-described capability, not demonstrated evidence of faster deployment or better margins. The inferred DINOv3 connection cannot supply the missing technology discontinuity; Hundred has not disclosed using it.

The three founding-partner deployments underway as of October 27, 2026 provide an opportunity to settle this quickly. They do not yet establish repeat usage or customer pull: completed installations, payments, and contracts remain undisclosed. I would keep demand at the absent-evidence default rather than interpret this as failed adoption. Similarly, onsite setup and customer-specific hardware changes could be sensible early learning, but could also make every installation a services project. The relevant red flag is physical delivery retaining service economics; the dossier leaves that unresolved rather than confirming it. Founder credentials establish relevant capability, but do not document the costly commitment or prior startup learning required for additional credit under my founder criterion.

I would pass on writing a personal check today. The known exceptions do not bridge the gap: this is neither a documented frontier-research opportunity with a credible capital path nor a physical business already showing reconstructed unit economics. Hundred could become a useful inspection supplier while customization limits its scale. My flip condition is a customer-verified, paid multisite rollout of the same station design achieving at least 50% contribution margin after hardware, installation, and support while meeting agreed production-throughput and defect-detection specifications. That would demonstrate that the product changes factory economics and can be deployed repeatedly—the two claims on which the investment depends.
