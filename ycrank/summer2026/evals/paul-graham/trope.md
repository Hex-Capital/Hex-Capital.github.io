# Trope -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Trope CUA’s 366 commits make Trope a schlep-heavy hacker-services startup whose strongest attribute is willingness to automate miserable ERP work and whose weakest is evidence that Matthew Chow or Victor Vannara personally suffered that work. Trope has built something substantial enough to operate, but the publicly attributed ERP expertise belongs to unnamed consultants with 200-plus implementations. The founders look more like capable toolmakers who discovered an ugly industry than users who built the tool they wished existed.

Stripe is the high rung for schlep blindness because the Collisons turned integrations, banking relationships, and compliance into the product instead of treating them as obstacles around it. Trope shares that mechanism: Business Central migrations require configuration, data conversion, testing, reconciliation, training, and human approval across projects lasting six to eighteen months. Trope diverges because Stripe’s founders were already commerce hackers, while Trope still routes scope and delivery gates through principal consultants and builds atop a Microsoft runtime available to other partners. The schlep is real. Trope’s ownership of it is not yet complete.

Loopt is the low rung for organic founder pain because Sam Altman’s force of personality had to override weak evidence that the product emerged from a repeated problem he personally needed solved. Trope shows the same gap without the known-exception signal: Chow built firmware at Tesla and Zipline, while Vannara worked on LLM, MCP, and distributed systems at Amazon, but neither biography contains Business Central, GP, NAV, accounting, or ERP implementation pain. Trope may have found a good problem. The dossier does not show that Trope’s founders found it by living inside it.

Trope’s prior workflow-guidance product had paying pilots, served a nonprofit whose legacy system reached 400-plus staff, and reportedly produced two-to-three-times faster completion with 90% fewer errors. That is the beginning of the animal test, not its completion. Over eighteen months, Trope’s resourcefulness should compound if Chow and Vannara personally perform migrations, encode every exception, and turn reconciliations into reusable machinery. Trope’s positioning changes from workflow guidance to partner tooling to end-to-end implementation weaken the signal, however: 366 commits are tracks, but there is no cereal-box stunt, rescued rejection, or named current-product customer.

Victor Vannara’s three Amazon internships and Matthew Chow’s production work at Tesla, Zipline, Generac, and Schneider Electric establish real technical ability, while Trope CUA supplies the shipped artifact that credentials alone cannot. Trope would move into the highest hacker-genealogy tier if its 27-star repository became infrastructure that independent Business Central partners actually extended, or if the custom APIs demonstrated a technical result Microsoft’s agent designer could not reproduce. Until then, Trope has hackers, but not yet a hacker-community pull comparable to the artifacts that preceded Dropbox or Reddit.

Stripe’s Collison brothers are the useful cofounder parallel: two technical founders with complementary abilities and a relationship predating the startup. Trope’s pair grew up in Vancouver and met at UBC, which is much better than founders matched weeks before applying. Trope’s dossier does not say Chow and Vannara built together for years, however, or show who owns the consultant-heavy operational half. The pair configuration is right; the evidence of tested chemistry is incomplete.

Microsoft’s December 31, 2029 GP support deadline gives Trope a finite migration wave into which ugly work can be sold immediately. My 36-month prediction is that by July 2029 Trope reaches $3 million to $7 million in annualized revenue, with more than half still coming from implementation and migration services rather than managed-agent subscriptions. Trope’s high schlep score should win projects; Trope’s weak organic founder-problem fit will make it harder to discover the narrow product abstraction that turns those projects into Stripe-like software.

Microsoft’s custom-agent designer is the strongest bear mechanism: Microsoft supplies the runtime, established partners supply the ERP knowledge, and DualEntry, Campfire, and Rillet each have roughly $100 million or more to eliminate the implementation layer altogether. Trope has disclosed no current-product revenue, and its most credible operating knowledge sits with unnamed consultants. But agent tooling does not clean ledgers, reconcile migrations, train users, or accept fixed-price delivery risk; Trope is explicitly offering to own those schleps, and its prior paid pilots plus 90% error reduction show that Chow and Vannara can make back-office automation work. I would flip to Pass if Trope cannot name three paid Business Central implementations by January 2027.
