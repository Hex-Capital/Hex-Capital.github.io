# GUILD -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Defense buyers describe a part once; software decomposes the drawings, clauses, testing, packaging, and deadline; qualified factories receive work they can actually perform; the resulting delivery teaches the network how to price the next job. That is a believable future. The leak is visible today: small-business participation in the defense industrial base fell more than 40% during the decade before 2023, while GUILD already presents itself as the accountable prime contractor rather than another supplier directory. The missing piece is the causal bridge. The dossier does not show how many bids became awards, how many awards became accepted parts, or whether execution memory improves the next bid.

The broken rule is unusually concrete. Government manufacturing demand is trapped inside scattered PDFs, portals, compliance clauses, and institutional knowledge. Small factories bear the translation cost before they can even determine whether a contract fits them, while buyers absorb delay and coordination risk. GUILD attempts to return agency to those factories by taking responsibility for pricing, compliance, testing, logistics, and delivery. The active supplier portal—with application, interview, and integration stages—is more meaningful than its 197 LinkedIn followers because it reflects the actual work required to make this system function. Still, the dossier quantifies the 40% supplier decline, not the rent GUILD removes or the improvement it produces.

I am less convinced by the founder evidence. Kaya Celebi has credible technical range—Columbia computer science, Duke computer science and statistics, AI leadership at Hartree, and a 183-star flight-analysis repository—and Erim Gurlemis adds finance, client operations, and AI-implementation experience. But neither biography shows defense-manufacturing tuition, a dated pre-company obsession with procurement, or an earlier artifact built because this problem would not leave them alone. The unexplained trail from CG Defense Consulting to Guild and Mentat may be harmless iteration, but it also leaves open whether this is an independently derived obsession or a recent convergence on a fashionable defense-and-AI market.

The bull case stings because GUILD may already be doing the thing I want to see. It states that it wins and executes government manufacturing contracts, assumes prime-contractor liability, and has built an ontology spanning requirements, quotations, supplier interactions, awards, workflows, and delivery outcomes. If real deliveries are feeding that system, the company is not a thin AI wrapper; it is converting ugly operational responsibility into proprietary execution memory. That could matter more than owning machines. But contract count, value, repeat awards, supplier performance, and delivered-part acceptance are all undisclosed. A claimed flywheel is not yet a spinning flywheel.

The competitive asymmetry is severe. Hadrian controls factories and raised approximately $1.89 billion; Machina Labs and Divergent combine software with physical production assets; Fictiv already coordinates a large external manufacturing network. GUILD’s asset-light structure could reach more processes with less capital, but it also leaves quality, cybersecurity, and schedule performance in suppliers’ hands while contractual accountability remains with GUILD. CMMC makes that exposure real. The team must demonstrate that its ontology controls this complexity rather than merely recording it after humans resolve each exception.

My 36-month base case is a technically capable defense-contracting operation producing $1 million to $4 million in annualized net revenue, but not yet a compounding manufacturing platform. I would not write the check today. One verifiable result flips me: three completed government contracts across at least two factories, followed by repeat awards showing at least a 25% reduction in quote-to-delivery time without a quality or compliance failure. That would turn the supplier portal and ontology from promising machinery into evidence that a piece of the 2032 world has arrived early.
