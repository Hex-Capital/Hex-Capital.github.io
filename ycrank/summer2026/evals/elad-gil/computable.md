# Computable -- Elad Gil Evaluation

GPU supply became fragmented and contractually rigid; AI developers now buy shorter-duration capacity, and the economic variable determining whether Computable becomes large is whether it can create enough two-sided liquidity that reservation holders consistently exit positions without the company supplying the bid. The dossier identifies this mechanism but does not quantify it. There is no target for weekly trading volume, bid-ask spread, utilization, repeat buyers, or the proportion of reservations resold. More importantly, the website promises a standing buyback quote while the terms disclaim any obligation to repurchase. If liquidity depends on Computable’s balance sheet, this is inventory financing disguised as a marketplace.

The underlying market is real. GPU-as-a-service was estimated at $8.21 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $26.62 billion by 2030. Reported 2x H100 price dispersion and 6–24-month commitments create room for transferable weekly contracts. Computable converts capacity into standardized eight-GPU H100 nodes, supports resting bids and offers, and permits either physical redemption or resale. That is a meaningful market-structure product, not a cosmetic interface. But the economic improvement remains asserted rather than demonstrated: the dossier does not show that users obtain lower prices, providers earn higher utilization, or weekly standardization reduces procurement time.

The strongest bull case is that the founders understand electronic-market infrastructure from years at Jump Trading and Coinbase, launched a functioning order-and-redemption system, and entered a category whose adjacent competitors already generate substantial revenue. Runpod reportedly reached approximately $240 million in annualized revenue, Vast.ai exceeded $1 million per month, and SF Compute manages more than $100 million of third-party hardware. Those facts validate demand for alternative GPU distribution. They also make this a crystallizing market rather than an undiscovered one. SF Compute already offers buy orders, sell orders, and resale, with $40 million raised. Computable’s calendar-week grid is a contract specification, not yet a winner case.

Product architecture is the best part of the company. Orders, matching, transferable positions, redemption, and generated price history could become a recurring procurement workflow. The product can also create reusable distribution if buyers begin with one week and later source portfolios of capacity through the same venue. Yet ownership of bid and trade data is not itself a moat. Without demonstrated transaction density, a funded competitor could reproduce both the dataset and the market mechanics. Public listings from August 2026 through January 2027 establish supply presentation, not completed trades or product-market pull; 45 Hacker News points and 26 comments establish attention, not usage.

The founder-market fit is credible but narrow. Trading-infrastructure experience supports building the exchange, while the dossier provides no evidence of prior startup learning, costly commitment, or firsthand GPU procurement pain. None of the known exceptions applies: this is not a frontier research team whose scarce technical capability justifies overriding absent demand, nor a physical-market reconstruction with documented margin transformation. My 36-month base case is that Computable either pivots into a broker or inventory-backed GPU procurement service or remains below $3 million in annualized net revenue because balance-sheet liquidity substitutes for marketplace liquidity.

I would pass today despite the technically coherent product. The single verifiable flip condition is six consecutive months in which at least $10 million of monthly reservation volume clears primarily between third parties, with repeat buyers and sellers, sub-10% bid-ask spreads, and no Computable-funded standing bid. That would validate the controlling variable, establish genuine pull, and turn the forward-price history from a contractual claim into a compounding market asset.
