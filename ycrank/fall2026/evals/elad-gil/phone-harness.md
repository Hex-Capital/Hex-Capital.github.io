# Phone Harness -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Better mobile agents create a buyer for phone-control infrastructure; Phone Harness becomes a large business only if developers repeatedly pay it to complete tasks more economically than they can operate devices themselves. That is the controlling variable: total cost per successful, permitted task, including device time, model calls, retries, and human intervention. The dossier establishes an accessible control layer, but provides no measured task economics or threshold at which customers choose hosted infrastructure over the free implementation. I see a plausible market opening, with the central underwriting proposition still unproven.

There is a concrete adoption improvement here. Personal iPhone control removes Xcode and phone-side installation through iPhone Mirroring, reducing setup requirements relative to iphone-use. The February 2026 competitor result covering all 116 AndroidWorld tasks also supports a capability shift in the broader category, although it establishes neither Phone Harness’s reliability nor its economics. Cloud pricing of $0.05 per phone-minute means $3 per occupied device-hour before other costs. That could be inexpensive for valuable work or expensive for repeated failures. The missing denominator is completed work; a low input price does not establish attractive customer economics or supplier margins.

The strongest argument against passing is distribution. Approximately 4,000 GitHub stars and 403 forks by October 27 suggest Phone Harness has reached developers, and Shawn Pana’s prior responsibility for growth, launches, and developer partnerships at Browser Use is directly relevant to converting that attention. A free local product could become the acquisition channel for hosted capacity as workloads expand. This fits my pattern of investing in infrastructure around a technology discontinuity. But forks establish developer engagement, not recurring production use or conversion to paid devices. The dossier documents neither. Pana’s operating background is useful, while costly personal commitment and specific prior startup lessons remain undocumented; team size adds no negative signal.

The architecture provides more substance than an output formatter: the harness reads screens and executes taps, typing, and scrolling. Stronger models could improve its ability to operate unlabeled controls, and dedicated devices offer persistent logins and app state. Those are foundations for owning execution. They do not yet establish ownership of a recurring customer workflow. MIT licensing, Mobilerun, and iphone-use make basic control reproducible, while unsupported Face ID, camera, and multitouch flows constrain completion. The September 16 telemetry issue reporting transmission of scripts, OCR output, and typing arguments is particularly relevant: retaining authenticated workflows requires customer trust. An opt-out is documented; independently verified remediation is absent.

I would pass with my own money today. My base case is that within 36 months Phone Harness narrows its general-purpose positioning toward hosted mobile development and QA, where repeatable workloads make reliability and device utilization easier to sell. Its distribution could overturn that forecast if developers already need persistent production devices across many applications. But the dossier supplies no rare frontier-research capability or demonstrated physical-economic transformation that warrants overriding the missing market evidence. My verifiable flip condition is a 90-day paid production cohort showing repeat purchases and positive contribution margin per successful, permitted task after device, model, retry, and support costs. That would connect developer adoption to a business rather than requiring me to assume the connection.
