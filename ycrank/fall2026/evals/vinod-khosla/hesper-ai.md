# Hesper AI -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Hesper AI could make insurance investigation substantially cheaper. That matters. But the consequence demonstrated here is narrower than the $8.5 billion adjacent fraud-detection market suggests: the dossier does not quantify investigation spending, how much Hesper can eliminate, or whether savings reach policyholders. Finding $15 million in *potential* fraud is not $15 million recovered, and neither figure is Hesper’s revenue. I see a useful mechanism for reducing expensive expert work. I do not yet see the quantified consequence that makes a low-probability breakthrough worth buying with my own money.

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders have already changed their minds. Their April 2026 fraud-scoring product failed to sell; they rebuilt around completed investigations and subsequently reported an MGA production deployment. That is a material revision after customer evidence. Pankaj’s document-automation engineering and Nitish’s AI distribution experience give the team complementary abilities to execute it. The earlier Nanonets revenue belongs to their former employer, but the experience is relevant. What remains undocumented is their ability to recruit exceptional insurance, investigative-data, and reliability owners around the next risks. Founder adaptability is Hesper’s strongest signal.

The product evidence deserves attention without being promoted into proof. The founders report more than 10,000 claims investigated and an 82% reduction in adjuster time at one unnamed MGA—approximately 5.6 times the labor productivity if the comparison holds. That is meaningful at pre-seed. But the dossier supplies no benchmark method, error-adjusted comparison, or result against Shift or FRISS. Those competitors already offer overlapping investigation capabilities. Completed case files could be a valuable application innovation; the evidence does not yet establish the technical discontinuity or proprietary scientific learning loop I seek. Restrictions on pooled customer-data training also mean that processing more claims does not automatically create a shared learning advantage.

The largest kill risk is whether Hesper’s apparent labor savings survive complete verification. Its contract acknowledges missed fraud, false indicators, and hallucinations, with qualified humans approving adverse decisions. These disclosures identify what must be measured; they do not establish that the system performs poorly. The reported 80% handling-cost reduction supports an economic hypothesis, but inference expense, external-data licences, reviewer rework, and actual usage pricing are undisclosed. The introductory 200 free claims and 90-day platform-fee waiver establish a temporary acquisition offer, not permanent subsidy dependence. Nor does the dossier describe a costed experiment with a reliability threshold and stop condition. I would keep capital focused on that uncertainty before expanding distribution.

Insurance fits my willingness to back execution-heavy fintech, and the founders’ pivot makes that exception worth examining seriously. My broader exceptions, however, require an exceptional instigator addressing a demonstrated enormous value pool, or a consequence sufficient to override missing milestones. Hesper has not established either override. There is also insufficient evidence to assert the AI-wrapper red flag as fact; the investment problem is that differentiated capability remains unproven. I pass. My verifiable flip condition is an independently audited production comparison against a deployed incumbent showing at least 10 times lower fully loaded cost per correctly completed investigation, with no deterioration in missed-fraud or false-positive rates. That would turn the current productivity claim into evidence of a discontinuity.
