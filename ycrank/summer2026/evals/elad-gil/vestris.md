# Vestris -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Computer-use models can now operate fragmented county systems, small title agencies can buy automated processing instead of adding labor, and Vestris becomes large only if it can reduce supervised hours per closing across jurisdictions without increasing title or fraud losses. The evidence supports the first half of that belief: one pilot reports an 8–10-hour process falling to one hour per file, roughly seven hours saved, and doubled capacity. What remains unspecified is the threshold at which those savings survive human review, exception handling, and jurisdiction expansion. That keeps the controlling variable credible but not yet proven.

The technology shift is real and dated. Public computer-use APIs arrived beginning in October 2024, allowing agents to traverse county portals that conventional title software could not economically integrate one by one. Vestris also does more than summarize documents: it opens orders, researches records, calculates prorations, drafts commitments, reconciles disclosures, and preserves human approval. If model improvement makes those actions more reliable, the product gets stronger. This is an owned workflow rather than a formatter, although its proprietary state and integrations remain thin.

The early product pull is promising but narrow. A title shop settling millions of dollars monthly is running the pilot alongside its existing process, and the reported capacity gain is economically meaningful. Yet settlement volume is not Vestris revenue, and the dossier supplies no paid conversion, repeat-file count, retention, or expansion beyond that shop. The product has demonstrated useful work; it has not demonstrated a repeatable market.

The bear case is market structure. Qualia owns ResWare, Flueid has underwriter-approved decisioning, and both already possess integrations and industry relationships that could absorb browser-based automation. Vestris’s claimed training on decades of approved and rejected titles does not answer the relevant moat question: who owns that data, and how much time and money would a funded incumbent need to reproduce it? Two-jurisdiction coverage compounds the risk because every new county can introduce portal behavior, calculations, legal exceptions, and security surfaces. A product that saves seven hours in Montgomery County but requires substantial local implementation elsewhere becomes services-heavy rather than software-scale.

The founders improve the odds of navigating those exceptions. Aahil Valliani and Joshua Tang both processed real closing files at the same title shop, so their workflow choices come from direct operating experience rather than a generic AI thesis. Valliani’s prior company and patents provide some evidence of building experience, while Tang’s Formula SAE telemetry work is relevant to reliable operational systems. The dossier does not document a prior exit, costly commitment, or the kind of second-time learning that would justify overriding a weak market, but here founder evidence is additive because the market gate passes independently.

I would invest, narrowly. Vestris has an unusually coherent pre-seed mechanism: a recent technical capability, a labor-intensive institutional workflow, an end-to-end product, and one customer reporting an order-of-magnitude time reduction. My conviction flips if, by the end of 2027, it cannot show at least five paying agencies across three states processing recurring files with less than two hours of human work per file and no material title or transaction-security incident. That result would distinguish a scalable processing platform from an impressive local pilot.
