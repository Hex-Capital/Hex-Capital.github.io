# Daqstra -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Daqstra begins with specific knowledge earned under physical consequences. Darell Chua and Anchit Kumar built and tested liquid rocket engines together, co-authored a 2025 AIAA paper on distributed data acquisition, and operated through more than ten Mojave test campaigns. The product is not an abstraction invented after reading a market report. It is the software they needed while valves, sensors, PLCs, and rocket hardware were live. That is unusually strong founder evidence for pre-seed.

The important technical question is what remains after a competitor copies the interface. Daqstra already connects NI, Siemens, Modbus, OPC-UA, and custom-TCP equipment and has executed version-controlled sequences, redlines, and abort logic during static-fire campaigns. Physical validation across heterogeneous hardware is harder to reproduce than ordinary application code. But it is not yet a compounding asset. Nominal and Sift have raised $182.5 million and $67 million respectively, instrumentation vendors can move upward, and no evidence yet shows proprietary data, declining deployment effort, or durable switching costs. Integration can become leverage, or it can become consulting disguised as software.

The wedge has room to maneuver. Aerospace testing leads naturally into energy, robotics, drones, industrial validation, and other environments sharing the same orchestration capability. The $48.65 billion test-and-measurement market confirms substantial surrounding expenditure, though it does not establish Daqstra’s software SAM. The causal expansion path matters more than the top-down number: one control plane for increasingly complex physical R&D could become infrastructure across several large engineering markets.

Adoption is early but informative. Active aerospace programs reportedly use Daqstra day to day, the system runs across multiple rocket-engine test stands, and an industrial-energy customer signed a pilot targeting more than $100,000 in annual savings. This demonstrates pain and operational trust, not yet direct value capture. No price, recurring revenue, take rate, or unit economics are public. The founder-led onboarding model also reveals the weakest part of the system: customers do not currently create value or distribution for one another, so growth may require proportional sales and integration labor.

The bear case stings because it follows directly from the product’s strength. Safety-critical heterogeneity makes Daqstra useful, but every bespoke controller and validation requirement can prevent the knowledge from compounding. The company fits my unstated preference for infrastructure bottlenecks and for converting specialized engineering knowledge into scalable output, but not the stronger permissionless pattern where participants improve the network for one another. I see no documented integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-instability red flag. I also do not need a known-exception override: the founders have already supplied the action-based evidence, while the missing network and monetization evidence remains properly discounted.

I would write a small angel check because the downside is bounded and the founders have unusually exact knowledge in a broad physical-computing market. My 36-month base case is $3–6 million ARR from aerospace and energy test programs, with scaling constrained until integrations become reusable rather than bespoke. One condition flips the decision: if the next three commercial deployments show no measurable reduction in engineering and validation time per deployment, Daqstra is a services business with a software interface, and I pass.
