# Whitespace -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Alex Tung and Leon Yao abandoned an AI slide maker and launched Whitespace for wholesale distribution within roughly three months. Whitespace is a high-learning-rate, low-capital vertical AI company: adaptable enough to find a real problem, but not yet consequential enough for my money. **Slash** is the high rung on learning rate because its documented Kanye-driven pivot converted surprising demand into a new company. Whitespace shares the willingness to discard a thesis, but Whitespace has disclosed neither the evidence that triggered its pivot nor usage from the abandoned product. That makes the pivot promising, not yet proof of exceptional learning.

A generic GPT-4 sales-call summarizer fails because modest capital, commodity models, and feature-level value make my check irrelevant. That low-rung mechanism appears in Whitespace: pricing scales with usage and workflows, the product connects existing models to SAP, NetSuite, Epicor, Sage, Gmail, and Outlook, and no roadmap requires more than $50 million before commercialization. Whitespace may become valuable software, but Whitespace does not need a Commonwealth Fusion-sized balance sheet or tolerate a QuantumScape-length science cycle. I am over-resourced for the deal.

A $60 trillion wholesale-distribution industry sounds consequential until one asks what economic mechanism Whitespace actually breaks. Whitespace targets the 30–50% of distributor staff assigned to back-office work and reports 80% productivity gains in selected workflows. Over 18 months, successful order entry could compound into procurement, inventory, quoting, and accounting, progressively collapsing clerical labor and weakening the legacy ERP as the user interface. But Whitespace currently accelerates workflows inside the incumbent system; Whitespace has not shown that the system disappears. Consequence is more than a 20% optimization play, but far below OpenAI’s civilizational redirection.

SAP, NetSuite, Epicor, Infor, Dynamics, and Sage create integration difficulty, not science risk. Whitespace must normalize inconsistent data, prevent erroneous purchase orders, and operate within customer-defined approval thresholds, but consensus VCs routinely finance those engineering problems. The technical-risk score flips a tier only if Whitespace demonstrates a proprietary execution architecture that achieves near-autonomous transactions across heterogeneous ERPs—or discovers that reliable automation requires a new model and data stack competitors cannot rent. Until then, Whitespace has two falsifiable implementation problems, not two miracles.

**Glean** made domain-expert continuity acceptable because Arvind Jain’s search expertise was paired with enterprise-scale consequence and capital depth. Whitespace instead gives me Alex Tung applying BCG distributor knowledge and Leon Yao applying InstaDeep machine learning to family businesses they already understand. That is excellent founder-market fit, but it is exactly how experts extrapolate the past. Whitespace’s recent pivot demonstrates flexibility; it does not demonstrate that Whitespace has contradicted wholesale orthodoxy. Proton.ai, Distro, Stockline, and Fresho confirm that the underlying thesis is already consensus.

Kingfisher Direct and Warwick give Whitespace more substance than most pre-seed agent companies, while two-to-four-week implementation and three-month payback support a fast commercial loop. **By July 2029, Sage will acquire Whitespace for under $100 million after Whitespace reaches $3–6 million ARR as a distributor-workflow product.** Whitespace’s learning speed can produce a useful strategic asset, but Whitespace’s low capital requirements, incumbent integrations, and crowded feature surface point toward acquisition rather than category collapse.

**Slash** is the strongest objection to my pass: a YC-backed, capital-light company used a sharp pivot to overcome weak civilizational consequence, and Whitespace combines the same learning signal with two named customers, 80% claimed productivity gains, and founders who grew up around distributors. The bull mechanism is that Whitespace becomes the transaction-control layer across a $60 trillion industry, with correction histories and workflow configurations producing switching costs before ERP vendors react. The dossier defeats that case today: customer data cannot train other customers’ agents, competitors already overlap, and no structural barrier prevents Sage or Infor from absorbing the interface. I pass. I would reverse that verdict if Whitespace reaches $2 million ARR across at least ten distributors while autonomously processing more than half of their order volume without human review.
