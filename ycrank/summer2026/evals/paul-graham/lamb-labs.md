# Lamb Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The strongest fact is not the founders’ degrees or the $97.24 billion inference market. It is the company-reported dev board running a 7-billion-parameter model at 6 W. Kotecha and Lanning have built something before receiving permission from a foundry, a large customer, or an incumbent chip company. Their reinforcement-learning, physics, and CERN backgrounds make the result plausible. But the dossier does not say what broke while they built it, what workaround they shipped, or how quickly they did so. I can see technical ability. I cannot yet see relentless resourcefulness.

The 6 W board is real enough to earn substantial technical credit, but not enough to carry the claimed “world’s fastest AI models.” The public targets—20,000-plus tokens per second, 63 times greater intelligence per watt, and twice the speed on existing GPUs—have no independent benchmarks. Nor is there a disclosed process node, tape-out, yield, foundry, or shipment schedule. This matters because Taalas has already fabricated a 6-nm chip embedding model weights, while Etched reports production silicon and more than $1 billion in customer contracts. Lamb Labs has a measured-looking artifact in a market where the decisive measurements have not yet been shown.

The missing fact is who wants the crude product now. Neoclouds, air-gapped data centers, high-frequency traders, robotics companies, wearables makers, and consumer-hardware developers are seven different user wells. None is named as a user. There is no repeated deployment, payment, LOI, or customer benchmark. The 402 X followers and 382 reactions to Kotecha’s launch post show that people find the claim interesting; they do not show that anyone needs the board. A hardware company can be early without revenue, but it cannot be early without choosing whose problem it is learning from.

The bull case is sharp. Two technically serious founders have combined model conversion, quantization, architecture search, and on-chip weights into a 7B-model demonstration at only 6 W. If independently reproduced, that could make local inference practical in environments where power, privacy, or connectivity rules out GPUs and APIs. It would also resemble the known exception for young scientific founders who replaced conventional credentials with numerical laboratory proof. The material difference is validation: Lamb Labs reports its own board result, while the exception had external laboratory evidence. The dossier does not justify crossing that gap by imagination.

There is genuine schlep here—silicon design, fabrication, model obsolescence, customer integration, and regulated deployment—but no evidence that either founder encountered this need through prior work. Their backgrounds explain why they might solve the technical problem, not why they discovered an urgent user problem. Nor is there evidence of a rejected hypothesis and a changed route. The belief that models are now stable enough to hardcode may be right, but if it is wrong, automated redesign must outrun both model evolution and fabrication cycles. That is still an assertion, not running-back evidence.

I would pass today. Lamb Labs fails the people-first gate because the dossier contains no named blocker followed by a shipped workaround, and its strongest proof remains self-reported. The verifiable condition that would flip me is a named customer case study documenting a deployment obstacle the founders overcame, the elapsed time, and an independently measured 7B-model power and throughput result on the resulting hardware. That single record would show both what the machine does and what the founders do when reality resists them.
