# Elimentary -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Elimentary has made something happen inside a financial operation. In its October 19 report, TresVista’s initial deployment completed more than 500 filing checks in under 30 minutes, followed by an hour of human sign-off, replacing approximately 20 hours of work. That is a useful starting point. But I cannot reconstruct the founder story behind it: what stopped the deployment, which founder removed the obstacle, and how long that took. The result earns technical credit. It does not automatically establish resourcefulness ([deployment report](https://www.elimentary.com/newsroom/tresvista-partners-with-elimentary-to-deploy-ai-agents-for-cfo-office-operations)).

The product attacks an appropriately unpleasant problem. Agreements change, records disagree, and someone has to explain every number. A prepared semantic layer, client-specific financial tools, and clause-level provenance are plausible responses to that mess. Elimentary also reports 36 filing-contradicting statements in its open-ended benchmark answers. Without a denominator, I cannot calculate the error rate. I can say that analyst review remains part of the product. The encouraging technical evidence is a functioning workflow with measurable time savings; the architecture and benchmark remain company-reported evidence, rather than independent validation ([benchmark](https://www.elimentary.com/research/prepared-data-vs.-raw-filings)).

Who wants this now? Private-credit operations teams provide a much better answer than “financial institutions.” TresVista is named, more than 25 agents are reportedly in production, and an unnamed $8B-plus fund reportedly uses the product across 150-plus portfolio companies. Those are meaningful pre-seed signals. They establish more than abstract interest, though they do not establish retention or willingness to pay. The claimed $150,000 annual saving belongs to a customer’s economics; it is not Elimentary’s revenue. I would give demand substantial provisional credit without turning savings into sales.

The founders have relevant experience. Gorjilla and Saini worked on large credit products, and Sarda previously co-founded a debt-collection AI company. Their overlapping employment also reduces the concern that this is a team assembled for an application. But experience near a problem does not tell me how they personally discovered this particular problem. Nor does the dossier show a rejected hypothesis followed by a changed product. Both remain unknown. I would not convert impressive biographies into evidence for either an organic origin or adaptability.

The strongest case for writing a check is that these unknowns matter less once real financial teams entrust work to the software. That invokes the working-product exception to my usual founder evidence requirement. Elimentary also fits my preference for concrete proof before a financing story. But the exception is not yet decisive: the deployments are company-reported, their commercial durability is unclear, and TresVista is also the principal disclosed distribution channel. The partnership red flag is partly answered by work already performed; it remains relevant to future growth. Meanwhile, Hypercore already combines agents, loan software, and servicing. Elimentary must show that its customized deployments accumulate into a repeatable product rather than consume the founders anew at every customer.

I would hold off on investing my own money. This is a credible product with a specific use, but my controlling founder test remains unanswered. One verifiable event would change my decision: an independently acquired second customer confirming that a named founder overcame a documented deployment blocker within weeks, shipped the fix, and converted that customer into recurring paid production use. That would connect founder behavior to durable demand—the connection currently missing.
