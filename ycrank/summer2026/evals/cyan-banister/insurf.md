# Insurf -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A physician orders treatment; software assembles the clinical evidence, cites the controlling payer policy, obtains attestation, submits through a standard interface, and follows the decision through payment. Insurf has one believable leak from that world: practices already lose roughly 13 staff-hours each week to about 40 prior-authorization requests, while CMS is pushing payers toward electronic workflows and FHIR APIs by January 2027. But the dossier stops after that first transition. It does not show how Inveto moves from automating paperwork to becoming the decision infrastructure rather than one more application riding the new pipes.

The broken rule is much clearer. Insurers impose a permission layer between physicians and treatment, and providers pay the resulting rent in staff time, delayed care, appeals, and uncertain reimbursement. Inveto returns some agency by keeping physicians responsible for attestation and staff responsible for submission while carrying the case from intake through payer decision and posted payment. That is an unusually complete loop. What is missing is proof that the loop changes the economics: no appeal-overturn rate, hours recovered, payment acceleration, or dollars collected has been published.

Chang Lu’s BS/MD path and cancer-modeling research provide clinical context; Bryan Chung’s two IEEE publications and Applied Math–CS/Physics background provide technical capacity. Those are credentials, not yet evidence of independently derived obsession. I cannot find a dated pre-company authorization wound, a series of shipped healthcare artifacts, or recruiting proof beyond two Brown students forming a company. Chang is clearly identified as CEO, which avoids a leadership shell game, but the dossier does not disclose ownership or a contractual tiebreaker. I would verify governance without treating its absence from public material as misconduct.

The selected specialty-practice pilots matter more than 442 LinkedIn reactions. A practice that signs a BAA and permits software into an authorization workflow is spending trust and operational effort, but Insurf has not disclosed a named customer, repeat volume, or outcome. My pre-revenue exception requires more than a functioning prototype: it needs unusually deep founder obsession and concrete institutional proof that survives contact with reality. Here, production PHI remains disabled, SOC 2 has not been issued, and the pilots use selected, non-random cases. The exception does not yet apply.

The strongest bull case is hiding in the audit trail. Versioned payer policies, submitted language, physician attestations, decisions, and payment evidence could compound into a payer-specific outcome system beneath many provider workflows. That would turn an unglamorous repair job into concealed infrastructure. Today, however, the company depends on declared CoverMyMeds and athenaOne interfaces, has no demonstrated dataset scale, and faces Silna’s 50,000 patients plus incumbents processing millions of requests. Staff-controlled submission and disabled PHI show appropriate caution around custody; incomplete compliance still makes healthcare data a gate, not a footnote.

My 36-month prediction is that by August 2029 Insurf reaches $1 million–$3 million in ARR from specialty practices but remains a useful denial-management vendor rather than the health-insurance decision layer. I am neutral today and would not write the check from my own money. One condition flips me: a named cohort of at least three specialty practices using both authorization and appeals for six consecutive months, with published case volume showing materially faster payment or higher overturn rates and no manual-service expansion per case.
