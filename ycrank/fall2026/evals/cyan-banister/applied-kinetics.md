# Applied Kinetics -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

A transformer delivery date can decide whether an entire project gets switched on. Move the clock to 2032: I can imagine developers buying a dependable commissioning date, with equipment specifications, factory capacity, testing, and freight coordinated behind it. Applied Kinetics could help build that world. But that is my extrapolation. The dossier establishes today’s equipment queue; it does not yet establish the intervening unlocks or a company-specific artifact showing that future arriving.

Why does the queue exist? Which portion is scarce manufacturing capacity, and which portion is coordination failure? Applied Kinetics’ founders describe waits of up to three years and quote seven weeks for pad-mounted transformers against up to 65 weeks on the open market ([YC launch](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/WJj-applied-kinetics-ai-native-manufacturing-for-data-center-and-energy-projects)). Their proposed intervention is concrete: review specifications, allocate production, and carry responsibility through approvals, testing, customs, freight, and service. That could return meaningful control over schedules to developers. This is the strongest part of the case. The unresolved distinction is how much delay their coordination can remove when the underlying factory capacity remains someone else’s.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that Applied Kinetics may already have assembled something much harder than its software: access to 40-plus partner plants. Combined with responsibility for acceptance and local service, that could become valuable industrial infrastructure. It fits my pattern of looking beneath a visible boom for the machinery that makes it possible. But access does not establish control over production slots, and the network’s reported 10-plus GW belongs to the manufacturing base’s history, not Applied Kinetics’ deliveries. Separately licensable agents also leave room for a useful software business, without establishing that either business controls a scarce asset. The physical bottleneck is real; ownership of the solution remains unproven.

Rohit and Adhik bring relevant pieces: GPU telemetry experience, AI-agent development at IBM, and a shared Cal Hacks project preceding the company. Those are affirmative signals of technical ability and an existing working relationship. They do not yet show years of independently pursued manufacturing obsession or firsthand command of factory qualification and equipment economics. I would not infer opportunism from that gap. I also would not turn degrees and employers into evidence of operating mastery. My pre-revenue exception can accommodate no sales when a working prototype, industrial commitments, and a founder’s long technical record supply the conviction. Here, no completed customer action is documented, and the seven-week capability remains a quotation rather than a named accepted shipment.

I pass at this point. No disclosed fact establishes a privacy violation, confused leadership, or dependence on a single closed model supplier; this is an incomplete fit rather than an active red-flag rejection. The important missing behavior is a developer trusting Applied Kinetics with a consequential order and then returning after delivery. My one verifiable flip condition is a referenceable repeat customer whose first transformer order passed the required acceptance tests and arrived within the quoted seven weeks. That would connect the attractive system repair to a result someone actually risked money and a project schedule to obtain.
