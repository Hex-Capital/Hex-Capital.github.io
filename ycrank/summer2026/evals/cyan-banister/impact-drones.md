# IMPACT Drones -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the interesting world is not “more drones.” Data centers, airports, and energy sites buy autonomous air defense the way they buy fire suppression: installed, monitored, and replenished as a service. IMPACT Drones has found the present-day leak into that world—the absurd exchange of a $1 million-plus legacy effector for a hostile drone costing under $50,000. But the path still contains three named gates: lawful civilian deployment, reliable classification and interception, and acceptable debris risk. This is a credible future chain, not yet a demonstrated one.

The founders are the strongest evidence. Jasper Lortije previously co-founded AeroIntel, built GNSS-denied visual navigation for long-range ISR drones, and helped secure the 2025 NATO Summit. Alessandro Fiume led AeroIntel sales before joining him. That is technical and commercial tuition earned before this company, plus evidence that the pair can operate around security institutions. Lortije is clearly identified as CEO, so I do not see the leadership shell game; the dossier simply does not disclose ownership or a contractual tiebreaker.

The broken-system economics are delicious, but the rule bites in the wrong direction. IMPACT proposes to return defensive control to infrastructure operators through sealed cells, autonomous interceptors, reloads, and monitoring. Yet 6 U.S.C. §124n does not expressly authorize private site owners to perform kinetic mitigation. The customer may subscribe to protection it cannot legally activate without a government partner. That makes the Middle East pilots more strategically important than 875 LinkedIn followers or two press mentions: they may reveal whether IMPACT has found a jurisdictional beachhead, not merely generated attention.

The missing artifact keeps me from writing the check. HUNTER and turbine-powered SABLE are concrete airframes, but there is no published intercept rate, environmental testing, false-positive rate, completed deployment, or named pilot customer. Fortem reports more than 5,500 captures, Origin’s BLAZE is deployed by four NATO states, and 9 Mothers reports $1.6 million in sales. Pre-revenue itself is not the problem. The relevant exception is CosmicBrain AI, where a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept made missing revenue tolerable; IMPACT currently supplies neither verified equivalent.

There is a real concealed-infrastructure pattern here: onboard visual navigation that survives GPS jamming could become a lower-stack protection layer for data centers, airports, energy assets, and bases. But IMPACT has not shown control of a scarce bottleneck, proprietary flight data, or switching costs, while several funded competitors already field alternative effectors. My 36-month base case is therefore a narrower business—$1 million to $3 million of annualized contracts protecting government-linked bases and energy sites in the Middle East, rather than broad civilian “air defense as a service.” The bull case stings because this team has already navigated NATO security work and may convert pilots faster than public evidence suggests. One event flips me: an independently witnessed live trial at a named civilian site, conducted under documented legal authority, that autonomously intercepts a GPS-jammed target without a false engagement.
