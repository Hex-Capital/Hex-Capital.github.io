# Kebra -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Kebra’s 1:24:06 field recording makes Kebra a tactile workflow built by founders who have never publicly worked in the field they are digitizing. That is the signature: highest on physical-world verifiability, lowest on lived founder origin. Kebra can photograph a missing equipment nameplate and chase a technician by SMS, but Luca Hadife and Mohamed Hosny arrive from Microsoft multimodal AI, Arista switches, and Berkeley—not from closing an HVAC warranty claim at 9 p.m. I see the artifact; I do not yet see the autobiography.

**Niantic** is the high rung because I played Ingress before I invested: the product escaped the pitch and entered my physical environment. Kebra shares that mechanism when Kebra turns a technician’s recording into notes, equipment records, warranty actions, and a request for a nameplate photograph. The divergence is load-bearing: Niantic had observable user compulsion, while Kebra offers only a free 30-minute walkthrough and no public usage, retention, or revenue. Kebra sits between Postmates’ observable delivery and Niantic’s lived product pull—touchable, but not yet encountered in the wild.

**DeepMind** is the low rung on founder origin: elite technical credentials substituted for lived contact with the problem, and the exception worked because Demis Hassabis had extraordinary polymath compounding across games, neuroscience, and AI. Kebra presents the same axis in miniature—Berkeley, Microsoft, Arista, signal processing—with no prior field-service employment found. Kebra does not yet earn the DeepMind exception because Hadife’s multimodal work and Hosny’s hardware knowledge establish Tiz, not a comparable record of cross-domain creation. The red flag is precise: Kebra’s founders appear able to build the product, but the dossier does not show why they were the people destined to notice the problem.

**ServiceTitan’s $961.0 million FY2026 revenue** makes Kebra’s “magically weird” quality fragile over the next 18 months. Kebra begins with an unsexy wedge—reconstructing warranty, parts, billing, and equipment documentation—but ServiceTitan already announced overlapping back-office and technician AI. If Kebra’s company-specific job history compounds into proprietary workflows, the ugly administrative wedge could become a delightful hidden infrastructure business. If it does not, Kebra becomes an AI feature assembled from generally available real-time audio APIs and integrations controlled by larger platforms. No public bidding-war evidence keeps Kebra moderately non-consensus, but YC plus “AI-native operations” is not Crusoe-level weird.

**Luca Hadife is explicitly Kebra’s CEO**, while Mohamed Hosny is explicitly CTO, which is better than shared titles but short of demonstrated tiebreaker authority. Kebra would flip this governance score upward with one concrete fact: Hadife holds final operating authority under a founder agreement covering deadlocks, vesting, and separation. Without that fact, Kebra sits near the HQ Trivia lesson—clean external titles can still conceal an unresolved two-founder partnership. Two Berkeley classmates working together from 2022 to 2026 is relationship evidence, not conflict architecture.

**Anduril** carried regulatory and technical difficulty as the moat; Kebra carries recording consent as a liability. California Penal Code §632 may force Kebra to design explicit consent, retention, and audit controls, but Kebra remains software layered over ServiceTitan, QuickBooks, Gmail, suppliers, and warranty portals rather than frontier hardware or a deeply regulated operating system. The single-anchor parallel therefore breaks where it matters: Anduril owned a difficult physical artifact, while Kebra depends on interfaces that incumbents can restrict or reproduce. Kebra gets some credit for entering the gloriously unsexy back office, not for capital intensity.

**ServiceTitan’s overlapping AI roadmap** leads me to a falsifiable 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Kebra will have abandoned the broad “AI-native field-service company” position, narrowed into HVAC warranty-claim documentation, and reached $1 million–$3 million ARR—or it will be dead. Kebra’s tangible workflow can discover a narrow pain through repeated use, but Kebra’s missing lived-domain origin makes a broad category-defining strategy less credible than a customer-taught vertical pivot.

**Mohamed Hosny’s signal-processing and computer-architecture background** supplies the strongest bull case: Kebra could be the DeepMind exception, with two young polymaths using audio as the sensor layer for an operational company brain. The HQ Trivia exception helps too—an intensely useful artifact can override founder and governance doubts—and Kebra’s automated photograph request shows unusually concrete workflow thinking. The bull case still loses today because Kebra has no public customer, revenue, retention, moat, or evidence that either founder has lived beside a field technician; ServiceTitan already owns the system of record. I would flip to Invest if five named field-service operators each processed at least 1,000 jobs through Kebra over six consecutive months with greater than 90% customer retention.
