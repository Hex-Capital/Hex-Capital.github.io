# Risklytics -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Risklytics is an unsexy regulated bet without a tactile product: Risklytics caught the January 2026 arrival of explicit AI exclusions, yet pending producer licensure means no customer can currently receive coverage through Risklytics. That high-regulation, low-verifiability pairing is the signature. I can imagine a valuable insurance business here, but I cannot yet observe the rocket, camera, ride, or policy placement that proves the founders are playing a deeper game.

**SpaceX** is the high rung because capital intensity and technical risk made the category repellent while compounding the moat. Risklytics shares the frontier-economy mechanism—robotics, autonomy, data centers, energy infrastructure—but Risklytics insures those artifacts rather than building one. Risklytics sits below that rung because brokerage requires neither a frontier breakthrough nor comparable capital commitment; Newfront can attack the same niche using licenses across all 50 states and $3.1 billion in annual premiums placed. The regulated ugliness earns real credit, but the moat presently belongs to the carriers and incumbents.

**DeepMind** occupies the low tactile rung: abstract intelligence could not be ridden or photographed, and only Demis Hassabis’s polymath compounding overrode that absence. Risklytics exposes the same abstraction without the exception. Risklytics offers a risk-intake form, while its website says coverage placement cannot begin until producer licensing is complete; even Ember’s property-level disaster model was replaced before its beta became current-product evidence. A normal founder cannot hand me a Risklytics policy, show me a Risklytics claim, or let me watch Risklytics survive a renewal. Nothing physical has closed the credibility loop.

The **91 LinkedIn followers** make Risklytics early enough to remain non-consensus, and the physical-AI exclusion wedge is just strange enough to get my attention. Over 18 months, magical weirdness compounds only if each Risklytics placement teaches the founders which carriers accept which machine, exclusion, and loss profile; that proprietary appetite map could turn a narrow brokerage into infrastructure in disguise. Before licensing, however, Risklytics accumulates commentary rather than underwriting history. No sophisticated investor passes, round pricing, or carrier commitments are documented, so “underpriced” remains a possibility rather than a fact.

Samuel Gold and Alexander Risio are both publicly titled **Founder**, and Risklytics supplies no named CEO, tiebreaker, operating agreement, or origin story for their working relationship. The governance score would flip a tier if Risklytics named one CEO with final operating authority and documented how two Harvard founders resolve an irreconcilable decision. Until then, the GameCrush lesson is alive: two capable people can still allow product DNA to become negotiable when nobody owns the last word. “Who is the tiebreaker?” is not paperwork; it is the first crisis simulation.

**Crusoe** worked because Chase Lochmiller was of the oilfield world rather than visiting it with a market map. Risklytics presents the opposite founder-origin evidence: Gold lists Harvard investment organizations and research assistance, while Risio lists Harvard computer science, Harvard Ventures, and “Building World Models”; neither public profile shows insurance brokerage, underwriting, producer licensing, robotics, or commercial-risk experience. Risklytics may have found a real discontinuity, but the dossier provides no specific moment when either founder personally felt an uncovered physical-AI loss. I am applying the absent-evidence default rather than inventing a biography for them.

The **January 2026 carrier exclusions** point to my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Risklytics will have pivoted from operating a commercial brokerage into AI risk-intake and policy-review software sold to incumbent brokers, with less than $1 million in ARR. Risklytics can encode exclusion language before Risklytics can reproduce Newfront’s carrier access, licensing footprint, and renewal book. The founders’ Ember-to-brokerage pivot already shows willingness to move toward the layer where software travels faster than regulated distribution.

Ember entered beta before Risklytics changed direction, which is the strongest bull case: perhaps Gold and Risio are young polymaths, the **DeepMind** exception, moving from catastrophe modeling into the neglected transaction layer just as carriers rewrite policies. Risklytics could use the first placements to build carrier-appetite data that Newfront’s broad organization overlooks, and every renewal could thicken that information advantage. The bull case still loses today because Risklytics has no license, revenue, carrier commitment, current-product usage, or founder history inside insurance; the pivot demonstrates motion, not compounding. Ten named frontier-technology customers renewing paid policies with affirmative AI coverage through Risklytics would flip my verdict. Until then, I keep one foot in love and my own money in my pocket.
