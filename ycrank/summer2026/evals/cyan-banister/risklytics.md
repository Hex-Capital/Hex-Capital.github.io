# Risklytics -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Autonomous machines are signing contracts, moving freight, touching patients, and causing losses that do not fit neatly inside “software error” or “physical accident.” Insurance becomes part of the control plane: which actions are permitted, what evidence must be retained, and who absorbs failure. Risklytics has found one small leak from that world—ISO’s 2026 optional AI-liability exclusions and their uneven adoption by carriers. That is real. What is missing is the path between this policy-form fragmentation and a durable company. The dossier names the first change, but not the next two or three transitions that make Risklytics indispensable rather than a competent specialist brokerage.

The broken system is more legible. General-liability and professional-liability policies can leave an AI or robotics operator exposed between forms; generalist brokers compare standard policies while exclusions vary by carrier. The rent is less well specified. I do not know how often these gaps prevent a placement, how many weeks they add, what they cost, or whether customers will pay Risklytics rather than lean on an incumbent broker. The product gives founders better visibility into coverage, but it does not yet return much agency: carrier appetite, wholesaler access, producer licensing, and binding authority remain elsewhere.

The founders have motion, but I cannot yet see obsession. Alexander Risio brings computer science, clinical-ML research, prior startups, and an insurance licensing examination; Samuel Gold brings finance training and has taken leave from Harvard. Yet neither has worked inside commercial brokerage, underwriting, or claims. Ember’s catastrophe-model beta proves that they can build and abandon an artifact, but the rapid pivot from catastrophe modeling to AI-exclusion brokerage could represent useful learning or simply movement toward the newest visible problem. There is no dated pre-company trail showing that either founder kept pulling this thread before it became a 2026 category.

The strongest bull case is that placing the first policies creates the missing asset. Every submission could connect a customer contract, governance practice, exclusion, carrier response, and eventual claim outcome. That would turn a brokerage commission business into a proprietary map of how frontier risk is actually priced. The company already says it continuously monitors policy forms, endorsements, regulatory filings, and carrier treatment. But today that is a database claim without disclosed scale, placements, customers, or repeat usage. Alton Risk already advertises overlapping policy-gap analysis and carrier matching, while Corgi controls more of the stack as a full-stack carrier. Risklytics currently depends on the institutions from which it hopes to derive its moat.

I also cannot run the immersion test. Risklytics is still obtaining producer licenses and is not placing coverage. YC acceptance, 123 LinkedIn followers, and a Center for Data Innovation feature are distribution artifacts, not costly customer behavior. Pre-seed companies need not have revenue, but I need some functioning act: a founder uploading sensitive contracts, a carrier changing terms because of the analysis, or a customer repeatedly returning before deployment. Without that, the product remains a plausible workflow wrapped around an unproven relationship business.

My base case is that by August 2029 Risklytics is either a small specialist brokerage below $2 million in annualized commission revenue or has pivoted again into policy-analysis software for incumbent brokers. I am neutral, but I would not write the check today. One condition flips me: show ten bound frontier-technology policies where Risklytics identified a material exclusion missed by the customer’s prior broker, with at least three customers returning for another placement or renewal. That would prove the artifact, the agency transfer, and the compounding data advantage in one stroke.
