# Aikido Technologies -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI power constraints create an opening for offshore infrastructure, but Aikido Technologies becomes a large company only if it delivers reliable compute capacity sooner and at a competitive all-in cost. That is the controlling variable. Wind generation alone does not establish it. The proposed 15MW+ turbine and four-plus hours of batteries must translate into contracted availability after accounting for backup power, marine maintenance, installation, and financing. The dossier establishes a plausible mechanism for bypassing constrained terrestrial sites, but no measured threshold or customer economics that make the mechanism investable.

The strongest economic claim is the combination of power generation, accessible data halls, and seawater heat rejection. Projected PUE below 1.08 gives the cooling proposition a measurable target. However, efficient cooling can coexist with expensive delivered compute. Batteries, subsea grid connections, diesel backup, and vessel-based servicing all sit elsewhere in the cost structure. Aikido’s March 2026 announcement identifies an actionable infrastructure problem; its proposed solution earns some technology-shift credit. The broader floating-datacenter forecast—$450 million in 2025 growing to $2.445 billion in 2032—does not establish which part of that spending Aikido can capture profitably.

The strongest argument against passing is that this team has already reduced a difficult engineering uncertainty. ABS witnessed a quarter-scale upending demonstration and issued a statement of maturity in February 2025. Kanner and Yu bring offshore R&D and hydrodynamics experience from Principle Power; Reeves adds turbine and helicopter engineering. The granted floating-wind patent supplies a concrete technical asset. This fits my pattern of entering physical markets through specialized expertise during a major infrastructure transition. But my physical-business exception requires evidence that technology changes the economics. The independently assessed milestone concerns the wind platform, while the datacenter retrofit and integrated operating performance remain unvalidated in this dossier. The research-stage exception also falls short: relevant expertise and prototype evidence are present, but the capital path to a commercial offshore datacenter is not established.

Customer interest could still turn the engineering platform into a valuable business. Commercial discussions around a UK site targeting 2028 operations are more relevant than NVIDIA Inception membership, but neither establishes repeat usage, payment, or a capacity commitment. The $4 million seed financing and October 2026 securities offering are financing evidence, not product-market pull. Likewise, owning physical infrastructure could create a recurring operating relationship, but the dossier does not document an owned customer workflow or operating feedback loop. I retain the missing-evidence defaults for demand and workflow durability. The founders’ domain experience lowers execution uncertainty; it does not establish the costly personal commitment or prior startup learning required by the fifth criterion.

I would pass with my own money today. The concern is specific: Aikido Technologies may solve floating-platform engineering without solving competitive compute delivery. Panthalassa’s $210 million raised through May 2026 shows that competing approaches have substantial development capital; it does not validate offshore compute economics. The dossier does not establish vanity fundraising, premature liquidity, or other founder misconduct. The unresolved issue is the physical-business red flag: whether integration produces attractive economics after all physical costs. My flip condition is an independently verified, paid integrated deployment demonstrating customer-required availability at a lower fully loaded cost per usable compute-hour than that customer’s documented terrestrial alternative.
