# GodHands -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Agents create a new buyer for operational knowledge: support and operations leaders who need their teams’ undocumented decisions converted into usable context. For GodHands, the controlling variable is whether captured exceptions let another person or agent complete work correctly without returning to the original expert. That would turn documentation into an operating dependency. The dossier establishes the problem—an employee departure reportedly disrupted the founders’ previous employer for months—but does not establish that transfer of capability. I would pass with my own money today.

The market exists, and it is already crystallizing. Scribe reports $100 million ARR and offers workflow context and agents; Mimica raised $26.2 million in September 2025 to pursue workflow-trained agents. Those facts support customer interest in the category, while making generic capture a difficult entry point. GodHands needs an advantage in capturing consequential decisions that these vendors miss. Its positioning describes that advantage, but the dossier contains no comparative evidence. Likewise, the technology shift could reduce training and handover costs, but there is no measured cost reduction or delivery improvement supporting an economic premium.

The strongest bull case is the anonymous support leader’s claim that GodHands documented previously undocumented escalations within two weeks. Exceptions are precisely where operational knowledge becomes valuable, and desktop observation could reach work spread across applications without waiting for integrations. Sourced playbooks also provide persistent customer context that better models could use more effectively. This fits my pattern of backing infrastructure around a technology transition. But the testimonial establishes documentation output, not successful handover, repeat dependence, or autonomous resolution. Earlier design-partner validation concerned engineering memory and cannot establish pull for the current operations product.

The distinction matters because GodHands could become either an operational knowledge layer or another documentation feature. Its playbooks give it more substance than an output formatter, earning some architectural credit. However, marketing suggests agents perform work while indexed security language describes reading playbooks and answering questions. I cannot credit control of an execution loop from that evidence. Nor would I assume accumulated playbooks create a data moat: the dossier does not establish their reproduction cost or migration difficulty. This is the workflow-durability concern in my red flags, rather than evidence that improving models have already erased the product.

The founders have relevant technical preparation: both worked at Graviton, and Aditya Bommakanti built low-latency systems and published NeurIPS research. Their experience of operational disruption makes the problem credible. It does not document costly commitment or prior startup learning, and those credentials do not establish the scarce frontier capability required for my research-stage or exceptional-talent overrides. My verifiable flip condition is a named support customer demonstrating that, over 90 days, incoming staff resolve at least 80% of a predefined set of previously undocumented escalation types using GodHands without consulting the original experts. That would directly test the variable on which this investment turns.
