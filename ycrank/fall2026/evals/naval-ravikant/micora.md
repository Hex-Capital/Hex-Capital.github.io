# Micora -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Choo has deployed solar and batteries at Tesla and designed drivetrains and manufacturing systems at Serve Robotics. Akemoto has shipped heat pumps at Normal Robotics. Those are useful collisions with physical reality. Micora combines their experience around a specific bottleneck: getting generation installed where electricity is scarce. That earns substantial founder credit. The remaining uncertainty is what these founders have personally made work in Micora’s deployment system; their prior experience cannot also serve as proof of its defensibility.

The investment turns on the claimed **100 kWp installed per day**. If autonomous deployment removes substantial field labor, Micora could turn a construction project into a manufactured product. That changes how quickly capacity can expand. But the public documentation describes forklift placement, and the October 21, 2026 LAKI collaboration concerns planned robotic deployment. These can be compatible stages of development; they do not establish autonomous field performance. Standard LFP batteries supply useful electricity, but the dossier identifies no demonstrated proprietary deployment asset that a competing integrator would struggle to reproduce.

Micora does fit my preference for attacking physical bottlenecks. Hardware, however, earns defensibility through what it makes difficult for competitors. BoxPower already sells prefabricated microgrids. A customer choosing Micora needs an advantage in delivered cost, installation time, or operating performance. The claimed Pacific island and manufacturing deployments are encouraging, but without capacity, labor requirements, or customer outcomes, they cannot establish that advantage. Nor is there evidence that one installation improves another customer’s product or brings the next customer into the system. Deployment learning could eventually compound; today that remains a hypothesis.

The market offers some room to maneuver. Remote operators are the initial buyers, and manufacturing sites provide an adjacent application for the same generation-and-storage package. Nevertheless, the **$43.47 billion** global microgrid estimate includes demand Micora has not shown it can serve. Purchase and 12- or 24-month leases identify plausible ways to capture value directly. With no retrievable monetary quote or disclosed paid transaction, they establish a commercial offer rather than demonstrated economics. Leasing also leaves an unanswered question about who finances the equipment while customers pay over time.

The strongest objection to passing is that this team may already possess precisely the integration knowledge that public research cannot expose. Choo’s deployment experience, Akemoto’s shipped hardware, and the claimed installations could support a working system well ahead of the published evidence. I can accept missing revenue when an exceptional prototype demonstrates the mechanism. Here, the prototype’s decisive capability is itself unresolved. The dossier supplies no evidence of integrity problems or artificial fundraising pressure; the installation descriptions warrant verification, not an accusation. But neither founder credibility nor the absence of red flags establishes a durable asset.

I would pass on Micora today. The founders have earned attention; the deployment system has not yet earned my money. My flip condition is **one independently witnessed, paid field deployment achieving 100 kWp per day with a documented fully loaded installed cost below a comparable BoxPower proposal**. That would connect the founders’ specific knowledge to a purchasing advantage and show why Micora could become more than another competent microgrid integrator.
