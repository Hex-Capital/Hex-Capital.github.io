# Orgo -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Orgo’s most revealing evidence is in its changelog. During July–August 2026, payment-webhook errors froze paid accounts, and MCP failures interrupted the product. Subsequent fixes are documented. Here is an actual obstacle followed by a repair. But the record doesn’t establish which founder intervened, how quickly, or whether affected customers resumed work. That distinction matters. Fixing bugs is ordinary; discovering that a founder can rescue a customer under pressure is investment evidence.

The product itself deserves substantial credit. Orgo has shipped persistent desktops with browsers, terminals, files, and programmatic control, accessible through SDKs, a CLI, and MCP. These are inspectable capabilities, beyond an architectural promise. The company reports thousands of desktops running in production. This fits the pattern I find attractive: technical users getting a programmable primitive for something they previously assembled themselves. The production count is self-reported, and there is no independently measured reliability threshold, so I would leave room above the technical score.

There is also a reasonably narrow well of demand. Developers and agencies running persistent business agents have a specific job to accomplish. Peter Steinberger is identified as the first paying customer, and Phil’s testimonial describes agents operating inside three small businesses. Those facts carry more weight than the reported 1,000 teams, because “using” can conceal a great deal. The $29, $99, and $399 monthly plans establish an inexpensive way to start paying. They do not establish recurring revenue or retention. Still, this is considerably better than developers saying they might want agent infrastructure someday.

The uncomfortable possibility is that Orgo has found demand for a useful feature whose economics accrue elsewhere. E2B already offers graphical desktops, and Daytona offers overlapping persistent environments across several operating systems. Open-source components make assembling an alternative possible. My preference for programmable infrastructure therefore cuts both ways: a primitive becomes valuable when developers keep building around it, but the dossier establishes functionality more clearly than embedded dependence. Frozen paid accounts are especially dangerous here. Persistence is the promise; customers must trust the computer to remain available.

The founders have relevant ingredients. Vasilescu operated computeruse.agency, Kinney worked as a software engineer, and Kinney and Vasilescu knew each other through a Berkeley hacker house. But prior agency work doesn’t establish the particular customer collision that produced Orgo, and shared housing doesn’t establish execution through conflict. There is also no documented rejected hypothesis followed by an evidence-driven product revision. I assign the missing-evidence defaults to origin and adaptability. Tutorials and podcasts could recruit early users effectively; nothing here establishes dependence on a ceremonial launch or an unnamed major partner. Nor does the dossier show founders waiting for permission.

I would hold off on writing a check. The strongest argument against that decision is already running: a working product, a named payer, and business agents doing actual work. The working-product exception deserves consideration. But Orgo’s evidence doesn’t yet show the unusual founder correction or unmistakable customer pull that would justify overriding my resourcefulness gate. One verifiable customer-rescue episode could change the decision: a named customer confirms that a serious deployment blocker arose, a founder shipped a workaround within 48 hours, and the customer continued paying and running agents for the following month. That would connect the capable product to the people I would actually be backing.
