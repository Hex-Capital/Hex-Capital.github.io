# RATLOG -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

RATLOG’s founders have acquired knowledge by building things that can fail in public. Kunhart designed Argonaut engine nozzles and led engine design at Innuendo; Weise worked on lunar-lander research; Kelm led propulsion-project electronics. These are relevant actions. The company-reported engine hot-fire, achieved in four months for under $30,000, adds a useful signal of experimental speed. I would take this team seriously before asking about its credentials. The central investment question is whether that speed produces an enduring propulsion advantage.

A physical engine survives someone copying the software. Its economic advantage still needs demonstration. RATLOG proposes removing the turbopump and reducing engine parts by 90%, while physically rotating the engine introduces increased bearing demands. Complexity can migrate. The hot-fire establishes an actual hardware artifact, but the dossier supplies no measured evidence that the architecture delivers superior performance, service life, or total operating cost. There is something here to develop and learn from; an accumulating proprietary advantage remains unproven.

The market offers room to maneuver. Satellite operators are the initial buyers; space stations and orbital data centers are named adjacent customers that could use the same proposed 20-tonne launch capability. The independently estimated $12.1 billion satellite-launch-services market establishes substantial existing expenditure, although it does not validate those emerging applications. SpaceX already operates reusable launches. RATLOG therefore needs its engine architecture to change the economics or availability of launch enough to overcome an incumbent’s operating experience. Reusability alone cannot carry that argument.

The $400 million in signed launch reservations deserves diligence. With payment, cancellation, and performance terms undisclosed, it provides limited evidence of direct value capture. Per-kilogram pricing identifies a sensible transaction, but neither the realized price nor contribution economics is established. Shared flights could improve utilization; the dossier shows no customer contribution or referral mechanism that improves the product for other customers. RATLOG fits my preference for investing in physical bottlenecks. It offers little evidence yet of the customer-created value or permissionless participation that makes my strongest network investments compound.

The strongest argument for investing now is the founders’ apparent ability to buy technical learning cheaply. My known exception for a demonstrated prototype and unusually capital-efficient builders applies enough to make passing uncomfortable. Four months and under $30,000 is consequential evidence. The unresolved question is how far that efficiency transfers: a flight-ready engine is targeted for 2027, orbital launch for 2029–2030, and launch-site arrangements remain unsettled. A cheap first experiment can precede an expensive development path. The dossier establishes no integrity violation, artificial financing deadline, or refusal to update; the CEO’s acknowledgment of bearing demands identifies a concrete problem to test.

I am neutral at 59/100 and would wait before committing my own money. RATLOG has stronger evidence of founder-specific knowledge than of a durable commercial system. My single flip condition is an independently witnessed, full-scale engine test completing a flight-representative duty cycle—including restart—with bearing and pressurization hardware included in the measured performance. That would show whether the proposed simplification survives the demands of flight and justify funding the next experiment.
