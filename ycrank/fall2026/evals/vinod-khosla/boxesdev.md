# Boxes.dev -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Boxes.dev removes a real constraint: parallel coding agents need isolated environments that keep running after the laptop closes. But the investment question is how much additional useful work those environments produce. The dossier supplies no baseline for engineering cost displaced, additional software delivered, or people newly able to build. I cannot attribute the coding agents’ entire productivity gain to the infrastructure hosting them. The consequence is unquantified, which earns my missing-evidence default rather than credit for transforming software production.

The technical evidence is less favorable than simple silence. Boxes.dev emphasizes persistent environments, snapshots, and native Claude Code/Codex support; it identifies E2B as its sandbox provider. Conductor already offers overlapping cloud execution, native agents, and mobile access. Buying infrastructure is sensible engineering, but these particular differentiators establish convenience without establishing a technical discontinuity. There is no measured advantage in successful tasks per dollar, environment reliability, or startup latency. My concern is that the agent and sandbox suppliers capture the capability gains while Boxes.dev competes over the interface between them. That overlaps my red flag for AI businesses differentiated through orchestration without demonstrated proprietary capability.

The strongest objection to passing is the team. Nick Bushak co-founded Gem and served as CTO; Drew Regitsky was its first engineer. They have built together, experienced this problem themselves, and shipped a working product across desktop and mobile workflows. My actual investing pattern includes execution businesses; scientific novelty is not compulsory. A small operational wedge can earn an exception when it opens a giant existing value pool and the founders can expand beyond the initial product. These founders make that possibility credible. However, the dossier documents neither a material belief revised after evidence nor difficult complementary recruiting. Their biographies support a serious conversation, but they do not satisfy those specific criteria. Product Hunt’s 113 points and the Android app’s 10+ downloads also do not establish the committed technical community or repeated usage that would strengthen the exception.

The cheapest decisive experiment should address substitution: why will developers continue paying Boxes.dev when another workspace offers the same agents? The dossier supplies no experiment, numerical threshold, budget, or stop condition, so risk sequencing receives the absent-evidence default. Economics remain similarly unresolved. Starter costs $19 for 50 hours and Pro costs $99 for 250, before separate model subscriptions. Those are selling prices, not evidence of delivery cost or savings against the developer’s alternative. Without infrastructure costs, utilization, and support requirements, I cannot infer attractive margins or globally competitive economics. The inconsistent public allowances make that calculation harder; they do not prove an uneconomic business.

I pass at 22/100. Missing revenue at pre-seed is not the reason. Boxes.dev has experienced builders and a usable answer to a real problem, but its disclosed differentiation sits in features an overlapping competitor already supplies. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently reproducible customer benchmark showing at least 5x more accepted coding tasks per total dollar than Conductor on equivalent workloads, holding the underlying agent constant and including setup and human review. That would establish that the execution layer creates substantial capability of its own. Today, that is the investment hypothesis, not the evidence.
