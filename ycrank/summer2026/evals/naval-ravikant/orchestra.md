# Orchestra -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have earned the right to attack this problem. Aamir Poonawalla spent ten years building Instacart’s ads-serving, auction, experimentation, and curbside infrastructure; Luis Manrique worked on AI products at Gumloop and Instacart. That is action-revealed knowledge adjacent to evaluation, routing, and high-volume inference—not résumé decoration. But the dossier does not yet show either founder previously training specialist LLMs or selling this exact optimization loop, so founder-market fit is strong rather than complete.

Orchestra’s best evidence is technical and economic. It processed 39,962 comments for $2.82 versus $12.48 with Sonnet and $139.63 with Opus, and its current benchmark reports an 83.4% cost reduction per task. The product also acknowledges the inconvenient result: loaded Fireworks cost was roughly $1.56 against $0.006617 in modeled token cost at low volume. Publishing where the economics break increases my confidence in the team. It does not prove that customers will pay.

The market supplies room to maneuver. Orchestra begins with companies already buying frontier inference, enters through a one-URL substitution, and can expand from routing into evaluation, training, deployment, and workload-specific optimization. Independent estimates put inference software at $21.1 billion in 2026, while Fireworks reports more than $1 billion in annualized revenue. This is real spend, not a TAM invented around a demo.

The central problem is ownership of leverage. Customer-specific evaluations, routing rules, and specialist weights may improve with use, but customers own those artifacts. Fireworks already offers continuous specialization, while Together, Baseten, and Portkey cover neighboring layers. If a competitor copies Orchestra’s workflow, the dossier identifies no scarce hardware, exclusive data, proprietary model, or cross-customer learning asset that remains with Orchestra. Eighteen GitHub stars and eight forks are distribution traces, not a network effect: one customer does not yet make the system more valuable for another.

The strongest bull case is that the combination matters more than its components. Exact-domain founders, a low-friction gateway, measurable cost reductions, retained frontier fallbacks, and a fast-growing inference market could produce an enterprise optimization control plane before larger providers integrate the full loop. The exception logic for an unusually capable team therefore deserves attention. But exception underwriting still needs revealed behavior from customers, and Orchestra currently discloses neither revenue nor repeat external production usage. Negotiated order forms establish a method of charging, not direct value capture.

I expect Orchestra to reach roughly $3–8 million ARR within 36 months as an enterprise inference-optimization platform, but not become a defensible independent neocloud unless deployments create an asset the customers and upstream providers cannot simply take with them. I see no founder-integrity or morale red flag in the dossier; the contradiction is structural, not personal. I would remain neutral today. The flip condition is verifiable: show at least ten paying production customers whose anonymized deployment data measurably improves optimization for later customers while Orchestra retains contractual rights to that cross-customer learning layer.
