# ORO AI -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most useful fact about ORO AI is that agents learned to win without shopping. The team responded by introducing public qualification followed by hidden-task races ([April 17 engineering post](https://oroagents.com/blog/how-we-stop-miners-from-abusing-our-incentive-mechanism)). That is an actual obstacle followed by a shipped workaround. It tells me more about the founders than their job titles do. But the dossier does not establish how quickly they made the change or how much exploitation it eliminated. I give them substantial credit for acting, with room left for evidence that the fix worked.

There is also something substantial underneath the competition. ORO has published working infrastructure and reports 4,891,831 scored agent-version/task pairs. Its research reports Qwen3-4B success rising from an 18.0% published baseline to 42.7% on a guarded held-out partition ([June research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10064)). This fits a pattern I like: technical founders assembling a useful substrate before asking people to believe in a platform. The experiment is company-authored, so I would discount the claimed improvement pending reproduction. Still, this is considerably beyond a diagram. The reported 0.71 practice score versus 0.18 hidden-task score also exposes the problem the infrastructure must solve. Generating millions of trajectories is useful only if they teach something that survives a changed environment.

The question is who wants the result badly enough to keep coming back. Over 345 agents and $85,000 distributed in 21 days show that ORO can recruit builders into a rewarded competition ([April 16 report](https://oroagents.com/blog/we-gave-away-85000-in-the-past-21-days)). Those builders supply the data. The prospective customers are teams that need better shopping agents. We have evidence about the suppliers and little about those customers. No disclosed revenue is ordinary at this stage. What matters is that paid participation cannot tell us whether anyone urgently needs the resulting corpus. Rewards governed outside ORO make that distinction especially consequential.

Bansal’s evaluation research and Bittensor experience, together with Schilbe’s AWS distributed-systems work, explain why these founders could build this machinery. Their shared Cloud Map background is relevant, although it does not establish a long history of surviving difficulties together. Nor does the dossier show either founder personally encountering an unmet shopping-agent need. I leave that origin question open. Similarly, I count the response to gaming as execution evidence; I cannot also use that single incident to infer a general ability to abandon a mistaken product direction.

The strongest argument for writing the check is that useful infrastructure can precede an obvious customer category. ORO already has contributors, running software, and a measurable training result. Requiring conventional sales evidence could miss the point. My exceptions for impressive prototypes and validated technical proof deserve consideration here. But the commercial leap remains large: Prime Intellect already supplies environments and reinforcement-learning infrastructure, while ORO publishes MIT-licensed code. Its advantage must come from continually producing unusually useful tasks and trajectories. The dossier establishes production volume more clearly than that continuing advantage. There is no disclosed launch-dependent or promised-partnership distribution plan; the unresolved issue is whether the reward mechanism creates something buyers independently value.

I would keep talking, but I would not write a check today. The founders have demonstrated technical initiative. They have not yet demonstrated a narrow well of customer need. My flip condition is one named model team renewing a paid contract after a controlled test shows ORO’s trajectories improve performance on that buyer’s own unseen tasks. That would connect the machinery to a customer who would miss it.
