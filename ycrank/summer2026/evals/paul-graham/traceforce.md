# Traceforce -- Paul Graham Evaluation

1,500+ employee devices across five enterprises make Traceforce a schlep-heavy security machine built by an unusually credible infrastructure pair, but not yet an organic founder-pain story. Traceforce is strongest where prompts, tool calls, MDM deployment, privacy rules, and false positives create work other hackers avoid; Traceforce is weakest where I ask whether Xia Hua or Varun Wadhwa built the first version because either personally needed it. That pairing makes Traceforce look like a very good answer to a newly visible enterprise problem, not yet a strange artifact its founders could not stop themselves from making.

**Coinbase** is the high rung on schlep: compliance was not incidental to the product but the unpleasant substance from which the product became defensible. Traceforce has the same structure. An endpoint agent that records prompts, maps 1,000+ MCP connectors, blocks tool actions, survives privacy review, and deploys through Jamf or JumpCloud is a thicket of chores disguised as software. Traceforce diverges from Coinbase because the dossier does not show Hua or Wadhwa personally enduring the ugliest installations, security reviews, and false-positive arguments; SOC 2 and ISO 27001 are useful, but certifications are not scars.

**Kiko** is the low rung on organicity: a competent product whose failure became more valuable as evidence about its founders than as evidence that they had found an inescapable personal problem. Traceforce has excellent adjacency—Hua ran Clumio engineering, Wadhwa led Clumio distributed infrastructure, and Hua holds two data-protection patents—but adjacency is not the same as Drew Houston repeatedly losing his USB stick. Traceforce never says that Hua or Wadhwa watched an AI agent leak data inside their own workflow and built Traceforce because they themselves needed the answer. None of my known exceptions repairs that gap: Traceforce shows neither an **Airbnb** cereal-box stunt nor a **Loopt**-level force-of-personality override.

One 500+ employee deployment gives Traceforce better animal tracks than most pre-seed security startups. Over the next 18 months, Traceforce can compound resourcefulness by turning every awkward installation and Fortune 500 proof of concept into another device integration, policy template, and MCP detection; the five current enterprises then become a learning engine competitors cannot reproduce from a slide deck. But Traceforce reports no revenue, no named customer, and no story of Hua or Wadhwa winning a customer through some improbable side door. The 1,500 devices prove Traceforce can ship and persuade. They do not yet prove the founders will refuse to take no for an answer.

Xia Hua’s two issued patents and Varun Wadhwa’s work on LinkedIn RAG databases and Microsoft payment systems put Traceforce above credential-only engineering. The score would flip into the highest hacker tier if Traceforce exposed a non-trivial technical artifact—a widely used repository, an independently reproduced detection benchmark, or the endpoint trick that makes 10,000 blocked actions per second possible. Without that, Traceforce has serious builders but no public hacker genealogy. Hackers usually leave debris.

Both Traceforce founders worked together at Clumio before its 2024 acquisition, which makes the pair closer to **Stripe** than to founders matched shortly before an application. Traceforce has complementary depth in data protection, distributed systems, and product engineering, while Hua’s director role and Wadhwa’s staff-level infrastructure work suggest they have already seen one another operate under load. The Collison brothers had years of shared hacking and Auctomatic behind them; Traceforce does not document how long Hua and Wadhwa worked together or whether they have the same instinctive trust. Still, Traceforce passes the two-founder test cleanly.

The 1,000+ curated MCP connectors point toward a useful outcome smaller than the black-swan outcome: by July 2029, I expect CrowdStrike to acquire Traceforce for $200–400 million after Traceforce reaches $8–15 million in annualized subscription revenue. Traceforce can get there because endpoint deployment and policy maintenance are genuine schleps, while Traceforce’s weaker organic origin makes an eventual platform sale more probable than an independent category-defining company. Useful security components tend to be absorbed once customers demand them inside the endpoint suite they already run.

SentinelOne’s acquisition of Prompt Security and Akamai’s agreement to acquire LayerX make the strongest bear case sting: an incumbent could bundle overlapping controls before Traceforce publishes a detection rate, while unmanaged devices and sensitive prompt telemetry weaken the supposed endpoint advantage. Traceforce nevertheless has evidence those products do not erase—1,500 deployed devices, four named MDM integrations, and Fortune 500 proofs of concept—which is enough for me to bet that the schlep is real rather than invented. I would flip to Pass if Traceforce fails by July 2027 to convert those proofs of concept into at least three paid deployments of 500 or more seats; that would show the current device count was experimentation, not user pull.
