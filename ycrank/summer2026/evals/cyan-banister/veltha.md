# Veltha -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Claims are not passed among adjusters as folders of scans, handwritten notes, state rules, and missing-record requests. Software assembles the file, applies the correct jurisdictional provisions, and shows a human exactly why the claim should be accepted or denied. The path is believable: adjuster retirements create capacity pressure, 24 states have adopted the NAIC AI Model Bulletin, and auditable automation becomes safer to buy than an uncited model. Veltha’s leak from that future is a $2B-plus specialty carrier already running live claims. What is missing is proof that this is becoming a system of record rather than a very good productivity feature.

The founders have paid unusually relevant operating tuition. Roland converted mortgage guidebooks into executable rules at a $642 billion servicer; Edin deployed AI inside Germany’s federal criminal police. They worked together at Palantir for roughly three years, and their division into CEO and CTO at least makes accountability legible. This is substantially stronger than two smart people discovering insurance during YC. But the biography begins at employers: I do not see dated pre-company experiments, an independent claims artifact, or evidence that either founder was pulling this thread before it became a company. That keeps the Rizz–Tizz–Biz signal below the top band.

The product proof is real but narrow. More than 500 FCIC, LAM, and PPSH checks have been encoded, and live review reportedly fell from one hour to five minutes—elsewhere described as six hours to ten minutes. Those are not waitlist promises. Someone has trusted Veltha with consequential claim files, and the output points to paragraph-, file-, and provision-level evidence for human approval. Yet the dossier gives no claim count, repeat-use period, revenue, reviewer override rate, or material-error rate. A working artifact earns credit; an unnamed deployment described entirely by the vendor does not yet establish costly or weird customer behavior.

The broken system is also more specific than “insurance is slow.” Adjusters manually reconcile poor records against federal and state rules while missing-document queues accumulate, and a shrinking workforce supplies the rent in hours and backlog. Veltha returns time to adjusters and processing capacity to carriers. The limitation is agency: this does not obviously transfer control to an individual whom an institution has artificially constrained. It may simply let the institution adjudicate faster. That can still be a good business, but the most important question is whether claimants receive more consistent decisions or merely encounter a cheaper gatekeeper.

Here is the bear case that hurts: regulatory citation is an implementation project masquerading as a moat. CLARA, Five Sigma, Sprout.ai, and EvolutionIQ already sit inside claims workflows, and EvolutionIQ’s $674.3 million acquisition shows incumbents what the capability is worth. Veltha discloses no contractual rights to outcomes, no learning loop, and no structural barrier preventing those platforms from adding provision-level citations. Worse, the company processes medical, employment, and handwritten claim evidence without a disclosed consent, custody, retention, or isolation architecture. Human approval reduces decision risk; it does not solve severe privacy risk. The 500 encoded checks are a useful controlled asset beneath the AI wave, but today they look more like hard implementation work than scarce infrastructure.

My base case is that Veltha reaches $3 million to $7 million in ARR within 36 months by becoming a focused regulatory-review layer for specialty and workers’ compensation claims, but remains independent because accuracy, privacy, and data-rights uncertainty prevent it from owning the broader claims stack. I am neutral today and would not yet write the check from my own money. One verifiable event flips me: the existing carrier pays to renew and expand after at least 1,000 live claims while disclosing its material-error and human-override rates. That would show both repeat institutional trust and whether the citations survive contact with reality.
