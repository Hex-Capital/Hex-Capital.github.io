# HERA -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

HERA’s proposed secret is that engineering drawings can be checked against customer-specific rules with enough accuracy and traceability to prevent expensive fabrication errors while leaving final authority with an engineer. What would prove it wrong? A blind production test in which experienced reviewers find consequential violations that HERA misses—or HERA generates enough false alarms that engineers abandon it. The dossier provides no such benchmark. Worse, CoLab, Axial, Redprint, and Glypad already advance substantially the same automation claim. The problem is real; the contrarian truth is not yet visible.

The product has reached three production plants, and the founders interviewed more than 200 manufacturing engineers, quality engineers, and machinists before launch. Those are informative pre-seed distribution signals. They show that Meera Patel and Noelle So can enter factories and get practitioners to expose sensitive workflows. They do not show that HERA’s reviews prevented a documented error, displaced manual review, or converted into paid deployment. The free NDA-covered review of 20 drawings is a plausible enterprise wedge, but pricing, sales-cycle length, implementation cost, and paid conversion remain unknown.

The beachhead is also too indefinite. Aerospace, medical devices, pressure vessels, semiconductor tooling, and custom machinery are five markets with different standards, procurement structures, and failure modes. Customer-specific rules and an accumulating error record could create switching costs, but no dataset scale, retention, exclusive input, or accuracy advantage is documented. A system configured separately for every customer may become proprietary infrastructure—or merely a services burden disguised as a moat. Four overlapping products, including Redprint at $1.98–$4 per review, make competition an observed fact rather than a hypothetical risk.

The strongest case for HERA is the founding structure. Patel and So share industrial-engineering training, factory exposure, and a long personal relationship from UW; they did not meet at a networking event assembled solely to start a company. Patel worked on manufacturing projects at Pfizer and TE Connectivity, while So built both workflow automation and physical water-treatment capacity. Yet the dossier does not establish that they previously shipped together, that every founder is full-time, that core engineering is entirely in-house, or that they retain meaningful ownership. These are missing facts, not adverse facts, but a company can recover from an imperfect model more easily than from an indefinite foundation.

The $40,000 scrapped-part example suggests economic value sufficient for enterprise pricing, but HERA has disclosed no revenue, paid price, gross margin, or expansion behavior. The power-law case therefore depends on moving from a drawing checker into the system of record governing engineering release and manufacturing compliance. That expansion is conceivable, but today the evidence supports one useful tool inside a crowded category—not a last mover capable of retaining the value it creates.

The relevant exception would require a verified zero-to-one technical event, as competitive uncertainty around OpenAI was overridden only after an extraordinary product result. HERA has no equivalent result yet: the cited 94.77% precision and 5.23% hallucination rate belong to a separate research system, not its product. My 36-month base case is that by August 2029 HERA has pivoted from horizontal drawing review into on-prem aerospace supplier-quality compliance and remains below $1 million ARR. I would reverse the pass if one aerospace segment produced five paid plants using the same core system, with an independently audited consequential-error miss rate below 1% and documented annual expansion. Until then: important problem, competent founders, but slogan rather than secret and commodity rather than monopoly.
