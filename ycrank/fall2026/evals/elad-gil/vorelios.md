# Vorelios -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Vorelios is attacking a real economic constraint: aerospace engineers explore fewer designs because conventional fluid simulations take hours, and learned physics models could make that exploration interactive. The buyer is an aerospace engineering team. The controlling variable is how much engineering time Vorelios saves after accounting for verification and corrective simulation. Faster predictions create a large business only if engineers can reliably use them to eliminate work. The dossier establishes the computational opening but does not establish that substitution rate or the accuracy threshold customers require.

The strongest evidence is the reported 1.1–31 millisecond inference time on an H100. That is a substantial technical discontinuity relative to the hours-long simulations described on the website. Vorelios also reports 6.37% pressure error and 3.81% skin-friction error on the NASA Common Research Model. These are company measurements against solver outputs, which limits their evidentiary weight. They demonstrate a working approach; they do not quantify total design-cycle savings, training costs, or the consequences of errors on unfamiliar geometries. I give meaningful credit to the technology shift without assuming equivalent improvements in customer economics.

The strongest argument for investing now is that waiting for commercial proof could miss the point when a technically superior model becomes an engineering platform. Vorelios already predicts surface quantities and derives lift, drag, and moments; this is substantive physics work. But the market is already crystallizing. Luminary has aircraft-wing models and a simulation-data platform, while Neural Concept offers CAD-native workflows. Their existence does not establish that Vorelios loses. It does mean benchmark superiority needs a mechanism for capturing the customer relationship. No documented integrations, persistent design state, or recurring optimization loop yet establish that stronger models would deepen an owned workflow. Internally generated simulations are also reproducible in principle; the dossier provides no estimate of the cost or time required to match the corpus.

My pattern of investing across technology discontinuities supports taking this seriously, but it does not establish which application captures value. Nor does the research-stage exception justify overriding absent product pull here: the dossier contains working benchmarks, but no comparable evidence of field-defining research, scarce technical leadership, or a credible capital path. Daniel McNeil’s physics background and Krish Kapadia’s 2025–2026 autonomy research are relevant preparation. They do not document prior startup learning or costly personal commitment. Likewise, YC participation and follower counts do not demonstrate demand. Those dimensions receive their missing-evidence defaults. There is no evidence of vanity fundraising or a frozen-model dependency; those red flags should not be invented.

I would pass with my own money today. The technical acceleration is promising, but Vorelios has not yet shown what fraction of an aerospace buyer’s workflow it can economically replace. My single flip condition is a paid aerospace deployment with a customer-confirmed study showing at least a tenfold reduction in end-to-end design-cycle time on previously unseen geometries, including verification work and meeting tolerances specified before testing. That would connect the model’s speed to the market variable that matters and establish useful customer pull in the same deployment.
