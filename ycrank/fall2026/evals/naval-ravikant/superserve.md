# Superserve -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Bhalla has built something people use. Superserve reports over 100,000 machines running weekly and names Lindy and YC as users. His earlier agent-engineering work at Dosu and the documented November 2025 launch of its predecessor, RayAI, make this more than a résumé argument. There is specific knowledge acquired through shipping here. The unanswered question is whether that knowledge becomes an asset the company owns.

Copy Superserve’s implementation tomorrow. What remains? Memory snapshots, persistent forks, and credentials kept outside the guest are useful engineering, but the dossier establishes no exclusive model, data, hardware, or accumulated customer asset. E2B already preserves memory through pause/resume; Modal already offers VM sandboxes. Superserve’s unrestricted session duration addresses a real constraint, but a competitor’s runtime policy is a fragile foundation for ownership. I give the founders credit for building the machinery. I cannot yet give the business credit for something competitors cannot reproduce.

The strongest argument for writing a small check is that production infrastructure can become indispensable before its defensibility becomes publicly legible. A three-person team supporting that reported machine volume, with Claude Managed Agents and LangChain integrations, deserves attention. Long-running workloads could accumulate state that customers become reluctant to move. But machine starts reveal neither workload duration nor customer dependence, and migration friction remains explicitly inferred. The prototype and compact team make the capital-efficient-builder exception plausible; the dossier does not establish the exceptional efficiency needed to override the missing durable asset. Nor is there an observed communication or contribution network that justifies overlooking monetization.

The market gives Superserve room to maneuver. Coding-agent builders are an identifiable initial buyer; training-environment builders can use the same isolation, checkpointing, and parallel execution capabilities. Modal’s reported $300 million sandbox run rate supplies concrete evidence that customers spend on this capability, although it is competitor revenue, not Superserve traction. This fits my pattern of backing infrastructure that lets individuals produce more without institutional permission. The missing companion is customer-created value: nothing shows that adding one Superserve user improves another user’s environment or brings the next customer.

The pricing is refreshingly direct: $0.0504 per vCPU-hour, $0.0162 per GiB-hour, and Pro from $1,000 monthly with annual billing. That identifies how value could be captured. It does not establish completed purchases or a profitable spread over compute costs. I see no evidence of dishonest dealings, manufactured financing urgency, or founders requiring reassurance. The RayAI transition alone does not demonstrate instability, and documented recovery states are not evidence of operational failure. My hesitation is structural, rather than a judgment against the founders.

I would keep Superserve on my watchlist and hold my money today. The founder evidence earns attention; the ownership mechanism does not yet earn conviction. One verifiable condition would change my decision: a referenceable production cohort demonstrating that accumulated execution state and credential policies create measured, material migration costs. That would turn persistent machines into a persistent business advantage.
