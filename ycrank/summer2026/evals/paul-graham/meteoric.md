# Meteoric -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The impressive fact is not the hurricane claim. It is that two recent Cambridge graduates built a device that dissipated an artificial cloud by 13%. Mete Karslioglu had already worked on cloud-reflectivity nozzles, while Eric Nilsson had worked on digital microfluidics and droplet removal from LiDAR lenses. This is not an “AI for climate” idea assembled from a market map. The founders encountered the underlying physics through their own work and produced a measured artifact before obtaining customers, permits, or a large fleet.

But the dossier never names the obstacle that threatened to stop them. I know what obstacles await Meteoric: natural clouds, wind, battery life, fleets of tens or hundreds of drones, and U.S. rules restricting altitude, beyond-visual-line-of-sight operation, and one-pilot-to-many-drone control. I do not know which one has already broken their plan, what workaround they shipped, or how many days it took. Building the chamber prototype proves technical initiative. It does not yet prove the kind of resourcefulness that appears only after reality refuses to cooperate.

The 13% result is promising but still self-reported and underspecified. There are no chamber dimensions, repeated trials, independent measurements, natural-cloud tests, or measured increases in solar output. The model’s projected 10–30% annual generation uplift therefore rests several causal steps beyond the experiment. Even the economics are missing their controlling variable: drone operating cost may be only $30–60 per hour, but Meteoric has not disclosed the drone-hours required to recover one MWh. A fleet can be cheap per aircraft and ruinous per useful outcome.

The weakest part is pull. Utility-scale solar operators are a plausible narrow niche, but none is named as a pilot customer, repeat user, payer, or LOI counterparty. Tom’s Hardware and TechSpot coverage are not demand, and three Hacker News points are almost a laboratory control for the proposition that attention is not pull. Directly soliciting operators is better than depending on an unspecified partner, but the founders have not yet shown that anyone will tolerate even a crude field version.

The bull case is that this resembles the rare young hard-tech team whose numerical experiment matters more than its age. The important difference from **Bindwell** is that Meteoric’s result has not been independently validated and has not crossed the threshold customers actually buy: recovered electricity in a natural environment. The hurricane objective makes this gap worse. It expands a potentially testable solar-farm wedge into a claim the World Meteorological Organization says lacks generally accepted evidence, before the smaller product has survived a field test. That is premature breadth, not yet black-swan evidence.

I would pass today. Meteoric clears the technical-proof half of my gate, but not the founder-response or user-pull half, and none of the known exceptions replaces both. The verifiable condition that would flip me is a named solar operator’s permitted field pilot in which the founders overcome the airspace constraint and demonstrate at least 5% net recovered MWh after drone energy and flight costs. That single result would show a real blocker defeated, a technical claim surviving nature, and a user willing to bring the crude product back.
