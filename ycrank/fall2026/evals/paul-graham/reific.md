# Reific -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Reific has built something, but the dossier leaves out the founder story I most want to hear: what went wrong, and what did Antanas Žilinskas do next? His first-authored EVEREST research establishes relevant technical experience. It does not establish how he behaves when blocked. There is no documented obstacle followed by a dated workaround, and no rejected hypothesis followed by a changed product. Those are unanswered questions, not evidence of passivity. But I cannot invest on the assumption that the answers are good.

The strongest evidence is the product itself. Releasing records on a clock tests something that presenting a completed history cannot: whether an agent acts while the answer is still becoming knowable. Reific reports running 64 simultaneous sandboxes without failures on November 2. That is concrete enough to deserve technical credit. The reported leading rewards—31.6%, 43.1%, and 29.7% across three task families—also suggest it has constructed difficult tests. But difficulty is only the beginning of the proof. A training customer needs environments that make models better. Reific has not published the magnitude of its claimed improvement, the compute required, or held-out transfer results. These remain company-reported measurements rather than reproducible validation.

The bull case is that this particular researcher has found an overlooked piece of infrastructure and has already started doing its unpleasant work. Purchasing longitudinal records and checking answer keys against actual outcomes could matter more than inventing another simulation generator. That fits my preference for founders who assemble something difficult before calling it a platform. It also makes Reific more substantial than an idea attached to an AI trend. Still, the prior forecasting research does not document a personal collision with this customer problem. And the licensing process establishes work performed, not exclusive access to a scarce substrate.

Who needs the crude version badly enough to keep using it? The dossier identifies frontier labs as prospective buyers, but supplies no repeat usage, customer payment, or named commitment. Sample requests and researcher calls are ways to find that out. They are not the answer. This matters because Prime Intellect and Bespoke already supply overlapping infrastructure: Reific needs a narrow group for whom its temporal records change training results enough to justify buying separately. Nonexclusive data rights make customer learning and grading quality particularly important.

I would pass today. I see no documented dependence on a major partner, premature delegation, or refusal to learn; the decision rests on missing affirmative evidence in my central founder and demand tests. I have considered the exceptions for a compelling working prototype, independently validated technical proof, and unmistakable user pull. The current measurements do not establish an override strong enough to replace those missing signals. One verifiable event would change my decision: a named lab renews a paid deployment after a dated founder-built correction to a documented temporal-training failure, with the lab confirming improved held-out performance. That would connect resourcefulness, technical usefulness, and demand in one observable sequence.
