# ClaimKit -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine an engineer shipping code and the evidence for an R&D claim assembling alongside it. No annual archaeological expedition through tickets, payroll, and somebody’s memory. ClaimKit’s engineering and accounting connections point in that direction. But connecting records, establishing defensible eligibility, and making the resulting evidence reusable are separate steps. The dossier establishes a claim-preparation workflow; it does not yet establish that those connections are becoming a durable record of innovation. That is the distance between a useful tax firm and the future I would want to own.

Why does a founder need a consultant to reconstruct work the company already recorded? Rachel Huang encountered that problem at KPMG, which gives ClaimKit a credible operating specification. Manual evidence assembly consumes founders’ time and supports consultant fees; automating it could return both money and attention to the company doing the research. Yet ClaimKit charges an upfront software fee plus the greater of a minimum service fee or a percentage of the benefit. That can be a sensible business. Without quantified savings or specialist effort per claim, I cannot tell how much of the old economics it removes and how much it inherits.

The most persuasive artifact is Edexia’s COO describing a switch from accountants midway through an engagement, followed by faster completion at lower cost. Switching advisers during a tax project requires effort and trust. The company-reported 80-plus customers within six months adds breadth, although it does not establish repeat usage or collected revenue. I give that behavior substantially more weight than the advertised 100% claim success rate: the latter lacks a denominator, and ClaimKit itself explains that receiving a refund does not eliminate subsequent review.

The strongest case against withholding my money is that unglamorous workflow repair is precisely where an enabling business can begin. Huang has worked inside the problem; William Mahon brings substantial engineering experience; the team includes experienced tax practitioners. Retiring the Grants module on April 22, 2026, also shows an actual narrowing of effort. But the founder record establishes relevant experience more clearly than longstanding independent obsession or repeated pre-company invention. My willingness to invest before revenue can accommodate missing sales history; it does not supply that missing founder evidence. Nor does the concealed-infrastructure pattern resolve the gap: retained claim records might become an asset, but no scarce dependency is demonstrated, while Neo.Tax already has distribution through Thomson Reuters’ ONESOURCE.

I land at **Neutral** and would keep my personal checkbook closed on this dossier. ClaimKit has credible customer value, but its strongest evidence concerns completing today’s tax engagement. Its weakest concerns owning something tomorrow’s businesses must depend on. The reported encryption and exclusion from model training are useful safeguards; a pending SOC 2 audit leaves custody diligence unfinished without establishing a severe privacy violation. There is also no documented founder-control conflict to invoke a governance exception around. My flip condition is one independently reviewed repeat-filing cohort demonstrating that ClaimKit’s accumulated evidence mappings reduce specialist hours by at least 80% without increasing substantiation errors. That would make the proposed transition from annual service to reusable infrastructure observable.
