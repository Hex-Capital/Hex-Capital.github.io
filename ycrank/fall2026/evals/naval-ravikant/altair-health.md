# Altair Health -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Aouss visiting Texas clinics and the founders spending a year getting one practice fully deployed tell me more than their credentials. Altair Health reports handling several hundred thousand dollars in monthly insurance collections for a substance-use-treatment practice. That is meaningful contact with the actual problem: someone has entrusted the team with getting paid. It is also founder-reported, the customer is unnamed, and collections are the customer’s money. I give the founders credit for acquiring specific knowledge through action, while leaving the depth of that knowledge partly unproven.

The decisive question is what that operating experience leaves behind. Altair’s theorem prover checks proposed actions against formalized insurer policies. This is a concrete technical approach, but a correct proof can still operate on an incomplete translation of the policy. More importantly for my investment, a competitor copying the implementation might inherit essentially the same capability. The dossier establishes no exclusive policy corpus, proprietary model, or demonstrated accumulation of corrections that becomes harder to reproduce with each deployment. I assign the missing-moat default; the verification risk itself does not establish that the technology fails.

There is room to maneuver. The initial buyer is an independent practice; authorization, claims, and appeals share the problem of translating clinical documentation into payer requirements. That capability could travel into other specialties and larger provider groups. The estimated $7.73 billion U.S. billing-outsourcing market supplies independent evidence of existing spend, although it exceeds Altair’s addressable segment. Adonis, Candid Health, and Anomaly also show how many directions this workflow can support—and how many competitors already understand its value. Market breadth earns credit. It does not establish ownership of the market.

The strongest bull case is that running billing is how Altair acquires the knowledge that software vendors never see. Human escalations could become reusable policy corrections, progressively reducing labor across practices. The year-long deployment could represent an expensive apprenticeship whose benefits arrive later. I make room for exceptional founders and working products before a distribution loop exists. Here, however, the dossier does not establish exceptional capital efficiency or repeatable deployment, and the proposed learning loop remains inferred. Altair touches my preference for organizing fragmented offline activity, but there is no evidence that adding one practice improves another practice’s results or acquisition.

I would pass today, with a **Neutral** assessment of the evidence. Altair has an operating foothold and a plausible expansion path; its compounding mechanism and economics remain unestablished. The free EHR and separate $395/year authorization listing do not reveal managed-billing pricing, realized revenue, or contribution margins. There is no documented integrity or financing-pressure red flag, and I would not interpret the product transition alone as indecision. My concern is whether deployment knowledge becomes a reusable asset or continues to require another billing team. **My flip condition:** a verified rollout across three unrelated practices showing that an exclusively controlled, reusable correction dataset cuts human billing minutes per dollar collected by at least half without increasing denial rates.
