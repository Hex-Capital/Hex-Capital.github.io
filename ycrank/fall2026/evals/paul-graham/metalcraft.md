# Metalcraft -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Metalcraft has built something substantial. What I cannot find is the story of what happened when building it became difficult. Marshall Kiely’s computational-biology PhD and first-author Nature and Science papers establish relevant ability. They do not establish how he responds when an experiment fails or a customer refuses to buy. There is no documented blocker, workaround, and elapsed time. For an investment that begins with the founder, that leaves the most important question unanswered.

The product supplies the strongest affirmative evidence. Metalcraft publishes runnable biological tasks and graders, including 1,724 metabolic-model cases, with 424 tested per model. Selecting experiments under a budget and repairing metabolic models are concrete jobs. Someone had to understand the biology and make the machinery work. This fits my unstated preference for technical founders who assemble a difficult substrate before announcing a platform. But the numerical evidence establishes an evaluation inventory, not that its scores predict useful scientific work. Some dimensions depend on judge estimates, and the 36 Auto-research tasks have no public results. I give substantial credit for the working artifact while keeping those claims separate.

Who wants this badly enough to keep using it? The dossier identifies no such user. Public samples, leaderboards, and a taskset-request calendar make it possible to discover Metalcraft; they do not show that a lab returns after its first experiment. Missing revenue is ordinary at this stage. Missing evidence of repeated use leaves demand untested. That distinction matters because LABBench2 offers a public dataset and harness, while Tacit and LatchBio pursue overlapping evaluations. A buyer needs a particular failure Metalcraft can expose that the available alternatives cannot. Otherwise, even excellent biological work may become occasional commissioned projects rather than a growing product business.

The strongest case against passing is that Kiely may be unusually well equipped to discover precisely that failure. His biology and ML experience connects directly to the shipped tasks. This is much stronger than credentials attached to a proposed product. The known exceptions based on a working prototype or validated scientific proof therefore deserve consideration. Yet the dossier does not establish independent validation strong enough to replace the missing founder evidence, or unmistakable user pull. Nor does it establish the familiar red flags of waiting for a partner, delegating the hard work, or planning growth around a launch. My objection is narrower: the evidence needed to override the gate has not appeared.

I also cannot turn the October 2 UFO.ai association and November biological-evaluation site into evidence of a good pivot. We do not know what observation caused the change, or even its exact sequence. That earns the missing-evidence default, not a penalty for changing direction. Similarly, Kiely’s prior research makes this an intelligible problem choice without documenting a personal collision with the need or a neglected operational burden he chose to endure. I would pass today. One verifiable flip condition would be a dated customer case showing a lab’s failed evaluation workflow, the founder-built repair shipped within weeks, and that lab repeatedly using the repaired product. That would connect the technical ability already visible at Metalcraft to resourcefulness and real need.
