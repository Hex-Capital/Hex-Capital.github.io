# HillStar -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

The interesting object here is the award rationale. In 2032, I can imagine a utility remembering why it rejected a supplier, which exception mattered, and whether the equipment subsequently performed—then using that memory before writing its next solicitation. HillStar advertises the ingredients: evaluator judgments, exceptions, supplier outcomes. But storing those records is only the beginning. The dossier does not show the transitions from document evaluation to institutional learning, or an artifact demonstrating that past decisions improve the next purchase. That future remains my hypothesis.

Behroyan and Stone make me take the hypothesis seriously. Building Paxon in utility services before its reported 2025 exit is meaningful operating tuition. Co-founding PlanGrid, acquired for $875 million in 2018, and working in geothermal software gives Stone experience getting software into physical industries. These are shipped businesses, not merely impressive employers. Their histories establish substantial first-believer proof, although the dossier does not establish the particular procurement wound that brought them together or a dated trail of experiments behind HillStar.

Why does buying equipment require so much reconciliation? Technical requirements, inconsistent submissions, and the obligation to defend an award all collide on the procurement team's desk. HillStar gives buyers source-linked comparisons while leaving the committee accountable. That returns some control over their time. But mandatory certification is a legitimate constraint: finding two uncertified bidders among fourteen does not establish that HillStar removes an artificial gatekeeper or redirects an incumbent's rent. I see a concrete administrative burden, with less evidence of the economic liberation that would make this especially compelling to me.

The strongest evidence is that people appear to have brought consequential work into the product. An eleven-person expert team evaluating twenty-two submissions comprising approximately 19,800 pages is a much better signal than a waitlist. Still, the nine-minute result comes from an unnamed, company-published case study, with a comparison baseline drawn from comparable procurements. It establishes a promising claimed interaction, not independently verified repeat behavior. I would not treat nine minutes of processing as nine minutes to a defensible award.

The case against my hesitation stings: these founders may already understand the tedious details that outsiders miss, and institutional memory could become indispensable through ordinary workflow repair. That fits my pattern of finding consequential businesses inside mundane operating problems. My willingness to fund pre-revenue technical teams also applies; missing revenue is not the objection. The unresolved distinction is whether HillStar accumulates something essential or remains software plus procurement professionals that Fairmarkit or Keelvar can approach through existing workflows. Retained histories suggest a possible asset, but exclusive rights, demonstrated switching costs, and control over a scarce enabling constraint are unestablished.

I am **Neutral** with my own money. The CEO and CTO roles are identified; there is no evidence of a governance shell game or dependence on one closed model supplier. Confidential bids do activate my privacy gate: workspace isolation, encryption, U.S. hosting, and advertised SOC 2 Type I compliance are relevant controls, while consent, retention, and model-training permissions remain unestablished. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently auditable deployment at a named utility showing that retained history materially improves decisions across three completed sourcing cycles. That would make the institutional-memory claim tangible.
