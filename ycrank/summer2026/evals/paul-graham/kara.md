# Kara -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The strongest fact about Kara is not that diamond conducts heat at 2,400 W/m·K. It is that these founders encountered diamond from three useful directions before starting the company: Aarin Jhaveri sold more than $5 million of it across a fifth-generation family trade, Anuveer Chadha worked on diamond quantum-sensing systems at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, and the team now claims to grow, process, and characterize single-crystal diamond in its own reactors. This is an organic idea hidden inside a formidable manufacturing schlep, not “advanced materials” chosen from a list of fashionable markets.

But the dossier omits the event I need most. What broke in Kara’s reactor or customer qualification process? What did the founders change, how quickly did they change it, and what improved? In-house production is evidence of technical initiative, but without wafer dimensions, defect density, yield, throughput, or an independently measured thermal result, it remains a partly inspectable claim rather than a measured proof. More than a dozen companies are pursuing single-crystal diamond wafers, and Diamond Foundry has raised $315 million. Kara cannot win merely because diamond has better theoretical properties. It must show that this particular team can make unusually good diamond repeatedly.

The same gap appears on the demand side. A specification-led catalog of substrates, membranes, heat spreaders, submounts, and custom fabrication suggests a real product, but no named customer, paid sample, qualification result, repeat order, or production shipment is public. The company’s 427 LinkedIn followers prove almost nothing about who needs the crude product now. NVIDIA’s 120-kW rack designs and Akash’s March 2026 diamond-cooled MI350X servers make the thermal problem timely, but they do not establish pull for Kara’s material. Market heat is not customer heat.

The bull case is sharper than the current evidence. Kara has the direct problem exposure, young technical team, and ugly production burden that can produce a surprising company. The closest exception would be Bindwell: youth and sector difficulty were overridden by numerical computational and laboratory results. The material difference is that Kara has disclosed no corresponding result. Its own strongest unstated pattern—the concrete proof spike before a financing round—is therefore missing. Nor is there evidence yet that the team changed routes after a failed hypothesis. I treat that as absent evidence, not rigidity.

There are no signs of launch theater, delegated founder sales, or dependence on a vague major partnership. The harder red flag is economic. The cited substrate segment is only $10.2 million in 2025; the $2.12 billion broader synthetic-diamond market matters only if Kara can expand from custom pieces into a qualified production platform. Otherwise this may become an impressive materials workshop without an equity-scale outcome. The founders’ backgrounds make that expansion plausible, but plausibility does not satisfy the operating gate.

I would pass today. One verifiable condition would flip me: a documented customer qualification failure followed within weeks by a founder-shipped process change that meets a named yield or thermal threshold and produces a paid repeat order. That single sequence would show resourcefulness, technical proof, and a narrow user pulling the product back. It would turn Kara from a good origin story into a logical proof.
