# Roma -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Roma’s post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and status as a first scarce check are all unknown. That leaves the deal ununderwritten; YC membership does not establish an auction. The capital plan is equally unspecified: no named experiment, deadline, or measurable result explains what this round would prove. “The to-do list that does itself” describes a product ambition. What truth does my money buy?

The interesting asset is the team’s ability to make connected software usable. Jan Senderek built Loom and sold it to Dropbox; Tobias Eichenwald co-founded Senic, acquired by Gira, and led product design and partnerships. Those are shipped receipts, substantially better than prestigious job titles. Roma also has actual Mac and iPhone distribution. This partially fits my preference for founders translating between systems. But the dossier establishes consumer-product experience more clearly than operating experience with reliable autonomous execution. Two exits do not establish that second capability.

Roma’s potential dark pool is the task record: persistent intent, personal context, and results that survive whichever assistant performs the work. Letting external assistants access that workspace could make Roma more durable than a particular model. The strongest bull case is that experienced product founders turn this shared record into the place users habitually delegate work. Nine US App Store ratings averaging 5.0 provide a small favorable reception signal. They do not establish repeat delegation, purchases, or proprietary access causing conversion. Todoist already offers external AI-agent integrations, so connectivity alone cannot carry the ownership argument.

The execution boundary is where product usefulness and investment economics meet. Roma’s browser stops at some verification barriers, and users must complete payments. Approval controls are sensible; they also mean that a task started is not necessarily a task finished. I would track cash contribution from completed, retained paid workflows, including model costs and support. The free iPhone download supplies no evidence about that metric, and the dossier provides no pricing, margin, burn, or seed-funded break-even plan. This is missing economics, not demonstrated bad economics. Likewise, provider dependence creates exposure to commoditization, but the dossier does not show that Roma assumes errors will disappear or that another round is necessary for survival.

I pass at **31/100**. The strongest objection is legitimate: these founders have already built products valuable enough to acquire, and the persistent task workspace could become the durable asset above interchangeable assistants. But the known exceptions to my price and venture-moment gates require demonstrated conversion, retention, cash economics, or an explicit alternative financing structure. None appears here. Nor does a three-founder team establish an equal-control committee; Senderek is identified as CEO. My verifiable condition for reopening is one paid cohort that, over 90 days, repeatedly delegates completed tasks and produces positive contribution after model and support costs. That would turn the proposed workflow advantage into observed economic behavior strong enough to reconsider the missing gates.
