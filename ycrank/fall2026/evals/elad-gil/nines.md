# Nines -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI data-center expansion creates a buyer for faster physical commissioning; Nines becomes a large company only if robotic fiber patching lowers the fully loaded cost of bringing compute online. That is the controlling variable. It includes robot utilization, technician interventions, deployment engineering, and the cost of mistakes. The dossier identifies idle compute waiting for physical work, but supplies no measurements establishing that robots improve this equation. The $16.2 billion broader robotics-market estimate does not establish the economics of this particular task.

Commissioning is a potentially useful entry point. It may let Nines establish reliability before attempting maintenance around operating equipment, where Microsoft’s November 2024 research documents the danger of disturbing adjacent cables. That same research provides a dated technical precedent for transceiver manipulation, earning some credit for feasibility. It does not establish a commercial cost reduction. Nines identifies its website sequence as simulated inspection; that is transparent presentation, but it leaves physical task performance unverified. I would distinguish evidence that this category is becoming technically possible from evidence that this company can deliver it economically.

The strongest bull case is that Nines can become an external robotics supplier to operators that cannot justify developing these capabilities internally. Commissioning could provide initial site access, followed by drive swaps, optics reseating, and other recurring work through the same customer relationship. Tiantian Li’s reported large-scale inference responsibilities at Meta and autonomous-underwater-vehicle experience give that possibility more substance than a generic robotics pitch. But Microsoft’s internal development and Exclaim’s overlapping product, backed by $4.95 million in disclosed financing, make supplier selection consequential. Neither the founders’ backgrounds nor the proposed task sequence establishes that Nines has won access to a facility.

Current demand evidence remains at the absent-evidence default. The November 13 launch attracted approximately 252,000 views, but attention does not establish operator usage or purchase intent. Likewise, developing control models for several tasks does not yet demonstrate ownership of an operational workflow: there are no documented production integrations, recovery loops, or accumulated deployment data. Better models could improve manipulation, but the dossier does not show which durable customer assets Nines would retain. The founders’ Cornell, Patreon, Meta, and PARC backgrounds are relevant experience; they do not document prior startup learning or costly personal commitment.

I would pass on a personal check today. My pattern of backing software that changes physical-industry economics makes this market worth investigating, but the corresponding red flag—site-specific engineering retaining service-business economics—remains unresolved rather than established. The research-stage exception also does not apply on the available evidence: Nines has not documented distinctive frontier research and a financing path sufficient to override missing customer pull. **My flip condition is one named paying operator’s independently verifiable 30-day commissioning deployment showing fully loaded cost per successfully completed connection below half the manual baseline, with equal or better throughput and connection-error rates.** That would test the economic mechanism directly and justify a different underwriting discussion.
