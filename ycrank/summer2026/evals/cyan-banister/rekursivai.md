# rekursiv.ai -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: a strong ML researcher no longer spends the week hand-authoring experiments. She specifies the disputed question, supervises a fleet of artificial scientists, and interrogates an evidence graph showing which hypotheses survived. rekursiv.ai has a real leak from that future: four agents using Claude Max and one GPU reportedly produced an ML paper for roughly $500, followed by 235 ARC experiments across 15 directions and 684 Sudoku experiments in four days. This is more than a fashionable category label. The dossier still leaves important steps unnamed—especially reliable cross-domain transfer and external validation—but the machine is already doing recognizable research labor.

The founders are the strongest part of the bet. Joshua Dillon created TensorFlow Probability, built the Veo prototype, and led foundation-model pre-training; Dan Kondratyuk was first author on VideoPoet and led Luma’s World Models team. Their obsession predates rekursiv.ai by years and produced tools, models, papers, and public code. They also survived contact with the exact bottlenecks their product attacks. This is not two people who discovered “AI scientist” during fundraising season. The open question is Biz rather than Tizz: the dossier proves unusual technical fluency, but not yet who buys, how spending expands, or which founder has final authority.

The working artifact is persuasive but remains mostly self-play. The reported 100% Sudoku-Extreme result, 99,768-puzzle stress test, and low-compute ARC performance demonstrate that the orchestration loop can generate and test ideas. They do not yet show costly external behavior. There is no named customer, revenue, repeat design partner, or independent researcher rearranging work around the product; 16 GitHub followers and 29 stars for `sagent` are modest signals. The pre-revenue exception is relevant because there is a functioning prototype and unusually deep founder history, but it is incomplete without the equivalent of a signed external proof of concept.

Here is the bear case that stings: rekursiv.ai may be an excellent research project inside a brutally crowded consensus round. Sakana AI has open-sourced a competing AI Scientist, the company’s early paper relied on Claude Max, its own discoveries and components use Apache 2.0, and neither an exclusive dataset nor an unreleased orchestration advantage has been demonstrated. Public ARC evaluation may overstate performance by four to six points, and the Sudoku discovery did not transfer directly to ARC. With Sakana, Periodic Labs, Lila Sciences, and Edison Scientific collectively financed at enormous scale, a $5 million YC raise is capital evidence—not customer evidence—and could be precisely the fashionable AI financing pattern I distrust.

What keeps me interested is the layer beneath the demo. The product is not merely generating research prose; it coordinates hypothesis formation, experiment execution, peer review, early termination, leaderboards, and evidence tracing. If external teams use it, the accumulated chain of failed experiments, repairs, and subsequent discoveries could become the controlled asset. Today that asset is inferred, not proven, and the dossier names only a generic human bottleneck rather than quantifying its rent or showing whose budget and autonomy are reclaimed. That missing economic mechanism is the lowest part of the case.

I would invest, but with a smaller personal check appropriate to the unresolved commercial layer. The founders have built enough of the strange future to earn belief before revenue, while the benchmark caveats and open-source posture keep this well below Strong Invest. My single flip condition is concrete: if the first three external research teams cannot each reproduce at least one material improvement on their own benchmark or model within twelve months, I move to Pass—the system would then be an impressive founder-operated laboratory, not a scalable company.
