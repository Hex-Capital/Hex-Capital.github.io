# Vendo -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Specific knowledge leaves artifacts. Zahzah and Helal previously built Aisle together, have known each other since kindergarten, and now show 3,539 repository commits, nightly tests against 16 applications, and a package receiving 1,220 weekly downloads. This is more than credential theater. They can build and persist. But the record proves AI-product development more strongly than it proves knowledge of enterprise product backlogs, security reviews, or software sales. The founders have shipped near the problem; they have not yet sold deeply into the exact problem.

The decisive counterfactual is simple: copy the code. Vendo is Apache-2.0, AG-UI is becoming standardized, and CopilotKit already offers authenticated tools, approvals, shared state, generative interfaces, and self-hosting. Nightly compatibility testing is excellent engineering discipline, but discipline is not a proprietary asset. The proposed accumulation of de-identified execution and sandbox patterns could become one; today it is an inference without comparative performance. The code creates leverage for customers, but ownership of that leverage has not yet accrued to Vendo.

The permissionless signal is real but incomplete. The 377 stars, 51 forks, and npm usage show that developers can discover and adopt the product without an enterprise sales gate. Yet consumption is not a network effect. The dossier does not show that a fork contributes improvements upstream, that one customer’s micro-app becomes useful to another customer, or even that coworkers repeatedly reuse one another’s generated interfaces. “Users build features” describes the product interaction; it does not yet prove that users compound the system.

The market can be large because the shared capability—scanning APIs, components, permissions, and themes—could extend from feature backlogs into workflows, automations, internal tools, and agent governance across B2B software. That is a credible adjacency, although no buyer behavior yet proves the path. Likewise, $49 Pro and $499 Teams pricing names a direct business model, but a pricing page is an offer, not value capture. There are no disclosed paying customers, renewals, completed transactions, or revenue.

The strongest bull case is that two persistent founders have already produced an unusually active open-source artifact, found developer distribution, and formed a product category before its final language is obvious. That can justify overlooking absent revenue or a missing user loop in a genuinely strange, capital-efficient product. But Vendo’s ambiguity is not merely early measurement: the company moved from Aisle to open-source deployment and customization, then to embedded agents, while better-funded competitors occupy adjacent layers. One transition is not abandonment, and the unfinished governing-law clause is operational immaturity rather than evidence of bad integrity. Still, standardized interfaces reduce integration work for Vendo and its competitors simultaneously.

I pass on the current product bet. My 36-month prediction is that Vendo pivots from user-generated product features into enterprise agent-action governance and integration, reaching $1 million–$3 million ARR there rather than winning the embedded-interface layer. I would flip if Vendo demonstrates that, for three consecutive months, at least 30% of active customer-generated micro-apps are reused by a second user without company assistance. That would convert a clever tool into a compounding system.
