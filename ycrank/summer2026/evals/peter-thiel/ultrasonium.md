# Ultrasonium -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Ultrasonium’s proposed secret is precise: a controlled acoustic field can shape molten metal into production-grade parts 15 times faster and 75% cheaper than laser-and-powder systems. What would prove it wrong? Failure to achieve repeatable submillimeter accuracy, greater than 99% density, and the claimed economics on aluminum or copper parts. Today those outcomes remain NSF Phase I objectives, while the speed and cost comparisons lack disclosed geometries, volumes, and test methods. This is a falsifiable natural secret, not merely enthusiasm for domestic manufacturing—but it remains an assertion rather than a demonstrated zero-to-one event.

The intellectual structure is stronger than the experimental record. U.S. Patent 12,208,450 protects integrated acoustic and thermal modulation, and the hemispherical phased array, computational control, and contactless confinement constitute a genuinely different technical mechanism from Seurat’s and VulcanForms’ laser powder beds or Fluent Metal’s drop-on-demand process. But a patent establishes exclusion over claims, not mastery of metallurgy. There is no disclosed production output, customer-qualified part, exclusive process dataset, or structural barrier. Ultrasonium may own an unusual method without yet owning a market.

The team is unusually credible for the technical problem. Jack Qiu brings MIT and LBNL control-hardware research; Chris Carter has worked on additively manufactured steel and NASA-funded melt-pool simulation; Hunter Brown contributed to ITER and CERN; Alexander Urbanski previously built DOE-funded energy hardware and joined Phase3D at its beginning. Carter is on leave from his PhD, and all four hold operating roles in a 17-person company. Yet shared institutional affiliations are not evidence of long alignment, and the dossier provides neither formation history nor founder ownership. Four impressive résumés can produce a laboratory; they do not automatically produce a durable founding unit.

The proposed beachhead—direct sales to small machinist shops—is admirably bounded but economically unresolved. No machine price, consumables revenue, gross margin, installation burden, sales-cycle evidence, or repeat usage is disclosed. Worse, the expansion sequence moves from small shops to research institutions, dental and medical facilities, while the public launch emphasizes aerospace, defense, energy, and compute. Those are not adjacent customers with one qualification process; they are several distribution and certification systems. A $35.33 billion projected market cannot repair that fragmentation.

The strongest bull case is that the $305,000 NSF award, issued patent, deep technical team, and 17-person buildout precede a decisive validation result. If acoustic manipulation really delivers the claimed cost and throughput advantage, heavily financed competitors may be trapped in laser architectures that cannot copy it economically. That would justify the unusually aggressive hiring and could turn the absence of revenue into ordinary pre-production timing. But no known exception applies yet: there is neither independent technical proof nor persistent customer behavior capable of overriding the coordination risk. Ultrasonium currently requires materials performance, machine reliability, manufacturing, qualification, and direct sales to mature together.

I would not invest today. This is a secret rather than a slogan, but still an experiment rather than a monopoly. The verifiable flip condition is straightforward: demonstrate, through an independent customer trial, repeatable aluminum or copper parts above 99% density at the stated speed and cost advantage, followed by at least one paid machine order from the small-machinist beachhead. That single event would validate the foundational claim while beginning to resolve distribution; without it, additional capital merely finances more simultaneous unknowns.
