# Pennant -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Pennant attacks a real expert workflow: institutional investors manually review thousands of pages while ISS and Glass Lewis control a reported 97% of proxy advice. But “The World Model for Corporate Governance” is larger language than the dossier supports. The adjacent eGRC market is $72.4 billion, not Pennant’s demonstrated cost pool, and there is no quantified analyst spend, affected population, or 10–100x consequence. This could make governance work faster and more auditable. Nothing yet shows it rewrites corporate governance economics.

The technical case is weaker. Pennant aggregates public filings, applies client policies, generates recommendations, and records approvals and overrides. Those are useful product functions, but the dossier supplies no accuracy, throughput, or cost benchmark against ISS, Glass Lewis, Minerva, or an internal analyst team. Worse, the stated components—public filings, general-purpose models, policy rules, and existing proxy infrastructure—are available to competitors. Client-specific precedent data could eventually compound, but a hypothetical future data loop is not a present discontinuity.

The strongest argument against passing is unusually substantive pre-seed validation. A large U.S. public pension plan, a Top AmLaw 100 firm, and governance advisors are already paying; a global asset manager is a design partner; S&P 500 companies are engaged. Buyers with reputational and fiduciary exposure do not casually adopt experimental voting software. This proves painful demand and some trust in the product. It does not prove that Pennant owns the underlying capability rather than providing better implementation around increasingly commoditized models.

The founders improve the odds of execution. Ryan Stewart has more than a decade across State Street proxy voting, BlackRock Aladdin, and PJT board advisory work. Tomas Taylor combines institutional-product experience with contributions to AutoGPT and the Agentic Commerce Protocol. They have known each other for 15 years and cover the domain and technical sides of the workflow. What is absent is evidence of fast belief revision, recruiting exceptional owners for named risks, or changing the product after disconfirming data. This is strong founder-market fit, not yet demonstrated learning velocity.

The relevant exception is Block: an initially incremental financial workflow can grow into infrastructure when an exceptional small team attacks an enormous existing value pool. Pennant shares the narrow wedge and domain-native team. The material difference is that Block’s wedge could expand across merchant and consumer financial activity, while Pennant has not shown either a similarly broad platform path or a proprietary mechanism that escapes internal builds by firms such as JPMorgan. My 36-month base case is that Pennant becomes a credible $3–8 million ARR governance-workflow vendor—or is acquired by Diligent—rather than becoming the foundational model its positioning implies.

I pass today. The company has customer evidence, but capital would currently fund enterprise execution before the largest kill risk—technical substitutability—has been decisively tested. The single flip condition is an independently auditable evaluation across at least 1,000 proposals showing Pennant delivers 10x analyst throughput with under 1% material policy-application error against incumbent workflows. Until then, this is a competent vertical AI business with real buyers, not a sufficiently asymmetric technical option.
