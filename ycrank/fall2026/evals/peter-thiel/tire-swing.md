# Tire Swing -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Tire Swing’s possible secret is that affordable-housing certification can be automated from the documents operators already possess, without replacing their existing systems. That is a proposition worth testing. It would fail if checking the software’s findings consumed the labor it supposedly eliminated. But the dossier establishes neither a disputed belief that named competitors reject nor a measured result against that belief. Document classification, income extraction, and source-linked findings describe a product; they do not yet establish a secret.

The distinction between creating value and keeping it is decisive here. Tire Swing targets a recognizable beachhead: move-in certifications and recertifications for affordable-housing operators. Its reported library of thousands of deterministic and AI checks represents implementation work. The underlying regulations, however, are not established as exclusive, and accumulated reviewer histories remain a hypothetical switching cost. Pronto Housing already offers qualification and compliance automation. Yardi approaches part of the problem through direct payroll and banking data. These are different approaches, so I would not declare them interchangeable. But Tire Swing has not demonstrated which indispensable capability its competitors cannot reproduce or bypass.

The founders provide the strongest affirmative evidence. Paul Witten and Lucas Irvine met studying computer science at Princeton; this is an existing relationship rather than an expressly documented convenience pairing. Witten’s cybersecurity investigations and Irvine’s Instacart and Cloud Custodian engineering experience support their ability to build consequential software. They do not establish prior joint execution, full-time status, or economic alignment, which remain unknown. Nor does the reported transition from healthcare compliance to housing prove opportunism. It does leave the founders’ specific discovery about this market unexplained. Against my unstated preference for companies formed around a definite operating thesis, the dossier supplies technical competence more clearly than a distinctive thesis.

The strongest argument against passing is concrete: HUD’s January 1, 2027 HOTMA enforcement deadline creates a reason for covered operators to change workflows, and Tire Swing already describes property-specific configuration, source-linked review, and human approval. Founder demonstrations using anonymized prospect files and Witten’s AHF Live sponsorship put the product in front of plausible buyers. A narrow regulatory workflow could become essential infrastructure before outsiders recognize its importance. Yet the deadline creates demand for competitors too. Conference access establishes a route to buyers, not repeatable acquisition economics. Subscription order forms establish one revenue model, but absent pricing and delivery costs leave its potential scale unproved; EliseAI’s company-wide ARR cannot supply Tire Swing’s arithmetic.

I would pass on a check today. This is not a finding that the founders are part-time, the technology is false, or human review makes the business impossible; none is established. The relevant red flag is exposure to an existing competitive field without demonstrated market ownership. My known exceptions require independent technical proof, persistent customer behavior, or realized economics strong enough to overcome that concern. None is documented here. Tire Swing’s resemblance to an adversarial information system—making eligibility evidence legible—also does not establish proprietary advantage. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independently supervised comparison showing tenfold reviewer throughput at equal or better certification accuracy against the operator’s best available alternative. Until then, I can identify useful work more readily than an enduring monopoly.
