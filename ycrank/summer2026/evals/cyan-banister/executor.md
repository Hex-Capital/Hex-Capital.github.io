# Executor -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. AI agents are no longer merely drafting text; they are operating payroll, deployments, customer records, and internal systems across several interfaces. Before that future works, agents must become dependable actors, protocols must multiply beyond MCP, and organizations must separate permission from execution. Executor is betting that the shared credential-and-policy layer becomes mandatory. The leak from that future is concrete: roughly 14,000 npm downloads per week, 3,000 local users, and 230 repository forks. What remains unproven is whether those users are coordinating multiple agents and accounts—the behavior that would make this a control plane rather than a convenient developer tool.

Rhys Sullivan is the strongest part of the dossier. He built Answer Overflow to 1.5 million monthly users before Executor, worked across Vercel, Microsoft, Raven Software, and Epic Games, and has now accumulated 2,580 commits in a repository with 3,000 stars. That is not a founder discovering infrastructure during a fashionable fundraising cycle. It is repeated proof that he can build and distribute technical products. His 57,245-person X audience supplies recruiting and distribution leverage, although the one-person team and absence of paid-customer evidence leave commercial command less tested than technical obsession.

The irritating system is also real. Every agent client asks teams to configure the same OAuth connections, services constrain them to one account, and destructive actions receive inconsistent controls. Executor returns custody through local and self-hosted operation, then adds allow, approval, and block policies around execution. But the dossier never quantifies the engineering hours lost, identifies who captures the rent, or shows that a buyer values the recovered control at $150 per month. I can see the wound; I cannot yet measure its economics.

The product has escaped the rendering stage. Thousands of local users are installing it, 230 developers have invested enough labor to fork it, and the founder is shipping across MCP servers, OpenAPI, GraphQL, custom integrations, desktop, CLI, cloud, and self-hosting. Those are meaningful pre-seed behaviors. They are not yet costly enough to prove dependence: npm downloads can include automated installs, stars can be admiration, and there is no cohort retention, production execution volume, or paid renewal data.

The bear case stings because Executor does not control the scarce layer yet. Microsoft publishes an MIT-licensed MCP gateway, Zapier brings 9,000 applications and 30,000 actions, and Nango already has several million dollars of ARR. Executor’s own MIT-licensed self-hosted version exposes the core functionality that its cloud must monetize. Protocol churn creates useful maintenance work but not necessarily durable power. Worse, concentrating production credentials creates exactly the custody risk I treat as a gate. Host-side resolution, token exclusion from the model and sandbox, and per-tool policies are the right architecture; an independent security review still needs to prove those boundaries.

I would write a small personal check. This matches my less glamorous pattern: a talented builder repairing concealed operational plumbing beneath a visible AI wave, with public artifacts showing that developers have already found it. I am not at Strong Invest because the present-day leak proves distribution more clearly than durable economics or control of a bottleneck. My one verifiable flip condition is security: if an independent review demonstrates that raw credentials can reach either the model or the execution sandbox under any supported configuration, I pass.
