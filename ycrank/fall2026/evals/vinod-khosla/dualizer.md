# Dualizer -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Dualizer could matter enormously if better simulation lets chemical plants consume materially less energy, waste less feedstock, or develop processes that are uneconomic today. But the dossier does not quantify any of those consequences. Its $1.77 billion simulator-market estimate measures software spending; it supplies no baseline for the industrial costs Dualizer could eliminate. I can imagine a consequential outcome. I cannot underwrite my own check against an outcome I supplied myself.

The technical distinction worth investigating is KinetIQ’s proprietary deterministic engine. Putting Vantage’s proposed changes through engineer approval is sensible, but approval does not establish physical accuracy. Calibration demonstrations use synthetic histories, with measured plant-data validation still outstanding. Meanwhile, researchers have already demonstrated natural-language control of AVEVA through MCP, and DWSIM advertises an MCP server. Conversational model construction therefore faces a concrete replication path. Dualizer needs to establish what its engine makes possible that these alternatives cannot deliver. No comparative benchmark answers that question. That leaves technical discontinuity unproven; it does not establish that the founders have merely built a wrapper.

The strongest argument against passing is the people assembled around that engine. Chris Donahue has worked at AVEVA; Nathan Liu brings controls and ML research; Dongha Kim contributes software and materials-computation experience. Advisors Paul Mathias and Stratos Pistikopoulos add directly relevant process-modeling expertise. This fits my pattern of backing teams where computation meets an expensive scientific workflow. Winning $100,000 against 531 competition teams in September 2026 gives me another reason to examine the work. But those facts establish domain preparation and external recognition. They do not document rapid belief revision, an unusually difficult recruit won, or a proprietary learning loop from operating plants.

The next dollar should attack transfer from synthetic examples to measured industrial reality. September’s stated next steps—unseen engineering cases and a first paid industrial pilot—point toward useful tests. The dossier supplies no numerical acceptance threshold, test budget, or response to failure, however. The prize cannot substitute for that experiment without knowing what the competition actually validated. Nor do three advertised jobs establish premature scaling: hiring intentions alone tell me too little about spending or risk removal.

Deployment economics could decide whether Dualizer becomes repeatable software or a succession of engineering projects. The forward-deployed role explicitly includes proprietary customer data, custom unit models, and kinetics. That work might create durable calibrated models; it might also recur at every installation. Pricing, implementation hours, and ongoing support costs are undisclosed, so neither outcome earns credit yet. I do back execution-heavy software businesses. I also make exceptions for an extraordinary instigator addressing a demonstrably enormous value pool. Here, the dossier establishes neither that exception nor the quantified consequence that would justify overlooking the missing technical and economic evidence.

I pass at 25/100. The missing revenue history is ordinary for this stage; the unresolved issue is what breakthrough my money would purchase. One verifiable result would flip my decision: an independently reviewed industrial pilot showing at least a 10x reduction in total engineering cost for a defined process-design task versus Aspen Plus, including customer-specific setup and human review, while matching agreed accuracy on withheld measured plant data. That would connect the engine, the customer benefit, and delivery economics in one decisive experiment.
