# Lyon -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Lyon’s strongest evidence is not Gabriel Noya’s Stanford, NVIDIA, or hackathon résumé. It is the model trained on 28 billion transactions for a fintech serving tens of millions of users, producing four times the precision of existing premium-card-conversion rules. A two-person company getting proprietary modeling infrastructure inside a financial institution’s cloud is costly action in the exact problem. I cannot attribute the work entirely to Noya or infer banking knowledge from technical credentials, but the deployed artifact is far more informative than the credentials.

The wedge has room to maneuver. The same event representation can support credit, fraud, collections, income, churn, cross-sell, and lifetime value; credit deployment following the conversion result is early evidence that this is a shared capability rather than seven unrelated products. Work with an insurer also suggests expansion beyond fintech. The $4.68 billion financial predictive-analytics market is less important than the causal adjacency: the institution already owns the transaction history, while Lyon can attach additional prediction tasks to the same deployment.

The problem is what remains after the code is copied. Training inside each customer’s VPC protects the customer, not Lyon. The customer owns the data and trained intelligence, deployments remain isolated, and published transaction-model research gives Taktile, Pecan, Feedzai, H2O.ai, and internal bank teams paths toward overlapping capability. Experience handling 28 billion transactions, SOC 2 Type 1 compliance, adapters, and deployment automation are real execution assets, but the dossier does not show an exclusive dataset, proprietary model, hardware advantage, or performance asset that compounds across customers.

This architecture also runs against my unstated preference for permissionless systems. One bank’s participation does not improve another bank’s model; each institution appears to require another sale, security review, private training run, and deployment. Lyon may become an excellent enterprise vendor, but there is no customer-created network accumulating value while the company sleeps. No direct red flag appears in founder integrity, morale, or financing behavior, although the dossier provides little evidence on those gates.

The strongest bull case is the known exception for a small team with a working, unusually capable prototype before a network or finished business model exists. Lyon has more than a demo: fourfold precision and a credit expansion inside a large fintech are meaningful pre-seed validation. But unlike Notion, the present product does not reveal capital-efficient distribution or a compounding product language; its value remains locked inside separately procured enterprise installations. No disclosed payer, contract value, or recurring revenue yet proves that technical lift becomes direct value capture.

My 36-month base case is $3 million to $7 million in ARR from a small number of financial institutions, with Lyon still constrained by customer-specific deployment rather than compounding distribution. That can be a good company without yet being the nonlinear option I want. I would not invest today. One verifiable condition flips the decision: three paying institutions each expanding the same Lyon deployment from one task to at least three, while implementation time falls across successive customers. That would prove transferable leverage rather than bespoke technical labor.
