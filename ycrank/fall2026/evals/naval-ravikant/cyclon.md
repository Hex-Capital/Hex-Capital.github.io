# Cyclon -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Cyclon’s strongest asset is the founders’ proximity to the machinery. Thomas Kiefer worked on Android at Google; Adam Zinebi researched LLMs and robotic agents and previously sold music-generation models ([founder profiles](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/cyclon)). These are relevant actions. They suggest the team can connect an agent to a phone’s operating system. The evidence stops before identifying a shipped handset, a demonstrated reliable agent, or customers using something the founders built for this exact problem. I give the founders credit for relevant experience, with conviction limited by the missing artifacts.

The decisive question is what Cyclon owns after another manufacturer implements the same interface. Its detachable camera and integrated hardware could enable experiences that ordinary apps cannot deliver. But the hardware remains a concept in development, and the prototype specifications can change ([Cyclon](https://cyclon.ai/), [terms](https://cyclon.ai/terms)). Owning a hardware project does not automatically create a hardware advantage. No proprietary component, exclusive model, or accumulating deployment asset is established. Brain’s April 2026 launch through SoftBank makes the competitive question concrete: another intention-based phone already has retail distribution.

The strongest argument against passing is that this particular pair could discover the interface through building it. Android experience, agent research, and microtechnology training form a coherent combination. More capable phone NPUs provide a genuine technical opening. My willingness to fund strange products before launch applies here in principle. But that exception needs a demonstrated prototype or unusually persuasive evidence of capital-efficient invention. Cyclon’s waitlist has no disclosed enrollment count, and its ten public repositories include operating-system forks; neither establishes the distinctive product behavior that would justify the exception.

User-created apps also touch my preference for tools that let individuals turn knowledge into scalable output. The missing mechanism is contribution: the dossier does not establish that one person’s app becomes useful to another person, or that another customer improves discovery, reliability, or distribution. Personal configurations could eventually create switching costs, but that remains an inference. Meanwhile, Gartner’s $393.3 billion smartphone forecast establishes substantial adjacent spending without identifying Cyclon’s initial buyer or a repeatable expansion path. Prototype ownership terms suggest direct sales, but disclose no price, completed purchase, or margin.

I pass with my own money. There is no documented integrity problem, artificial financing deadline, or founder instability triggering a personal veto. The obstacle is structural: Cyclon must persuade users to replace their phone while competing against vendors already selling overlapping capabilities. Its relevant founder knowledge has yet to become a durable asset or a customer-created loop. One verifiable result would reopen this: a paid, independently observed pilot in which users repeatedly replace their primary phones with Cyclon because of a measured capability that requires its particular hardware-software integration.
