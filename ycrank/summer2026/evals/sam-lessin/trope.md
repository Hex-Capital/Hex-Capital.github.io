# Trope -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I begin with three blanks: no post-money valuation, no competing-term-sheet count, and no evidence this is genuinely the first scarce check. That earns 6/28, not a presumption that YC’s standard financing is cheap. The second blank matters almost as much: Trope has not said what finite experiment the round buys. “Build agents, deliver implementations, and grow” is a business plan; it is not a named hypothesis with a deadline and a result that dramatically changes value.

The underlying mechanism is more interesting than the financing evidence. ERP implementations are expensive because requirements, configuration, data, testing, and accountability cross organizational boundaries. Trope proposes compressing that work with field-level Business Central APIs, an in-house agent harness, release-wave tests, and human approval gates. Fixed pricing converts automation gains into Trope’s potential margin rather than merely reducing billed hours. That is the right economic architecture for disrupting an hourly consultancy.

The strongest bull case is that the prior workflow product already produced paid pilots, a contract covering more than 400 staff, two-to-three-times faster completion, and 90% fewer errors. Add principal consultants credited with 200-plus ERP implementations, and this could be a legitimate translation layer between AI engineering and the ugly operational reality of ERP delivery. Microsoft’s GP and NAV support deadlines also create a time-bounded migration wave rather than a generic “AI is coming” story.

But the product transition breaks the causal chain. Those paid pilots validated a prior workflow-guidance product, not the Business Central implementation service launched in July 2026. Trope discloses no customer, revenue, deployment volume, or managed-agent conversion for the current offering. Its 594 LinkedIn followers and 28 GitHub stars do not show that owned access causes purchases. The inferred corpus of migration mappings, exceptions, and tests is not yet a dark pool; without deployment volume, it is merely data the company might eventually own.

The economics are similarly plausible but unproven. Fixed fees plus recurring managed agents could create a profitable, one-and-done business, yet there are no prices, delivery costs, gross margins, burn figures, or route to break-even. Delete the next round and the present dossier leaves a two-person team assuming financial-process liability while depending on Microsoft infrastructure and competing with both incumbent Dynamics partners and heavily financed replacement ERPs. The most likely 36-month outcome is an independent, sub-$3 million annualized-revenue Business Central consultancy whose services revenue outruns its managed-agent revenue—not a scalable software platform.

I am passing at this evidence level. The founders have shipped credible software across Tesla, Zipline, Amazon agent tooling, and a paid workflow product, but the dossier does not show that either founder has personally delivered ERP transformations; the 200-plus implementations belong to unnamed consultants. My verifiable flip condition is a cohort of five paid Business Central implementations completed at positive contribution margin, with at least three converting into retained managed-agent contracts. That one result would demonstrate purchase causality, reusable workflow ownership, and a business capable of surviving without manufacturing Series A metrics.
