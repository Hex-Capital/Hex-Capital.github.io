# Deploy -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

By 2032, a warehouse operator should be able to change what a robot packs without commissioning another integration project. Deploy offers a plausible route: break unfamiliar work into steps, recognize failure, bring in a human, and learn from the correction. The interesting possibility is that each exception makes the next deployment easier. But that last step remains a hypothesis: the dossier establishes an intervention-to-training process, without longitudinal evidence that assistance declines or learning transfers between customers.

The founders make that hypothesis worth taking seriously. Sahith Chada researched vision-language-action and world models at Honda and published an object-pose-estimation implementation with 36 GitHub stars. Irene Polson worked on deploying autonomous airport vehicles at AeroVect. Those are complementary encounters with the distance between a model working and a machine doing its job. Their progression from IIT Kanpur laboratory partners to co-founders also predates this launch. I see technical commitment and relevant operating experience; I do not yet see the dated sequence of independent experiments, recruited believers, and commercial decisions that would establish exceptional founder obsession.

Why are these warehouse stations still manual? Variable work makes fixed automation difficult to amortize. Deploy removes the hardware purchase and separate integration fee, offering monthly coverage below replacement labor cost. That could give smaller operators access to capabilities previously reserved for predictable, high-volume workflows. But the installation bill has moved onto Deploy’s balance sheet. It disappears economically only if equipment utilization, servicing, and remote assistance fit inside the subscription. This is a concrete financing and workflow constraint, with no demonstrated artificial gatekeeper rent or quantified customer savings.

The October 2026 demonstration—800-plus pens packed after approximately 30 minutes of fine-tuning data—shows a functioning artifact. The reported failures make exception handling especially relevant. What it does not establish is a warehouse manager trusting Deploy with a shift, reorganizing work around it, and paying again. Exhibition throughput earns technical interest; it supplies little evidence of costly customer commitment. Manifold already offers robotic labor priced per pick, so monthly billing alone gives me little reason to expect durable differentiation.

The strongest case against waiting is that these founders could learn the deployment business precisely by operating it. Deploy fits my pattern of finding concealed infrastructure beneath a visible service: task decomposition and failure recovery could support returns, kitting, and packing across changing robot capabilities. The pre-revenue exception illustrated by **CosmicBrain AI** is relevant because both bets connect robotics expertise to physical automation; the material difference is that its documented industrial proofs of concept supplied customer commitment missing here. Deploy’s software occupies a useful enabling position, but neither exclusive training rights nor control of a scarce technical asset is established. The dossier also establishes no severe privacy violation, misleading leadership arrangement, or dependence on one closed model supplier; those red flags cannot be assumed from silence.

I am neutral on investing my own money today. My expectation over the next 36 months is that Deploy narrows into a supervised service for a few repeatable warehouse tasks before achieving broad generalist coverage. That could become a good business, but it would weaken the claim that deployment experience compounds into general capability. My flip condition is one independently verifiable, 90-day paid warehouse deployment showing declining human intervention and positive station gross margin after remote labor, hardware, and servicing. That would connect the future I can imagine to an operating machine I can underwrite.
