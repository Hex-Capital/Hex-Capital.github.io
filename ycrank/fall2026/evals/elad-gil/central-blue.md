# Central Blue -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Central Blue becomes interesting if externally supplied power lets satellite operators earn more from their payloads than the full cost of receiving that power. That is the controlling variable: incremental mission value after service fees, receiver mass, thermal constraints and retained backup capacity. The dossier establishes why operators might want more electricity, but does not establish when buying it beats adding onboard generation and storage. Line-of-sight interruptions matter because customers may still need both systems. Without that comparison, “the power grid for space” describes an architecture rather than a demonstrated market opening.

There is a measurable technical change behind the idea. DARPA delivered more than 800 W across 8.6 km for 30 seconds in May 2025. That earns some credit for timing, although terrestrial transmission does not establish orbital availability or attractive delivered-power economics. Central Blue’s HELION figures—300 kWh of storage and 10 kW delivered for four to five hours—remain company targets. They do not yet show a reduction in customer cost, an increase in payload revenue or a change in supplier margins. I would fund proof of that economic step before funding a fleet.

The strongest bull case is that Central Blue can develop the complete system around a real spacecraft constraint. Brian Wu brings JPL navigation and robotics experience; Jeewoo Choi brings Boeing thermal and environmental-testing experience. A nearly completed prototype and the planned Wave Motion demonstration give that work a concrete next test. This fits my pattern of entering physical markets when technology can reconstruct their economics. But the exception requires evidence of that reconstruction. The research-stage exception also needs demonstrated frontier capability and a credible capital path; the disclosed backgrounds and YC backing do not yet establish those for orbital power delivery.

Market structure makes the receiver decision consequential. Star Catcher has raised $88 million and reports an October 1, 2026 demonstration-payload launch, while emphasizing compatibility with existing solar arrays. Central Blue proposes dedicated receivers. Those receivers could eventually create integration depth, but currently introduce a customer adoption requirement without a documented performance advantage. CORE could own a recurring operational loop through fault detection and recovery; its Blackwing demonstration is targeted for 2027, so I would not yet award credit for an integrated, operating workflow. Neither planned demonstration establishes customer pull, and earlier drone and lunar-rover ventures establish entrepreneurial exposure without documenting the specific learning or costly commitment required by my founder criterion.

I would pass with my own money today. This is an economic-validation gap, rather than evidence that the technology cannot work. There is no disclosed customer commitment that resolves the receiver tradeoff, and no measured system economics that clears my market gate. My one flip condition is a named operator signing a paid deployment contract backed by an independently witnessed, end-to-end test showing that its incremental payload value exceeds the fully loaded power-service and integration cost, including retained backup capacity. That would connect the prototype to a business.
