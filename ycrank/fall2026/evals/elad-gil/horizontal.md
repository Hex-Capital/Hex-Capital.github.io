# horizontal. -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Industrial agents are moving from analysis toward execution; plant managers become buyers if those agents resolve production disruptions economically across existing systems. For horizontal., the controlling variable is how much implementation and human supervision each additional plant requires. If that burden falls with successive deployments, the company can sell repeatable software into a substantial operational budget. If it stays constant, revenue growth requires proportional engineering growth. The dossier identifies the integration problem and a sensible single-process starting point, but supplies no deployment economics that establish which business this becomes.

The product scope is the strongest affirmative signal. Replanning production, checking materials, investigating quality issues, and coordinating order releases are concrete operational actions ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/horizontal)). Better models could improve execution while horizontal. retains the connections and operational context. That earns limited workflow credit. However, the descriptions are company-reported; the dossier does not document functioning integrations, permission controls, or retained workflow histories. I would not underwrite a data moat from procedures the company might accumulate.

The strongest argument for investing now is that the founders may already understand how to make these deployments repeatable. Niklas Marxer reports bringing agentic AI into more than **150 industrial companies before horizontal.**; Jakob Hermeler brings robotics research, including published work on autonomous quadrotor collision recovery. They met doing robotics research at Stanford. Starting with one factory process could let that experience produce a useful deployment quickly, then create distribution for adjacent processes. But the 150-company figure describes prior work, not this product’s adoption, and the biographies do not establish prior founder learning or costly personal commitment. The company’s statement that it works with international manufacturers offers a lead for diligence, without establishing recurring usage or customer pull.

Market timing creates opportunity and competitive pressure simultaneously. Siemens launched Intelligence Center X in **June 2026**, while Pelico already connects agents to ERP and MES data. This category is crystallizing around existing industrial distribution. The adjacent **$20.7 billion MES market in 2025** establishes substantial spending, but does not quantify horizontal.’s accessible budget or an improvement in customer economics. My pattern of investing across a technology discontinuity supports examining this company; it does not resolve why this particular entrant wins. The relevant red flag is a deployment-heavy service claiming software economics. That remains an unanswered question here, rather than an established defect.

I would pass on a personal check today. The research-stage exception requires scarce frontier capability and a credible capital path; the robotics credentials alone do not establish that exception. Likewise, the physical-market exception requires demonstrated economic reconstruction, which this dossier does not show. This is not evidence of failed adoption or poor founders: most dimensions remain at their specified absent-evidence defaults. **My flip condition is one verified deployment cohort: three unrelated manufacturers paying for the same production workflow, with the third deployment requiring no more than half the implementation hours of the first and all three renewing after six months.** That would directly test whether horizontal. is accumulating reusable software and distribution.
