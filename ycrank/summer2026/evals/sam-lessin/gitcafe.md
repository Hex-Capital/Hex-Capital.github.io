# GitCafe -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first three numbers I need are missing: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and check size. That is not evidence of scarcity; it is financing opacity, so the deal begins at 6/28. The more consequential omission is what the money flips. GitCafe has real engineering—a custom libgitz engine, kiln runtime, native binding, forge, CLI, and stacked diffs—but no named, dated experiment connecting the round to a truth-revealing result. “Finish migration and CI parity” would be a roadmap, not yet a venture moment.

The strongest case is that the team has built below the fashionable application layer. This is not a cosmetic GitHub skin: owning the implementation through the forge could permit agent-native repository behavior that incumbents cannot add cleanly without protecting existing workflows. Veronica’s founding-engineer experience at Corgi, Rust and TypeScript work, platform bindings, 72 public repositories, and the functioning beta are shipped receipts. But they establish one side of the bridge—developer tooling—not demonstrated operation inside the enterprise security, procurement, and migration systems that determine whether a company entrusts its private source code to a new forge.

Eight named organizations present engineers as users. That is more informative than follower counts, but GitCafe does not show who migrated a primary repository, returned repeatedly, referred another team, or paid. Private repositories remain free, pricing is unpublished, and one-command migration plus GitHub Actions and GitLab CI compatibility are still planned. The alleged dark pool is therefore repository workflow that GitCafe may eventually own; today the evidence shows sampling, not a customer action with economic consequence.

The bull case stings because the vertical stack may be the technical proof, and the eight organizations may be early users whose behavior simply has not been published. A terminal-first forge designed around coding agents could become valuable while GitHub serves 180 million developers through browser-centered assumptions. This is the kind of pre-seed product where demanding mature revenue would be stupid. But the known exception for an expensive technical seed requires hard external evidence—thousands of users, enterprise demand, or comparable conversion—not merely architecture and logos. GitCafe has not earned that override.

Delete the next financing and the model becomes uncomfortable. There is no price, gross margin, burn, runway, paid conversion, or controllable cash metric; meanwhile, GitHub, GitLab, and Bitbucket can reproduce individual workflow features, and migration requires matching systems already embedded in customers. My 36-month base case is that GitCafe remains a technically respected niche forge below $1 million ARR or pivots toward an agent-specific code-review layer rather than replacing GitHub. The custom engine may be excellent, but excellent infrastructure without a migration wedge can become an expensive way to discover that the repository network—not git implementation quality—owns distribution.

I pass today. The one verifiable flip condition is ten organizations running their primary private repositories on GitCafe, with at least five paying after six months of continuous use. That would simultaneously prove workflow ownership, migration viability, retention, and the beginnings of a business that does not exist merely to finance another round.
