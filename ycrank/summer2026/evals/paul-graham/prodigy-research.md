# Prodigy Research -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The obstacle is unusually real: markets do not care about credentials or demos. Michael Wang and Yuhua Wang have built a proprietary finance model, attached it to an agent harness, and let it trade company capital. That is much better evidence than a diagram. But the dossier does not say what broke while they built it, what workaround they shipped, or how quickly they reacted. DeepMind, Jane Street, Apple, and Salesforce establish formidable ability. They do not by themselves establish relentless resourcefulness.

The strongest fact is the reported 108% delta-neutral return over two months, with no down week. If accurate, Prodigy has converted research into a measured artifact before asking permission. This also fits an unstated pattern I care about: exceptional technical founders producing a concrete proof spike before a financing round. The difficulty is that every important measurement remains inside the company. There is no daily return series, audited brokerage statement, drawdown, volatility, capital base, benchmark protocol, or statistical test behind the claims of beating GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Fable, and a 90th-percentile Jane Street trader. A live system earns substantial credit. An unpublished scoreboard does not earn the same credit as an independently inspectable result.

The bull case is severe enough that passing could look foolish. Michael has worked on both sides of the problem—training Gemini 3.1 Pro and trading at Jane Street—while Yuhua built AI infrastructure used at Apple and Salesforce scale. Their product joins precisely those skills. If the return is robust rather than a small-capital accident, proprietary executions could create a compounding dataset that larger general models lack. The absence of customers would then be irrelevant because the market itself is the customer.

But that formulation also exposes the missing narrow well. Prodigy has no named external user, revenue, repeat usage, or signed capital relationship. Nor does the dossier show users bending a crude product toward an urgent job. Proprietary trading can be a fine business, but it prevents customer behavior from testing whether the model is a product, a strategy, or merely a short favorable run. The business model is still inferred, and better-capitalized competitors already include EquiLibre, which reports systems trading billions of dollars daily, and Numerai, with $550 million in AUM. Prodigy has not yet shown which ugly operational burden it handles better than they do.

The founders’ backgrounds make the origin plausible: this is not an arbitrary “AI for finance” idea assembled from a market map. Michael’s direct experience in foundation models and quantitative trading is meaningful evidence of founder-problem contact, and live deployment entails genuine execution schlep. Still, the dossier gives no dated origin story, route change, rejected hypothesis, or response to contrary market evidence. I cannot infer running-back behavior merely because the starting route is technically impressive.

I also checked the red flags and exceptions. There is no evidence of a press-dependent launch, premature sales hiring, or reliance on a vague large partner. The more relevant danger is investor-legible precision standing in for inspectable proof: spectacular percentages and named-model comparisons can become startup theater when the underlying series is withheld. None of the known exceptions supplies an override, because each depends on working evidence whose crucial result can be independently validated or observed. My verifiable flip condition is an independent administrator or auditor confirming at least six months of net live returns, including capital base, daily series, volatility, and maximum drawdown. Until then, Criterion 1 fails the operational gate, and neither technical pedigree nor a two-month self-reported result replaces it. I would pass today.
