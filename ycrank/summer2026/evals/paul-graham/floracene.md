# Floracene -- Paul Graham Evaluation

I start with the missing event. What broke, and what did the founders do next? Jonah Kaye and Asaph Kupferman have unusually relevant résumés: both worked on Palantir’s hospitals team, Kaye was a founding engineer at Metriport, and Kupferman later built products at Garner Health. But the dossier names no resistant surgeon, unusable contract file, failed installation, or other obstacle followed by a shipped workaround. These founders may be resourceful. Their credentials do not prove it.

The product attacks a real and suitably ugly problem. Implants account for roughly 40–60% of orthopedic and spine procedure costs, while independent centers must reconcile manufacturer contracts, clinical equivalence, surgeon preference, and device-representative influence. That is genuine schlep. Yet Floracene has not shown that its founders encountered this problem personally, rather than selecting it after working near healthcare operations. The distinction matters because proximity to a market is not the same as being unable to tolerate a missing tool.

The technical claim is still a claim. Floracene says it can present at least three clinically equivalent implants with contract prices for each procedure, and that more than 90% of expensive implants are interchangeable. But there is no disclosed equivalence methodology, normalized device dataset, working integration, measured recommendation accuracy, or purchasing result. Curvo already claims coverage of 2.3 million clinical supplies and about $110 billion in weekly clinical spending. A small company can beat a large dataset with a sharper abstraction, but it has to ship the abstraction first.

Nor is there yet a narrow well of users pulling the product back. The dossier identifies a plausible buyer—physician-owners and ASC administrators—but no named center, pilot, LOI, repeated use, revenue, or surgeon who changed an implant choice. The $7 billion estimate for annual surgery-center implant spending explains why savings could matter. It does not show who wants Floracene now. CMS adding 271 ASC procedure codes in 2026 may enlarge the opportunity, but market motion is not user motion.

The strongest bull case is the team itself. Two technical founders who worked together for roughly two years on hospital systems could know exactly where contract data is broken, and physician ownership gives independent centers a more direct incentive to save money than large health systems have. They also avoid two common red flags: this is not an application-only cofounder pairing, and their distribution plan does not visibly depend on a ceremonial launch or unnamed strategic partner. But none of the known exceptions applies yet. There is no working prototype, unmistakable user pull, or independently measured proof capable of replacing the failed resourcefulness gate. The unstated pattern I care about—concrete proof assembled before the round—is precisely what is absent.

I would pass now. In 36 months, my base case is that Floracene has pivoted from point-of-decision implant selection to broader ASC procurement or contract analytics and remains below $1 million ARR, because getting surgeons to substitute devices will prove harder than identifying price differences. One result would flip me: three independent centers using Floracene’s recommendations in at least 20 procedures each over 90 days, with documented implant switching and realized savings, after the founders personally solved a named onboarding or surgeon-adoption blocker. That would simultaneously prove the founders, the product, and the narrow well.
