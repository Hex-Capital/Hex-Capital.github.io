# Petrichor -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Petrichor proposes to remove a real obstacle: AI companies need experiments before they can finish building laboratories. But I want to know what happened when these founders encountered that obstacle themselves. Which experiment could they not run? What did they assemble instead? How quickly did it work? The dossier describes a proposed workaround—scientists executing experiments in parallel—but supplies no completed episode connecting a founder’s action to an observable result. That leaves my most important test unanswered.

The strongest evidence is the team’s earlier work. In September 2026, Qokedas was offering scientific instrument data; the current Petrichor site offers experimental execution and records. This gives the founders some earned proximity to the problem. They have worked on the data coming out of instruments before proposing to organize the work going into them. Laboratory scheduling, protocol execution and capturing failures are also precisely the sort of inconvenient work that can conceal an opportunity. That earns limited credit for the origin and wedge. The change in offering does not yet earn credit for learning: we do not know which customer observation caused it.

There is a good bull case here. Petrichor could discover what scientific models need by doing experiments manually, long before a fully automated laboratory could accommodate them. Photographs, timestamped actions and failed attempts might be more useful than a clean final result. This fits my preference for infrastructure that lets technical users do something new, and manual service could teach the founders what to build. But my recurring infrastructure bets have involved an actual substrate or users already exploiting it. Petrichor’s website specifies the records it intends to deliver; the dossier supplies neither a completed experimental trace nor a customer using one to improve a model. A repository listing without demonstrated functionality does not close that gap.

The decisive question is who wants this badly enough to order the next experiment. “Frontier AI laboratories” is a prospect category. A laboratory that repeatedly submits protocols would be evidence. YC participation, the October 11 coverage and Qokedas’s 48 followers do not establish that behavior. Nor does Lila’s reported experimental volume establish demand for Petrichor. ECL and Strateos make remote experimentation a credible alternative already, so the founders need to discover a particular job their approach handles better. Customer ownership of commissioned data also means I cannot assume every experiment compounds into a shared training-data asset. Execution and workflow integration would have to earn the advantage.

I would pass on the evidence available. The three founders’ history as high-school classmates offers some reassurance about the relationship, and the dossier does not establish passive founders, dependence on a large partner or premature delegation. This is missing evidence, rather than demonstrated bad behavior. But the exceptions that would let me override a weak founder record require a working product, unmistakable pull or validated technical proof; none is documented here. My one verifiable flip condition is a dated customer episode showing a blocked experiment, a founder-built workaround delivered within weeks, and the same customer commissioning another experiment after using the returned data. That would turn Petrichor’s proposed mechanism into something I could invest in.
