# Async -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have learned by shipping. Smith and Park moved from outcome-based AI billing infrastructure in 2025 to legal agents in early 2026, then deployed the current product at Martinez Legal, Clemente Mueller, Joshua Lowenthal, and Clifton Law. Those customers reportedly automated thousands of hours across intake, conflict checks, engagement letters, task assignment, and administrative work. The transitions create strategic uncertainty, but they also reveal repeated contact with the same underlying problem: translating messy business procedures into reliable agent workflows. That is specific knowledge acquired through action, not résumé theater.

The problem is that Async currently converts this knowledge into labor more readily than leverage. Every account begins with an audit, workflow scoping, custom integrations, approval logic, deployment, and ongoing maintenance. The four published customers are all law firms even though the company markets to healthcare, real estate, and childcare. This looks like a capable forward-deployed automation service with software margins still unproven—not yet a system that improves while the founders sleep.

Copy the code and much of the visible advantage disappears. Async relies on Claude and other frontier models, while Lindy already offers more than 100 integrations and Relevance AI lets customers construct agent teams without code. SOC 2 Type I, tenant isolation, company memory, and embedded approval logic matter to buyers, but they are deployment capabilities rather than demonstrated proprietary assets. Customer-specific workflow definitions could become scarce data; the dossier supplies no evidence that learning from one customer improves another deployment or that customers cannot export the resulting procedures.

The bull case is real. Outcome pricing aligns Async with value rather than token consumption, named firms are using the product, and thousands of hours automated is unusually substantive for pre-seed. Narrowing around legal operations could turn repeated implementations into a reusable operating layer, after which healthcare and other regulated service businesses become adjacent markets sharing documents, approvals, scheduling, and system integration. But today the $7.63 billion AI-agents TAM is a category statistic, not proof of Async’s accessible market, and no prices, revenue, gross margins, retention, or paid expansion figures establish direct value capture.

The unstated-pattern test fails for now: customers do not contribute supply, distribution, or intelligence for other customers, and deployment still appears proportional to company labor. The known-exception logic also does not rescue it; a working product can justify missing monetization or network evidence when it exhibits a uniquely compounding substrate, but Async has not shown one. I see no integrity, artificial-deadline, or reassurance-seeking red flag in the founders’ history. The repeated repositioning is only a red flag if customer evidence stops driving it; the legal deployments suggest adaptation rather than random reaction.

My base case is that Async narrows back to legal operations within 36 months and builds a respectable vertical automation business, but not yet a venture-scale compounding network. I would not invest today. One verifiable condition flips the decision: a 12-month cohort of at least 20 law firms showing that accumulated workflow data cuts implementation labor per new deployment by at least 50% while net revenue retention exceeds 120%. That would turn customization from a services burden into a proprietary learning loop.
