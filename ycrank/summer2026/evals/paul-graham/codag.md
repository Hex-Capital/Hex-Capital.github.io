# Codag -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Michael Zhou built an open-source CLI that compresses 1.2 million log lines into 3,317 tokens, but Codag still has only one founder. That pairing is Codag’s signature: a real hacker artifact attached to a fragile founder configuration. Codag is not the usual AI wrapper whose founders plan to hire programmers after raising money. Zhou worked on infrastructure at Okta and Shopify, shipped a previous product that earned 638 GitHub stars, pivoted, and shipped again. The code is evidence. The unanswered question is whether one hacker can turn the code into a company.

**Stripe** is the high rung for hacker genealogy: the Collisons were building commerce software before investors decided payments was interesting. Codag shares the important mechanism. Zhou’s prior 638-star repository, current one-line installer, fine-tuned models, schema-constrained output, and source-line attribution suggest someone who makes artifacts rather than presentations. But Codag diverges from Stripe where the analogy matters most. Zhou has only 30 GitHub followers, no named open-source contributions outside Codag, and no programming-contest or hacker-community history comparable to Patrick Collison’s Lisp trail. Codag has the hacker phenotype, but not yet the genealogy at full strength.

Codag lists one founder, and the low-rung failure mechanism is the absence of anyone who can keep a solo hacker from becoming the bottleneck. Airbnb had three founders; Stripe had two brothers; Reddit had a pair. Codag has no public cofounder history because Codag has no cofounder. That is an active red flag, not a missing pre-seed metric. Loopt shows that exceptional force of personality can override weak timing, and Airbnb shows that animal resourcefulness can override a bad-looking idea, but neither known exception removes the need for overwhelming founder evidence. Codag’s shipped products help; they are not yet the equivalent of Altman’s interview or the cereal boxes.

The prior Codag visualizer accumulated 638 stars before Zhou abandoned it, and that makes resourcefulness compound in an interesting way over the next 18 months. Codag has already completed the useful loop of shipping, attracting attention, discovering the product was wrong, and shipping a second artifact. The current CLI and the 108 MB benchmark are animal tracks. But Codag has not published a current customer, a dollar of revenue, or an unscalable stunt used to obtain production logs. Unless Zhou starts personally installing Codag inside engineering organizations and watching incidents fail, Codag risks becoming good benchmark software built at a comfortable distance from users.

Okta and Shopify put Zhou near the organic-idea threshold, but the dossier never says Zhou personally lost hours because an agent could not digest production logs. For Codag to move up a tier on founder-problem fit, Codag’s origin would need to be a specific incident Zhou experienced, followed by a tool he repeatedly used before deciding to sell it. Employment in infrastructure is domain proximity, not the Dropbox forgotten-USB-stick story. No personal-use evidence is present, so the absent-evidence penalty applies. The red flag is subtle: Codag may be a perceptive response to the 2026 agent market rather than something Zhou urgently wanted for himself.

**Coinbase** turned compliance into an asset because Armstrong kept walking into a schlep everyone else avoided. Codag faces a smaller, mostly technical schlep: PII redaction, schema validity, evidence preservation, and the risk that compression deletes the one line needed to diagnose an incident. Those details are real, but Codag is still pure software with no named regulatory burden, manual integration program, or production customer forcing unpleasant edge cases. Codag’s source-line citations mitigate information loss elegantly; they do not yet prove that Zhou will embrace the months of incident-by-incident debugging needed to make compression trustworthy.

Axiom already offers native MCP access, while Sazabi raised $8 million to build an agent-native observability stack. Within 36 months, I expect Codag to pivot from standalone log compression into broader agent-observability middleware and be acquired by Axiom before reaching $1 million in ARR. Codag’s hacker strength makes a technically useful component plausible. Codag’s solo-founder weakness, absent distribution, and platform substitution risk make an independent company less plausible. This is the common fate of clever infrastructure components: the artifact survives, but the company is absorbed by the system around it.

The 1.2-million-line demonstration is the strongest case that this forecast is too pessimistic: Codag reduces 26.6 million tokens to 3,317 in under one second, retains cited evidence, has working Claude Code and Codex integrations, and comes from a founder who has already attracted 638 GitHub stars. That could be Codag’s Dropbox video—the compact demonstration that makes an apparently narrow tool spread among hackers before incumbents understand it. But the benchmark is company-controlled, the 638 stars belong to Codag’s abandoned product, and neither Axiom nor Sazabi must wait for Codag to educate the market. I would flip to Invest if Codag reached $10,000 in monthly recurring revenue from the current compression product and sustained it for three consecutive months. Until then, the artifact has passed the hacker test, but Zhou has not yet passed the animal test strongly enough for my own money.
