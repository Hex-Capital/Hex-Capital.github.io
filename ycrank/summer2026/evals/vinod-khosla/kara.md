# Kara -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The attractive consequence is not a better diamond supplier. It is computing hardware that tolerates far higher power density because heat stops being the binding constraint. NVIDIA’s 120-kW Blackwell racks establish a real bottleneck, and diamond’s reported 2,400 W/m·K conductivity is roughly 5x SiC and 16x silicon. But Kara has not translated those material properties into system economics: no reduction in cooling power, rack cost, device failure, or semiconductor footprint is quantified. A $2.12 billion materials market is respectable; it is not yet a 100x societal consequence.

The technical case is materially better than the market case. Kara grows, processes, and characterizes single-crystal diamond in its own reactors, controlling orientation, roughness, impurities, and isotope fraction. This is physical process work, not an AI wrapper. The named comparisons are substantial: 2,400 W/m·K against 490 for 4H-SiC and a 10 MV/cm breakdown field against 3.3 MV/cm or less. Yet these are diamond-property benchmarks, not demonstrated Kara-product benchmarks. There is no disclosed wafer size, defect density, yield, thermal-interface resistance, or device-level result. A theoretical 5x material advantage can disappear inside packaging and manufacturing.

The bull case is real. A fifth-generation diamantaire who has sold more than $5 million of diamonds is paired with a founder who worked on diamond quantum sensing at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and a third founder with Amazon AI/ML experience. That combination resembles the pattern I like: a scarce scientific workflow becoming a repeatable production platform, with commercial, materials, and computational competence inside three people. Akash’s March 2026 diamond-cooled AMD MI350X server also suggests that qualification demand is no longer hypothetical.

But the dossier shows no belief revised after data, no exceptional recruit brought in to own manufacturing scale-up, and no milestone sequence attacking the largest kill risk. Kara should not spend its next dollars broadening a catalog of substrates, membranes, heat spreaders, and custom fabrication. It should determine whether one Kara-grown part survives qualification while delivering a device-level discontinuity. More than a dozen firms are pursuing single-crystal diamond wafers; Diamond Foundry has raised $315 million, and Kara discloses neither customer qualification nor production capacity. Without a decisive experiment, vertical integration can become expensive activity rather than risk removal.

The terminal problem is economic gravity. Kara provides no bottom-up path from reactor throughput, growth time, processing yield, and utilization to a price competitive with SiC, GaN, copper, or another incumbent solution. The giant-value-pool exception is not earned because the dossier quantifies a small $10.2 million substrate segment and does not show what larger semiconductor or cooling cost pool Kara can capture. Nor does the all-in-founder exception override the missing evidence: the team’s credentials are relevant, but no founder action demonstrates that this mission has become non-optional.

My 36-month base case is that Kara becomes a $1–3 million annual-revenue custom diamond-materials vendor serving research, RF, and specialized thermal applications rather than qualifying a wafer-scale computing platform. The strongest objection is that proprietary reactor recipes and customer-specific characterization could compound quietly under NDA, making today’s missing public evidence misleading. That possibility keeps this from being a strong pass, but I do not buy options merely because the evidence might be hidden. I pass today. One verifiable condition flips me: a named customer qualification demonstrating at least 5x higher device power density at the same junction temperature using Kara-grown material under contracted production pricing.
