# Enact -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Enact sits near a consequential bottleneck: robots that fail unpredictably in the physical world require expensive observation, judgment, resets, and new demonstrations. If an automated post-training loop could turn those failures into reliable improvements, it could accelerate deployment across manufacturing and robotics. But the dossier quantifies a $3.2 billion training-platform market, not the underlying labor or capital cost Enact could collapse. There is no affected-worker count, robot-utilization baseline, or credible path to a 10x consequence. A growing market is not a societal multiplier.

The product description is a loop—identify failures, generate recovery data, retrain, and evaluate—but integration is not yet a technical discontinuity. Enact publishes no improvement in policy success, evaluation accuracy, human-reset hours, dataset efficiency, or cross-embodiment transfer. Instance has tested success detection on more than 10,000 held-out episodes across seven robot platforms; Axis already combines distributed collection with human-gated post-training; NVIDIA and Runway can absorb adjacent capabilities into broader platforms. Against those named alternatives, Enact has neither a 5x benchmark nor a disclosed proprietary mechanism. Today this looks more like assembling the workflow than changing its physics.

The largest kill risk is measurable: task-specific real-world data may remain too expensive to collect, while platform vendors commoditize evaluation. Yet Enact has not named the cheapest experiment, threshold, cost, or stop condition that would resolve that risk. Capital should first prove that failure-targeted data produces materially more policy improvement per collected episode than ordinary demonstration gathering. A website and an August 9, 2026 YC directory listing establish existence, not risk removal; there is no disclosed customer, repeat usage, revenue, or technical milestone.

The bull case is real and uncomfortable. Gemini Robotics and NVIDIA Cosmos are expanding the population of robot-model developers, and every deployed policy will encounter edge cases absent from pre-training. An integrated improvement loop could accumulate proprietary mappings among failures, recoveries, and subsequent policy outcomes. That normal-use learning loop would be more defensible than annotation labor. But the dossier labels this moat as inferred, while well-funded competitors already occupy each component. The known exceptions do not rescue Enact: there is no documented instigator, founder record, giant customer commitment, or evolving platform wedge comparable to the evidence required to override missing technical discontinuity.

The two founders may possess exceptional robotics depth, but no biography, prior belief revision, complementary recruiting, or co-founder history is publicly available. I do not penalize a Summer 2026 team for lacking press; I do refuse to invent learning rate. Economics are equally unformed. A “Request data” motion suggests enterprise projects, but there is no price, collection cost, gross-margin structure, automation rate, or route from services-heavy recovery data to software-like economics. Expensive real-world collection could make the product useful and still prevent it from becoming a large company.

I pass today. My verifiable flip condition is a controlled, customer-reproduced benchmark showing that Enact’s loop reduces human evaluation and reset hours by at least 5x at equal or better policy-success improvement. Without that result, I expect the company within 36 months either to pivot into project-based robotics data services or disappear beneath model-platform and better-capitalized data suppliers. The downside is one check, but the uncapped consequence has not yet been connected to a non-commodity mechanism.
