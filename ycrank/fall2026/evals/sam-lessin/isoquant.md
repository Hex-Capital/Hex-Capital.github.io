# isoquant -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Isoquant has demonstrated demand for cheaper inference. It has not demonstrated what my money would buy. Proposed post-money valuation, competing term sheets, and whether this is the first scarce check are all undisclosed. That does not establish an auction. It leaves my price gate unresolved. Likewise, commercializing optimizations developed for Agent FM explains where the product came from; it does not identify the hypothesis this round funds, its deadline, or the result that would materially change the business’s value.

The founders are the strongest part of this dossier. Deshpande’s model infrastructure work and Patankar’s developer tooling and distributed serving experience connect the supplier’s problem with the developer’s problem. More importantly, they shipped Agent FM to 60-plus paying users before turning its internal optimizations into infrastructure. Those customers belong to the prior application, so I will not count them as inference revenue. They do establish operating experience on both sides of the bridge. This fits my pattern of backing people who can translate between systems because they have actually operated inside them.

The strongest argument against passing is that isoquant may already have found a much larger business hiding inside that application. The CEO reports 1.2 trillion tokens served in a month and more than 1,000 customers; a named user reports roughly twice-as-fast agent inference after switching. The banking experiment reports 91.8% accuracy against a 74.7% baseline and a 91.7% estimated inference-cost reduction. Together, those are meaningful evidence of engineering capability and customer interest. But the banking result is a company-published experiment, and the customer count has no paying breakdown. Neither establishes an owned channel, exclusive workload data, or embedded access that reliably causes purchases. Customer training consent also has to be obtained; serving the traffic does not automatically create a proprietary data asset.

The economic question is whether isoquant keeps any of the value it creates. At listed prices of $0.07 per million input tokens and $0.15 per million output tokens, token volume cannot substitute for GPU bills, utilization, support costs, and collected cash. No gross margin, burn, or funded route to break-even is supplied. I therefore use the missing-economics default rather than assume either profitability or losses. Together, Fireworks, and Baseten already sell overlapping capabilities, while compatible APIs make switching easier. Faster inference can win a customer and still become a benefit competed away through price.

This is infrastructure engineering rather than a generic application wrapper, so I would not mechanically apply that avoidance rule. The relevant red flag is dependence on a performance-and-cost advantage competitors can pursue with the same underlying inputs. My known exceptions permit overriding missing financing evidence when conversion, retention, cash economics, or unusually hard external validation carry the underwriting. Isoquant’s reported usage and technical results make that possibility credible, but they do not yet supply those receipts. Nor does the dossier establish compulsory serial financing, a rushed auction, or dysfunctional founder governance; those remain unknown rather than charges against the team.

I pass at **32/100**. The team has shipped; the scarce asset and capital-funded experiment remain unproven. My single verifiable flip condition is a 90-day current-product cohort whose repeat paid usage produces enough contribution profit, after GPU and direct servicing costs, to cover the company’s fixed cash burn. That would demonstrate a functioning base business capable of financing its own optimization work—and provide a concrete reason to override the missing round-plan evidence.
