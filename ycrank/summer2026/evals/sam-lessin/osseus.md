# Osseus -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I first write down three numbers: proposed post-money, competing term sheets, and checks already committed. Osseus discloses none of them. That does not prove a YC auction, but it gives me no evidence that my money would purchase a cheap, non-consensus belief rather than another fashionable robotics-and-AI round. More importantly, the dossier never says what this round will prove. “Connect CAD, ECAD, GitHub, Jira, documents, messages, and tests” is a product program containing several hard problems, not one finite experiment with a metric and deadline.

The valuable version of Osseus is a translation layer between engineering systems that currently mistrust one another. A permissioned dependency record could tell a robotics team that an electronics change invalidated a mechanical assumption, a requirement, and three tests. That is real workflow leverage, especially where mistakes escape into physical hardware. But today this is a claimed architecture rather than an owned dark pool: there is no disclosed accumulated engineering history, repeat usage, completed test rerun, or customer action caused by the graph. The 418 LinkedIn followers and “onboarding R&D teams now” announcement do not establish conversion.

The bull case is that pre-seed evidence often looks exactly this thin before an embedded workflow begins compounding. Mariathasan has built embedded medical-device projects, conducted cancer-computer-vision research, and previously founded Regulos; Patel built a self-driving robot and worked on an agentic QA system during two Apple internships. Together they span hardware, ML, and software testing unusually well for a two-person team. If one R&D customer is already allowing Osseus to ingest confidential CAD, code, requirements, and test data, that permission itself may be stronger evidence than public revenue.

The objection still bites because neither founder has documented operating receipts from deploying a cross-domain robotics system inside an enterprise engineering organization. Prototype robotics and Apple QA experience are useful, but Osseus must also understand requirements ownership, supplier constraints, security review, and the political reality of changing systems of record. Its reviewable-draft model means the product initially adds an approval layer before it can remove work. Altium’s acquisition of Valispace also shows that incumbents can bundle adjacent dependency analysis through existing distribution.

The business model compounds the ambiguity. Builder, Team, Enterprise, and marketplace commissions imply both workflow software and a two-sided component market, yet there is no disclosed customer count, transaction, price, gross margin, burn, or route to break-even. That is too many possible progress metrics. Delete the next financing and I cannot identify the controllable cash driver. I also cannot tell which co-founder has final authority over product, hiring, and financing—an unresolved governance risk, not proof of dysfunction.

The known-exception case would require hard external evidence strong enough to override the missing price and capital plan: paid conversion, retained deployment, a rejected acquisition offer, or proprietary access that competitors cannot reproduce. Osseus has none disclosed. My verifiable flip condition is one named robotics customer paying for a deployment in which the dependency graph catches a cross-domain change, triggers the correct linked test, and remains in weekly use for 90 days. Until then, I expect the broad “intelligent development platform” to narrow toward requirements traceability or another adjacent workflow. I pass.
