# SubVysion -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: contractors now have portable sensing, geospatial processing, and phone-based AR, but SubVysion becomes large only if it can produce materially more reliable utility maps than 811 at repeatable, product-like margins. Accuracy is the controlling variable because a compelling visualization has little value if crews still cannot trust it before excavation. The dossier identifies centimeter accuracy and “100% visibility” as the proposition, yet supplies no detection threshold, independent study, or certification. FHWA’s evidence that moisture, clay, and small-diameter lines degrade GPR is not generic execution risk; it attacks the mechanism that is supposed to make the product indispensable.

The strongest evidence is that this is already more than an AR demo. The rover collects multisensor data, aligns and validates it, and turns it into a georeferenced 3D map used by crews in the field. That is an owned workflow from capture through consumption, and stronger processing models could improve the system rather than simply absorb its interface. Maxwell Stone’s work on inference deployed in more than 10,000 pharmacies and railyard mapping is relevant technical preparation. Wenson Tang’s LiDAR search-and-rescue work and emergency-dispatch experience reinforce the team’s ability to build safety-critical field systems.

There is also legitimate, if still thin, product pull. The founders acquired their first customer and investor through cold calling, report contracts, and say the product is live on construction sites across the United States. At pre-seed, persuading contractors to admit new hardware and an unproven mapping system onto active sites is more informative than a waitlist. But the evidence does not show paid revenue, repeat deployments, usage expansion, or retention. “Contracts” could mean anything from one successful survey to the beginning of a recurring operating system.

The difficult issue is physical economics. The inferred catalyst—better GPR, positioning, processing, and mobile AR—does not yet quantify a cost, throughput, or margin discontinuity. Each project still appears to require rover deployment, collection, alignment, quality control, and validation. That can produce a valuable surveying service without producing a large technology company. The published U.S. market estimate of $294.4 million is modest, and heavily funded competitors already occupy adjacent positions: Exodigo reports more than $200 million raised, 86.1 million square feet mapped, and over 100 customers; 4M owns a records-based planning layer; RodRadar embeds detection directly into excavation equipment.

The bull case is that SubVysion’s early field adoption and integrated workflow let it accumulate corrected subsurface data, improve detection, and distribute recurring maps across subsequent construction activity. That would turn an initial mapping job into a reusable channel for site intelligence. The dossier does not yet establish that loop, however. No dataset scale, repeat-use rate, switching cost, or structural barrier is documented, and a well-funded competitor could reproduce the proposed data moat by operating the same scanning workflow. This resembles the unstated pattern of entering a physical category when software changes its economics, but the necessary economic change is still missing.

I would pass today. No known exception applies: this is not a uniquely scarce research team pursuing a newly opened technical frontier, nor is there evidence that product economics reconstruct an incumbent cost structure. The verifiable flip condition is three contractors each purchasing SubVysion on at least five separate sites at greater than 60% gross margin while an independent field benchmark demonstrates greater than 95% utility-detection recall across wet or clay-heavy conditions. That result would validate accuracy, repeat demand, and scalable economics in one cohort.
