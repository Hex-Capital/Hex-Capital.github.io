# Ploid -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Ploid’s two-billion-person index tells me how much information it claims to organize. It does not tell me what scarce asset I would own by writing a check. The dossier supplies no post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or round-funded experiment. YC and Founders Inc backing establish neither cheap entry nor an auction. I use the missing-evidence defaults for both financing dimensions. I’m passing at the evidence available; I have no basis to underwrite the deal.

The interesting possibility is that cross-site identity resolution becomes a proprietary correction layer: agents make mistakes, customers correct them, and Ploid accumulates knowledge that improves the next paid lookup. That could fit my preference for businesses connecting fragmented systems. But the dossier labels that feedback loop as inferred. Ploid also acknowledges third-party contact suppliers whose sourcing and refresh practices it does not control. Who owns the useful information after everyone can access the same underlying records? Monid’s October 20 announcement places Ploid alongside Apollo, People Data Labs, and other vendors behind one connection. That arrangement could deliver customers while leaving Monid owning the relationship and choosing which supplier gets the next query.

The strongest objection to my pass is that Hernandez may already have built substantially better retrieval. Ploid reports 81% accuracy over 1,000 queries against Exa’s 63% and Apollo’s 23%, using Gemini 3.1 Pro as grader. Its documented API, CLI, MCP interface, and field-level provenance give developers something concrete to use. If those accuracy differences survive customer workloads, interchangeable inputs could still produce a differentiated product. That is a serious bull case. Yet the company-published benchmark measures retrieval performance, while “thousands” of companies and the September 4 Monid integration do not establish paid conversion or repeat purchasing caused by proprietary data. My exceptions for AI businesses and expensive rounds require harder operating evidence—conversion, retention, cash economics, or external purchase demand—than Ploid currently discloses.

The posted $200 and $600 monthly plans make the proposed transaction understandable. They do not establish realized revenue or what remains after data purchases, retrieval, inference, and verification. Without margins, burn, or a cash plan, I cannot determine whether this seed round can produce a self-funding business. I also cannot call it a financing factory: no public use-of-funds plan establishes that adverse conclusion. The disclosed product suggests a test worth running—whether customers repeatedly pay for correctly resolved identities at a positive contribution margin—but that is my proposed experiment, not evidence of what Ploid’s round will fund.

Hernandez’s work building recommendation systems and embedding pipelines at Wander earns genuine execution credit. The team has also shipped the current people-search product. The missing half of the bridge is demonstrated operation inside the sales or recruiting workflow whose budget they want; Karic’s research background does not supply that receipt. Naming a CEO gives me no reason to invent an equal-founder governance problem, and the dossier shows no pressure-selling of the round. The concern that does apply is distribution and data advantage remaining contestable as incumbents offer agent access. My one verifiable condition for reopening this pass is a 90-day paid customer cohort showing repeat purchases driven by Ploid’s identity-resolution advantage, with positive contribution margin after data, inference, and verification costs. That would establish an economic asset beneath the agent-era positioning.
