# Archangel Health -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a physician delegating an EHR task and checking the completed work instead of supervising every click. Getting there requires agents that execute reliably, tests that expose dangerous failures, and evidence that passing those tests transfers into practice. Archangel Health is pointed at the middle of that chain. The distinction between knowing the correct medical answer and actually performing the correct action is concrete. Stanford’s reported 46% single-attempt completion on harder PhysicianBench tasks makes the gap visible. But that is evidence of the problem; Archangel still needs to establish that its environments help close it.

The sandboxed EHR offering is what catches my attention. Physician grading, adversarial cases, and interactive environments could become the machinery underneath several medical-agent products. That fits my preference for finding the enabling layer beneath the visible application. Yet the product description comes through an indexed page, and the claimed physician network and provenance system do not establish a scarce asset under Archangel’s control. There is a meaningful difference between supplying useful evaluation work and owning something developers cannot readily replace.

Why does the customer need this particular supplier? That question changes the investment for me. Centaur and iMerit already supply expert feedback, while MedAgentBench makes some infrastructure publicly available. Archangel could win by catching consequential execution failures those alternatives miss. The dossier does not yet demonstrate that advantage. Nor does it identify a specific institutional rent or restriction that Archangel removes to return control to a clinician or patient. The advertised $25–60+ per evaluated case tells me something about contributor compensation, but nothing about customer pricing or the economics of escaping an incumbent.

The founders have more substance than a fashionable category description. Tej Patel’s SimCare and Cedars-Sinai experience supplies relevant clinical-agent context; Aryaa Bhatia’s technical background and reported $70,000 annual revenue at Origins Customs suggest some experience turning effort into a business. Those matter. What I cannot reconstruct is the sequence of independent experiments that made this particular problem unavoidable for them. The April 2026 surgery-center description followed by October’s infrastructure positioning establishes a change in direction, not opportunism. It also does not establish years of obsession with clinical evaluation.

The strongest argument against waiting is that Archangel reports frontier-lab and medical-AI users already using its infrastructure. A small team might be discovering the difficult cases directly inside customer work, accumulating a valuable evaluation library before outsiders can see it. I can back a company before revenue when a working artifact and unusually deep founder evidence carry the conviction. Here, usage lacks dates, repeated behaviors, and identifiable outcomes, so that exception is not established. Consent, de-identification, and provenance claims are encouraging responses to my privacy gate, although their implementation remains unverified. Two founders alone provide no evidence of governance trouble; dependence on one closed model supplier is likewise not established.

I would hold my personal check today: **Neutral**. Archangel Health has identified a layer I want to exist, but the evidence currently supports a potentially useful supplier more strongly than an indispensable one. My flip condition is one referenceable paid customer renewal attributable to Archangel’s proprietary EHR evaluations catching physician-verified execution failures missed by the customer’s existing benchmark stack. That would turn the most interesting possibility here into observed customer behavior.
