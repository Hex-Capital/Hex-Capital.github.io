# Mosaic -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Mosaic’s contested truth is that coding agents will not remain private tools; the valuable unit will be a shared, persistent workspace in which several humans and agents can inherit one another’s decisions. What would prove this wrong? Teams would continue using isolated sessions despite the cost of reconstructing context. The multiplayer Claude Code demonstration proves Mosaic has built the interaction, and its contrast with Conductor’s and Superset’s isolated workspaces makes the proposition concrete. But it has not reported repeat usage, faster handoffs, or paid adoption. This is a testable secret, not yet a tested one.

The harder problem is value capture. Mosaic names engineering teams broadly rather than a small market it can dominate. Conductor, Superset, and Pentagon already cover adjacent orchestration, persistence, or multiplayer functions; Pentagon even advertises persistent memory and human multiplayer. The dossier identifies no proprietary technology, exclusive data, network effect, or structural barrier. Organizational histories and permissions could eventually create switching costs, but a possible future moat is not a present monopoly mechanism. Here competition is not incidental—it is already converging on the product’s defining feature.

The strongest bull case is that the collaboration layer becomes the system of record above interchangeable agents. Mosaic works across Claude Code, Codex, and OpenCode, while Rohani brings distributed-inference experience from Tenstorrent and research experience at NVIDIA, Vector Institute, and Deep Genomics. Patil has built developer-collaboration interfaces. A free client feeding a $200-per-seat Team product could turn agent neutrality into both distribution and leverage over model vendors. The July 2026 coverage shows that the demonstration was legible enough to attract outside attention. If shared histories compound across employees, the apparent feature could become infrastructure.

But the dossier contains no evidence that this compounding has begun. There are no customers, retention measurements, conversions, or security architecture despite shared-terminal access and documented prompt-exfiltration risk. The inferred developer-led funnel names neither the buyer nor the sales cycle, and two articles are attention rather than distribution. The $200 price supplies a coherent revenue engine—twenty seats would yield $48,000 annually per team—but there is no gross-margin evidence, customer expansion, or arithmetic supporting a fund-returning outcome.

The foundation is also unresolved. Both founders have relevant technical backgrounds, but Mosaic discloses neither their prior working history nor full-time status, ownership, salaries, or whether all core engineering is internal. I do not infer a defective founding structure from silence; I also cannot infer the long alignment required to survive a fast-converging market. The current evidence therefore supports the persona’s missing-evidence default, not affirmative founder credit.

My 36-month expectation is that Mosaic pivots from a multiplayer terminal into an enterprise agent-permissions and audit-control layer and remains below $3 million ARR, because security and governance are more durable problems than shared terminal control. The painful opposing case is that Mosaic has already found the human interface every autonomous-agent company will need, and its cross-agent position lets it accumulate the context competitors cannot recreate. A genuine zero-to-one usage event could override today’s missing monopoly. The verifiable flip condition is ten independent teams each paying at least $2,000 monthly and expanding seats after six months because retained shared context makes switching measurably costly. Until then, this is a timely product in a fashionable market: useful, perhaps, but commodity rather than last mover. I pass.
