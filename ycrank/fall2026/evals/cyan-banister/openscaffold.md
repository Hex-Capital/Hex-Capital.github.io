# OpenScaffold -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, a deployment engineer could spend the morning deciding what a customer needs and the afternoon watching software implement it. OpenScaffold’s deployment-specific scaffold points toward that world. But I have to walk backward: customer intent must become testable requirements, those requirements must become reliable actions, and deployment results must improve the next implementation. The dossier describes generating and operating the scaffold; it does not establish those transitions or show a small working artifact that makes this future feel inevitable. I can imagine the destination. I cannot yet see it leaking into the present.

The founders give me more to work with. Rushil Knagaram was employee number three at USUL, where Param Mehta was a founding engineer; Mehta’s biography also describes leading an AI platform for transaction-error handling at JPMorgan Chase. That is relevant operating experience, especially for a product that must survive messy customer environments. It earns some founder credit beyond a list of degrees. It does not establish a long-running deployment obsession, independently built experiments, or an operating wound that became this product’s specification. The October 26, 2026 directory change from Sigma’s “Lakehouse-as-a-Service” positioning establishes a public repositioning, not when the team pivoted or why.

Why does deployment require so much human translation in the first place? OpenScaffold could have an interesting answer. But the dossier’s meetings-and-spreadsheets problem is inferred from another company’s description. There is no identified fee, contractual restriction, quantified delay, or gatekeeper whose power this product removes. I cannot tell whether an individual gains control or an enterprise simply purchases another coordination tool. Nor is the proposed deployment-history advantage an asset yet: data rights, accumulated implementations, and measured reuse are undisclosed. My pattern of backing concealed infrastructure makes me curious here; it does not turn a possible knowledge layer into a controlled bottleneck.

The strongest argument against passing is that these two founders have already worked together inside an early startup, and Mehta has relevant automation experience. They might recognize deployment complexity that the public description barely captures. I do make pre-revenue exceptions: **CosmicBrain AI** paired its manufacturing ambition with a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a founder’s longstanding robotics record. OpenScaffold shares the ambition to make machines execute useful work, but the dossier supplies no comparable prototype interaction or completed customer action. Missing revenue is ordinary at this stage. Missing behavioral evidence receives my default, rather than an invented failure. Auctor, Tandem, and June already describe adjacent implementation capabilities, so the scaffold’s distinctive contribution still needs to become observable.

I pass with my own money today. The dossier establishes neither a severe privacy violation nor confused founder control; Knagaram and Mehta have identified CEO and CTO roles, although those titles do not settle governance. Customer-context custody remains an unanswered architectural question, not evidence of misconduct. My verifiable flip condition is a permissioned customer demonstration in which knowledge from one completed deployment measurably reduces human intervention in a second, with execution logs showing what the scaffold actually did. That would give OpenScaffold something much more persuasive than “superagent”: an observable piece of the future it wants to build.
