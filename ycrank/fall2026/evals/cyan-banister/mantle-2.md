# Mantle -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, a small machine builder should be able to ship equipment across the country without building a repair organization in every city. Getting there requires serviceable machines, local technicians with parts, and someone willing to own the consequences when equipment stops working. Mantle proposes to take that last job. There is a small leak from this future already: IFR reported 31% growth in the surveyed robot-as-a-service fleet in 2025. Rental economics put ongoing reliability back on the supplier. That creates a reason to outsource maintenance, although it does not establish that Mantle will become the organization suppliers trust.

The founders have already paid some expensive tuition. Kieran White built Curo, whose YC biography reports 5,000 charging stations before acquisition; Graham Smith previously operated an electronics-repair business and co-founded Babylon Micro-Farms, reporting more than 450 deployed units. Those are prior-company achievements, not Mantle customers. Their importance is biographical: both founders encountered distributed-equipment maintenance before starting this business. White specifically describes missed uptime guarantees affecting contracts with Uber, Zoox and Google. This looks like an operating wound becoming a product specification, which matters much more to me than their accelerator or follower counts. Evidence of recruiting complementary field talent remains absent.

Why does every equipment builder need to learn the same repair lessons? Mantle could return engineering time and deployment freedom to manufacturers that currently carry their own support burden. But the dossier identifies an operational failure, without establishing an artificial gatekeeper or quantifying the money it extracts. Maintenance is necessary work. Someone still drives to the machine, diagnoses it and finds the replacement part. Assuming uptime responsibility can free the customer while leaving Mantle holding an obligation it has priced badly. Installation fees and maintenance contracts are only inferred here; actual pricing and contractual remedies are undisclosed.

The strongest case for writing a check is that these founders understand those obligations precisely because they have suffered them. The physical-AI wave will need people who answer the phone when something breaks. My pre-revenue investment in **CosmicBrain AI** illustrates why missing revenue alone would not stop me: deep founder obsession supported a manufacturing-enablement thesis. The material difference is that it also had a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept. Mantle’s dossier supplies announcements, without a completed installation, repair or repeat customer action. I give the missing behavior its pre-seed default rather than treating it as failure.

The concealed-infrastructure pattern is attractive, but Mantle has not demonstrated ownership of the scarce part. Roboworx already advertises nationwide, white-label robot installation and maintenance. A repair dataset or dense technician network could eventually improve Mantle’s economics; both remain hypotheses. Its direct service scope establishes an enabling role, but no controlled network, diagnostic asset or parts advantage yet distinguishes it. Nor does the dossier establish an affirmative governance, privacy or leadership red flag. Naming a CEO provides accountability on paper; founder voting arrangements remain unknown. The concern that changes my decision is more concrete: experienced operators could build a useful service business while every additional uptime promise adds roughly proportional labor and liability.

I am **Neutral at 56/100**. Mantle’s founders make me want to investigate; the evidence does not yet make me want to commit my own money. My flip condition is one auditable six-month service cohort in which two unaffiliated equipment manufacturers renew after Mantle meets contracted uptime and earns positive contribution after technician travel, parts and repeat callouts. That would show whether these founders have converted their operating scars into a repeatable advantage.
