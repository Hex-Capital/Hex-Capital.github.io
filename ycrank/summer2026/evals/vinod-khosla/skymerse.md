# Skymerse -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence case is real but not yet enormous. Skymerse claims three hours of planning work per flight can be compressed by continuously reconciling NOTAMs, weather, aircraft limits, and operator procedures. That attacks expensive expert labor and potentially reduces operational mistakes. But the dossier quantifies only a $1.03 billion flight-operations-software market, not the number of dispatcher-hours, delay dollars, or safety events that disappear. This could become useful infrastructure; the evidence does not yet show a 100x societal consequence.

The strongest evidence is technical and operational. Skymerse has processed 3 million NOTAMs into more than 2 million structured affected-element exposures rather than merely presenting text or graphical overlays. LOT Polish Airlines uses the system across its network, with roughly 30-second processing, delivery within three minutes, and 15–20 filtered alerts per day. Those are production facts. They prove more than an interface wrapper, but they do not prove a 5–10x discontinuity: there is no controlled comparison against Jeppesen, NAVBLUE, ForeFlight, or human dispatchers on planning time, missed restrictions, false positives, or operational outcomes.

The bull case stings. Damian Szumski is a licensed dispatcher who has worked inside LOT, Lufthansa Systems, FLYR, and Flightradar24. Notamify already has 3,800 registered users and thousands of daily users, unusually informative traction at pre-seed. The movement from transcription to structured NOTAM intelligence and then broader flight operations may demonstrate tactical flexibility. My unstated willingness to back execution-heavy software and regulated workflows therefore applies. The known-exception override—an adaptive tiny team entering a large existing value pool—partially fits, but the dossier provides no documented belief revision, complementary recruiting, or evidence explaining why the earlier co-founder is no longer listed.

The largest kill risk remains untouched by a falsifiable milestone. Notamify explicitly disclaims being an authoritative briefing source, while the end-state product proposes operational actions from inputs that may be incomplete or stale. EASA’s AI requirements are still evolving. LOT validates alert filtering, not safe autonomous flight planning. There is no prospective test specifying an acceptable false-negative rate, comparison with approved briefings, certification pathway, cost, or stop condition. Meanwhile Jeppesen ForeFlight’s Airflow can combine aviation data, segregated customer data, and certification expertise through an incumbent distribution system. Overwatch AI already claims similar integration across weather, NOTAMs, MELs, manuals, and flight plans.

The economics are plausible but mostly unmeasured. A $24.90 monthly product can obviously underprice three hours of professional labor, and software delivery does not require permanent subsidy. Yet consumer pricing says little about enterprise integration, liability, support, data licensing, or certification costs. Skymerse has not shown a bottom-up route from today’s monitoring product to an authoritative operational system that remains cheaper than incumbent software and dispatcher labor. My 36-month base case is therefore a useful aviation-monitoring vendor at $1–3 million ARR, not an autonomous flight-operations platform.

I pass today. The company has built a credible domain-specific data product, but the option remains bounded by incremental software economics while safety authority and technical superiority are unproven. One condition flips the decision: an independently audited trial across at least 100,000 airline flights showing a minimum 5x reduction in dispatcher planning time, no safety-critical misses versus approved briefings, and a paid network-wide rollout. That would convert the structured-data asset from promising implementation into a defensible operational discontinuity.
