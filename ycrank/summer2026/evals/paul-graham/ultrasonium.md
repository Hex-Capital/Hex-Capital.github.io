# Ultrasonium -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what broke. Ultrasonium has four unusually technical founders, a $305,000 NSF award, and a patent issued in January 2025. But the dossier never names an acoustic-control, thermal, or metallurgical obstacle that stopped the machine, what the founders improvised, or how quickly the workaround changed a result. Credentials are not resourcefulness. A 17-person company advertising fourteen more roles makes this omission more serious: I cannot tell whether the founders are still bending the apparatus themselves or trying to hire their way through unsolved research.

There is nevertheless a real technical idea here. A hemispherical phased array would confine and shape molten metal without powder or high-powered lasers. The issued patent makes the approach more than a diagram, and the reported 15x speed and 75% cost advantages imply some operating artifact. But those figures lack test methods, geometries, comparison systems, production volumes, and independent measurements. The NSF documents still describe submillimeter accuracy, density above 99%, and aluminum and copper trials as objectives. This is provisional proof, not a crossed threshold.

The founders are plausible people to cross it. Carter worked on additively manufactured steel at Argonne and NASA-funded melt-pool simulations; Qiu built acoustic and quantum-control hardware; Brown worked on ITER and CERN; Urbanski invented a nuclear thermoelectric generator and joined manufacturing-monitoring company Phase3D early. Their backgrounds also explain why they might notice an ugly opportunity others avoid. Yet the dossier does not say that one of them personally needed a part conventional processes could not make, or that an actual manufacturing failure led to Ultrasonium. Domain adjacency is weaker than a founder-originated need.

Who wants the crude machine now? “Small machinist shops” is a market segment, not an answer. No shop is named, and there is no disclosed paid job, repeated prototype use, letter of intent, qualified part, or customer rejection that caused a product change. TigerLaunch, LinkedIn followers, investors, and an NSF grant may help finance the experiment, but they do not pull the product back. The later list—aerospace, defense, energy, compute, research, dental, and medical devices—adds incompatible qualification and sales workflows before Ultrasonium has demonstrated one narrow well.

The strongest bull case is that this is exactly the kind of industrial problem where public demand appears after technical validation, not before it. The team has relevant laboratory experience, government funding, patent protection, and competitors that have raised as much as $575 million, proving that valuable production capacity can justify heavy capital. If Ultrasonium really has a machine producing dense parts 15 times faster at 75% lower cost, the absent customer data would matter much less. But the dossier does not yet supply the measured artifact required for that exception. It supplies claims about one.

I would pass today. The operational gate fails on the first question: there is no documented blocker-to-workaround sequence. It also fails to replace that evidence with independently validated technical performance or urgent user pull. The hiring pace and diffuse customer list increase the risk that the company is scaling an organization before discovering which machine and which customer matter. One result would flip me: an identified machinist shop repeatedly paying for parts from the current system, accompanied by third-party measurements showing greater than 99% density and a documented production failure the founders corrected themselves within weeks.
