# Lyon -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Training on 28 billion transactions means Lyon has apparently persuaded someone to let it near a consequential system. I want to know how. What stopped Gabriel Noya and Markus Höhn from getting that first model trained, and what did they do about it? The dossier gives the result without that sequence. Noya’s NVIDIA and finance affiliations make the accomplishment plausible, but they cannot establish resourcefulness. I would keep the founder-execution score at its missing-evidence default.

The technical result deserves attention. Lyon reports four times greater precision at identifying premium-card converters than the customer’s existing rules ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/lyon)). That is a specific task with a measurable outcome. But precision depends on the evaluation setup: selecting ten obvious converters is different from improving precision at the same recall across a useful population. Without methodology or customer confirmation, I give this substantial provisional credit. The 28 billion transactions establish the reported scale of training; they do not independently establish predictive quality.

The strongest case for investing is that an unnamed fintech serving tens of millions of active users has supplied data, obtained a result, and is proceeding toward credit deployment. Perhaps Lyon has already found its narrow well: financial institutions whose transaction histories contain useful patterns their rules cannot capture. The proposed second application suggests interest beyond one experiment. It does not yet demonstrate repeated production use, payment, or customer dependence. The insurer engagement adds another conversation, with no disclosed outcome. Revenue being undisclosed is ordinary at this stage; the distinction that matters is between a successful evaluation and something users insist on keeping.

Customer-cloud deployment puts Lyon near work that competitors might dislike: sensitive data, integration, security reviews, and operational responsibility. Its Type 1 compliance is concrete progress, while Type 2 remains in progress. Yet the dossier does not connect the founders’ prior work to a particular encounter with this problem, or document a rejected hypothesis followed by a product change. Those dimensions retain their defaults. Against SAP-owned Prior Labs, the interesting question is whether Lyon’s event models solve a customer problem sufficiently better to justify installing another supplier. Customer-specific models could become embedded infrastructure, or remain expensive individual projects. Customer ownership alone does not settle which.

Lyon fits my unstated preference for technical teams producing numerical proof before fundraising rhetoric. Its evidence is weaker on the other part of that pattern: proof someone outside the company can inspect. I see no affirmative evidence of partner-dependent distribution, premature delegation, or a hastily assembled cofounder relationship; an undisclosed relationship history does not establish that red flag. I have also checked the exceptions: a compelling working prototype can outweigh relationship uncertainty, and validated technical results can outweigh unconventional credentials. Lyon’s reported benchmark does not yet supply enough independent evidence to override the missing founder-execution gate. I would pass today. One verifiable event could change that: a customer-confirmed account of a specific deployment blocker, the founders’ shipped workaround within weeks, and sustained production use afterward.
