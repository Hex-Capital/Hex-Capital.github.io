# Computable -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

If Computable works, GPU commitments become transferable instead of expiring as stranded capacity. That could compress the reported 2x H100 price dispersion and improve allocation across an $8.21 billion market growing toward $26.62 billion. But the dossier never converts this efficiency gain into an order-of-magnitude consequence: no utilization baseline, stranded-capacity estimate, customer cost reduction, or mechanism for making compute 10x cheaper or more abundant. This is a useful market, not yet a world-changing option.

The product is market design rather than technical discontinuity. Weekly reservations, resting orders, redemption, and resale are coherent features, but SF Compute already offers buy orders, sell orders, and resale while managing more than $100 million of third-party hardware. Runpod and Vast.ai further demonstrate abundant supply-side competition. Computable provides no benchmark showing that its calendar-week grid improves price, utilization, reliability, or liquidity by 5x. A different contract interval is not a Black Swan.

Liquidity is the existential risk, and the evidence does not show that the founders are spending dollars against it. Listings from August 2026 through January 2027 at $2.56–$2.69 per GPU-hour prove inventory presentation, not matched demand. The Show HN launch produced 45 points and 26 comments, but there is no trading volume, repeat-counterparty count, redemption record, or bid-ask spread. Worse, the website promises a standing buyback quote while the terms disclaim any repurchase obligation. That ambiguity matters because manufactured liquidity can make an empty market look alive until the balance sheet stops supporting it.

The strongest bull case is the founders’ reported experience building trading infrastructure at Jump Trading and Coinbase. The **Block** exception is relevant: a small infrastructure-oriented team can begin with a narrow mechanism and expand across a large existing value pool. The shared mechanism is market plumbing built by technically credible operators; the material difference is that Computable’s biographies remain unverified and collective, while its initial functionality is already available from a much better-capitalized competitor. There is also no documented belief revision, complementary recruiting, or proprietary technical owner for delivery risk.

The 2% buyer fee plus 2% seller fee could become attractive software economics if liquidity is organic. The dossier supplies no unit model showing how those fees cover provider failures, expired capacity, customer support, or balance-sheet-backed buybacks. My 36-month forecast is that Computable pivots from a standalone weekly exchange into managed GPU procurement and capacity brokerage because customers will value guaranteed delivery more than speculative secondary liquidity.

I pass today. The unstated-pattern case—execution-heavy fintech applied to an expensive infrastructure workflow—is real, and no prohibited-category red flag applies. But the company currently asks me to finance a marketplace feature, not a non-incremental capability. One result would flip the decision: three consecutive months above $10 million in secondary trading notional, with at least 70% generated by unaffiliated repeat counterparties and no Computable-funded buyback support.
