# Pacific -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Pacific’s contested truth is precise: unused grid connections, factory-built compute pods, and dry diamond-wafer cooling can turn stranded power into operating AI capacity faster than conventional data-center construction. The falsifier is equally clear: if a production-scale pod cannot sustain competitive power density and reliability without evaporative water, or cannot reach operation materially faster at a stalled site, there is no secret—only an expensive container. Pacific publishes no thermal capacity, field-test, certification, or comparative deployment result. The proposition is interesting and testable, but today it remains a founder assertion.

The stalled-site beachhead is narrower than the generic AI-infrastructure market, yet Pacific has not shown it can own it. There is no measured inventory of accessible sites, exclusive site pipeline, patent, production learning curve, or deployed fleet. Armada already operates modular units internationally, while Crusoe is pursuing prepackaged data centers with billions in capitalization. Diamond cooling might become proprietary technology; vertical integration of transformers, switchgear, and power distribution might instead multiply precisely the component bottlenecks Pacific promises to escape. The dossier currently shows competition, not monopoly.

The founding evidence is unresolved rather than disqualifying. Harper left university to build the company, and Ralph’s Airbus thermal-engineering experience directly touches the hardest technical problem. But there is no public history of the founders working together, no ownership information, and no confirmation that Ralph’s incoming Cambridge MPhil is compatible with full-time commitment. The earlier £10K MRR digital-twin product shows that the team can ship and sell something; because the company subsequently pivoted to physical compute infrastructure, it does not validate the present secret or establish durable founder-product alignment.

The commercial model is similarly indefinite. Hosted GPU hours, tokens, training capacity, long-term contracts, and managed defense pods are several possible businesses sharing hardware, not yet one demonstrated revenue engine. Pacific publishes no rates, utilization assumptions, gross margins, pod cost, sales cycle, or current-product revenue. “Working with unnamed large aerospace companies” is not a repeatable distribution route. JLL’s forecast that modular-system and micro-data-center sales could rise from $11 billion in 2025 to $48 billion in 2030 establishes a large trend, but a large trend can attract value while competition captures it.

The strongest bull case is that a two-person aerospace team has found a real coordination bottleneck at exactly the moment 57% of data-center projects are experiencing delays. Ralph brings thermal experience, Harper has already produced early revenue in an adjacent product, and Pacific’s terrestrial-first sequence is more definite than beginning in orbit. The relevant exception is Panthalassa: an apparently over-coordinated physical stack can merit investment when an unusual energy frontier meets urgent compute demand. The material difference is that Pacific has disclosed neither an independently validated unit nor a specific factory and deployment path. Its resemblance to that exception is thematic, not evidentiary.

I pass. My 36-month forecast is that Pacific will abandon ownership of hosted GPU capacity and pivot toward supplying cooling or power-distribution modules to established data-center developers, remaining below $3 million ARR. One milestone would flip the decision: a named customer paying for a one-megawatt pod that operates continuously for 90 days at a previously stalled grid-connected site, with independently verified power density and zero evaporative-water use. That would simultaneously turn the secret into proof, the pod into a beachhead, and the sales claim into distribution.
