# ernd -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The possible secret at ernd is that small AI buyers can collectively obtain purchasing terms that general-purpose corporate cards cannot match. That proposition is testable: remove ernd’s own promotional subsidy and see whether customers still concentrate their spending through it. But it is my reconstruction. The dossier names neither a prevailing belief the founders reject nor an experiment establishing that belief is wrong. Pooled purchasing is a business hypothesis; its application to AI has yet to reveal a secret.

The immediate problem is ownership of the advantage. Firecrawl’s doubled usage limits establish that ernd negotiated something concrete. They do not establish exclusivity, proprietary technology, or a benefit that improves automatically as more customers join. Ramp already combines corporate cards, vendor offers, and AI spending controls; Slash supplies another established financial bundle. “Companies that pay for AI” describes potential customers, but it does not yet define a market ernd can dominate. The adverse evidence is competition through benefits and pricing, rather than merely missing moat disclosures.

The strongest argument against passing is the speed of the initial coalition: ernd reports ten startups onboarded and five vendor partnerships within 48 hours. Those figures are informative at pre-seed. Together with an actual Firecrawl offer, they suggest the founders can recruit both sides before building a large financial institution. Partner application pages provide a specific acquisition route, and the credits guides give buyers a reason to discover it. If vendors increasingly pay ernd for access to concentrated purchasing demand, the coalition could become more valuable with scale. Yet onboarding is not recurring card spend, and five agreements do not demonstrate that vendors need this intermediary.

The rewards terms expose the distinction between acquiring attention and capturing value. OpenAI and Anthropic rewards are funded by ernd, neither provider endorses the program, and exhausted budgets can leave new points pending. Redemption restrictions further weaken the equivalence between advertised rewards and unrestricted cashback. These facts do not prove negative unit economics. They do mean that “up to 6% back” cannot establish a durable revenue engine. Retained partner fees, margins, and spending per customer are undisclosed, leaving no supported arithmetic from purchasing volume to a power-law outcome.

The founders offer a better signal than the current market position. Dawson and Wengel are on leave from Penn, while Ruia is a former Penn student; these are concrete indications of commitment. Ruia’s payments experience and the team’s finance and AI backgrounds make the product credible to investigate. Shared attendance, however, does not establish prior work together, and ownership and the organization of core engineering remain unknown. I see no documented part-time-founder or capitalization red flag. My unstated preference for financial infrastructure also favors taking this seriously; the AI label contributes little by itself.

I pass because ernd has demonstrated an ability to assemble offers before demonstrating a mechanism for keeping their value. The known exceptions do not resolve that problem: there is no independently verified technical breakthrough, persistent transaction behavior, or validated deployment path that directly removes the monopoly concern. Ten onboarded startups cannot yet supply that override. One verifiable result would change my decision: a six-month customer cohort sustaining repeat spend at positive contribution margins after ernd-funded promotions end, because of contractually exclusive vendor benefits. That would turn the proposed purchasing coalition into evidence of a business customers and suppliers cannot readily replace.
