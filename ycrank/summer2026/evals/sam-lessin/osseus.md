# Osseus -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Osseus forward-deploys a permission-aware engineering graph into customer clouds, while Anish Mariathasan’s roughly 2,000 LinkedIn followers are the closest thing Osseus has to owned distribution. That pairing is the analytical signature: Osseus is a promising Wizard-of-Oz service-with-software business whose strongest feature is sleeve fit and whose weakest is founder-owned community. “Robotics intelligence platform” sounds factory-model fashionable; custom integrations across CAD, electronics, code, tests, and suppliers sound like Type 2 fun. I like the second Osseus more than the first.

Fin is the high rung because manual operation exposed the workflow before automation became the product. Osseus shares that mechanism through forward-deployed onboarding, custom rollout pricing, and customer-cloud or VPC deployment. That is genuinely better than tossing another AI agent over the wall. Osseus diverges from Fin because the dossier documents no six-month manual operating history, repeat pilot, or paying customer; forward deployment is currently a sales promise, not accumulated workflow knowledge. Osseus therefore sits below the high rung but above generic AI infrastructure on category fit.

Sublime Security’s low-community profile makes enterprise selling dependent on product differentiation and operator-network access rather than founder-owned distribution. Osseus shows the same weakness without Sublime’s demonstrated pre-consensus security position: Anish Mariathasan has approximately 2,000 LinkedIn followers, Ayush Patel has 594, and neither founder has a visible GitHub audience, newsletter, or category-specific community. Those follower counts are résumés, not distribution. Osseus will have to rent every customer conversation through founder-led demos, so the founder-as-brand score stays near the absent-evidence default.

No Osseus round price, financing instrument, competitive process, or investor-pass evidence is public. Applying the value-seed test, silence means the 9/30 default, especially when Osseus sits at the intersection of AI, robotics, infrastructure, and enterprise software—the Summer 2026 consensus bingo card. Over 18 months, that matters because a hot SAFE price forces Osseus to manufacture SaaS growth before its connector economics are understood; a cheap round would instead let Osseus discover which integrations deserve productization. Without terms, I cannot underwrite mispricing merely because the work looks difficult.

Osseus offers custom rollout pricing, managed subscriptions, customer-provided model keys, and VPC deployment, but Osseus still appears to be financing that work through an ordinary software cap table. The counterfactual that moves capital structure into the Invest tier is concrete: implementation fees must fund connector development, deployments must become contribution-margin positive, and three customers must reuse substantially the same integration layer. If every robotics customer requires bespoke CAD, supplier, simulation, and permission connectors, Osseus is not venture-shaped—it is a systems integrator being shamed into SaaS clothing.

Drop.io showed asymmetric conviction by pursuing a file-sharing primitive against free incumbents until the operating insight became undeniable. Osseus has relevant technical exposure—Anish Mariathasan’s biomedical-engineering and computer-vision work, plus Ayush Patel’s Apple agentic-QA internships and self-driving-robot project—but Osseus does not yet show the equivalent missionary artifact. No public repositories, opinionated category writing, or three-year robotics-workflow obsession appears in the dossier. The founders may possess conviction privately, but personal angel underwriting cannot capitalize invisible conviction at full price.

CoLab’s $72 million Series C and Duro’s AI-native PLM relaunch make my 36-month forecast falsifiable: Osseus reaches $1 million–$3 million ARR from fewer than 12 robotics customers, but remains a services-heavy engineering-change layer rather than displacing PLM. Osseus can win narrow deployments because permission-aware evidence and revision context solve real pain; Osseus struggles to compound beyond them because low owned distribution and connector maintenance keep each sale founder-led. That is a sound niche business outcome, not yet a defining venture outcome.

Ro is the strongest objection: capital-intensive deployment can itself become the moat when regulatory and operational complexity keeps lightweight competitors out. Osseus could be the Ro exception for robotics—customer-cloud deployment, mirrored permissions, and a version-aware graph may convert integration schleps into proprietary workflow data. The objection stings because CoLab’s funding validates buyer demand, while Osseus spans software, supplier constraints, and test results more broadly. The dossier still gives the deciding evidence to the bear case: no named pilot, revenue, repeat usage, retention, or structural barrier exists, while CoLab and Duro already overlap the core features. Three named customers expanding after six months, with at least two reusing the same connectors at positive gross margin, would flip my verdict.
