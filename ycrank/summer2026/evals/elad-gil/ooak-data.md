# Ooak Data -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Tool-using models now expose a measurable training gap—under 50% task success and roughly 25% eight-run consistency—frontier labs are buying realistic workflow environments, and Ooak Data becomes a large company if it can convert licensed enterprise operations into reusable RL tasks faster and more profitably than labs or scaled data vendors can reproduce them. That conversion rate is the controlling variable. The dossier identifies its components—data acquisition, anonymization, digital-twin construction, and expert task generation—but provides no cycle time, reuse rate, or contribution-margin threshold. The market thesis is therefore credible but not yet fully specified.

The strongest evidence is customer pull. Seven-figure contracts with the two largest AI labs, plus several smaller-lab partnerships, are unusually informative at pre-seed. These are not waitlist registrations or unpaid pilots; sophisticated buyers are allocating material budgets to an unfinished supplier. The six open roles across engineering, operations, and US go-to-market further suggest a delivery constraint. Ooak has already answered whether someone will buy. It has not yet answered whether each contract creates a scalable asset or another bespoke services obligation.

The technical discontinuity is also real. Agents require multi-step work spanning communications, documents, and software tools, while static text datasets cannot reproduce that environment. Ooak’s licensed operational data and automated anonymization could make previously inaccessible workflows trainable. The missing economic proof is important: source companies may receive up to $300,000, privacy obligations are substantial, and the plan to acquire more than 300 company ecosystems in six months could produce either a compounding library or an expensive data-production operation. Improved models strengthen Ooak only if they increase demand for harder environments faster than they reduce the amount of supervised workflow data required.

The bear case is sharp. Mercor already combines an expert network with hundreds of reconstructed enterprise applications, AfterQuery reports a $100 million annual run rate, and Scale AI can bundle environments into a much broader platform. Ooak’s two principal customers could internalize production, pressure pricing, or consolidate spend with those vendors. The claimed data moat is still forward-looking: the dossier does not estimate what it would cost a well-funded competitor to license comparable workflows, and data provenance may limit reuse across customers. Six simultaneous hires before documenting dataset-level margins also resembles broad scaling before the production system has been proven.

The team provides relevant execution coverage but not a founder override. Thomas Aubry brings ML leadership, autonomous-vehicle startup experience, and financial-data work; Grégoire Lamy spent more than five years moving from product into operations at Epsor; Pierre-Louis Vouteau contributes distribution experience. A year of work and one pivot show persistence and some startup learning, but the dossier documents neither a prior exit nor a costly personal commitment. No known-exception logic is needed: this investment stands on present customer demand and a real market shift, not extraordinary founder credentials excusing absent pull.

I would invest, but with a narrower conviction than the contract headline initially implies. Ooak Data has crossed the market and product-pull gates; the unresolved question is whether it owns reusable infrastructure or merely brokers scarce data through bespoke projects. My verifiable flip condition is that at least one of the two largest labs must renew or expand within 12 months at greater than 60% contribution margin after source-data acquisition and environment-production costs. Meeting that condition would show that the first contracts are becoming a distribution and asset-compounding system. Missing it would move me to pass despite the revenue.
