# RATLOG -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

RATLOG built and hot-fired an engine in four months for under $30,000. That is the strongest reason to take this company seriously. Four people bought physical evidence cheaply in an industry accustomed to expensive promises. But my money must purchase an option on radically cheaper access to orbit. The question is whether eliminating the turbopump removes a dominant cost and reliability constraint—or transfers it into bearings, rotating machinery, and vehicle mass.

The consequence could be substantial. RATLOG cites launch waits of 3–4 years and prices reaching $15,000/kg; shortening those waits would let satellite operators start earning sooner. The dossier identifies a $12.1 billion launch-services market, although that includes services beyond RATLOG’s offering. It does not establish how much spending a 20-tonne launcher could eliminate or what new activity becomes economical. An existing cost pool is evidence. A quantified order-of-magnitude consequence is still missing.

The proposed engine cycle is a genuine technical hypothesis. Isar uses turbopumps; RATLOG proposes alternative pressurization and 90% fewer engine parts. The hot-fire makes this more credible than a drawing. Nevertheless, ten times fewer parts does not establish ten times better propulsion economics. SpaceX already operates reusable launch vehicles. RATLOG supplies no comparable thrust-to-weight, specific impulse, service life, or delivered launch-cost result. The CEO’s acknowledgment of increased bearing demands identifies exactly where the apparent simplification might become a system-level penalty.

The strongest argument against passing is the combination of inexpensive experimentation and company-reported $400 million in signed reservations after 70-plus customer discussions. That deserves attention at pre-seed. My willingness to fund manufacturing, certification, and long development cycles applies here; a 2029–2030 orbital target is not itself a defect. But reservation terms are undisclosed, so the agreements establish interest more clearly than committed financing or revenue. The exceptional-case argument would be a demonstrated technical trajectory opening an enormous new economic possibility. Neither reservation value nor proposed payload capacity establishes that trajectory yet.

Weise and Kunhart bring propulsion experience, Kelm brings electronics, and Driesnack brings startup operations. This is a relevant combination of disciplines. The dossier does not document belief revision or difficult recruiting against a particular weakness, so I retain the founder dimension’s missing-evidence default. The completed hot-fire earns affirmative milestone credit; the next roadmap earns little without a decisive test. Flight-ready propulsion in 2027 needs a bearing-life threshold, an accountable owner, a budget, and a response if the experiment fails. The premature-scaling red flag is not established here, but the next financing should retire that engineering uncertainty before expanding the organization.

I pass with my own money today. RATLOG has demonstrated economical experimentation; it has not demonstrated economical launch. There is no disclosed price per kilogram or bottom-up model covering vehicle mass, refurbishment, flight rate, and utilization. The €30,000 government grant is not evidence of permanent subsidy dependence; unsubsidized competitiveness simply remains unproven. My single flip condition is an independently witnessed, flight-representative engine endurance campaign that meets preregistered bearing-life and performance thresholds and supplies measured inputs to a credible, unsubsidized launch-cost model competitive with Falcon 9. That would connect the cheap experiment to the investment thesis.
