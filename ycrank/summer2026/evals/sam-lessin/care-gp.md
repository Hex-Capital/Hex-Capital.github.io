# Care GP -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Care GP’s 400+ clinic operators and reported profitability make Care GP an unusually capital-efficient healthcare operator trapped inside the least attractive financing wrapper in the batch: a consensus AI-agent SAFE with no disclosed price. The analytical signature is high venture-shape at the operating layer and low evidence of entry mispricing at the security layer. Care GP may become a very good business; my problem is whether Care GP can become a good investment from today’s cap table.

Marina Mogilko / LinguaTrip is the high rung on capital structure because profitable operations, owned demand, and downside-protected cash flow made the asset financeable without pretending it was conventional SaaS. Care GP shares the load-bearing mechanism: 85% pilot conversion, zero reported churn, minimal outbound selling, and profitability imply that Care GP can finance growth from customer value rather than five successive venture tacks. Care GP diverges because there is no unusual instrument or founder-linked asset—just contact-priced software plans and, presumably, standard YC equity. The underlying business looks venture-acceleratable; the financing has not yet been made intelligently venture-shaped.

Robinhood’s later consensus-round mechanism is the low rung on price discipline: velocity attracts famous capital, the markup becomes the thesis, and new investors confuse a great company with a great entry. Care GP already carries the canonical 2026 labels—YC, healthcare AI, and “agents”—while Heidi has raised nearly US$100 million and Care GP discloses neither round size nor valuation. Robinhood’s exception worked because Slow’s entry preceded the heat; Care GP provides no evidence of a comparable discount or operator-network allocation. Silence on price earns the absent-evidence default, then the consensus category pushes Care GP slightly below it.

Melvin Chen interviewed more than 150 healthcare providers before Care GP’s May 2025 beta exit, and Care GP subsequently processed more than 3 million documents. Over the next 18 months, that conviction compounds only if Chen keeps turning clinic corrections into workflow knowledge: every 5%–20% human edit creates either proprietary operating data or an expensive support burden. Care GP has credible founder-market immersion and an Oxford-to-healthcare-investing history, but no public essays, GitHub work, or category argument showing that Chen pursued this before the market made healthcare agents fashionable. Missionary behavior is visible in the schlepp; asymmetric conviction is not yet visible in the writing.

Melvin Chen’s 18,000 LinkedIn followers and Care GP’s 5,176 company followers put Care GP above the founder-distribution floor, but follower counts are not owned demand. The founder-brand score flips upward only if Care GP can show that Chen’s audience produced a measurable share of the 400+ clinic operators, or that a pre-existing practitioner community repeatedly converts without paid acquisition. Care GP reports largely organic demand and minimal outbound selling, which is promising, but the dossier does not connect those customers to Chen’s audience. Distribution may be earned product pull rather than founder-owned community, and those are different assets on the cap table.

Ro is the single useful category parallel: regulatory rails, operational complexity, and direct healthcare workflows made capital intensity a moat rather than simple founder drainage. Care GP has a smaller version of that mechanism through ISO 27001 certification, Australian data residency, multi-identifier verification, and federal-plus-state health-privacy obligations. Care GP also lacks Ro’s broad prescription infrastructure; dependence on a Best Practice Premier site ID makes the present moat look like an integration wedge that Heidi or Lyrebird could copy. “AI healthcare SaaS is worthless” is the provocative version; the walk-back is that Care GP becomes interesting where compliance and workflow depth overpower model commoditization.

Care GP will reach A$12 million–A$20 million in ARR and be acquired by Best Practice Software within 36 months. Care GP’s 400+ operators, profitability, organic demand, and document volume support a durable regional workflow business, while the Best Practice Premier dependency gives the platform owner both strategic motivation and bargaining leverage. That is a strong operating outcome and potentially a mediocre venture outcome—the exact regatta problem created when a default-alive business enters a consensus-priced AI race.

Heidi’s nearly US$100 million, Lyrebird’s A$12 million, and Care GP’s 5%–20% edit rate form the strongest bear case: Care GP could be squeezed between better-funded product expansion and clinics unwilling to delegate safety-critical filing. Care GP’s evidence still wins narrowly because 85% pilot conversion, zero reported churn, six hours reduced to under 30 minutes, and 3 million processed documents demonstrate deployed utility rather than demo-day theater. The verifiable condition that flips my verdict to Invest is a priced or SAFE-equivalent entry at no more than A$20 million post-money while Care GP remains profitable; above that price, Care GP’s likely strategic acquisition cannot generate enough alpha for my own check.
