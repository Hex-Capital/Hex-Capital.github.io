# Executor -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first problem is not MCP; it is that I cannot underwrite the check. Executor discloses neither post-money valuation nor competing-term-sheet count, so I cannot tell whether this is a cheap first belief or another consensus-priced YC infrastructure round. More importantly, the dossier names no use-of-funds experiment: no dated security deployment, paid-conversion threshold, or enterprise-governance milestone whose result would materially reprice the company. “Build the integration layer for AI” is a market thesis, not a venture moment.

The strongest bull case is real. Rhys Sullivan has shipped far more than a résumé: Answer Overflow reached 1.5 million monthly users, Executor has accumulated 3,000 GitHub stars, 230 forks, roughly 14,000 weekly npm downloads, 3,000 local users, and 2,580 commits. For a solo pre-seed founder, that is substantial product and distribution proof. The MIT-licensed CLI, desktop client, self-hosted server, and cloud service also show an unusually complete attempt to bridge open-source developers with organizational security and credential management.

But attention is not yet the dark pool. The 57,245-person X audience and open-source adoption demonstrate reach; they do not show that an owned relationship, proprietary dataset, or embedded workflow caused a purchase, retained paid usage, or even a pilot. This matters because Executor gives away the functional core while charging $150 per organization per month for hosting and administrative controls. Without conversion evidence, I cannot distinguish a business from a well-distributed public good.

The competitive counterfactual is harsh. If Executor disappeared, Microsoft still offers an MIT-licensed MCP gateway, Zapier exposes 9,000 applications and 30,000 actions, Composio has 1,000-plus packaged toolkits, and Nango already reports several million dollars of ARR. Protocol churn adds recurring compatibility work without necessarily creating proprietary value. The potentially defensible asset is not arbitrary-schema ingestion; it is the accumulated credential graph, policy configuration, and execution history inside customer organizations. The dossier describes how that asset might form but provides no evidence that it has.

Sullivan earns substantial founder credit because he has built developer infrastructure, distributed open-source software, and operated a prior product at scale. What is missing is the other side of the bridge: a documented enterprise security deployment, identity-governance sale, or production integration program. Vercel and Microsoft employment make that access plausible, but titles cannot substitute for operating receipts.

I would pass today. Executor may become valuable, but the current round appears to finance broad product expansion in a crowded, commoditizing layer without disclosed price, finite test, commercial conversion, margins, burn, or a seed-funded route to break-even. One fact flips me: at least 20 organizations paying the posted $150 monthly price and remaining active for 90 days because shared credentials or policy controls displaced their prior integration workflow. That would turn open-source reach into causal purchasing evidence and identify the narrow experiment worth financing.
