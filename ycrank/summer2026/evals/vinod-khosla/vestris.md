# Vestris -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Vestris attacks 8–10 hours of processor labor per closing, but Vestris has the signature of a useful labor-automation business with a consequence profile larger than its capital ambition. The highest dimension is consequence: autonomous contract-to-closing processing could remove much of the repetitive labor inside an $18.5 billion title-insurance industry. The lowest is required capital: browser agents, document generation, and county integrations do not describe a $50 million technical milestone where my capital changes what is possible. Vestris may make a great product. “Too important not to do” remains unproven.

**Cognition** is the high rung on consequence because replacing software-engineering labor attacks part of the $15 trillion labor economy rather than improving one workflow. Vestris shares the labor-displacement mechanism: Vestris retrieves records, calculates prorations, drafts title schedules, balances settlement statements, and assembles lender packages that processors now spend 8–10 hours producing. The analogy breaks on scope. Cognition points toward replacing a general class of knowledge work; Vestris currently covers Washington, D.C. and Montgomery County, Maryland inside an $18.5 billion premium pool that overstates Vestris’s software-addressable market. Vestris collapses a job bundle, not yet an economic regime.

A generic GPT-4 sales-call summarizer is the low rung on capital fit because any generalist can finance it and an incumbent can reproduce it inside one product cycle. That failure mechanism appears uncomfortably often in Vestris: Vestris is software-only, sells one custom-priced enterprise plan, and faces Qualia’s built-in AI plus Dono’s record-extraction system. County-by-county deployment across more than 3,700 systems creates expensive operational work, but expensive integration is not capital-hostile science. No foundation-model training program, fleet, plant, or falsifiable scientific milestone explains how a Khosla-sized check earns an asymmetric advantage.

Vestris’s technical risk compounds through jurisdictional entropy. Every new county can introduce a different portal, record format, exception taxonomy, and failure mode; over 18 months, Vestris either converts those exceptions into proprietary closing intelligence or becomes a services organization maintaining brittle browser automations. Vestris’s claimed Schedule B system trained on decades of approved and rejected titles could create the former outcome, but dataset ownership, scale, and exclusivity are undisclosed. Underwriter sign-off remains necessary. Call the required miracles two: reliable cross-county retrieval and title-grade document accuracy. Both are falsifiable, but neither repels consensus venture capital the way solid-state chemistry did at **QuantumScape**.

Aahil Valliani and Joshua Tang would move the founder-defiance score into the invest range if Vestris could show that title experts believe autonomous pre-closing is unsafe or impossible—and then publish error data proving them wrong. Vestris instead presents two University of Michigan students who spent roughly six weeks inside a settlement company and then automated what they observed. That is refreshing non-expert discontinuity, and Valliani’s supervised-content patents show prior software construction, but Vestris documents no controversial thesis, public contradiction of industry orthodoxy, or technical result that embarrasses the experts. McKinsey forecast fewer than one million U.S. cellphones by 2000; reality delivered 109 million. Vestris has not yet identified its equivalent failed forecast.

**Slash** earned an exception because a documented pivot exposed extraordinary learning rate strongly enough to overcome modest civilizational consequence. Vestris has no comparable evidence: Valliani’s Safe Kids history and Tang’s settlement-company work establish motion, but Vestris discloses no prior pivot, rejected thesis, or “we were wrong” moment. At pre-seed I will not confuse absence with rigidity, so Vestris receives the default rather than a penalty. Learning rate becomes visible when reality resists the plan; Vestris’s expansion beyond two jurisdictions will provide that test.

Qualia’s $160 million position and Vestris’s two-jurisdiction footprint imply a specific 36-month outcome: Vestris will reach $2–5 million ARR as a specialized pre-closing automation layer and be acquired by Qualia for less than $75 million rather than become an independent title-infrastructure platform. Vestris can sell measurable labor savings while county fragmentation narrows the standalone outcome and makes incumbent distribution increasingly decisive. Useful automation compounds into a feature acquisition more readily than into **OpenAI**-scale consequence.

Vestris’s strongest bull case is the **DoorDash** exception: no scientific miracle was required, yet operational complexity compounded into a category-defining network. Vestris has real ingredients for that override—founders who performed the work, a claimed dataset spanning decades of title decisions, an 8–10-hour labor target, and fragmented county systems that could make accumulated integrations defensible. The objection stings because a title-processing network could become the system of record rather than an AI wrapper. The dossier still gives Vestris only a waitlist, no revenue, no repeat production usage, no exclusive data rights, and incumbents Qualia, Snapdocs, and Dono with funded distribution. I would flip to Invest if Vestris demonstrates at least 1,000 completed closings across 25 jurisdictions with underwriter-accepted outputs requiring human correction on fewer than 2% of files.
