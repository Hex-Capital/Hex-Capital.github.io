# Helios Intelligence -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Helios Intelligence is betting that broader molecular measurements and home collection will turn occasional health testing into repeated consumer monitoring. The controlling variable is the fully loaded cost of a clinically actionable result that customers will buy repeatedly. Measuring 1,000 biomarkers matters only if it improves that variable. The dossier supplies no test price, validated collection performance, or demonstrated clinical decision advantage. I can formulate the market hypothesis, but I cannot yet underwrite it.

The strongest technical evidence is the founders’ May 2026 **mAge research**, which reports a **21% reduction in baseline mortality-prediction error** by combining proteomics and wearable measurements. That is a substantive scientific signal and earns some credit for a technology shift. Its scope matters: this is related research, rather than validation of Helios’s commercial assay, home-sampling process, or customer economics. The documented UK Biobank population limitations further constrain the inference. Better risk prediction could create a useful product; the paper does not establish that measuring more molecules creates actionable monitoring.

The market already contains buyers, but Helios has not demonstrated that it can acquire them. Function’s estimated **$100 million annualized revenue in February 2025** supports demand for consumer testing, while SiPhox already offers home collection covering up to **60 biomarkers**. Those facts establish a competitive opening with existing distribution, rather than an uncontested category. Helios’s Roon storefront is an interesting attempt to build a consumer channel and fund data collection. Advertising subscriptions from **$9.16 per tin**, however, establishes neither supplement demand nor conversion into molecular testing. I assign no customer-pull premium to availability alone.

The strongest case against passing is the team. Abudayyeh and Gootenberg have founded multiple biotechnology companies, have worked together extensively, and bring relevant molecular-diagnostics and computational-biology experience. Reported acquisitions in Abudayyeh’s history provide evidence of prior startup experience beyond academic credentials. This fits my revealed willingness to fund physical and scientific businesses when expertise can change their economics. The **BioAge** exception is relevant because specialized scientific judgment can precede commercial demand; the material difference is that Helios’s dossier establishes an associated predictive improvement without establishing a causal intervention or validated commercial endpoint. Its rare team, dated research, and YC/Formation backing make a research-stage override plausible, but there is no disclosed capital plan connecting that research to a proven assay.

Helios’s proposed longitudinal dataset could eventually become the durable asset. Today, integrations with wearables, records, laboratory results, and outcomes remain plans. Stronger models would help interpret those inputs, but the dossier does not show an owned operational loop that retains customers or improves decisions. My proprietary-data red flag therefore remains unresolved: how much time, money, and exclusive access would a competitor need to reproduce the useful portion? Nor can I award software economics to a testing business before seeing collection, processing, and interpretation costs. These are unanswered questions, rather than demonstrated adverse facts.

I would pass on investing my own money today. The founders are substantially more convincing than the current evidence for the business’s controlling variable. **One verifiable flip condition:** a paid repeat-testing pilot showing that Helios’s home-collected assay delivers a prespecified clinically actionable result at lower fully loaded cost than a matched conventional testing pathway. That would connect the scientific asset to the market mechanism rather than simply add another biomarker claim.
