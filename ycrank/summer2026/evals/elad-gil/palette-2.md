# Palette -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: rapidly improving video models have created a new orchestration layer for creative teams, but Palette becomes large only if routing and retained production context produce enough recurring customer value to outweigh the distribution and proprietary-model advantages of Runway and Luma. The controlling belief is therefore not that customers want access to many models. They already have that. It is that measurable cost and iteration gains cause teams to move an enduring production workflow into Palette.

There is preliminary evidence for the economic mechanism. Palette reports 35% faster generation, 50% lower cost, task-capable routing across its internal benchmark, and 130-plus regression tests. The proliferation of Veo 3.1, Runway Gen-4.5, and Kling 3.0 makes routing more useful because model capabilities and prices vary. But these are company-reported benchmarks without customer baselines, gross margins, or a threshold at which a creative team changes behavior. The dossier identifies a plausible variable—workflow-adjusted generation economics—but does not yet demonstrate that it controls purchasing.

The product is more substantial than a model picker. It retains prompts, source assets, edits, storyboards, characters, and model choices across image, video, music, and editing tasks. Better upstream models should improve what Palette can complete, while the workspace preserves customer context. That is the right architectural direction. The problem is market structure: Runway and Luma already combine models inside unified workspaces, and Palette has not shown proprietary workflow state, integrations, permissions, or routing data that a well-funded competitor could not reproduce. The inferred brand-context moat receives no credit until its reproduction cost is measurable.

The strongest bull case is the team. Josephine Lee brings MIT computer science and competitive-programming depth; Serena Pei has worked on diffusion models and multimodal systems across CSAIL, the Media Lab, and LIDS. They have shipped both a public studio and an API with only two people. In a still-crystallizing AI-media market, technical breadth plus a working product can justify investing before demand is legible. But this is not the rare-team exception: the dossier documents capable multimodal researchers, not frontier-model work or a technical asset scarce enough to override missing product pull. It also documents no costly founder commitment or second-time learning beyond choosing to start the company.

Today there is no public revenue, repeat usage, retention, named customer, or capacity constraint. The $500,000 financing, YC launch, OpenAI backing, and posted credit price establish capital and availability, not demand. Meanwhile enterprise adoption carries practical friction: SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 remain roadmap items, and the legal policies still identify themselves as starter templates despite the product processing enterprise media. Those gaps matter because an enterprise-workflow strategy depends on customers entrusting Palette with durable brand assets and permissions.

I would pass at this round. Palette has found a real technology shift and built a credible orchestration product, but the market gate remains unproven and the current layer is reproducible by heavily capitalized competitors that own models, distribution, or both. One verifiable condition would flip the decision: five named enterprises each paying at least $50,000 annually and still running recurring production workflows through Palette after six months. That would show that routing economics and retained context—not temporary access to fashionable models—control the market.
