# hiloop -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is action, not biography. Brar and Boser worked together on ML, post-training, and platform systems at Reducto; Brar also led ML infrastructure at DynamoAI. The two-person team then built a system that executed 4,188 agent-directed experiments across 50 B200 GPUs in two days and verified the result through 25 paired runs. That is specific knowledge made legible through an artifact. The founders understand the operational problem because they have built inside it.

The harder question is what remains after a competitor copies the code. Forkable compute, full traces, and reproducible experiment lineage are useful, but Prime Intellect already combines environments, training, evaluation, inference, and sandboxes, while Runloop offers adjacent orchestration infrastructure. Hiloop’s campaign depended on third-party models and commodity accelerator capacity. Technical complexity proves execution; it does not prove durability. The dossier identifies no exclusive data, proprietary model, hardware, or established workflow lock-in.

The bull case is sharper than an “AI infrastructure” label suggests. A two-person team produced a confirmed 0.9016 median validation result and a NanoGPT implementation with 12.54% lower runtime. If every campaign preserves failed paths, verified outcomes, and researcher decisions, hiloop could convert private research labor into a compounding experiment graph. That fits the pattern of turning individual knowledge into scalable output. But today this is a possibility inferred from one public campaign, not an existing proprietary asset. A database becomes a moat only when accumulated use makes the next result materially harder for a new entrant to reproduce.

The market provides room to maneuver: hiloop can apply the same propose-run-verify machinery across post-training, inference optimization, prompts, tools, harnesses, and classical ML, inside an MLOps market projected to grow from $2.19 billion in 2024 to $16.61 billion by 2030. The path is plausible, but the evidence remains top-down. Erdos Miller appears under “Trusted by” without engagement details, there is no published price or revenue, and the current go-to-market asks the founders to run initial campaigns personally. That is high-skill service delivery until proven otherwise.

No user-to-user loop is visible. Each new customer currently appears to require direct outreach, private compute, and founder effort; one team’s experiments do not demonstrably improve another team’s acquisition or results. This weakens hiloop against my permissionless-network pattern even as the infrastructure orientation fits my recent preference for deeper technical bottlenecks. The dossier shows none of the behavioral red flags—unfair dealing, artificial deadlines, or reassurance-seeking—but the known-exception logic does not override the missing leverage: there is neither observed self-reinforcing adoption nor documented founder conduct under pressure that substitutes for it.

I would not write the check yet. Hiloop has unusually credible builders and real technical proof, but its most probable failure mode is becoming a valuable experiment consultancy inside a market whose platforms absorb its features. The single flip condition is three unaffiliated paying customers renewing after 90 days while a retained experiment graph measurably reduces the cost or time of their subsequent campaigns without founder-operated execution. That would turn the system from impressive code into compounding leverage.
