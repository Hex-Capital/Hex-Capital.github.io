# Dock -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Mike Molinet built Branch past $100 million ARR, making **Dock** a repeat-operator adaptation machine whose highest dimension is founder learning rate and whose lowest is technical risk. **Dock** could become the shared operating layer for agent labor, but today Dock coordinates software rather than reinventing it. The world does not change because agents share tables and files. A workflow becomes less annoying. That is an optimization play, not yet a consequence-first bet.

**Slash** is the high rung for learning rate because its documented Kanye West pivot revealed founders willing to discard their original map. **Dock** sits below Slash: Molinet and Govind Kavaturi progressed from Branch to Thena to Dock, and their six-year working relationship produced two subsequent startups after scaling Branch internationally to $100 million ARR. That is meaningful adaptation. But **Dock** provides no specific “we were wrong about X” moment, and serial company formation is not automatically rapid learning. Experts often repeat a successful playbook long after the world has changed.

**DoorDash** occupies the low technical-risk rung because its capital-light logistics model required no scientific miracle; ambition and distribution overrode that weakness. The same failure mechanism appears more starkly in **Dock**: MCP connectivity, API keys, permissions, actor attribution, REST, and signed webhooks are engineering work, not breakthroughs that repel consensus capital. Dust, CrewAI, Relevance AI, or a model provider can reproduce the stack. **Dock** requires perhaps two miracles—an open workspace becoming the default state layer and customers trusting its agent authorization model—but both are adoption miracles, not falsifiable advances in physics, biology, or foundational AI.

Dock’s $19 Pro and $49 Scale plans reveal how its consequence could compound over 18 months: every integration adds agent activity, every activity adds historical state, and every permission decision raises switching costs. If **Dock** becomes the system of record through which agents perform work, Dock could mediate a meaningful portion of the labor economy. The dossier, however, documents one GitHub star, commercial-beta status, and no proprietary-data advantage. **Dock** currently breaks the economic mechanism of manual handoffs between chat sessions—not the $15 trillion labor mechanism itself. The gap between collaboration feature and agent operating system is the entire consequence score.

Mike Molinet and Govind Kavaturi are career B2B operators applying a familiar company-building playbook, the exact expert-continuity pattern my TED framework discounts. **Dock** would flip this dimension if the founders publicly committed to a falsifiable heresy—for example, that vertically integrated agent platforms will lose to an open, vendor-neutral state layer—and demonstrated that agents from Claude, ChatGPT, and Cursor repeatedly collaborate inside the same Dock workspace. Without that evidence, **Dock** is consensus timing around MCP, supported by six distribution surfaces but challenged by three well-funded competitors. Credentials tell me they can execute. Credentials do not tell me they see what Dust and CrewAI cannot.

**Commonwealth Fusion** required a $115 million Series A because capital was inseparable from reaching the first technical milestone. **Dock** is the opposite profile on required capital: a two-person software business selling organization plans for $19 and $49 per month, with no model-training program, fleet, plant, or hardware deployment identified. **Dock** may eventually raise substantial growth capital, but that would finance distribution against Dust’s more than $60 million—not unlock a technical milestone that only concentrated capital can reach. I am over-resourced for Dock today.

Six public repositories with one star support my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, **Dock** will have pivoted from a general multiplayer workspace into an enterprise agent-identity and audit control plane, reaching $2–5 million ARR while remaining independent. **Dock** has founders capable of updating the product, but its weakest dimension—proprietary technical risk—will push Dock toward the security and governance layer where permissions, attributed histories, and signed webhooks can become budget-bearing infrastructure.

Branch’s $100 million ARR outcome supplies the strongest objection to my pass. **Dock** combines three precedents I have already allowed: DoorDash’s capital-light exception, Slash’s learning-rate override, and Glean’s expert-founder exception. Molinet and Kavaturi have operated together for six years, built multiple companies, accumulated more than 26,000 LinkedIn followers, and launched **Dock** across web, Mac, npm, MCP, templates, and ChatGPT. That distribution machine could make Dock the default before technically superior competitors react. The objection still loses because Dock has one GitHub star, no documented retention, no adoption-based network effect, and prices that imply a tool rather than a system of record. Verified repeat use by 100 organizations with at least $1 million ARR—and evidence that multi-vendor agent histories materially prevent churn—would flip my verdict.
