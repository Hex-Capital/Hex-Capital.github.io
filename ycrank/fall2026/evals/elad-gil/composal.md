# Composal -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Coding agents increase software output, engineering teams still buy confidence before release, and Composal becomes a large company only if independent verification removes enough human checking to justify its operating cost. That is the controlling variable: human verification time eliminated per release at an acceptable escaped-bug rate. Finding more bugs is useful, but a system whose clean result still requires someone to repeat the checks has weaker economics. Composal’s own evidence puts that distinction at the center of the investment.

The October 18 benchmark recorded 157 successful attempts out of 166, including one false success. These were local fixtures, and the company explicitly disclaims production generalization. A false success matters disproportionately when customers are buying permission to ship. Separately, half of ten recent setup calls stalled on authentication or network restrictions. Composal must make trustworthy verification work inside real customer environments before parallel browser execution becomes a repeatable economic advantage. Neither finding disproves the business, but neither establishes the threshold needed to make it work.

The strongest argument for investing is that Composal already reaches beyond generating test scripts. CLI, MCP, and repository integrations connect verification to development; persistent product knowledge and journey maps supply application context; recordings, logs, and reproduction steps feed investigation by coding agents. Better browser models could improve execution while these surrounding assets remain useful. That is the strongest dimension here. It also fits my pattern of investing across the infrastructure surrounding a technology discontinuity: verification can grow alongside code generation without requiring one coding-agent vendor to win. However, documented context and integrations establish workflow substance, not yet durable switching costs or an owned remediation loop.

The economic step change remains less established than the architecture. The Team plan costs $500 monthly for 3,000 browser minutes—about $0.17 per included minute if fully consumed—while prepaid additional minutes cost $1 each. Those are selling prices, not evidence of inference costs, gross margins, or engineering hours saved. More generated code plausibly expands verification demand, but it could also expand low-value agent activity. Composal needs to demonstrate that its minutes replace expensive human work. The dossier gives a credible why-now mechanism without quantifying that conversion.

Competition makes that distinction urgent. mabl introduced overlapping independent verification in October 2026, and Composal reports nine interviewed teams already building internal QA agents. The latter is evidence of a problem worth solving, but also of an accessible substitute. Ten setup calls show customer discovery, not repeated product use; subscription pricing does not establish paying customers. I give no investment weight to 175 LinkedIn followers, eight repository stars, or the 80,000 repositories attributable to the CEO’s prior employer. Zembashis’s coding-agent infrastructure experience makes the team relevant, and Davies has previously co-founded mmtm. Neither employer credentials nor a co-founder title alone establishes the costly commitment or specific second-time learning my founder criterion rewards.

I would keep Composal on the watchlist and would not write a personal check today. The bull case is substantial: improving models could make an already integrated verification product much better, while a genuine bottleneck creates distribution through existing developer workflows. The unresolved issue is whether that improvement creates a separate company or an increasingly available feature. There is no documented scarce model-building asset or frontier research program that warrants overriding missing product pull, and no demonstrated proprietary-data advantage to resolve the competitive question. **My flip condition is a customer-verified, eight-week production cohort of five paying teams showing at least a 50% reduction in manual pre-release checking, with no increase in escaped critical bugs and positive contribution margins after compute and onboarding support.** That would test the market’s controlling variable rather than merely produce a better demo.
