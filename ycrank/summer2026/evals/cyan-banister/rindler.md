# Rindler -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, software agents are doing the clerical work trapped inside bank sites, government registries, payer portals, and private dashboards that were never designed for machines. Getting there requires agents to inherit authenticated sessions safely, translate pages into stable actions, and survive interface changes without relearning every site on every run. Rindler has a small leak from that future: Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, and state-registry workflows reportedly operate through verifier-gated mappings today. That is more meaningful than attaching “agent infrastructure” to a large market, although the dossier does not yet show how this library becomes broad enough or reliable enough to serve as the web’s translation layer.

The broken rule is legible. Institutions expose websites while withholding usable APIs, so operations teams pay in manual labor or maintain brittle RPA scripts whenever an interface moves. Rindler centralizes that repair burden and can reuse one mapping across customer accounts. But who captures the present rent, how large it is, and whose authority materially changes remain unquantified. The $100 Starter and $1,000 Teams prices establish an offer, not proof that customers recover enough time or control to keep paying.

Arthur De Los Santos and Michael Serrano can plausibly build this system: both worked at MIT CSAIL, Arthur studied AI and machine learning, and Michael combined physics and computer science with machine-learning engineering at Roblox. What I cannot find is the pre-company obsession. There is no dated history of either founder repeatedly fighting inaccessible portals, maintaining brittle automations for customers, or building several artifacts before this market became fashionable. Michael is clearly identified as CEO, which resolves basic accountability, but credentials and a relevant employer do not yet establish Rizz, Tizz, and Biz.

The product is real enough to test, but user behavior is still hidden. Rindler offers a working chat demo, an MCP installation, paid checkout, production mappings, and a 38-run internal benchmark reporting four-times-faster completion, three-times-lower cost, and half as many failures as Browser Use. Those results demonstrate technical motion. They do not show a customer repeatedly entrusting payroll, recruiting, compliance, or banking actions to the system. Pricing is not revenue, “in production” is not a dated cohort, and two grocery tasks are too tidy to reveal what happens when a bot defense, credential refresh, or consequential write action fails.

The strongest bull case is the layer itself: every new portal mapping could serve many tenants, while repair histories could compound into an asset that hosted-browser vendors do not possess. My harder question is whether Rindler controls that layer. Authenticated sessions can traverse Kernel, Browserbase, BrightData, or Capsolver; Browserbase and Kernel could move upward into reusable schemas; and Rindler will stop operating a site if its owner objects. Add the absence of SOC 2, HIPAA, or PCI certification and the transmission of instructions and results to model providers, and the system handling the most sensitive workflows is also the system with the most consent, custody, and procurement exposure.

My 36-month expectation is that Rindler narrows into maintained actions for recruiting and compliance portals and reaches roughly $1 million to $3 million in ARR, rather than becoming a horizontal translation layer for the web. I am Neutral and would not write the personal check today. I can waive revenue when a working artifact is paired with unusually strong founder obsession or institutional commitments; the public record supplies neither yet. One condition flips me: ten paying customers across at least three portal categories, each completing 100 or more authenticated production actions monthly for 90 days with at least 99% verified completion.
