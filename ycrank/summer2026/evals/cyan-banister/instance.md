# Instance -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

10,000 labeled robot episodes make Instance a physically grounded verification business whose weakest point is human governance. Instance watches real machines fail in real rooms, not agents rearranging abstractions in a browser; 0.76 macro F1 versus Claude Opus 4.8’s 0.53 is an observable artifact. Yet Instance rests on two lifelong friends with CEO and CTO titles but no disclosed tiebreaker protocol. The robot gives a verdict. The founders’ disagreement machinery does not. That high-tactility, low-governance pairing is Instance’s signature.

**Niantic** is the high rung because I could play Ingress, walk through the world, and feel John Hanke’s location machinery before a spreadsheet explained it. Instance shares that reality loop: camera footage enters an API, grounded subtask captions emerge, and a physical rollout receives a success, failure, or null verdict across seven robot platforms. Instance diverges because a normal person cannot deploy Instance within 48 hours without a robot laboratory; 10,000 company-curated episodes are evidence, but not the lived public pull that made Niantic feel like the closest we ever came to world peace.

**HQ Trivia** is the low governance rung because tactile product magic temporarily concealed a co-founder structure that later fractured. Instance names Claire Mao CEO and Lucy Cai CTO, which is better than shared titles, but a friendship dating to middle school is not a tiebreaker agreement. Instance discloses no majority operating authority, founder vesting, or exit-conflict protocol. The HQ Trivia exception applies enough to prevent a pass—Instance has a product I can watch work—but the exception also warns me not to romanticize ten years of friendship as governance.

**Lucy Cai’s CSAIL robot-learning research and SpaceX satellite work** give Instance a real autobiographical thread into physical systems, while Claire Mao’s JPL atmosphere simulations and MIT propulsion research strengthen the technical half of Biz, Tiz, and Rizz. Over 18 months, that background can compound: every customer correction can sharpen Instance across new tasks, cameras, and robot morphologies. The unstated pattern is less clean than Crusoe, though. Instance marketed an AI data analyst in February 2026 and pivoted to robot evaluation by July, so Instance may have discovered the workflow while searching for a market rather than carrying a specific rollout scar into the company.

**Robocurve’s MIT-licensed tooling and Bifrost’s open-source Manifold** keep Instance below the magically weird high rung. Instance occupies an unglamorous corner—watching robot footage and labeling failure—that could become essential infrastructure, but the dossier names no sophisticated investors who passed because the idea looked ugly, difficult, or absurd. Instance would move up a tier if two robotics operators repeatedly used the verifier in production while consensus investors dismissed the category as a feature; Instance would move down if YC’s cluster of Robocurve, One Robot, and adjacent evaluation startups turns the round into another fashionable robotics-AI chase.

**Anduril** turned software, sensors, and autonomy into a physical artifact whose technical and political difficulty compounded into a moat. Instance touches that axis through real-camera rollouts, seven robot platforms, and the proposed autonomous reset rig, but Instance currently sells the judge rather than the robot: the second machine needed to reset scenes has not launched. Instance therefore inherits some frontier-hardware difficulty without yet owning the hardware or regulatory barrier. I like software hiding inside physical infrastructure—I’m never playing the game I seem to be playing—but the open-source substitutes keep this closer to robotics tooling than an Anduril-class swing.

**Foxglove** is the most plausible endpoint within 36 months: Instance reaches roughly $2 million to $5 million ARR from robotics labs and small production fleets, then Foxglove acquires Instance to add automated success verdicts to its multimodal observability stack. Instance’s mechanism is the combination Foxglove lacks today: per-rollout judgment plus customer-approved labels accumulating across cameras and robot types. Instance does not need to become the system of record independently; Instance needs its verifier to become the missing semantic layer inside one.

**No named production customer and no third-party validation** form the strongest case against that forecast. The bear mechanism stings: Instance could be two MIT-credentialed founders who pivoted from a generic AI analyst into a hot YC robotics category, benchmarking a fine-tune against Claude while Robocurve, Bifrost, One Robot, and Runway compress the feature toward zero. That activates my pedigree red flag, my consensus red flag, and my fear that the model—not the founders—is the moat. Instance survives that objection because 10,000 labeled episodes, seven robot platforms, Lucy Cai’s robot-learning work, and 2.0-second latency show more than pitch-deck cosplay. I would flip to Pass if Instance cannot produce three referenceable production customers with repeat weekly usage and independently reproduced accuracy above a frontier general model within 12 months.
