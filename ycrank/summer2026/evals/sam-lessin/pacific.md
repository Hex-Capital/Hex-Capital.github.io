# Pacific -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first numbers I need are missing: proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely Pacific’s first scarce check. YC and NVIDIA Inception logos tell me the company has institutional access; they do not tell me whether the belief is cheap or already being auctioned. With no financing terms or investor-competition evidence, I cannot underwrite the deal rather than merely admire the market.

The potentially valuable experiment is legible but unfunded on paper: prove that a factory-built pod using diamond-wafer dry cooling can turn an already-powered, stalled site into reliable compute faster than conventional construction. Yet Pacific does not name this as the round’s finite test. There is no target power density, dated deployment, acceptance threshold, customer commitment, or proposed use of proceeds. “Mass-produce terrestrial pods, vertically integrate transformers and switchgear, serve defense workloads, sell hosted GPU hours, and later go to space” is several capital-intensive ambitions, not one card the capital flips.

The strongest bull case is real. JLL says 57% of data-center projects suffered delays of at least three months in 2025, and the modular market is forecast to grow from $11 billion to $48 billion by 2030. Ralph Heward-Mills worked as an Airbus thermal engineer, Harper Dammann Smith previously deployed AI systems, and Pacific says it is working with large aerospace companies. The earlier digital-twin product reportedly reached £10,000 MRR in its first month, evidence that this team can move and sell rather than remain trapped in aerospace PowerPoints.

But that receipt belongs to a product Pacific has already left. The current pods have no disclosed revenue, paid pilot, deployment, thermal benchmark, patent, certification, production volume, or comparative performance data. “Working with” unnamed aerospace companies does not establish that proprietary access caused a purchase. The known-exception bar requires hard operating evidence—measured performance, conversion, retention, or a similarly costly customer action—not YC affiliation, hiring, or an unpriced relationship.

Pacific does fit one unstated pattern: software and manufacturing could reorganize neglected physical infrastructure by matching stranded interconnections with standardized compute. The problem is that the translation layer is incomplete. The founders have aerospace and thermal credentials on one side, but no documented data-center manufacturing, grid deployment, or fleet-operation receipt on the other. Armada already has deployed modular units, while Crusoe can combine energy, construction, and cloud operations with $3.9 billion of financing. Verticalizing scarce components before Pacific has validated one pod risks magnifying the incumbents’ advantage.

Delete the next financing and the model becomes difficult to defend. There are no pod prices, gross margins, burn, runway, supplier commitments, or controllable cash metric. My 36-month base case is that Pacific pivots away from owning hosted compute and toward selling managed thermal-and-power systems to aerospace or defense customers, remaining below $3 million ARR. That could still become a useful business, but it is not presently an underwritten venture moment. One verifiable condition would flip me: a financing package below a $10 million post-money valuation whose proceeds fund a named customer’s paid, dated pod-acceptance test with explicit thermal capacity, uptime, and deployment-time thresholds.
