# SpaceFlow Technologies, Inc. -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have learned procurement by shipping into it. Hakan sold a tender-matching engine for five figures, the team built an autonomous procurement system with a medical manufacturer before YC, and SpaceFlow interviewed 200 companies before putting hundreds of people into daily production. The national restaurant contract and the customer executive’s personal investment are unusually dense evidence for a Summer 2026 company. Credentials describe potential; these transactions reveal specific knowledge.

The product is more than a thin AI interface. It runs inside the customer’s network, inherits identity, constrains actions through approval thresholds, and records corrections as customer-specific evals. Schema checks, short-lived credentials, allowlists, and signed audit logs show that the team has encountered the real security surface rather than merely describing “enterprise readiness.” If competitors copy the visible agents, they still must reconstruct customized system mappings, approval histories, supplier patterns, and business rules.

But the durable asset is not yet proven to compound. The accumulated context appears customer-specific, with no reported cross-customer learning, retention, or reusable deployment advantage. SAP already owns the system of record and introduced overlapping procurement agents in May 2026. If every implementation requires remapping customized tables and decades of business logic, SpaceFlow becomes a skilled integration firm attached to commodity models. The code scales; the exceptions do not.

The strongest bull evidence is direct value capture. SpaceFlow has six-figure contracted annual revenue, a signed enterprise customer, $400 million of supplier spend under management, and reported outcomes including four-times-faster RFQs and $120,000 in annual savings. Those figures are company-reported and reveal neither pricing nor gross margin, but they prove substantially more than pipeline. Someone has paid to place the system inside a consequential workflow.

Procurement software is already a $10.1 billion market projected to reach $21.3 billion by 2033, and the governed runtime could extend into other ERP-bound workflows. Yet the dossier proves the procurement wedge, not the horizontal expansion. There is also no permissionless loop: one customer does not make the product more useful or easier to acquire for another, and each deployment may demand proportional company labor. The portfolio pattern I prefer—participants creating leverage for subsequent participants—is absent.

I would write a small, option-sized check. The founders, production use, and revenue clear the pre-seed bar; the unresolved question is whether customer complexity becomes proprietary leverage or permanent services work. My 36-month base case is $8–15 million ARR concentrated in procurement automation, with the horizontal enterprise-agent-runtime claim still unproven. The verifiable flip condition is deployment economics: if the next three production rollouts leave gross margin below 60% after implementation labor, I would change this to a pass.
