# Allia Health -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The surprising fact is not that Allia Health built another mental-health EHR. It is that 8,000 clinicians across 1,000 practices reportedly use it daily for nearly one million monthly visits. Those users are far stronger evidence than the company’s market map or $2 million funding round. But the dossier never names the obstacle that nearly stopped the founders, what they built around it, or how quickly the result changed. Scale proves execution occurred; it does not reveal whether Amie Leighton and Saroosh Khan become more resourceful when something breaks. That distinction matters most to me.

The product itself is real technical proof. A three-person team has assembled an operating system spanning clinical records, documentation, prescribing, billing, referrals, and revenue-cycle management. Saroosh is also credited with a 94.2% ARC-AGI-2 result and a population-health agent. I discount the benchmark because the dossier supplies no independent validation, but I do not discount functioning software embedded in 300,000 patients’ care. This is a fierce technical artifact built before the institutional machinery is complete.

Demand is Allia’s strongest evidence. The narrow well is independent behavioral-health practices, and their repeated daily use is much more informative than five App Store ratings or 100 Google Play downloads. Six hundred clinicians across 70 locations have also joined the clinical group. Yet software adoption and economic pull are not identical. Practices with fewer than 20 clinicians pay nothing, while the reported $50 million is annualized care capacity under contracts, not collected revenue. The decisive experiment begins when care delivery and claims submission start in September 2026.

The bear case stings because Headway can offer the same free-EHR wedge with $327.5 million already raised. Allia must then win through the ugly work behind the interface: payer contracting, claims, multi-state clinical operations, and 42 CFR Part 2 compliance. That is promising schlep blindness, but SOC 2 and HITRUST were still in progress while the system handled protected health information. The distribution plan also leans on national payers without identifying their incentives, internal owners, or the mechanics of the contracts. A signed partner is useful; it is not yet a growth engine.

The founders have relevant exposure—healthcare work and research from Amie, technical work from Saroosh, and clinical perspectives from Dr. Ujjwal Ramtekkar and Lucas Volini—but the dossier does not show that Allia originated in a specific problem one of them personally encountered. Nor does it show a rejected hypothesis and rapid route change. These are missing signals, not evidence of rigidity. Still, they prevent me from distinguishing a running back from a team executing one plan that has not yet met resistance.

My unstated pattern favors companies that arrive with a concrete proof spike, and Allia plainly has one: daily use at substantial clinical scale before monetization. The red flags pull the other way: partner-dependent economics, incomplete certifications, and no documented founder response to a named blocker. None of my known exceptions resolves that missing founder evidence; impressive adoption does not explain how the team behaves when payer operations fail. I would not write the check yet. One verifiable condition would flip me: by December 2026, show collected claims from at least 100 activated clinicians and a dated account of the founder-built workaround that overcame the first material payer or compliance failure.
