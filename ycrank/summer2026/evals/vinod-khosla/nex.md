# Nex -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence is too small. Nex proposes replacing $100–$500 per month of GTM tooling per use case inside a $3.01 billion sales-assistant market. Even complete technical success reorganizes sales operations; it does not make a scarce expert service radically cheaper or rewrite a consequential resource curve. This is an efficiency product in an already crowded software budget, not a 100x societal option.

The technical case is also unproven. A live context graph, 1,000+ integrations, granular RBAC, SOC 2 Type 1, and code-backed rerunnable workflows show real implementation work. They do not demonstrate a 5–10x discontinuity. Nex supplies no measured comparison on workflow-build time, execution accuracy, human supervision, or total cost against Clay, Gumloop, or Relevance AI. Worse, those funded competitors already combine models, integrations, organizational context, and workflow runtimes. The product can send messages and open pull requests while Nex’s own terms warn that outputs may be inaccurate. That makes reliability the central technical question, not the number of integrations.

The strongest evidence is the team’s learning rate. Mohammad built a ChatGPT plugin used by 48,000 people and worked in HubSpot product; Dias was HubSpot CRM’s fourth engineer and later worked on its AI infrastructure. They shut down an AI-native CRM, moved through context infrastructure, and produced WUPHF, which attracted 1.2K GitHub stars, 260 Show HN points, and 115 comments. This could be fast belief revision by founders who know the workflow intimately. It could also be three positions in six months without a durable customer problem. The dossier does not disclose what evidence killed each thesis, so I credit adaptability but not disciplined learning.

The bull case is that the context graph becomes the system of action above fragmented GTM software. Clay’s $100 million ARR proves customers spend heavily, while Nex’s open-source response shows that a five-person team can attract technical users despite better-capitalized competitors. That is the plausible exception: a modest initial wedge evolving into a broader platform because the founders keep changing the route. The missing ingredient is proprietary learning. No retention, production workflow volume, customer correction rate, or decision-history advantage demonstrates that usage compounds rather than merely exercises third-party models and integrations.

Nex’s $100-per-seat unlimited plan could undercut several $100–$500 monthly tools, but price is not yet cost parity. There is no bottom-up account of inference expense, integration maintenance, onboarding, human review, or gross margin at high workflow volume. Nor has management identified a cheapest decisive experiment against permission leakage or erroneous autonomous actions. SOC 2 and RBAC are useful controls; they are not pass/fail evidence that the product can safely act across email, Slack, and CRM systems.

My base case is that HubSpot acquires Nex for its workflow and context technology within 36 months, before Nex reaches $10 million ARR. That may be a respectable outcome, but I am investing my own money for uncapped consequence, not likely strategic liquidity. I pass. One condition flips the decision: three named customers must each run at least 10,000 production actions per month with under 0.5% human correction while documenting a fivefold reduction in fully loaded workflow cost versus a Clay-plus-HubSpot-plus-GTM-engineer stack.
