# Marker -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Marker’s contested truth is that enterprises do not primarily lack better AI models; they lack an embedded team capable of discovering undocumented workflows and converting them into production agents. The falsifying event is simple: after several deployments, each new customer still requires roughly the same bespoke interviewing, integration work, and human supervision. Marker has evidence that agents can create value—an unnamed Fortune 500 retailer reports 80% less manual product-operations work, while a prop-tech company cut onboarding from 120 to 60 days and manual QA by 95%—but not yet that the operating model compounds.

The founders are the strongest evidence. Richard Berwick, Will Drevno, and David Pastewka have built together for 15 years across Twindom, Drapr, Gap, and Marker. They sold Drapr to Gap in 2021, and Berwick subsequently founded Gap’s Office of AI. This is not a team assembled around the phrase “AI agents.” They encountered the institutional problem from inside a large enterprise and remained together through acquisition. Full-time status, ownership, compensation, and secondary liquidity are undisclosed, so economic alignment is not fully proved; nevertheless, the documented shared history decisively clears the founding-structure concern.

The difficulty is that a secret can create value without creating a monopoly. Marker’s closest competitor, Distyl AI, already combines engineers with an enterprise-agent platform and has raised approximately $202 million. Sierra and Writer approach adjacent workflows with far greater capital. Marker names no bounded initial segment: it serves mid-size through Fortune 500 organizations and proposes organization-wide transformation. Its claimed moat—customer-specific process knowledge that makes later agents faster—is currently qualitative and largely trapped inside each customer. That may create switching costs for an account, but it does not yet show proprietary technology, cross-customer data effects, or scale economies for Marker. At present this is competition through superior execution, not an observable monopoly mechanism.

The bull case is sharper than the generic enterprise-AI pitch. These founders have already navigated an enterprise acquisition, one has run internal AI strategy, and the reported customer outcomes are large enough to suggest urgent rather than cosmetic demand. Executive introductions and consultation-led selling may fit high-value transformation contracts, especially when the team can use its operating credibility to win access that ordinary SaaS vendors cannot. The known exception for a genuine zero-to-one technical event does not apply, however: the dossier shows effective implementations, not an independent technical result that changes what agents can do.

Economically, Marker has disclosed neither pricing nor revenue. Project fees, retainers, and Command Center subscriptions are inferred rather than documented, leaving unresolved whether software expands faster than embedded labor. There is also no sales-cycle, conversion, implementation-cost, gross-margin, or retention evidence. A consultancy can produce excellent customer outcomes and still be structurally unable to generate a power-law return. The decisive question is whether the Command Center captures reusable knowledge across deployments, or merely records the work of scarce founders and forward-deployed engineers.

My 36-month base case is a respected, services-heavy enterprise AI firm producing $5 million to $15 million in annual revenue, but without the software margins or market control required for a defining company. I would not invest today. One result would flip the decision: a named cohort of at least ten enterprise customers showing that the second deployed workflow goes live in less than half the labor hours of the first while maintaining at least 70% gross margin and producing repeat software expansion. That would turn the present slogan—deployments make later deployments faster—into a falsifiable secret and an embryonic monopoly mechanism.
