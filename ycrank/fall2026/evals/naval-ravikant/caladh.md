# Caladh -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Kristen Kita has acquired specific knowledge the expensive way: working on the problem. Her February 2022 research demonstrated passive tracking of an underwater vehicle amid passing boats. That is a dated artifact with an experimental outcome, and it earns substantial founder credit. Caladh’s proposed navigation denial and sensing deception demand additional capabilities, however. Knowing how to hear a vehicle establishes relevant expertise; disabling its ability to navigate remains a separate technical claim.

The investment turns on whether that expertise has become something Caladh owns. Eclipse advertises denial from buoys or tow-fish; Cloak advertises seabed-based concealment. These products address a physical bottleneck, which fits my recent preference for businesses whose value survives cheap software implementation. But the dossier establishes no tested hardware advantage, proprietary model, exclusive data, or deployment-derived improvement. I cannot capitalize an advertised capability as an existing asset. Founder knowledge receives credit once; it becomes defensibility only when the company captures it in something competitors cannot readily reproduce.

The strongest case for writing a small check now is that public evidence will lag a capable defense founder’s actual work. Kita has relevant experimental experience, and non-kinetic underwater denial could occupy a valuable position alongside detection systems. Sonardyne advertises UUV detection at 1,200 metres; Caladh proposes acting on the threat. If that capability works reliably, incumbents could become integration channels. The opposing possibility is equally concrete: established suppliers already possess sensors, customer relationships, and integration capabilities, leaving Caladh to perform expensive engineering for each installation. The dossier does not yet distinguish those outcomes.

Ports, naval bases, and subsea infrastructure give the initial product several plausible buyers. Buoy, tow-fish, and seabed deployments suggest some room to adapt across those environments. Still, the cited $1.98 billion maritime counter-drone market includes adjacent applications and does not establish the accessible underwater segment. I give limited credit for the expansion path. Pilot request forms establish a way to contact the company; YC participation supplies neither customer validation nor distribution. There is also no observed mechanism through which one installation improves another customer’s utility. Shared deployment learning could eventually matter, but its existence and ownership remain hypotheses.

Caladh fits my pattern of backing physical infrastructure bottlenecks. The other compounding patterns—permissionless participation, customer-created supply, and distribution generated by users—are unproven here. My exceptions for investing before conventional validation require something concrete: an emerging network, a demonstrated prototype, or exceptional recruiting evidence. None is documented. Equally, the dossier supplies no evidence of dishonesty, manufactured urgency, or founder instability. Missing commercial information warrants uncertainty, without implying a character problem. Pricing, revenue, and contribution economics receive their absent-evidence default.

I would keep the relationship open and hold my money today. Caladh has a founder signal strong enough to deserve serious attention, with the asset and economic mechanism still unresolved. My single flip condition is an independently validated paid deployment of Eclipse demonstrating repeatable underwater denial through a technical asset Caladh owns. That would connect the founder’s accumulated knowledge to customer value and an advantage the company can retain.
