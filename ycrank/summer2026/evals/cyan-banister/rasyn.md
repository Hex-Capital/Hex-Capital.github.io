# Rasyn -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, a chemist should describe an objective while software assembles the literature, simulations, instruments, and verification chain around it. Rasyn has one little leak from that world: Marigold is live and can route plain-language tasks across 54 executable tools, including 40 on user-connected hardware. But “general intelligence for chemistry” skips several floors of the staircase. The dossier does not show how today’s orchestration becomes trusted experimental judgment, regulated auditability, or autonomous laboratory work—especially when Rasyn itself reports a 30.3% Top-1 retrosynthesis mismatch rate and disclaims GLP, GMP, and Part 11 validation.

The present wound is real but incompletely decoded. Chemists manually ferry outputs among literature tools, structure predictors, spectra packages, and calculation software. That is irritating workflow repair, one of the patterns I like. Yet Rasyn has not identified the economic rent: how many scientist-hours disappear, which institutional gatekeeper benefits, or what decisions a researcher can newly own. Prices from $89 per researcher to $2,000 per institution make the buying motion legible, but pricing is not proof that the workflow has become economically necessary.

The strongest evidence is the artifact itself. A live product, hosted execution, connected hardware, intermediate checks, and 54 integrations constitute considerably more than a rendering. The prior claim of use by seven medicinal-chemistry teams could be important, but the source now returns 404 and provides no dates, renewals, completed experiments, or behavior showing that scientists trusted Rasyn with consequential work. The disappearing Larmor and Marcus pages, followed by a rapid shift from a 500-plus-tool retrosynthesis story to Marigold’s 54-tool orchestration story, make me wonder whether Rasyn is discovering a wedge or repeatedly renaming the search for one.

The bull case stings. Daood Hashmi studies computational chemistry at Caltech, the product is already operating across software and physical compute, the consent policy protects customer scientific data, and seven teams may have encountered enough value to try an earlier version. That resembles the mechanism that can justify backing a pre-revenue technical company: a working artifact plus real institutional experiments. The material difference is that the dossier supplies neither a documented pre-company obsession nor signed proofs of concept, and Ayush Chauhan’s relevant history is absent. I also cannot tell who is ultimately accountable between the two founders. I will not infer a 50/50 governance problem, but I will not ignore missing leadership clarity.

Rasyn may sit below the fashionable drug-discovery interface as a useful execution layer, but it does not yet control the scarce layer. Many integrations are third-party or open source, Rowan already exposes computational workflows through web and API products, and customer data cannot enter a proprietary training loop without written consent. The privacy architecture is correct; the strategic consequence is that defensibility must come from verified workflow execution, unique models, or deep instrument connectivity. None is yet demonstrated strongly enough to overcome commoditization.

I pass today, narrowly rather than dismissively. My verifiable flip condition is three named laboratories renewing paid contracts after six months and publishing or privately demonstrating audit trails in which Marigold completes multi-tool workflows with fewer than 5% expert-overridden steps. That would show costly user trust, expose the real bottleneck, and turn the 54-tool artifact from an integration catalog into infrastructure.
