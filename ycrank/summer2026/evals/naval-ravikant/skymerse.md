# Skymerse -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Damian Szumski is not learning aviation from customer interviews. He is a licensed flight dispatcher who worked across airline navigation, dispatch, airline-software implementation, and Flightradar24’s API product before building Notamify. The resulting evidence is unusually concrete for pre-seed: 3 million NOTAMs processed across 487 days, 2,005,126 affected-element exposures, and a production deployment monitoring LOT Polish Airlines’ network. Specific knowledge has become an operating system, not a résumé claim.

The harder question is what remains when a competitor copies the code. Skymerse has built more than an AI interface: it converts unstructured notices into a schema covering runways, taxiways, navigation aids, procedures, timing, and applicability. That accumulated processing history is real technical proof. But the underlying aeronautical information is not exclusive, and the dossier does not show that operator decisions improve a proprietary model. Jeppesen ForeFlight already combines commercial data, segregated customer data, certification knowledge, and contextual reasoning; Overwatch AI connects much of the same operational stack. Today Skymerse owns a head start in structured interpretation, not yet a durable substrate.

The wedge has room to maneuver. NOTAM filtering leads naturally into evaluating entire flight schedules against weather, aircraft limits, MELs, manuals, runways, and company procedures. The same capability can serve airlines, cargo carriers, charter operators, medevac providers, and business aviation inside an estimated $11.78 billion aviation-software market. This is broader than a single alert product, although “autopilot for flight operations” also multiplies integration and liability surfaces. A system that is expressly not an authoritative briefing source must earn trust before it can capture the operational layer it describes.

The weakest mechanism is compounding among users. The 3,800 registered users and thousands of daily users demonstrate utility, but one dispatcher does not visibly improve the product or distribution for another. Each airline appears to require its own integrations, manuals, fleet constraints, and sales process. Operator-specific data could eventually compound, but an inferred feedback loop is not a network effect. Without evidence that deployments produce exclusive training data or reusable decision intelligence, growth may add service obligations as quickly as it adds leverage.

Direct value capture is plausible but only partially demonstrated. Notamify has a real $24.90 monthly Pro price, credit-based API usage, and custom enterprise deployments; LOT supplies named production validation. Yet the dossier reports no paid subscribers, contract value, ARR, renewal, or transaction economics. Published pricing proves that Skymerse knows who should pay. It does not prove that they have paid.

The bear case stings because the incumbent can bundle similar reasoning into an authoritative ecosystem while Skymerse remains an additional system users must verify. The unexplained shift from an ATC transcription assistant to Notamify and then end-to-end operations, together with a previously identified co-founder no longer listed by YC, also deserves diligence—though neither fact establishes abandonment or misconduct. I would still write a small option check: founder-specific knowledge, shipped infrastructure, real usage, and a large adjacent operating surface outweigh the absent network loop. The single flip condition is measurable: if Skymerse cannot secure three paying airline operators with annual contracts and documented renewal or expansion within 18 months, I would treat the structured-data lead as a feature incumbents can absorb rather than a company-level advantage.
