# Ooak Data -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Seven-figure contracts with the two largest AI labs make **Ooak Data** a contrarian data-acquisition thesis trapped in an atoms-light market structure. **Ooak Data** correctly sees that synthetic benchmarks omit the disorder of real business workflows; its highest dimension is the secret that acquired company ecosystems are more valuable than expert-authored simulations. Its lowest is structural moat: privacy-sensitive data creates friction, but friction is not monopoly. The decisive question is whether Ooak Data owns an irreplaceable library or merely operates an expensive supply chain.

**Stripe** is the high rung for the secret: developer-first payments exposed a specific failure inside processors that believed payments were already solved. **Ooak Data** shares the mechanism by naming Snorkel’s expert-authored simulations and Mercor’s expert datasets as inadequate substitutes for structurally faithful company workflows. The divergence is load-bearing: Scale, Surge, Mercor, and Snorkel are already building RL environments, so **Ooak Data** has identified a better input without proving why incumbents structurally cannot acquire it. A secret becomes a company only when the incumbent cannot copy it.

**Asana** is the low rung on atoms-heavy or regulated-sector protection: pure software lacked the structural barrier, and Dustin Moskovitz’s Facebook pedigree supplied the exception. **Ooak Data** processes emails, financial figures, documents, and collaboration data under NDAs, regional storage, deletion rights, and automated anonymization, but its customers are commercial AI labs rather than governments or regulated buyers. **Ooak Data** therefore inherits compliance cost without yet inheriting Palantir-like certification depth or contractual lock-in—and its founders lack the shipped-to-scale pedigree that rescued Asana.

Scale AI’s $14.3B strategic investment and Surge AI’s reported $1.2B revenue explain how **Ooak Data’s** monopoly hypothesis could fail over the next 18 months. If each acquired ecosystem improves environment fidelity, attracts more labs, and finances further acquisitions, **Ooak Data** can compound a proprietary library and raise prices for scarce workflow coverage. If the rights are non-exclusive, the same mechanism runs backward: suppliers collect up to $300K, laboratories sponsor interchangeable vendors, and Ooak Data becomes a services-intensive procurement layer with two concentrated buyers. Seven-figure contracts validate demand, not durable pricing power.

More than 300 targeted ecosystem acquisitions within six months is a definite operational target, but **Ooak Data** supplies no two-to-five-year architectural commitment comparable to a reusability roadmap. **Ooak Data** would flip this dimension into invest territory if those acquisitions served a declared sequence—exclusive workflow rights, standardized executable twins, measurable transfer to frontier agents, then a reusable environment exchange—rather than simply expanding inventory. The prior pivot and six simultaneous hiring tracks leave open the indefinite interpretation: sales, operations, and product are still discovering which business exists.

Thomas Aubry’s Samsung applied-ML work and PayLead Head of Data role give **Ooak Data** credible technical execution, but the relevant single-anchor parallel is **Anduril**. Palmer Luckey had already shipped Oculus to a billion-dollar exit; Aubry’s strongest public repository has 23 stars, Pierre-Louis Vouteau’s prior product reported 15,000 users, and Grégoire Lamy reached Head of Ops & Data at Epsor. **Ooak Data** has domain-adjacent operators, not verified power-law lineage. This is better than the median current-batch résumé, but it does not override missing exclusivity as Moskovitz or Luckey might.

Two unnamed largest AI labs will, in my 36-month forecast, carry **Ooak Data** to $15M-$30M ARR while procurement costs and non-exclusive data rights keep **Ooak Data** services-heavy, customer-concentrated, and short of monopoly pricing power. The secret is commercially real: frontier models need workflows that simulations omit. The low structural-moat score determines the endpoint: revenue arrives faster than defensibility, and the laboratories eventually internalize the most valuable acquisition and anonymization capabilities.

Seven-figure contracts are the strongest objection to that forecast because pre-seed companies do not accidentally sell seven figures twice; the bull case is that **Ooak Data** has already crossed the trust barrier and can turn 300 acquired ecosystems into the default real-world workflow corpus. Asana and Lyft show that a missing atoms moat need not be fatal when lineage or network effects supply an override. Neither exception presently fits **Ooak Data**: no founder has shipped at Facebook scale, no supplier network effect is disclosed, and no contractual exclusivity prevents Scale, Mercor, or the labs themselves from buying equivalent workflows. I would flip to Invest if Ooak Data verifies exclusive reusable rights across at least 300 ecosystems with four independent lab customers and no customer exceeding 30% of revenue.
