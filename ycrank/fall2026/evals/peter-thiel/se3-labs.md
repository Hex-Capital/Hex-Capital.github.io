# SE3 Labs -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

SE3 Labs has demonstrated that it can measure robot failures. The investment question is whether it can own something that makes those measurements indispensable. The potential secret is that deployment-specific failures contain training information that simulation and internal laboratories systematically miss. A controlled comparison showing no advantage from SE3’s collected trajectories would falsify that proposition. The dossier supplies neither that comparison nor evidence that named peers reject the proposition. I will not manufacture a contrarian thesis for the company.

The strongest affirmative evidence is operational. SE3 published 100 Dream evaluation episodes across 10 tasks on October 30, 2026, with recordings, telemetry, and scoring sheets. Its September experiment reported 18 completed placements with agentic control against six with code-as-policy, from 20 attempts each. These are concrete experiments rather than a robotics presentation. But the latter compares control approaches; it does not establish that SE3’s failure-directed collection improves customer policies. The distinction matters because an evaluation service and a proprietary improvement engine have different economics.

Who owns the improvement if the loop works? Poke & Wiggle already offers physical benchmarks and targeted data collection; BodenAI supplies real-world RL datasets and scenario infrastructure. SE3’s physical operations distinguish it from simulation-only evaluation, but physical operations are already available elsewhere. Customer raw data also requires written authorization before reuse for general-purpose model training. That restriction does not make a monopoly impossible, but it prevents me from assuming that each customer automatically strengthens the product for every subsequent customer. Today, the observable offering has direct substitutes without an established mechanism for retaining exceptional value.

The bull case is more serious than “robotics is growing.” API-accessible models let an independent operator test systems without possessing their weights. Public Python documentation and task specifications give SE3 an actual route to technical users. If developers increasingly outsource physical evaluation, the operator accumulating difficult deployment tasks could become essential infrastructure. This fits the unstated preference for the physical production stack beneath AI. Yet none of the known exceptions removes the ownership problem here: the dossier establishes neither a foundational technical breakthrough nor persistent paid customer behavior, and its working stations do not establish an exclusive deployment advantage.

I would leave the founding-team assessment at its cautious default. The three-person team’s founders, shared working history, ownership, and employment commitments are unverified. Paige Xu’s public association cannot establish those facts. There is no evidence of part-time founders, outsourced core engineering, or an investor-dominated capitalization, so those red flags should not be invented. Separately, contract-specific project fees identify a commercial format, but without prices, margins, or expansion economics they cannot support a power-law revenue case. The developer entry points earn modest distribution credit; an inquiry form does not establish an economical sales process.

I pass on the current evidence. SE3 Labs could build a useful business while its customers retain the models, data rights, and resulting economic leverage. My verifiable flip condition would be a signed recurring customer contract granting SE3 exclusive access to a meaningful deployment-task family and reusable rights to the resulting failure data. That would move me to diligence by establishing a bounded asset competitors cannot simply offer alongside it.
