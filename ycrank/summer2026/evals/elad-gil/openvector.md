# OpenVector -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Vision-language models and edge hardware now let physical-operations teams automate existing camera feeds; the variable determining whether OpenVector becomes large is whether it can execute consequential actions reliably enough to replace human monitoring at attractive per-site economics. The dossier identifies this reliability problem, but not the required accuracy, intervention rate, or customer savings. A $37.84 billion video-analytics forecast does not answer that question. Neither does the $449 monthly price without deployment cost, gross margin, or measured labor displacement.

The strongest evidence is architectural. Gizdov’s CVPR 2025 foveated-vision work samples roughly 3% of pixels and claims 5–10× lower bandwidth. OpenVector then connects detection to email, calls, POS or CRM updates, and webhooks, with human review and configurable confidence thresholds. This is more than output formatting: the product creates and operates a camera-to-action workflow, and better vision models should improve it. But foveated sampling primarily addresses bandwidth and latency. It does not yet resolve the admitted long-horizon reasoning problem or establish that autonomous actions can run without expensive review.

Product-market pull remains weakly evidenced. OpenVector reports live use cases across four sectors, but supplies no named customer, deployment count, revenue, repeat usage, or retention. The refundable $100 reservation is a purchasing mechanism, not demonstrated demand. Meanwhile, Spot AI already supports existing-camera video agents, Ambient.ai deploys edge-optimized models, and Voxel owns a defined industrial-safety wedge. OpenVector’s demos across gyms, car washes, offices, warehouses, and restaurants may indicate product flexibility, but they also suggest the market has not crystallized around a buyer, workflow, or reusable distribution channel.

The founders are credible for the technical problem. Gizdov left a Harvard PhD after producing relevant computer-vision research, while Urlam brings embedded-systems, camera, and network-protocol experience from CMU and Tata. Their continued work after the 2025 MIT Grand Hack is useful evidence of commitment. It is not second-time-founder learning, however, and the team’s technical quality cannot substitute for identifying which customer has both sufficient pain and sufficient tolerance for false actions.

The bull case is that existing-camera compatibility removes a major adoption cost, low-bandwidth edge inference enables offline deployments, and completed workflows generate correction and outcome data that compound inside one vertical. That could produce a strong physical-operations platform. Today, though, the proposed data moat is inferred: there are no disclosed data rights, correction volumes, or reproduction costs. The research-stage talent exception also does not apply. OpenVector has a good applied-vision team, but it is entering a funded application market rather than controlling scarce frontier technology. The additional Foveal Vision identity further leaves the current product boundary unresolved.

I would monitor rather than invest. My base case is that by August 2029 OpenVector has narrowed to one industrial vertical and reached $1–3 million ARR, rather than becoming a horizontal automation layer for every camera. I would flip to invest on one verifiable condition: a 90-day cohort of at least 10 paying sites in one vertical, each paying at least $449 monthly, with a majority expanding workflows while autonomous-action errors remain below a customer-agreed threshold without routine human review.
