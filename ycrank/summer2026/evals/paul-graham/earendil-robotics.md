# Earendil Robotics -- Paul Graham Evaluation

James Walton operated UAS systems in Ukraine, while Dan Komorny spent roughly a decade building drone payloads, optics, and AR displays. Earendil Robotics is therefore strongest where startups are usually weakest: in the schlep. Earendil Robotics is weakest on the hacker trail that proves what these founders personally built before fundraising. This is an operator-native defense startup with unusually organic founder-problem fit, but the dossier shows experience more clearly than animal resourcefulness.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung because Brian Armstrong ran toward both broken software and regulatory drudgery while everyone else recoiled. Earendil Robotics shares that mechanism: contested electromagnetic environments, export licences, military procurement, rapidly obsolete hardware, and autonomous interception above 300 km/h are a whole nest of schleps. Earendil Robotics diverges because Coinbase had Armstrong’s working Android wallet as proof. Earendil Robotics calls Aeglos combat-proven but publishes no interception rate, deployment, unit cost, or independent test. The schlep is real; evidence that the founders can win it is not yet equally real.

A polished AI wrapper fails the hacker test because its founders intend to hire the people who will make the product. Earendil Robotics does not display that exact failure: Komorny previously worked on AR heads-up displays, optical systems, drone payloads, and turrets, and Earendil Robotics already presents an integrated interceptor, thermal sight, and vision system. But neither founder has a public repository, competitive-programming record, open-source artifact, or independently inspectable demo. Earendil Robotics may contain serious hackers; the public evidence presently proves technical proximity, not hacker genealogy. Hackers leave tracks.

James Walton previously founded and scaled two IT and telecommunications businesses, and Earendil Robotics exhibited IVAS at DPRTE Farnborough in April 2026. Those are useful tracks, but neither is an Airbnb cereal-box moment. Over the next 18 months, resourcefulness will compound only if Earendil Robotics turns procurement friction into an advantage: personally running field trials, adapting Aeglos after each electronic-warfare failure, and getting a small unit to pay before a program office blesses it. A seven-person Earendil Robotics that merely attends defense exhibitions will become another contractor. A seven-person Earendil Robotics that rebuilds hardware beside soldiers could become an animal.

Walton’s British Army UAS work and Komorny’s operational exposure in Ukraine make Earendil Robotics unusually organic. The founders did not discover counter-drone defense in a market report; they encountered contested electromagnetic environments and then built across the detection-to-destruction chain. Earendil Robotics would fall a full tier on this dimension if “operational exposure” meant observation rather than personal use—if neither founder had actually faced the failure modes Aeglos and Holo address. The evidence currently points the other way, though it does not identify the exact battlefield incident that caused them to start building.

**Stripe** (2010) had two technical brothers whose long working relationship was visible before the company. Earendil Robotics also has the structurally right number—Walton and Komorny—and sensible coverage across military operations, prior company-building, optics, computer vision, and drone hardware. Earendil Robotics lacks the more important part of the Stripe parallel: no public evidence says how long Walton and Komorny worked together, what they built together, or how they behave when a prototype fails at 2 a.m. Two names on a YC page are a configuration, not yet a cofounder bond.

Earendil Robotics has only 75 LinkedIn followers, no disclosed revenue, and no independently verified deployment; by July 2029, I expect Earendil Robotics to reach $3–8 million in annualized defense-contract revenue from small-unit interceptor and training deployments, but not yet become a program-of-record prime. Earendil Robotics can reach that range because its high-schlep, founder-native knowledge should win narrow field deployments faster than it can win large procurement programs. Export controls, integration work, and constant electronic-warfare adaptation will slow scaling while making each successful deployment harder for a generic software entrant to copy.

Anduril, Fortem, Epirus, and RTX make the bear case sting: Earendil Robotics is entering a weapons market where competitors already possess funded production, deployments, or program-of-record access, while Earendil Robotics offers no patent, contract, proprietary dataset, or verified interception result. The bleak version is a defense exhibitor assembling commodity components beneath the phrase “combat-proven.” I still lean invest because Walton and Komorny have direct Ukraine and UAS experience, and the unpleasant integration and training work is precisely where schlep blindness hides opportunities. One independent contested-environment trial showing repeat autonomous interceptions, followed by a named paid military deployment within 18 months, would confirm the bet; failure to produce either would flip me to pass.
