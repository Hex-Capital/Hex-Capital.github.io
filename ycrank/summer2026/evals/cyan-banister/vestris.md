# Vestris -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the closing file should largely assemble itself: documents arrive, county records are queried, prorations are calculated, discrepancies are surfaced, and a human approves the legal judgment. Vestris has one small leak from that future—a live pilot reportedly compressing 8–10 hours of processing into one hour—but the dossier does not yet map how two supported jurisdictions become a national closing substrate. Every new county portal, calculation convention, and underwriter relationship may add operating complexity faster than the agent removes it.

The founders have paid some of that operating tuition. Aahil Valliani and Joshua Tang worked the same title desk and processed actual closing files before building the product. Valliani also founded Safe Kids and appears on three patents; Tang built live telemetry and communications systems for Michigan Formula SAE. That is more persuasive than two students discovering an $18.5 billion category through a market report. It shows a wound, a working relationship, and the ability to ship. It does not yet show a long pre-company obsession with title infrastructure or how they divide authority when two roommates and best friends disagree. Equal control without a tiebreaker would stop me.

The sharpest fact is economic: a title processor reportedly spends 8–10 hours checking emails, comparing documents, researching records, and reconciling a file. Vestris claims to return roughly seven of those hours and double a small agency’s capacity. The rent is fragmented clerical labor imposed by county portals and document-heavy settlement rules; the agency returned is the ability of a small title shop to process more closings without proportionally hiring attorneys and processors. That is a real system mechanism, although the dossier does not separate unavoidable legal review from removable administrative rent.

The bull case stings. One shop settling millions of dollars monthly is already running Vestris alongside its operation, the product performs substantive work rather than generating a summary, and decades of approved and rejected titles may contain the exception knowledge incumbents spent years accumulating. If reviewed outcomes compound into jurisdiction-specific workflow data, this could become much more than an AI interface. But those outcomes are company-reported, the training data’s ownership and exclusivity are undisclosed, and the current evidence is capped at one pilot with no revenue, cohort, error-rate, or repeated-use detail.

My larger concern is that Vestris currently sits on top of the wave rather than beneath it. Public computer-use APIs let it operate browser-based county systems, while Qualia, Flueid, and Stavvy already control systems of record, underwriting relationships, or closing infrastructure. The company has not demonstrated control over a scarce technical asset that those incumbents cannot reproduce or acquire. Worse, it handles closing documents and party communications in a market that lost $275 million to real-estate fraud in 2025, yet the dossier identifies no consent, custody, access-control, or transaction-security architecture. Human approval mitigates title errors; it does not solve compromised instructions or sensitive-data exposure.

I am neutral today. The founders understand the irritating machinery, and the pilot suggests a functioning artifact with meaningful labor economics. I still need proof that jurisdictional complexity becomes proprietary learning rather than permanent services work. The verifiable flip condition is ten paying agencies across at least five jurisdictions, with independently measured error rates no worse than human processors and a documented security architecture covering document custody, permissions, and wire-instruction handling. Show me that, and this starts looking like infrastructure rather than rented model capability.
