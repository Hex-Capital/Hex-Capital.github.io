# Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. is a frontier-hardware company with a governance-shaped shadow: WaveSight claims one-millimeter vision through walls, while four Humane alumni share the founder line and only Bilgehan Avser is identified as CEO. Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. has abundant Tiz—radio hardware, imaging algorithms, and 250+ reported patents—but the Biz and Rizz still have to resolve into one person with unquestioned tiebreaker authority. I can touch the ambition; I cannot yet see who wins the argument when four accomplished inventors disagree.

SpaceX is the high rung here: capital-intensive electromagnetic hardware that conventional investors could dismiss before the artifact proved itself. Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. shares that load-bearing mechanism—radio physics, custom hardware, regulated operation, and a device intended to reveal something the human eye cannot—but Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. diverges where the evidence becomes real. The across-room scan and one-millimeter accuracy are self-published, no independent validation exists, and the reported 250+ patents are not publicly shown as assigned to the corporation. SpaceX risk was enormous, but the machine eventually had to leave the ground in public; WaveSight still needs its launch.

HQ Trivia is the low-rung warning: a tactile product can become electric while unresolved founder authority quietly writes the ending. Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. names Avser as CEO, which is materially better than shared titles, and the four founders have worked together for more than eight years. Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. nevertheless provides no equity split, operating agreement, vesting protocol, or explicit tiebreaker—precisely the silence that makes me ask who decides whether construction, cargo, security, industrial inspection, or defense gets the next eighteen months. Four founders are not automatically four directions, but they are four gravitational fields.

Bilgehan Avser’s 35+ patents and leadership of UWB, 5G, and mmWave antenna work across multiple iPhone generations make Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. rhyme with Anduril on founder origin: the product grows from prior technical artifacts rather than a market-analysis exercise. Brian Huppi co-invented the Nest Learning Thermostat, George McLean built X-ray imagers, and Paul Leutheuser shipped iPhone hardware, so Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. was built by people who have spent years making invisible physical phenomena legible. The missing piece is autobiographical contact with construction rework itself; these founders lived sensing hardware, not apparently the moment when a contractor drills into an unseen pipe.

Vayyar’s $199.95 Walabot makes the counterfactual unusually clean: Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. becomes truly “magically weird” if WaveSight demonstrates across-room, one-millimeter mapping through realistic building assemblies where Walabot stops at four inches. Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. falls toward ordinary industrial instrumentation if Qorvo’s integrated UWB components let several competent hardware groups reproduce the result. A named contractor paying for repeated scans—or a sophisticated investor passing because the physics looks impossible—would push this dimension into Crusoe territory; preorder registration without a disclosed count does not.

WaveSight’s physical verifiability can compound quickly over eighteen months because every scan can be compared with demolition, as-built drawings, or a later-opened wall. Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. could turn those comparisons into a labeled materials dataset, tighter reconstruction algorithms, and trust that a brochure cannot manufacture. Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. could also compound errors: material-dependent penetration, FCC coordination under 47 CFR §15.525, and a one-millimeter claim that fails on reinforced concrete would make every deployment expose the same weakness. I can imagine carrying WaveSight through a building within 48 hours; I cannot yet buy one or inspect a validated scan.

The $457.32 million ground-penetrating-radar proxy and fragmented construction procurement point to a narrower 36-month outcome: by July 2029, Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. will have pivoted its primary commercial focus from general construction to defense and industrial inspection, producing $2 million–$5 million in annualized revenue from low-volume systems rather than mass-market hardware. Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. has the frontier capability to win technical pilots, while four-founder governance and five named application categories make ruthless market focus less likely. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing: the wall camera may be the wrapper, and regulated sensing infrastructure may be the business.

Vayyar’s $300 million-plus funding, Qorvo’s integrated UWB chip, and FCC restrictions form the bear case that stings: Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. could be four elite Humane veterans rebuilding their identities around an impressive laboratory demonstration that is neither independently reproducible nor commercially legal for the broad use cases advertised. Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. has no disclosed revenue, preorder count, patent assignment, or customer validation, and “hundreds of conversations” at the Nashville Build Expo can evaporate before procurement. I still invest because Avser’s antenna depth, Huppi’s Nest sensing work, McLean’s X-ray background, and Leutheuser’s shipped hardware make the claimed artifact more than pitch-deck cosplay. I flip to Pass if Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. cannot show one named customer completing a paid, repeat deployment under a documented FCC-compliant operating classification before the next priced round.
