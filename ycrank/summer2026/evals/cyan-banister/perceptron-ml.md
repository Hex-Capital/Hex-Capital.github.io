# Perceptron ML -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

$1.445 billion of legal-AI spending makes Perceptron ML an unsexy regulated deployment shop whose hardest verification problem may be sibling governance. Perceptron ML scores highest where confidentiality, sanctions, and customer-controlled infrastructure make the category difficult; it scores lowest where Michael and Peyton Marcotte are both called co-founders but neither is identified as CEO or tiebreaker. I like systems that live where polite investors fear to tread. I do not like two hands resting on one steering wheel.

**Vigil Labs** is the high rung on the regulated-swing ladder: financial rules create an accumulating moat rather than merely complicating sales. Perceptron ML shares that mechanism because law firms face confidentiality duties, hallucinated citations, and court sanctions, while Perceptron ML promises customer-environment deployment, no shared training, and primary-source verification. The divergence is load-bearing: Perceptron ML publishes no SOC 2, ISO certification, verification benchmark, or independent error rate, and Harvey and Legora already sell data isolation, audit controls, and grounded workflows. The regulation is real; Perceptron ML has not yet shown that the regulation belongs to it.

**HQ Trivia** is the low rung on governance because unresolved co-founder authority became an accelerant once pressure arrived. Perceptron ML has two brothers, Michael and Peyton Marcotte, with no named CEO, equity structure, operating delineation, or conflict protocol in the dossier. Brotherhood may supply trust, but trust is not a tiebreaker. Perceptron ML is already crossing from Notyfi event monitoring into legal AI, exactly the kind of identity-changing moment when an unspoken 50/50 arrangement becomes expensive. HQ Trivia earned an exception through tactile, explosive product pull; Perceptron ML has 38.4 captured hours in one displayed week, not an addictive artifact strong enough to override the governance red flag.

**Michael Marcotte’s** NVIDIA work on production agents, hardware debugging, and formal verification gives Perceptron ML real Tiz, but it does not show that either founder lived inside a law firm’s billing, docket, or privilege problem. Over the next 18 months, that missing origin compounds: bespoke deployments require knowing which lawyer complaint is workflow truth, which is institutional folklore, and which is a billable customization trap. Perceptron ML could learn that knowledge through installations, but Harvey, Legora, and internal law-firm engineers are learning simultaneously. Michael’s Stanford mathematics and computer-science path plus Peyton’s Brown and NASA-supported PMARC work is closer to impressive people entering a market than founders surfaced by the market itself. The pedigree is not the sin; the absence of a specific moment when Perceptron ML’s founders personally felt the legal problem is.

**Harvey’s** estimated $300 million ARR and Legora’s estimated $100 million ARR make legal AI consensus, not magically weird. Perceptron ML flips this score into invest territory only if sophisticated buyers or investors reject its customer-controlled, built-to-order model as too slow or too services-heavy while named law firms repeatedly pay for precisely that architecture. No such passes, named customers, revenue, or renewals appear in the dossier. Perceptron ML may be underfollowed, but being small beside four heavily funded competitors is not the same as being misunderstood. “Magically weird” needs a hidden door; right now Perceptron ML looks like a private-deployment door already visible to Harvey, Legora, Anthropic, and every ambitious legal CIO.

**Flock Safety** is the physical-verifiability parallel because a camera on a street produces an observable artifact before a spreadsheet explains it. Perceptron ML can produce a weaker but genuine artifact: 38.4 billable hours captured and 7.5 recovered during one displayed workweek. I can understand the recovered time, but I cannot independently install Perceptron ML, inspect the underlying matters, or verify whether the counterfactual billings would have been lost. Perceptron ML therefore sits well below Flock’s photographable reality; its value remains mediated by a case study published by Perceptron ML itself.

**38.4 captured hours** point to my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Perceptron ML will be a services-heavy private-AI integrator for law firms producing $1 million to $3 million in ARR, with Michael Marcotte serving as sole CEO and Peyton Marcotte no longer operating full-time. Perceptron ML should find demand because controlled legal deployments solve a real fear, but separate infrastructure inside every customer environment turns learning into implementation labor rather than software compounding. The same pressure will force the sibling tiebreaker question into the open. That can make a respectable business; it does not make the check I write with my own money.

**Michael Marcotte’s** formal-verification experience is the strongest bull case against that forecast: Perceptron ML could turn primary-source gating into trusted infrastructure, use the 38.4-hour case study as the first wedge, and convert bespoke deployments into reusable legal-workflow components. Peyton’s 5,000 LinkedIn followers and NASA-supported project also suggest more Rizz and maker energy than a generic two-engineer résumé. The HQ Trivia exception still does not rescue Perceptron ML because there is no independently observable user pull, and the unstated pattern still cuts against Perceptron ML: neither founder is publicly *of* legal practice, the round sits in a consensus AI category, and no CEO is named. One verifiable event would flip my verdict: a named Am Law 200 firm renewing a Perceptron ML deployment above $250,000 ARR after twelve months under a publicly designated Marcotte CEO. Until then, this is a rebuttal, not a hard no—but it is a pass.
