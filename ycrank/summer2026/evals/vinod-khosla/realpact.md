# RealPact -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Ranvir Deshmukh’s prior CourseMe exit and the SignPact-to-RealPact pivot make RealPact a fast-learning software company with a capital profile too small for my edge. RealPact’s analytical signature is high founder learning rate paired with low required-capital intensity: capable founders may build a valuable workflow business, but no economic mechanism breaks. The $2.01 billion transaction-management market describes a slice of software spending, not the disappearance of an incumbent regime. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself” does not mean every competent SaaS company is a great Khosla company.

**Slash** is the high rung on learning rate because its documented Kanye West pivot revealed founders who converted an anomaly into a new financial product, helping justify a $100 million Series C. RealPact shares the repeat-founder mechanism: Deshmukh and Erik Peterson built CourseMe to 4,000 monthly users and approximately 90% Dartmouth penetration, then moved from SignPact’s $9.99 e-signatures into brokerage operations. RealPact diverges because the dossier documents evolution but not the insight behind it—no stated error, rejected thesis, or customer discovery that explains why external property-record retrieval became the wedge. A pivot without its causal lesson is evidence of motion, not yet proof of exceptional learning.

**DoorDash** sits on the low rung of my required-capital ladder because its early roadmap lacked a scientific or capital barrier; founder ambition, distribution, and eventual category scale had to override that mismatch. RealPact exhibits the same capital-profile weakness without DoorDash’s enormous flow-of-commerce consequence: $500,000 funds a three-person software company selling pilots, while RealPact identifies no foundation-model training, fleet, plant, or other milestone requiring a $50 million check. RealPact may be capital-efficient. That is attractive to many investors. It makes my capital an undifferentiated commodity.

RealPact’s $2.01 billion market and Four Seasons Sotheby’s pilot define the consequence mechanism over the next 18 months: jurisdiction integrations accumulate, contract population improves, and brokerages reduce administrative labor. RealPact can compound workflow depth, but the endpoint remains faster transactions inside the existing brokerage system; SkySlope already supplies AI, forms, compliance, voice assistance, and back-office products to more than 900,000 professionals. Even complete execution by RealPact does not eliminate brokers, title infrastructure, MLS systems, or public-record agencies. This is a 20–30% optimization play unless RealPact expands from transaction administration into replacing a named labor pool measured in tens of billions.

RealPact’s technical-risk score would flip if public-record reconciliation became a falsifiable technical breakthrough rather than integration labor—for example, independently verified contract-grade accuracy across thousands of inconsistent county systems with materially lower error rates than human coordinators. RealPact currently faces stale records, model errors, unfinished SOC 2 work, and jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction maintenance. Those are serious execution and liability risks, but serious is not the same as non-consensus science. RealPact retrieves records and populates contracts using capabilities already demonstrated in 2024 research and partially offered by ListedKit and SkySlope; the required miracles count is zero.

RealPact’s founders resemble the **Glean** exception on the consensus-defiance axis only weakly. Glean’s domain-expert continuity was tolerable because enterprise information asymmetry offered large labor displacement and capital-scale ambition; RealPact’s Dartmouth founders are outsiders to brokerage operations, which protects RealPact from the career-expert red flag. Outsider status alone is not defiance. RealPact provides no controversial claim the founders made against real-estate orthodoxy, no expert forecast they disproved, and no proprietary stack that incumbents dismissed as impossible. McKinsey forecast fewer than one million American cellphones by 2000; reality produced 109 million. I need RealPact’s equivalent disagreement, not merely youth.

RealPact’s prior SignPact pivot and current property-record wedge predict that by July 2029 RealPact will have narrowed from an “AI-native OS” into a property-record intake and contract-automation product producing $2 million–$5 million ARR while remaining independent. RealPact’s learning rate should find a sellable workflow, while its low capital and technical-risk profile will keep the outcome inside vertical SaaS economics. Geographic fragmentation will make broad brokerage-OS coverage expensive enough to force focus, but not technically difficult enough to create a black-swan moat.

RealPact’s strongest bull case is the **Slash** exception: YC pedigree, a prior exit, a documented product pivot, paying brokerages, and a firm-wide Four Seasons Sotheby’s pilot could let learning rate override modest consequence. RealPact could turn fragmented government portals into proprietary operational data and become the default transaction layer before SkySlope reacts. The objection stings because RealPact has more pre-seed validation than most companies. It does not win today because no exclusivity, retention, revenue amount, accuracy advantage, or structural barrier supports that compounding story, while SkySlope already processes more than three million transactions annually. I would flip to Invest if RealPact converted the Sotheby’s pilot into deployment across at least 100 offices and demonstrated proprietary record-resolution accuracy that improves measurably with every transaction.
