# Keres -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The question I want to ask Keres is what went wrong on its last test flight and what the founders changed afterward. The dossier cannot answer it. Christopher Palazzolo spent more than four years at Boom Supersonic working across maintenance, avionics, flight testing, and XB-1 production operations, then served as H3X’s Head of Operations. That is relevant experience. But it supplies no particular obstacle he overcame at Keres, no workaround, and no elapsed time. My most important founder test remains unanswered.

Garmur’s interesting claim is that it destroys the target and brings the aircraft home. Anduril’s Roadrunner-M can return when it has not been expended in an engagement. If Keres can survive a successful interception, the distinction could matter enormously. But the YC description supplies no flight result demonstrating that sequence. Recovery also has to survive an economic test: inspection, repair, and turnaround must leave the next engagement cheaper. Without those measurements, “reusable” describes an intended capability rather than an established advantage.

The strongest argument for investing now is that Palazzolo has worked on precisely the unglamorous parts of aircraft development that an outsider might overlook. Maintenance and production experience could help the team design an interceptor that is economical to fly repeatedly. DIU’s 2025 counter-UAS demonstrations and follow-on funding establish that buyers are looking for alternatives. This fits my willingness to back industrial companies when founders produce concrete technical proof. Keres has the relevant experience, but the proof that activates that pattern is absent. My exceptions for unconventional teams likewise depend on a working artifact or unmistakable demand; neither is documented here.

Who wants this particular aircraft badly enough to help a crude version work? The October 12, 2026 media profile does not answer that. There is no documented operator repeatedly testing Garmur or asking for another unit. Fortem’s Army and DHS contracts show that procurement is possible, while DIU’s integration and safety requirements identify some of the schlep involved. They do not establish Keres’s access to a buyer. Nor does the dossier connect the idea to a specific problem either founder personally encountered. I also see no documented product revision prompted by evidence. Those are unanswered questions, not evidence of obstinacy, a manufactured idea, or dependence on a promised partnership.

I would pass on the evidence available. Keres does not trigger a demonstrated fatal red flag; it leaves the central investment gate unfulfilled. The score therefore uses the missing-evidence defaults rather than treating an undisclosed test program as a failed one. One verifiable condition would change my decision: a dated flight-test record showing a specific interception or recovery failure, the founders’ subsequent fix, and successful repeated interception-and-recovery flights using the revised aircraft. That would reveal both the machine and how its builders behave when reality resists them.
