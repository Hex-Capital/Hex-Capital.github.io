# Kailash Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Kailash Labs has shipped something real. What I cannot find is the episode that would make me bet on the founders: something broke, they found a way around it, and a user’s situation changed. Marlin’s May 14 release establishes that they can build. It does not establish how they behave when blocked. The apparent move from video models to factory robots could be a good discovery. Without the observation that caused it, I cannot distinguish learning from another idea.

The strongest evidence is the downloadable model and public CLI. Sharma’s robot-learning research and precision-welding motion planning make that output more credible than a fashionable project attached to an impressive résumé. But the artifact deserves the credit, not the credentials. The reported fifth-place TimeLens-Bench result is weaker evidence: the authors themselves describe annotation errors in video benchmarks. Identifying those errors shows technical judgment; proposing a better evaluation pipeline does not yet demonstrate a shipped correction. And a working video model does not establish a working robot station ([research blog](https://www.kailashlabs.com/blog/marlin-2b-the-map-was-wrong)).

There is some evidence that people want what this team builds. Marlin has 934 monthly downloads and nine Hugging Face Spaces using it. Those Spaces matter more to me than its 610 likes because someone incorporated the model into software. This fits the pattern I like of technical users adopting a programmable building block. But the dossier does not identify a narrow group returning to solve an urgent problem, and this adoption belongs to the earlier product. Kailash cannot carry developer downloads across a product change and count them as demand from manufacturers ([model card](https://huggingface.co/NemoStation/Marlin-2B)).

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders may have found the ugly work worth doing. Their robotics backgrounds are relevant, and factory walkthroughs followed by seven-day pilots could put them close enough to customers to learn quickly. A removable station with no customer capex lowers the cost of trying it. It also transfers equipment financing and redeployment burdens somewhere, potentially onto Kailash. Formic already offers managed automation, so the opportunity depends on what these founders discover and execute at a particular station. Prior welding work provides domain context, but the dossier does not connect a specific firsthand frustration to this offering. Nor does it show which customer observation caused the change in direction ([company website](https://www.kailashlabs.com/)).

I would pass today. The working-prototype exception is the strongest challenge to that decision: good builders sometimes deserve a check before their customer story is complete. Here, however, the demonstrated artifact belongs to the earlier offering, with neither independently validated robot performance nor unmistakable factory pull to bridge the gap. I see no documented dependence on a grand launch or major partner, and the founders’ shared IIT Bombay background gives no basis for calling the relationship hastily assembled. Those red flags are absent; the resourcefulness gate remains unmet. One customer-verified deployment would flip my decision if it documented a specific production blocker, a founder-built workaround shipped within weeks, and paid repeat operation with measured cycle time and uptime. That would show Kailash turning its technical ability into something a factory needs.
