# Dualizer -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Dualizer has a technical proposition I can understand and a deal I cannot yet underwrite. Post-money valuation: undisclosed. Competing term sheets: undisclosed. Whether my money would be the first genuinely scarce check: unknown. YC membership proves neither an auction nor a bargain. The $100,000 competition prize is useful non-dilutive capital, but it says nothing about the price of equity. I will not manufacture cheap optionality from financing silence.

The potentially valuable asset is the deterministic simulation engine underneath KinetIQ. Vantage proposes changes that engineers approve; that architecture avoids making an unreliable language model the final authority on chemical-process physics. But where does the scarce advantage accumulate? Dualizer’s forward-deployed role calls for proprietary customer data, equipment models, and custom kinetics. That could embed the product in consequential engineering decisions, matching my preference for software that reorganizes difficult physical operations. It could also produce bespoke implementation work while customers retain the valuable data. Neither outcome is established. Meanwhile, the January 2026 demonstration of natural-language control over AVEVA shows that conversational access alone is already reproducible.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ ability to cross that boundary. Chris Donahue brings AVEVA experience and simulation teaching; Nathan Liu combines controls and ML research with a reported 17,000-plus deployments at his separate prior venture. Dongha Kim adds software and scientific-computing experience. These are more useful together than three generic AI résumés. Winning first place among 531 teams provides additional technical encouragement. Still, prior deployments belong to the founder’s operating record, not Dualizer’s customer traction. The missing receipt is a plant engineer paying to rely on this engine with measured operating data. The website’s disclosed calibration demonstrations use synthetic histories, and the September announcement still described the first paid industrial pilot as a next step.

That next step could become a legitimate venture moment, but the dossier does not connect round proceeds to a defined experiment, acceptance metric, and deadline. I cannot award credit for the capital plan I would like them to adopt. Nor can I delete the next financing from a financial model that has no disclosed pricing, margins, burn, or runway. Customer-specific deployment creates a concrete question about labor per installation; it does not establish bad economics. Likewise, 246 LinkedIn followers and accelerator selection establish neither owned distribution nor conversion. Those dimensions receive the missing-evidence defaults, rather than invented positive or negative conclusions.

My exceptions do not rescue this check today. An expensive AI investment can become underwritable through demonstrated paid conversion and retention; neither is documented here. A person-level financing exception needs a deliberate structure and capital plan, which are also absent. The deterministic engine and engineer approval weaken the generic AI-wrapper objection, but incumbent overlap remains real. Three co-founders do not establish an equal-vote committee, and hiring advertisements do not establish a financing factory. I pass because the evidence does not yet establish the purchase mechanism or the investment terms. One verifiable result would reopen diligence: a paid industrial pilot meeting a pre-agreed accuracy threshold on withheld measured plant data within 90 days, with its fee covering deployment labor and compute. That would begin to distinguish an embedded engineering business from an impressive demonstration.
