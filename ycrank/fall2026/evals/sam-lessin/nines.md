# Nines -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Nines has picked a physical bottleneck where solving the problem could release expensive idle compute. That makes fiber commissioning worth investigating. It does not yet make this round worth buying. Post-money valuation, competing term sheets, and whether my money would be the first scarce check are all undisclosed. I cannot call the deal overpriced, but I cannot underwrite cheap uncertainty either. My money stays in my pocket.

The strongest bull case is the choice to begin with commissioning rather than maintenance inside operating racks. A bounded installation task could offer a cleaner experiment than immediately trusting robots with live infrastructure. Tiantian Li’s large-scale inference responsibilities at Meta and autonomous-underwater-vehicle experience, alongside Taerim Eom’s Patreon livestream development, give the team relevant operating context. This fits my pattern of backing software that reorganizes difficult physical operations. But the documented bridge is incomplete: software delivery and compute experience are present; successful robotic manipulation at a customer facility is not. The website sequence is identified as simulated inspection.

What exact truth does the check reveal? Nines names fiber patching as its initial task, but the dossier supplies no capital-funded test with a deadline, acceptance metric, or budget. A product sequence—patch fibers, then swap drives, reseat optics, and restart servers—is not itself a venture moment. Microsoft’s published transceiver-manipulation work makes the task more technically plausible while showing that prospective customers can develop overlapping capabilities internally. The valuable experiment would establish whether an outside supplier can deliver reliable commissioning economically enough that an operator chooses to buy it. That experiment is conceivable; it is not documented.

The proprietary access is equally unproven. Approximately 252,000 views on the November 13 launch establish attention, while the request for operator introductions establishes prospecting. Neither demonstrates owned workflow causing a purchase. I would not attribute metric theater to the founders because a research dossier reports views, but I give those views no conversion credit. Deployment data might eventually become a defensible asset; today that is an inference. Likewise, service-contract monetization is inferred, and pricing, margins, burn, and runway are undisclosed. Remove the next financing from the model and there is no documented cash engine to examine. That earns the missing-evidence default, not a claim that the business is uneconomic.

I pass on the evidence available. The objection that could sting is straightforward: these founders may understand the compute bottleneck well enough to turn one constrained task into paid operational access before broader robotics competitors arrive. But the exceptions to my price and capital-plan gates require receipts—conversion, cash economics, proprietary access, or a demonstrated operating bridge. Nines has not supplied those yet. One verifiable condition would reopen my decision: completion of a paid operator-site fiber-commissioning trial that meets pre-agreed throughput and connection-integrity thresholds while producing positive contribution after hardware, supervision, and rework costs. That would establish something launch attention and simulation cannot: a customer buying a functioning base business. I would then have a reason to negotiate the price.
