# Insurf -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Insurf quantifies pain, not consequence. Forty prior-authorization requests and 13 staff-hours per practice each week define an expensive administrative workflow, but the dossier never converts that baseline into national spending, patients denied care, or a plausible 10–100x change. A $1.32 billion denial-management software market is respectable. It is not evidence that an unlikely success rewrites healthcare economics.

Inveto is thoughtfully assembled: source-cited drafting, deterministic matching, physician attestation, controlled submission, appeals, payer tracking, and payment evidence. But integration breadth is not technical discontinuity. There is no measured accuracy, approval-rate lift, staff-time reduction, or cost comparison against CoverMyMeds, athenaOne workflows, Silna, or manual specialists. Dependency on incumbent interfaces makes the current product look more like systems integration around prevailing models than a new decision architecture.

The strongest bull case is real. CMS decision-time rules and the January 2027 FHIR mandate may force electronic adoption; selected specialty practices are already piloting the product; and every resolved case can preserve policy versions, submitted language, payer decisions, and payment outcomes. That could create the scarce-workflow pattern I like: use produces proprietary evidence, which improves the next decision. But Insurf admits that pilot cases are selected and non-random, while its dataset remains insufficient for dependable coverage decisions. A possible learning loop earns no credit until it measurably compounds.

Capital allocation is only partly disciplined. Keeping production PHI disabled until BAAs and launch gates are satisfied is sensible, and deterministic matching plus staff-controlled submission addresses known LLM administrative errors. Yet the company has not named its dominant kill risk or specified a decisive test, threshold, cost, and stop-or-pivot response. SOC 2 remains unfinished, AWS monitoring is incomplete, and branch protection is still open. These are work items, not a risk-removal plan.

Chang Lu supplies clinical proximity through Brown’s BS/MD program and cancer-modeling research; Bryan Chung adds applied mathematics, computer science, physics, two IEEE publications, and USACO Platinum experience. That is useful complementarity for a two-person pre-seed team. The dossier supplies no observed belief revision, difficult recruit, or evidence that either founder has converted practice feedback into a materially different product thesis. The closest known-exception pattern is Block: a narrow operational wedge expanding into consequential infrastructure. The material difference is that Insurf has not established either an American Express-scale value pool or an instigator’s demonstrated ability to mutate the route.

My 36-month base case is $2–6 million ARR as a specialty-practice authorization and appeals vendor, not a category-defining healthcare decision platform. There is no disclosed bottom-up model showing cost per completed case, implementation labor, gross margin, or parity with existing staff and outsourced revenue-cycle services. I pass today. One result flips the decision: a prospective multi-practice study showing at least a 5x reduction in staff time per case, no deterioration in clinical or administrative accuracy, and profitable pricing below the fully loaded incumbent cost.
