# FlowManual -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and I can imagine contractors treating every bid, contract, invoice, and change order as one continuously reconciled ledger. FlowManual’s source-line comparisons are a small leak from that future. But the dossier does not map the intervening transitions—how review becomes purchasing authority, how corrections compound, or why this team owns the resulting system of record. Today’s artifact supports a possible future; it does not yet make that future feel inevitable.

The present wound is much clearer. Contractors reportedly lose hundreds of hours each month to page-by-page review, and one missed clause can create $5,000–$10,000 of exposure. FlowManual attacks that leak by placing scope, price, deadline, and exclusion changes beside their source text. That is useful, unglamorous workflow repair. It still does not identify the broken rule or gatekeeper creating the rent, however. The product saves estimators time and catches mistakes, but it has not shown that it transfers durable economic control from an institution to an individual.

David Bae and Michael Lin built quickly after April 18, interviewed hundreds of construction professionals, and raised $500,000 from YC roughly a month later. Michael also has technical research experience and participated in work associated with Dijie’s growth from $3,000 to $336,000 MRR. Those are encouraging indicators of velocity and commercial exposure. They are not pre-company obsession: neither founder is documented living inside construction, shipping earlier construction artifacts, or recruiting around an independently developed thesis. College-roommate history explains trust, not why these two were the first believers in this problem. David is identified as CEO, but I would still verify the ownership split and contractual tiebreaker before investing.

The strongest bull case is that the team has already assembled a broad working product while behaving responsibly around a dangerous workflow. Exact-line citations, audit logs, tenant isolation, zero-retention processing, and customer-controlled data pooling are thoughtful choices when an incorrect annotation can become a five-figure mistake. The free tier also lowers the cost of discovering strange behavior. Yet the dossier reports no completed review, repeat cohort, paid conversion, or contractor who entrusted FlowManual with a consequential buyout or invoice decision. Hundreds of interviews are learning; they are not immersion evidence from users risking money or reputation.

My harder objection is stack position. FlowManual calls itself construction’s back office, but today it is an application atop Anthropic and Azure, surrounded by Document Crunch, Trunk Tools, LightTable, and Primepoint. Document Crunch already reports more than 500 customers and is being acquired by Trimble. Customer-specific price history could become valuable, but the decision not to pool data without opt-in means each account must generate its own advantage, and no retention or correction-scale evidence shows that happening. The current breadth—from drawings through invoices and change orders—looks more like validation surface area than a controlled bottleneck.

I pass. My 36-month expectation is that FlowManual narrows into bid leveling and invoice reconciliation for MEP contractors and remains below $1 million ARR, because the quantified workflow pain is stronger than either its infrastructure position or its founder-specific advantage. The verifiable condition that flips me is three named contractors renewing paid deployments after six months while each documents at least $50,000 in recovered leakage. That would turn the product from an articulate document wrapper into an artifact people repeatedly trust with costly decisions.
