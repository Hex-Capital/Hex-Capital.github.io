# CoArena -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

CoArena’s contested truth is precise: fixed benchmarks become training data, while continuously generated real-work tasks reveal computer-use failures that static tests cannot. What would prove this wrong? If its rankings and paired trajectories fail to predict production reliability better than OSWorld. The company has shipped the experiment—14 agents, 57 measures, blind votes, and full action trajectories—but has not yet published task-category data, annotation fields, or an independent predictive comparison. This is a falsifiable secret, not merely an AI slogan, but the decisive result remains missing.

The strongest evidence is economic. CoArena generated $60,000 within two weeks while users doubled weekly. Its free arena attracts tasks and judgments; AI labs and robotics companies then license the resulting data and evaluations through consumption-priced contracts. The 2,550-person hackathon and Product Hunt launch describe the acquisition funnel, while the revenue proves that somebody will pay. Yet the customers, contract values, sales cycles, margins, and renewal behavior are undisclosed. Demand has been demonstrated; repeatable distribution and fund-return arithmetic have not.

The harder question is whether CoArena creates a monopoly or merely supplies a feature to larger evaluation platforms. Computer-use workflows form a coherent beachhead, and each battle can compound into a proprietary corpus of unseen tasks, paired trajectories, and preference labels. But “can” is doing too much work. The dataset page reports no annotation fields, LMArena already has more than three million votes and $250 million in financing, and Patronus AI and Braintrust approach adjacent agent-evaluation problems from simulation and production traces. CoArena has differentiated data collection, but no structural barrier preventing these companies—or a frontier lab—from reproducing it.

Trust is also part of the product. Coasty Systems operates the benchmark while developing its own computer-use agent, and raw prompts and unmasked screenshots reach model providers without SOC 2, ISO 27001, or an outside audit. Excluding Coasty’s agent from new matches mitigates the visible conflict without removing the institutional one. A benchmark becomes valuable when rival labs accept it as neutral; operator conflict and sensitive-data exposure therefore weaken both distribution and the prospective data moat.

The founders are substantially better than generic benchmark entrepreneurs. Prateek Jannu abandoned Stanford graduate admission to build Coasty, while Nitish Kovuru brings computer-vision study, AI teaching, and enterprise-agent experience. Together they previously built a computer-use agent reporting 82.81% on OSWorld before launching CoArena. That establishes domain commitment and some working history, but the dossier does not establish founder ownership, compensation, secondary liquidity, or a relationship predating Coasty. The foundation appears sound, not yet fully verified.

CoArena fits two revealed patterns: it supplies the AI production stack, and it makes contested machine performance legible. The opposing red flag is more important: crowdsourced AI evaluation is already a financed category, and the current corpus has not produced a verified zero-to-one result. The OpenAI exception does not rescue this—there, monopoly uncertainty was overridden by an unmistakable technical discontinuity; here, early revenue validates urgency but not technological uniqueness. My 36-month base case is acquisition by LMArena for its computer-use corpus rather than emergence as an independent category owner. I would flip to Invest if one unaffiliated frontier lab signed a $500,000-plus annual contract specifically because CoArena’s trajectories were independently shown to predict production failures better than OSWorld. Until then: a real business, but not yet a last mover.
