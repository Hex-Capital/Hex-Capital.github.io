# Locke -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A startup should be able to understand legislation, reach the right policymaker, satisfy procurement rules, and submit a credible government proposal without assembling four firms and an internal policy department. Locke has one artifact leaking from that future: more than $500,000 in annualized revenue after six weeks. What is missing is the causal bridge. The dossier does not show which parts of government affairs become autonomous, which remain relationship-bound, or why Locke accumulates leverage as the work moves from agents to lobbyists and back again.

The present system is easier to decode. Legacy firms charge prices designed for incumbents, while research, lobbying, procurement, and proposal writing sit in separate rooms. Locke returns some agency to early-stage companies by putting that workflow behind one accountable interface. That is a real broken rule, but the rent remains fuzzy: there is no quoted incumbent fee, procurement delay, or customer outcome showing how much time or money Locke releases. I understand the irritation. I cannot yet measure the escape.

Parth Badhwar has unusually coherent domain exposure for this stage: the White House, Deloitte policy work, a presidential campaign, the DNC, and GTM at Clipbook. He should know both the institutional maze and how software is sold into it. But this is still an employer chronology, not evidence of a pre-company obsession expressed through independent artifacts. The team is listed as two while YC identifies only Badhwar as an active founder, and the Justinian-to-Locke entity transition adds ambiguity. Neither proves a leadership problem, but I will not infer clear accountability where the dossier cannot identify the other founder or the decision structure.

The strongest opposing case is painful: clients in defense, healthcare, and GovTech apparently began paying almost immediately. At pre-seed, a $500,000 run-rate can be more revealing than a beautiful technical moat. Yet the accounts are unnamed, the number is annualized, and there is no pricing, retention, proposal win, regulatory outcome, or repeated user behavior. A customer buying urgent expert labor is not yet evidence that an AI-native government-affairs product has changed how the customer behaves. Locke must also carry lobbying-disclosure obligations and accuracy responsibility for briefs and proposals; those are operating systems, not footnotes.

Below the visible AI story, I do not find a controlled enabling layer. Quorum already serves more than 2,000 organizations with policy data, AI intelligence, stakeholder management, advocacy tools, and professional services. Locke may eventually own valuable client-specific outcome data, but the dossier labels that possibility as inferred and provides no data scale, retention, or proprietary asset. Today the company appears to combine third-party AI capabilities with scarce human policy labor. Monitoring stakeholder engagement through Slack also raises a data-custody question, and no limiting architecture is described.

My 36-month forecast is that Locke reaches roughly $4 million to $8 million in ARR and is acquired by Quorum as a services-and-execution layer rather than becoming an independent government-affairs platform. That outcome follows from the pairing here: a real institutional rent and startling demand, but no demonstrated lower-stack control. I am Neutral today, not because the revenue is small, but because it may be measuring a good lobbying firm before it measures a compounding software company. One verifiable condition flips me: a twelve-month cohort showing at least five clients renewed while ARR per policy employee materially increased.
