# Hickory & Company -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Joshua Howland has operated a fintech and watched it close. That experience can produce specific knowledge that survives the company. His reported 500,000 accounts and $10 million revenue at Seis belong to his founder record, not Hickory’s traction. Scott Grow previously worked with him there and brings more than 20 years of compliance experience; Kevin Anderson brings banking-systems engineering. This team has encountered the institutions it wants to sell into. What remains unproven is whether Howland personally built or sold the compliance machinery Hickory now offers.

The strongest argument for Hickory & Company is that bank compliance contains institutional knowledge that a general-purpose agent cannot acquire from a prompt. Sponsor banks remain accountable for partners they do not directly operate. Source-linked research, acceptance criteria, and human approval address that concrete problem. The claimed live-bank integration, although unnamed, suggests the team has crossed beyond a demonstration. A founder-led exception deserves consideration here: deep operating experience and a working deployment can precede a visible moat. But the dossier does not establish exceptional product attachment or a proprietary technical asset sufficient to make that exception decisive.

My central question is what Hickory owns after a competitor reproduces the software. Institution-specific policies and examination records could make replacement expensive. The dossier establishes neither that accumulated asset nor measured dependence on it. Parcha already offers configurable agents and audit logs; Bretton offers investigations across existing systems. Hickory’s published workflow is useful, but usefulness alone does not establish durable ownership of the value. I give no additional moat credit for the team’s experience after counting it under founder knowledge.

There is room to expand. Sponsor-bank oversight leads naturally into partner documentation, complaint evidence, compliance testing, and regulatory research using the same retrieval and approval machinery. The cited $2.3 billion global AML market provides independent evidence of adjacent spending, though Hickory cannot assume that entire budget is accessible. The weaker fit with my investing patterns is how value accumulates: no demonstrated participant network, outsider contribution system, or customer-created distribution loop appears here. Humanfold’s outreach and 22 launch videos establish sales activity. They do not show that one bank makes the product better for another. Public pricing and realized revenue are also absent, so direct value capture receives only the default.

I see a credible specialist software company and remain neutral on the angel investment. Seis’s closure supplies no evidence of misconduct; the dossier also shows no coercive fundraising deadline or founder instability. Human review and tenant restrictions are sensible controls, without proving economical deployment. The unresolved issue is whether institutional learning compounds inside Hickory faster than competitors can reproduce its workflows. My verifiable flip condition is a documented renewal in which a bank demonstrates that Hickory’s accumulated control mappings and examination history materially increase the cost of replacing it. That would turn the proposed durable asset into observed customer dependence.
