# Risklytics -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The obstacle is licensing, and Risklytics has not yet turned it into a placement. Alexander Risio passed an insurance licensing examination, which is real action, but the company is still obtaining producer licenses state by state and cannot bind coverage. Carrier access creates a second blockage: insurers want placement history, so the founders propose working through wholesale brokers and MGAs. The crucial result is still missing. I see motion, but not yet a shipped workaround that changed the situation.

The best evidence is the change of route. The founders put Ember, a property-level catastrophe model, into beta, decided it was a non-starter, and moved to commercial brokerage while remaining attached to frontier risk. That resembles a running back changing direction without leaving the field. But the dossier does not name the observation that killed Ember, how quickly the decision followed, or what customers taught them. It could be disciplined learning, or merely a second plausible idea.

There is some fierce-nerd evidence, though less than the product description implies. Ember reached beta, and Risklytics says it has built a continuously updated database of policy forms, endorsements, regulatory filings, and carrier treatment of AI exclusions. Yet there is no database size, benchmark, caught exclusion, working API, or bound policy. The company has an architecture and a prior artifact, not the concrete pre-round proof spike that often distinguishes an unusually strong technical team.

Who wants the crude product now? The dossier cannot answer. YC acceptance, 123 LinkedIn followers, and a Center for Data Innovation feature are investor or media legibility, not demand. There is no revenue, named customer, LOI, repeated use, or placement. ISO’s 2026 AI exclusions make the problem timely, but Alton Risk already advertises overlapping gap analysis and carrier matching. A $335.87 billion brokerage market does not tell us whether ten robotics companies urgently want Risklytics.

The bull case is that two young founders went full-time in May 2026, one has ML and prior-startup experience, the other has finance exposure, and they chose a market full of licensing, policy forms, carrier negotiations, and other useful schleps. The exception for very young founders represented by Bindwell does not carry over: the shared mechanism is technical ambition before conventional credentials, but Bindwell had numerical and laboratory validation plus direct exposure to the problem. Risklytics has neither disclosed founder-originated need nor equivalent proof. Worse, its distribution plan currently depends on “partnering” with wholesalers, MGAs, and carriers—the very institutions blocking progress.

I would pass today. This looks more like a capable team still searching than a brokerage being pulled into existence. I would flip if Risklytics documents ten founder-sold, bound policies for named AI or robotics companies, with at least three customers arriving through unsolicited referrals. That single result would show that the founders defeated licensing and carrier resistance themselves and found a narrow well of urgent demand.
