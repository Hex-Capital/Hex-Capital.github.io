# Hop Aero -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: defense customers now fund rocket-based contested logistics, but the variable that determines whether Hop Aero becomes large is not 15-minute delivery—it is whether Rook can repeatedly move 250 kilograms over 750 kilometers at a cost and reliability that converts experimental funding into procurement.

The performance target is measurable, but the dossier supplies no mission-cost estimate, reliability threshold, launch cadence, or procurement volume. A tethered small-scale flight and an engine-and-igniter hot-fire validate pieces of the system, not hypersonic re-entry, landing on an unprepared surface, or operational turnaround. Hop Aero has therefore identified a potentially decisive technical threshold without establishing the economic mechanism behind it.

The proposed container launch and infrastructure-independent landing could materially change delivery speed and deployment flexibility. Yet this remains a designed advantage rather than a demonstrated margin or adoption shift. The inferred why-now—broader AFRL awards and the March 2026 Part 450 transition—is modest beside the capital intensity, licensing burden, and government-backed competition from SpaceX, Rocket Lab, Inversion, and Outpost. There is no evidence yet that Rook has better mission economics than aircraft, forward-positioned inventory, or competing rocket systems.

The $1,249,909 Air Force Phase II SBIR award is meaningful pre-seed pull from the correct buyer. It is substantially more informative than social followers or press coverage, but it pays for development rather than repeated cargo delivery. The technical architecture is more durable than a single component: propulsion, re-entry, landing, payload integration, and hopOS could accumulate operational state and flight data. Today, however, the owned workflow ends before the crucial full-scale point-to-point flight.

The strongest bull case is the team. Matija Milenovic previously founded a propulsion company and sold its IP, led a 20-plus-person supersonic-UAV team, and scaled loitering-munition production from 10 to more than 100 units monthly. Jacob Balaj adds Marine Corps avionics and integration experience across SpaceX, Virgin Orbit, Masten, and SpinLaunch. The relevant **Anduril** analogy is a defense-hardware team using product engineering against legacy procurement; the material difference is that Hop Aero has not yet shown either a strategic supplier vacuum or product-style economics sufficient to override the physical-business red flag.

My 36-month base case is that Hop Aero wins $5–15 million of additional defense prototype funding but still has no routine paid cargo service and negligible commercial revenue. I would not invest today because the market and economic gates remain open, despite unusually strong founder-market fit. The verifiable flip condition is a named DoD production or mission-services contract following a full-scale 250-kilogram, 750-kilometer point-to-point flight at a disclosed all-in mission cost—not another R&D award.
