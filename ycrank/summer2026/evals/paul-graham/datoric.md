# Datoric -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Datoric’s 300,000 active contributors make it a schlep company whose operational cleverness exceeds its visible hacker genealogy. Datoric is strongest where most founders flinch: recruiting people, policing fraud, obtaining consent, isolating projects, and attaching rights records to individual samples. Datoric is weakest where I usually find the cleanest early signal: neither Nikhil Reddy nor Jeffrey Lin has a public GitHub trail or Hacker News artifact. This is an animal-looking business built by founders whose best hacks happened offstage.

**Coinbase** is the high rung for schlep blindness: Brian Armstrong ran toward compliance while everyone else treated compliance as a reason not to start. Datoric shares the load-bearing mechanism. Residential video, contributor authentication, sample-level licensing, and QA across 300,000 contributors are not peripheral chores; they are Datoric’s product. Datoric diverges because Coinbase’s schlep hardened into regulatory and protocol knowledge, while Scale AI, Sama, and Toloka can copy many of Datoric’s operating processes. The schlep is real. Its permanence is not yet proven.

**Loopt**’s failure mechanism was timing: Sam Altman’s force of personality outran usable mobile infrastructure, making Loopt an exception I funded for the animal rather than the artifact. Datoric shows a milder version of that missing-artifact pattern because both founders lack public repositories and Reddy’s credentials include internships rather than a long hacker-community record. But Datoric is above the Loopt rung: Reddy automated more than 15,000 hours of annotation by bypassing QA and authentication controls, while Lin co-authored VidWork-Bench. Datoric’s hacker evidence is private and adversarial rather than public and generative. That counts, but less than Patrick Collison’s Lisp trail or Drew Houston’s demo.

Nearly seven figures of Datoric revenue in 30 days is an animal track, not a pitch-deck metric. Reddy and Lin had already taken Arzule to more than $16,000 in monthly revenue after five weeks, then abandoned the comfortable path and assembled Datoric’s contributor network, datasets, and project machinery. Over 18 months, that resourcefulness compounds because every fraudulent submission, rejected sample, and strange customer request becomes another operating rule competitors must rediscover. Datoric has the Airbnb override signal: even if data collection initially looks like a commodity service, founders who repeatedly manufacture revenue quickly deserve more weight than the category deserves.

Nikhil Reddy’s 15,000-hour annotation exploit makes Datoric plausibly organic, but not as cleanly organic as Dropbox arising from Drew Houston repeatedly losing a USB stick. Datoric’s thesis comes directly from Reddy seeing how contributors could defeat platform controls, while Lin’s robotics and ML work explains the demand for better video and physical-interaction data. The counterfactual is simple: if Reddy merely found an annotation arbitrage and never personally encountered the buyer-side rights problem, Datoric falls toward a market-opportunity idea. Verified evidence that Reddy built the collection and provenance system first to solve his own customer engagement would move Datoric into the top rung.

**Stripe** is the useful single-anchor parallel for Datoric’s founder configuration. The Collisons had already built Auctomatic together, so the second company inherited shared language, technical trust, and scar tissue. Reddy and Lin likewise built Arzule before Datoric, and Datoric benefits from a two-founder configuration spanning operations, software, ML, and robotics. The parallel has a limit: Arzule launched only in March 2026, so Datoric has months of demonstrated collaboration rather than years or a sibling bond. It is a strong pair, but the dossier cannot yet prove the pair survives prolonged failure.

Nearly seven figures in Datoric’s preceding 30 days implies a roughly $12 million annualized starting rate, though project revenue is not necessarily recurring. My 36-month forecast is that Datoric reaches $35–60 million in ARR-equivalent revenue, with more than half still coming from custom multimodal collection programs rather than catalog licensing or software. Datoric gets there because the 300,000-contributor machine can absorb voice, video, robotics, and computer-use jobs faster than customers can assemble compliant networks themselves. The same low hacker-genealogy signal predicts an operations-heavy data supplier, not a software platform that becomes the Stripe of training data.

**Scale AI** is the strongest bear case against Datoric: Scale AI already has a $1.5 billion annualized run rate, while Datoric’s nearly seven figures could be one concentrated, non-recurring project and its 300,000 “active” contributors could be a loose registration count. Datoric has no named customer, renewal, retention rate, comparative error rate, or structural barrier preventing Scale AI, Sama, or Toloka from copying project isolation and consent records. The missing GitHub trail also violates my unstated hacker pattern, and the March 2026 Arzule pivot could conceal opportunism rather than conviction. But the bear case loses today because Datoric’s founders have twice generated unusually fast revenue and Reddy’s annotation exploit supplies direct knowledge of the fraud surface. Stripe’s Auctomatic precedent makes the prior startup a positive, while Airbnb’s exception says animal evidence can override an initially unappealing business. I would flip to Pass if, within two quarters, Datoric cannot show repeat purchases from at least two independent customers and its largest customer still supplies more than 70% of revenue.
