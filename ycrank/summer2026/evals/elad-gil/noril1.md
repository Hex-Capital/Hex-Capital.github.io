# Nori -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The **$1,288 L2** makes Nori an execution-forward founder bet trapped inside multi-miracle consumer hardware. Nori’s strongest dimension is Antonio Sitong Li’s shipping profile: his prior anti-cheating product reached 2,000 users and 1.5 million impressions, while his Columbia research covered VR-demonstrated goals for robot teams. Nori’s weakest dimension is architecture: low-cost manufacturing, reliable manipulation, safe teleoperation, transferable skills, and consumer adoption must all work simultaneously. That is closer to a science project than a single miracle.

**Perplexity** is the high rung for founder iteration because Aravind Srinivas repeatedly built the discussed artifact within a week. Nori shares the underlying mechanism: Li moved from relevant robotics research and an independently shipped software product to a working product offer whose first production batch reportedly reached capacity. Nori remains materially below Perplexity because the dossier provides no sequence of robot revisions, delivered units, public repositories, or task-success improvements demonstrating that Nori learns and ships between conversations. The founder signal is credible, but the iteration loop is not yet observable.

**Lyft** is the low rung on miracle architecture because the investment case required an improbable share inversion inside a winner-take-all market. Nori requires several independent outcomes rather than one inversion: manufacture a bimanual robot for roughly $1,300, make dish loading and food preparation reliable, secure in-home teleoperation, and create consumer willingness to tolerate failures. Nori publishes no payload, runtime, safety certification, or task-success rate, so these dependencies cannot yet be collapsed into one validated capability. Nori therefore activates my red flag around capitalization risk, technical risk, and market risk arriving together.

**Gemini Robotics’ 2025 demonstration** of learning some tasks from roughly 100 examples is a real recent catalyst, and Nori receives meaningful market-reopening credit for building after that step-function. Over the next 18 months, however, the same models should lower task-training costs for 1X, Sunday, and Weave; without proprietary fleet data, the catalyst compounds the category faster than it compounds Nori. Nori has identified the puck, but Nori has not shown why it owns the skating lane once embodied models diffuse across manufacturers.

**Nori’s first production batch reached capacity**, but the order count, deposit terms, and delivered-unit count are undisclosed. The counterfactual that flips this dimension is straightforward: Nori would need to disclose paid orders, ship successive cohorts, and show revenue or active robots compounding above 20% month over month. Until then, Nori’s 41,000 launch views and 575 likes are attention, while an unspecified full batch could mean twenty units or two hundred. That is a potentially useful pre-seed signal, not yet evidence that Nori is “just working.”

**Stripe** became an index on e-commerce because every additional merchant generated payment volume through the same rails. Nori’s transferable-skills marketplace could eventually become an index on household robotics: every deployed robot could contribute demonstrations and consume reusable skills. Nori today instead sells proprietary hardware directly, discloses no marketplace activity, and names no fee model; each sale must be won against 1X, Sunday, and Weave. Category growth does not yet flow automatically through Nori, so the marketplace is an architectural option rather than a distribution position.

**Sunday Robotics’ $165 million Series B** and 1X’s disclosed funding of at least $123.5 million make prolonged consumer-hardware competition a difficult capitalization game. My 36-month forecast is that by July 2029 Nori will have pivoted from general household chores to teleoperated light-commercial food-service or hospitality workflows and will remain below $3 million ARR. Nori’s low price and founder shipping ability should keep Nori alive, but constrained environments offer repeatable layouts, measurable labor savings, and fewer privacy problems than unstructured homes.

**Color Genomics** is the strongest objection because workflow infrastructure on top of a commoditizing hard layer can outperform the underlying science bet. Nori could use Gemini-class models as the commodity layer, exploit a price roughly six times below Weave’s $7,999 robot, turn a capacity-filled first batch into fleet data, and make the skills marketplace the index on embodied work. The objection fails today because Nori has disclosed neither one delivered robot nor one repeatably successful task, whereas Color was deliberately structured around an already-usable sequencing layer. I would flip Nori from Neutral to Invest if Nori delivers 500 paid robots and documents more than 70% weekly repeat usage with fewer than 10% of task-hours requiring teleoperation.
