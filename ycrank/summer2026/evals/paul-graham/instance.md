# Instance -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Lucy Cai and Claire Mao have been friends for more than ten years, but Instance has not yet shown the corresponding ten-year trail of animal resourcefulness. That is Instance’s signature: an unusually strong founder pair attacking a problem adjacent to their own technical lives, with less evidence that either founder will crawl through a wall when the obvious route fails. Instance has built a real artifact—10,000 labeled episodes, eight benchmarks, seven robot platforms, and 0.76 macro F1—but artifacts prove ability more readily than determination. The February-to-July pivot from an AI data analyst to robot evaluation is some evidence of speed. It is not yet an Airbnb cereal-box moment.

Stripe is the high rung for co-founder configuration: two people with a long shared history, complementary abilities, and an organic technical obsession. Mao and Cai’s friendship since middle school puts Instance unusually close to that pattern; Mao brings physical-system simulation from NASA JPL and Cai brings robot learning from MIT CSAIL. Instance diverges from Stripe because the Collisons had already built and sold Auctomatic before Stripe, while Instance’s prior AI data-analyst product lasted only months and provides no documented customer or revenue proof. Still, founder pairs cannot manufacture a decade of trust during YC. Instance receives full credit for having what most hastily assembled startups lack.

The polished AI-wrapper failure pattern begins with credentials and ends with plans to hire the people who will build the product. Instance does not fit that pattern technically: Instance already exposes an HTTP API, splits videos into attempts, produces grounded captions, and beats Claude Opus 4.8 by 0.23 macro F1 at less than half the latency. But the resourcefulness ladder measures tracks left before funding, not benchmark quality. Instance has no named customer, revenue stunt, rejection overcome, or manual deployment story. The 10,000 human-labeled episodes are the best available track, yet the dossier never says Mao and Cai labeled them, procured them through some ingenious side channel, or persuaded reluctant robotics labs to contribute them. No Known Exception rescues that silence: Loopt had Altman’s force of personality, and Airbnb had cereal boxes. Instance has a good demo.

Cai’s CSAIL robot-learning work and robot-policy testing make Instance more organic than a market-map startup. The counterfactual that would move Instance into Dropbox territory is simple: Cai repeatedly suffered through manually watching rollouts, built the verifier for her own experiments, and kept using it before deciding to form Instance. The dossier supports the first two links only by inference. Instance’s pivot from a generic AI data analyst also cuts the other way; it suggests Mao and Cai may have searched for a sharper application after starting, rather than starting because this problem would not leave them alone. A dated prototype from Cai’s research workflow or repeat pre-company use would raise this score immediately.

Instance’s schlep compounds if every new robot, camera angle, task description, and customer-approved correction makes its verifier harder to replace. The unpleasant work is real: physical rollouts, scene variation, thousands of videos, human adjudication, and eventually robot resets. Instance has already crossed seven robot platforms, which is more convincing than a slide about universal compatibility. But Instance currently automates the clean software layer—judging recorded footage—while leaving the ugliest physical operation, resetting scenes with a second robot, outside the launched product. Over 18 months, Instance either embraces onsite installations and exception labeling or discovers that Robocurve, Bifrost, and Runway can commoditize the verifier from cleaner open-source and simulated paths. Schlep blindness only helps founders willing to do the whole schlep.

Stripe is also the relevant hacker parallel, though Instance is a rung below it. Cai wrote satellite software at SpaceX, built automated unit-test systems at Amazon, worked on brain-computer-interface pipelines, and helped produce a verifier that runs in 2.0 seconds. Instance therefore has more than credential-only technicality. Yet Cai’s most visible GitHub repository has one star, Mao has no public repository, and neither founder has a documented pre-Instance hacker-community artifact comparable to Patrick Collison’s Lisp work. Instance contains hackers in the occupational sense; the missing evidence is hacker genealogy—the compulsion to ship strange things before anyone assigns them.

Instance reaches $3–7 million in annual recurring revenue within 36 months by becoming the verification layer robotics labs call after every physical rollout, but Instance remains a narrow evaluation product rather than the operating system for robot training. The mechanism is the high-low pairing: Mao and Cai’s durable relationship lets Instance survive repeated customer-specific integrations, while the absence of a demonstrated animal stunt limits how aggressively I assume Instance will absorb resets, onsite deployment, and bespoke labeling. Seven-platform validation gives Instance a credible starting wedge; customer corrections could turn that wedge into a proprietary corpus.

Robocurve’s MIT-licensed tooling, Bifrost’s open-source Manifold, Runway’s world-model research, and Instance’s own unverified 0.76 F1 benchmark form the strongest bear case. Instance could be a technically polished feature launched five months after an unrelated product, just before simulation makes recorded-rollout judgment less valuable. That case stings because the weak public GitHub trail contradicts my unstated hacker pattern, and the pivot plus absent revenue resembles searching rather than relentless pursuit. I still invest because Instance has shipped a differentiated local verifier, processed 10,000 labeled episodes, and paired two technically complementary founders with a decade of trust—facts absent from the generic wrapper pattern. Three paying production customers using Instance repeatedly across distinct robot platforms would push me toward Strong Invest; failure to produce even one externally validated deployment within twelve months would flip me to Pass.
