# Dydema -- Elad Gil Evaluation

REVE’s 2025–2026 releases make EEG classification easier to build; Dydema sells that capability to developers through a hosted API. The controlling variable is whether useful commands can be decoded reliably with little enough calibration that people choose brain input repeatedly. More recordings matter only if they improve that variable. The dossier establishes a modeling service, but it does not establish the data-to-performance relationship that would make consumer adoption accelerate.

The technical evidence makes that distinction consequential. Wizard reports 52% zero-shot left-versus-right imagined-movement accuracy across nine datasets against 50% chance. Dydema appropriately does not offer that zero-shot task; this result does not invalidate its calibrated classifiers. It does, however, leave the low-friction generalization thesis unsupported. Separately, the demonstrated control interface has an approximately 1.3-bits-per-second theoretical ceiling before filtering and errors. That is adverse evidence for the showcased everyday-input use cases, although a specialized application could tolerate much lower throughput than general computer control.

The strongest opposing case is that consumer input is the wrong initial market boundary. Wizard already fits, evaluates, and hosts classifiers from labeled trials. Commissioned recordings could supply developers who cannot collect suitable EEG data themselves. This is a small but real operational workflow, and better pretrained models could improve its usefulness while Dydema retains customer-specific fitted models. My recurring pattern of investing across layers of a technology transition supports examining this infrastructure wedge. The limitation is ownership: the dossier documents no downstream application integrations, customer feedback loop, or exclusive recording rights that would make the service increasingly difficult to replace.

The founders make that alternative credible enough to investigate. Julian Weaver combines neuroscience and computer-science training with EEG/fMRI research; Christopher Oosthuizen brings machine-learning research and startup engineering. Their previous collaboration at Syntra reduces uncertainty about whether they can build together. But founding-engineer titles do not establish specific prior startup lessons or costly personal commitment. The research-stage exception also does not apply: relevant experience is present, while differentiated frontier results and a credible financing path to the required technical milestone are unestablished. This is not a reason to reject first-time founders; it is a reason not to override weak market evidence.

The economics remain a posted tariff rather than demonstrated market reconstruction. Pricing of $1 per model creation, $1 per model monthly hosting, and $0.003 per prediction window supplies no evidence of customer savings or contribution margins. REVE’s releases establish a dated technical change, but no quantified economic step change. EMOTIV already combines hardware, APIs, and EEG-model infrastructure. Dydema’s recording activity could become valuable, but dataset size, reuse rights, performance advantage, and reproduction cost are unknown. I would not capitalize that possibility as a moat. Likewise, the live API and company demonstrations prove availability, while 82 LinkedIn followers establish no product-market pull; absent customer evidence receives the pre-seed default.

I would pass with my own money. The more plausible near-term business is specialized EEG development tooling, whose demand still needs to be demonstrated. One verifiable flip condition would be an independently observed paid deployment in which users repeatedly choose a Dydema-powered command workflow over their existing input method for eight consecutive weeks, with calibration time and error rates recorded. That would directly test the market’s controlling belief and give the infrastructure wedge a concrete customer outcome to own.
