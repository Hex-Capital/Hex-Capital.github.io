# Neuron Industries -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence case is real but not yet extreme. Industrial controls projects take weeks or months, 45% of controls engineers are at least 60, and the PLC market is $13.45 billion. Neuron Industries could make scarce controls expertise more programmable and factories faster to deploy. But the dossier never converts that into a 10x consequence: no engineering-hours baseline, factory commissioning cost, affected production capacity, or quantified reduction in downtime. This is an important efficiency opportunity, not yet a demonstrated 100x option.

Cortex is more ambitious than an AI interface. It combines the controller, IDE, HMI, historian, and data stack; Synapse produces readable deterministic Python, identifies ambiguities, runs timed tests, and checks cycle budgets. That is the right mechanism. The missing fact is a measured result. Neuron provides no benchmark against Siemens, Rockwell, or conventional controls engineering for commissioning time, reliability, latency, or total system cost. More seriously, Synapse generates both the control code and its test specifications. A shared interpretation error can make the implementation and its examiner agree while the machine does the wrong thing.

The bull case is the deployment evidence. Two live hard-tech pilots and reported use across chemical processing, electronics manufacturing, wastewater treatment, and small modular reactors are unusually informative pre-seed environments. These customers cannot tolerate entertaining demos. Yet the company has not identified which pilot attacks its largest kill risk, what constitutes success, or how it responds to failure. UL and CE remain targets, Cortex reaches only SIL 1, specifications remain subject to change, and there is no disclosed test threshold for the common-mode verification problem. Capital is buying activity, but the dossier does not show how much existential risk each dollar removes.

Dennis Ren has worked on Tesla industrial test systems, Amazon Go embedded hardware, Apple Vision Pro hardware, and Freeform production systems. Kenneth Rhee adds Amazon infrastructure experience and a prior bootstrapped exit. This is a credible, complementary team for rebuilding factory controls. What is absent is evidence of the behavior I care about most: a consequential belief revised after field data or a specialist recruited to own certification, functional safety, or formal verification. Strong résumés establish the right to attempt the problem; they do not establish learning rate.

The economics are suggestive, not proven. Cortex Mini at $2,950 undercuts the cited $5,000 controller, while the $6,450 Cortex may replace several software and hardware layers without runtime-license fees. But there is no bill of materials, gross margin, manufacturing yield, support burden, or bottom-up comparison with the installed stack. Siemens and Rockwell also possess proprietary code and switching costs, while OTee, PLCs.ai, and Interkey already occupy adjacent portions of the architecture. Neuron has disclosed no proprietary learning loop or compatibility asset that keeps Synapse from becoming another AI-assisted interface.

The Block exception does not rescue this today. Both begin with integrated hardware that could expand into broader infrastructure, but Block attacked an enormous payment value pool with a product path unconstrained by industrial certification; Neuron has a $13.45 billion controller market, two unnamed pilots, and several unresolved safety gates. My 36-month base case is that Neuron pivots Synapse into brownfield programming and verification software for Siemens and Rockwell installations, with controller revenue remaining below $5 million annualized. I pass now. I would reverse the decision if one named pilot publishes an independently auditable production result showing at least a 10x reduction in controls-engineering time with zero safety-critical specification mismatches across repeated deployments.
