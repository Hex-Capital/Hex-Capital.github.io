# OpEra -- Paul Graham Evaluation

OpEra’s founders have built something more convincing than their description of it. “AI decision engine for factories, warehouses and fleets” could mean almost anything. A tariff-aware routing implementation using JuMP and Gurobi means something specific. Rossignol and Chalita met at MIT’s Operations Research Center and worked together on retailer deliveries. The important unanswered question is what happened when that work encountered an obstacle: what broke, what did they change, and how quickly did it work? The dossier establishes collaboration and technical ability. It supplies no such sequence, so resourcefulness receives my missing-evidence default.

The retailer result deserves a careful reading. OpEra reports 38% lower delivery costs and 10% more on-time deliveries. But the published implementation backtests historical data against dispatching individual orders from the nearest store. That establishes a useful optimization artifact; it does not establish that a planner adopted it or that it beats an already optimized system. I give the technical work substantial, provisional credit. The reported 10.4% sugar-price premium and 54% reduction in unfulfilled marketplace orders strengthen the case that these founders can formulate operational problems. They remain company-reported results with undisclosed deployment conditions.

The strongest case for investing is that the founders encountered this problem through actual work. Carrier tariffs and operational rules are precisely the sort of unpleasant details that can conceal a good company. Their retailer project gives the origin story substance beyond their MIT degrees or employment at McKinsey, BMW, and Sanofi. OpEra could discover a repeatable product by doing difficult installations personally. But its current offer spans factories, warehouses, and fleets. Those customers share mathematical techniques without necessarily sharing a product. With pricing undisclosed, I cannot yet distinguish a repeatable software business from a succession of valuable custom projects.

Who wants the crude version badly enough to keep using it? The launch seeks introductions to several kinds of operational executives, but the dossier identifies no returning planner, payment, or referral. The savings figures cannot also count as evidence of demand. My unstated preference for concrete proof before fundraising favors the routing repository; my preference for tools users appropriate and depend on has no supporting evidence here. Nor does the launch establish a launch-dependent distribution plan or reliance on one large partner. Those red flags are unproven. There is also no documented rejected hypothesis or evidence-driven product revision, so adaptability stays at its default.

I would pass on the present evidence. OpEra clears my technical threshold narrowly but misses the founder-resourcefulness gate. I checked whether an exception should override that: the working artifact is the strongest argument, but its historical baseline and the absence of demonstrated user pull leave it short of a compelling override. Existing solvers and FICO’s acquisition of Nextmv make the implementation and learning loop especially important; access to optimization software will not distinguish OpEra. One verifiable deployment sequence would flip my decision: a named retailer encounters a real operating constraint, the founders ship a working correction within a week, and its planner then uses the corrected system daily for four weeks and pays. That would connect the code to resourcefulness and urgent demand.
