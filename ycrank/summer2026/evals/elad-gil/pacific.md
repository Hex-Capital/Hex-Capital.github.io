# Pacific -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI-compute buyers face multi-year construction delays while stalled sites retain grid connections; Pacific’s opportunity is to convert those stranded megawatts into sellable GPU capacity, and the controlling variable is whether a standardized pod can reach operation materially faster than conventional construction at a competitive delivered cost.

The market evidence makes that belief plausible but not yet investable. JLL found that 57% of data-center projects experienced delays of at least three months in 2025 and projects modular-system and micro-data-center sales rising from $11 billion to $48 billion by 2030. Yet Pacific has not quantified the threshold that matters: months from order to operation, dollars per deployed megawatt, utilization, or gross margin on hosted compute. Reusing a grid connection also leaves permitting, equipment procurement, and local opposition intact. The company has identified the bottleneck, but not demonstrated that its system removes enough of it.

The strongest bull case is the technical architecture. Dry diamond-wafer cooling could eliminate water dependence, while integrating transformers, switchgear, power distribution, and compute could replace one-off construction with a repeatable manufactured product. This is the mechanism behind the relevant physical-world exception: integrated technology can justify backing an atoms business when it changes incumbent economics, as Anduril did. The material difference is that Pacific publishes no thermal-capacity results, reliability tests, certifications, production data, or comparative margins. Vertical integration may shorten deployment—or expose a two-person company to the same 33-week equipment lead times it proposes to escape.

Demand is the weakest part of the case. “Working with” unnamed aerospace companies does not establish a paid deployment, repeat usage, or even a signed order. The reported £10,000 MRR in May 2026 came from a prior digital-twin product and therefore cannot validate the current physical pods. YC and NVIDIA Inception improve access but are not product-market pull. This matters because Armada already has international deployments and nearly $500 million raised, while Crusoe has approximately $3.9 billion and an integrated energy-to-cloud stack. Pacific is entering a capitalizing category after credible operators have begun manufacturing similar systems.

The product is more substantial than an AI wrapper, but its owned workflow remains prospective. Better chips and models would increase demand for compute, yet Pacific captures that benefit only if it controls sites, deployment, operations, and customer utilization—not merely a cooling component or pod design that larger infrastructure providers can reproduce. The space roadmap adds no present credit and risks broadening the financing plan before the terrestrial system works.

The founders provide the best company-specific signal. Harper Dammann Smith left aerospace studies to build Pacific, and Ralph Heward-Mills brings Airbus thermal-engineering and ESA experience. That is relevant technical commitment, not generic prestige. It is not, however, documented data-center operating experience or second-time founder learning. The known-exception case therefore does not clear: this is not yet a rare technical team with validated frontier research, nor a physical product with demonstrated margin reconstruction.

I would monitor, not invest today. The verifiable flip condition is one paying terrestrial deployment showing at least a megawatt of sustained capacity, independently measured thermal performance, and an order-to-operation timeline and delivered cost materially below conventional construction. Without that proof, my 36-month base case is a pivot toward specialized thermal systems or defense/aerospace pod integration rather than a scaled hosted-compute network.
