# Collar -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Collar changed from private-fund diligence in September to public-company investor relations by October. What made the founders change their minds? That is the most important missing fact. A customer discovering an unexpected use for the product would be encouraging. Two founders choosing another financial workflow would tell me much less. The dated change establishes movement, but it does not establish learning. I cannot infer resourcefulness without seeing the obstacle and what Kazel Koh and Jamie Yau did about it.

There is something here worth investigating. Collar reports mapping 12,000 portfolio managers and describes a product that retrieves source-linked talking points during earnings calls. That is more concrete than a proposal to build an AI assistant. But the mapping is company-reported, and the dossier supplies no inspectable deployment or measured accuracy and latency. Collar weakly fits my pattern of founders assembling a useful substrate before claiming a platform. It does not yet show the numerical technical proof that makes that pattern persuasive. A wrong answer delivered quickly during an earnings call could make the product worse than the spreadsheet it replaces.

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders may already have found a small group with an expensive, recurring problem. Private-credit work and an asset manager’s chief-of-staff role put them near financial-information workflows. They met in 2022 and began collaborating in 2025, so this is not an apparently improvised cofounder relationship. Reported savings of 40 hours monthly and 50% of Q&A preparation time suggest a useful product. If those results describe issuers repeatedly using Collar through earnings cycles, the business could be substantially better than its public evidence suggests. But “partners representing $300B” measures the size of the companies nearby, not how badly anyone wants Collar. The quoted $1,000 monthly price also belongs to the previous diligence product; it establishes neither current IR revenue nor retention.

The decisive question is which unpleasant job the founders will do better than anyone else. Q4 already offers AI capture and briefings, while Irwin supplies targeting with FactSet data. Their presence would not stop me from investing. It makes the first urgently satisfied user more important. Approved-source retrieval, financial-data integration, and dependable earnings preparation contain plenty of schlep, but the dossier does not connect those burdens to a problem the founders personally encountered and solved. Nor would I treat a demo-led website as evidence that they depend on ceremonial launches or large partnerships. The problem is insufficient affirmative evidence, rather than a demonstrated founder red flag.

I would pass with my own money today. Collar has some product substance and plausible user benefit, but neither establishes the founder-under-resistance evidence my investment gate requires. The exception mechanisms do not rescue it: there is no demonstrated prototype performance or unmistakable repeated user pull strong enough to replace that missing evidence. One verifiable case could change my decision: an issuer encounters a specific failure, the founders ship a workaround within a week, and that issuer pays to use the repaired product through its next earnings cycle. That would connect the people, the software, and the need.
