# Ryhla -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Ryhla reports saving a developer approximately $160,000 by eliminating eight of ten planned battery projects within days. That is an economically legible customer action: stop spending money on sites that will fail. But savings belong to the customer until Ryhla demonstrates how it captures them. My proposed post-money, competing-term-sheet count, and evidence that this is genuinely scarce capital are all unknown. I cannot underwrite the deal from the usefulness of the product alone.

The strongest evidence is that Ryhla’s workflow changed project selection. The reported Midwest solar deployment gives its claimed proprietary grid models an actual operating context. The separate claim that utilities approved 91% of model-approved sites among 2025 applications strengthens the case for useful screening, although the missing denominator prevents me from treating that percentage as validated predictive performance. More consequentially, we do not know how many viable sites the model rejects. Avoiding every application would avoid plenty of fees. The valuable asset would be utility-specific information that reliably improves developers’ allocation decisions, with rights to retain and reuse the resulting data.

The founders make that possibility credible. Sara El Baghdadi and Yassine Guennoun reportedly researched grid models at Stanford and initially deployed them with utilities; that is operating evidence beyond their Polytechnique and Stanford credentials. This fits my pattern of backing people who translate between difficult systems, and my willingness to tolerate physical infrastructure complexity when software improves its operation. Their developer deployment extends that bridge. Still, Enverus already owns interconnection simulation capabilities through Pearl Street, and Paces offers overlapping diligence. Ryhla needs information or access those businesses cannot simply bundle. A proprietary-model claim does not establish ownership of that advantage.

The strongest argument against passing is that Ryhla has already produced a consequential customer outcome before disclosing revenue. I take that seriously at pre-seed. This also avoids the obvious AI red flag: the proposition is grounded in grid behavior and utility decisions, rather than a promise that the next model release makes everything profitable. But my exceptions to missing financing gates require unusually strong conversion, economics, or operating receipts. Here, payment, repeat purchasing, exclusive data rights, and delivery costs remain unestablished. The founders’ long friendship supplies no evidence of a governance problem; equally, it does not answer those commercial questions.

I pass on the available deal. The dossier names no round-funded hypothesis with a metric and deadline, so I cannot distinguish a finite experiment from funding ongoing interconnection services. Pricing, burn, margins, and a route to break-even are also undisclosed; the customer’s $160,000 saving supplies none of those numbers. My flip condition is a signed financing plan at a sub-$10 million post-money that funds one dated, customer-backed cross-utility replication test through a documented cash break-even milestone. That would connect the useful workflow to an investable use of my money.
