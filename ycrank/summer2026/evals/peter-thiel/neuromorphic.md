# Neuromorphic -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The contested truth is that a robot brain can turn plain-language instructions into reliable physical workflows across different bodies, making robot deployment a one-week software problem rather than a months-long integration project. What would prove it wrong? If the biotechnology deployment requires frequent human intervention, body-specific engineering, or unsafe recovery behavior, then “embodiment agnostic” is a slogan. Neuromorphic has supplied one meaningful test: a customer progressed from initial conversation to daily robot operation within one week. But it reports no success rate, intervention rate, safety record, or cross-body benchmark. The secret is plausible and partially tested, not yet demonstrated.

The strongest evidence is the founding structure. Ege Doğanay researches manipulation and policy generalization; Vatan Aksoy Tezer has worked on navigation and deployment at PickNik, Rapyuta, KABAM, and Augmentus; the team collectively claims experience deploying more than 1,000 robots. More important, the three founders competed together in high-school robotics and won championships. This is long alignment around the underlying problem, not a team assembled to imitate the current physical-AI market. Public evidence does not establish that every founder is full-time, how much equity they retain, or whether core engineering is entirely in-house, so the foundation is promising rather than proven.

The difficulty is capitalism versus competition. Wet labs are a credible narrow beachhead, and signing a contract five days after the strategy change suggests urgency. Yet Neuromorphic has disclosed no proprietary technology, exclusive data, measured network effect, or technical advantage. FieldAI, Skild AI, Physical Intelligence, and Generalist AI have raised more than $4 billion collectively to pursue overlapping cross-embodiment intelligence. Packaging general models into wet-lab workflows may be a good service business, but features do not constitute a monopoly. The future data loop is merely inferred from one deployment; the current evidence shows a bounded market without a mechanism for owning it.

The prior $5,000–$10,000 per-robot monthly offering gives the company a legible revenue engine. At $7,500 per month, 1,000 deployed robots would represent roughly $90 million in annual revenue, but hardware, monitoring, support, and deployment engineering may make that revenue capital-intensive and low-margin. Current wet-lab pricing is undisclosed. Distribution has one unusually fast data point—contract in five days and daily operation within a week—but no named buyer, renewal, conversion rate, or repeatable acquisition channel. Founder-led demonstrations can reach the first ten laboratories; the dossier does not show how Neuromorphic reaches the next thousand without becoming a deployment consultancy.

The strongest opposing case is that the company has already done what most robot-foundation-model startups have not: converted technical capability into daily physical use for a paying customer. Its founders’ long relationship and deployment experience could let them accumulate proprietary failure-and-recovery data while larger competitors optimize general benchmarks. That would fit the operator-network pattern better than the company’s public positioning suggests. It also supplies the only credible exception to the crowded-market objection: independently verified customer behavior can outweigh apparent competition. One unnamed deployment, however, is not persistent demand, and no zero-to-one technical result overrides the missing monopoly mechanism.

I would not invest today. My 36-month expectation is that Neuromorphic becomes a vertical wet-lab automation provider with $1 million–$3 million ARR rather than the general, embodiment-agnostic robot-brain company it describes. The verifiable flip condition is a cohort of at least ten independent laboratories renewing deployments across three robot bodies while maintaining below 5% human intervention and above 60% gross margin. That would simultaneously establish technical transfer, compounding data, and viable distribution. Until then, this is an unusually coherent team entering an unusually crowded market: founders, yes; monopoly, not yet.
