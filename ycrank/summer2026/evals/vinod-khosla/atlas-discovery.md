# Atlas Discovery -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence is large enough to investigate. Nine of ten drugs fail in clinical trials after consuming hundreds of millions of dollars and as much as 15 years. If Atlas Discovery could predict treatment response before enrollment, it could compress a vast expert workflow, reduce patient exposure to ineffective drugs, and redirect capital toward therapies that work. But the dossier does not quantify how response prediction changes industry-wide failure rates, development costs, or time. A $77.3 billion market forecast is not a consequence model.

The technical evidence is the strongest part of the company. Atlas reports 0.7–0.9 retrospective AUROC across cancer and autoimmune trials, with 0.760 AUROC on 358 treated UNIFI patients and a modeled reduction of 458 participants at matched statistical power. Those are measurable results, not an AI adjective. The problem is that the UNIFI model was trained and evaluated within the same trial. Atlas itself says direct cross-trial generalization is currently infeasible for stratification decisions. A model that explains a completed trial can be useful research software; a model that predicts a new trial before outcomes exist can become infrastructure. That distinction contains most of the company’s value.

The strongest bull case is real. The three founders coauthored two relevant preprints; Sanjukta Bhattacharya brings AI-biology PhD work and Los Alamos research, while Shaamil Karim and Christian Gensbigler have computational-biology and cancer-research experience. Normal product use could eventually create the scarce scientific learning loop I like: pretreatment biology linked to treatment and outcome across many trials. Even one prospective success could unlock partnerships because pharmaceutical companies already spend heavily on failed development. This is enough to prevent a pass.

But Atlas has not designed the next dollar around its largest kill risk. It needs linked hospital and biopharma outcome data, has no disclosed exclusive dataset, and is still requesting partnerships. There is no named prospective or leave-one-trial-out experiment, pass threshold, cost, timing, or stop condition. Meanwhile, Owkin claims relationships with more than 800 hospitals, and Unlearn and PhaseV have raised $134.85 million and $65 million respectively. Open-source model weights with four GitHub stars do not compensate for that data disadvantage.

The adverse pattern is also familiar: a foundation-model architecture whose future moat is described as data it does not yet possess. The founders are technically relevant, but the record shows neither a material belief revision nor recruitment of clinical-development, regulatory, or hospital-data operators to own the missing risks. I have accepted absent business models when extraordinary technical progress made the consequence unavoidable. Atlas has not earned that exception: its evidence remains retrospective, and its pricing, unit economics, and path from analytical performance to unsubsidized customer savings are undisclosed.

My base case is that Atlas becomes a useful biomarker or retrospective trial-analysis vendor rather than a general predictor of human drug response. I do not write a check today. One result flips the decision: a preregistered, fully held-out prospective study in which pretreatment transcriptomic predictions materially improve responder enrichment or trial power against the sponsor’s existing method, using data unavailable during training. That experiment would convert an attractive scientific result into an option on rebuilding clinical development.
