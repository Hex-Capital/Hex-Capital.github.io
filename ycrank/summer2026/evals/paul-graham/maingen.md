# Maingen -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most revealing fact is not that Maingen chose industrial reinforcement learning. It is that industrial knowledge was trapped inside operators’ heads, CMMS systems, and vendor portals, and the founders nevertheless converted hundreds of hours with solar personnel into a working environment. SolarBench contains eight tasks, evaluates 11 models across 880 simulated weeks, and produces a sharp result: the best model passed 53.8% of runs while the authored oracle passed 100%. This is measured technical proof built before anyone granted them privileged access to the whole industry.

The resourcefulness evidence is good but incomplete. Phillip Yan and David Yang found a laborious route around the data-access blocker by embedding with operators and encoding telemetry, work orders, SOPs, inventory, vendor behavior, and expert grading rubrics. The result shipped. But the dossier does not say what access attempt failed, how they obtained the operators, or how quickly they changed course. I can see the workaround and its artifact; I cannot yet see the running back changing direction under pressure. Their prior work at Axpo, Scale AI, Coinbase, and Amazon makes the execution plausible, but credentials are not substitutes for the missing sequence.

The strongest part of Maingen is the combination of technical proof and schlep. Phillip encountered energy operations through Axpo, while David worked on model training and interpretability. They are not tourists who appended AI to a large GDP statistic. More importantly, the apparent moat is not an algorithm alone. It is the unpleasant process of extracting operator judgment and turning it into scenarios and grading criteria. The fact that only solar is documented limits the claim, but it also makes the claim inspectable.

The weak point is pull. “Working with frontier labs” names neither a lab nor a contract, repeat usage, payment, or a model-training decision changed by SolarBench. The public benchmark may attract researchers, but public visibility is not demand. Maingen currently has stronger evidence that it can manufacture an environment than that a narrow group of buyers urgently wants to purchase successive environments. There is also no documented rejected hypothesis or product-route change, so I cannot award flexibility merely because the founders might possess it.

The bear case is severe. Anthropic’s reported discussion of more than $1 billion in environment spending could attract suppliers without creating durable suppliers. Frontier labs already build environments internally; AfterQuery reports a $100 million revenue run rate, and Bespoke Labs, Mechanize, and Prime Intellect have raised substantial capital. Meanwhile Maingen needs cooperation from both industrial operators and model labs. If each environment requires another bespoke operator study, this could become a consulting business whose best customers eventually internalize the work. Its distribution plan currently includes requests for introductions to operating sites, which risks making “partner” the decisive verb.

I would still invest, narrowly. The founders have built an unusually concrete artifact in a difficult domain, and the 53.8% result exposes a real capability gap rather than concealing it behind a demo. My 36-month expectation is that Maingen reaches $3–8 million in ARR selling several operator-derived energy and industrial environments to model developers, without yet controlling live plants. The verifiable condition that would flip me to a pass is failure to secure one named paying lab that commissions or repeatedly uses a second environment beyond SolarBench within twelve months. That would show the hard work produced an impressive benchmark, not a pulled product.
