# Veltha -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I cannot underwrite the check before knowing the check. Veltha discloses neither post-money valuation nor competing-term-sheet count, and YC participation does not tell me whether this is genuinely scarce first capital or another auction. More importantly, there is no use-of-funds plan naming the finite result this round purchases. “Hire, sell, and encode more rules” would be a capital factory; “prove fewer than 5% of recommendations require substantive correction across 1,000 live claims by a fixed date” would be a venture moment. The dossier supplies no such commitment.

The product evidence is much better than the financing evidence. A $2 billion-plus specialty carrier is running live claims, review time reportedly fell from one hour to five minutes, and more than 500 FCIC, LAM, and PPSH checks have been encoded. Paragraph-level citations and human approval attack the real institutional constraint: a regulated buyer needs an auditable decision, not merely an impressive model output. This is a credible translation layer between unstructured claim files and rules-bound insurance operations.

But live usage is not yet a demonstrated purchase. The carrier is unnamed; there is no contract value, claim volume, conversion status, renewal, or retention figure. The proprietary dark pool is also prospective. Carrier-specific rules, reviewer overrides, and resolved outcomes could become valuable data, but Veltha has not disclosed ownership rights or a learning loop. Today, CLARA, Sprout.ai, and Five Sigma can plausibly add regulatory citation, while Veltha cannot yet show that its embedded workflow creates switching costs.

The founders are the strongest evidence. Roland Jaloszynski shipped mortgage-underwriting automation at a $642 billion servicer, while Edin Citaku led an AI deployment at Germany’s federal criminal police. Those are operating receipts from regulated environments, not résumé decoration. Still, neither founder is shown to have adjusted insurance claims or sold into a carrier, and the dossier does not identify who has final authority between the two Palantir alumni. That missing insurance-side access and accountable decision structure keep this below the top band.

The strongest bull case is the known AI exception represented by Ando: functioning enterprise deployment can outweigh model commoditization when measured operating performance proves the base business. Veltha’s live carrier workflow and 12x review-time reduction point in that direction. The material difference is that Veltha publishes no accuracy, override, paid-conversion, or retention data. A five-minute review is not valuable if rare errors create expensive denied claims, and human review can preserve regulatory safety while quietly destroying the promised margins.

My 36-month base case is that Veltha reaches $2 million-$5 million ARR as a compliance-review module for specialty and workers’ compensation carriers, rather than becoming an autonomous claims adjuster. That may be a good company, but I would not invest my own money on undisclosed terms before the business has shown that deployment becomes recurring revenue without requiring another round. One verifiable flip condition would change the decision: the current carrier signs and then renews a paid annual contract after processing at least 1,000 claims, with the renewal disclosure including the substantive override rate.
