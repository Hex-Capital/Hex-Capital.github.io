# Marker -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Are businesses operated by small teams supervising fleets of agents, with undocumented workflows converted into executable systems? Probably. Marker has two leaks from that world: an agent reportedly cut manual product-operations work 80%, and another reduced onboarding from 120 to 60 days while removing 95% of manual QA. But the dossier does not map the intervening transitions—how deployment becomes repeatable, how agents earn authority, or what Marker controls when models and enterprise platforms improve. I can see the destination; I cannot yet see why this vehicle owns the road.

The founders are the real signal. Berwick, Drevno, and Pastewka have worked together for 15 years, built multiple products, sold Drapr to Gap in 2021, and then operated inside the buyer. Berwick founding Gap’s Office of AI is unusually relevant tuition: he has watched enterprise pilots encounter legacy systems, institutional ambiguity, and production requirements. This is not three people discovering agents during YC. It is a durable team returning from inside the wound with a product hypothesis.

The wound, however, remains imprecisely diagnosed. Marker says consultancies take quarters and leave, internal expertise is scarce, and workflows are undocumented. Why do pilots fail? Who benefits from the delay? Is the rent captured by consultants, software incumbents, internal gatekeepers, or organizational risk avoidance? The reported reductions in onboarding time and manual labor prove economic value, but not the broken rule Marker is uniquely positioned to remove. The customer receives some operating capacity back; the dossier does not yet show an individual gaining meaningful autonomy or ownership.

The strongest bull case is that embedded delivery is how the founders discover the hidden common system. If every engagement adds process knowledge and subsequent agents ship faster, today’s consultancy-shaped work could become tomorrow’s workflow infrastructure. The two anonymized deployments suggest functioning software operating against real enterprise systems, not a rendering or waitlist. Yet the evidence is founder-published, unattributed, and silent on repeat usage, contract value, retention, or whether customers perform any costly behavior beyond allowing an implementation team inside.

That silence matters because Marker currently sits on top of the wave more than beneath it. Distyl AI already offers the closest platform-plus-engineers model with roughly $202 million raised; Writer and Sierra possess larger platforms and distribution. Marker has not demonstrated a scarce technical asset, proprietary data scale, or reusable workflow component that those companies need. Worse, it touches real operational data without publicly identifying model providers, custody limits, or security certifications. Three equal-seeming co-founders are also presented without a clearly accountable leader. A long working relationship reduces interpersonal risk, but it is not a contractual tiebreaker. I looked for exception-grade product behavior that could override those gates; anonymized efficiency claims do not reach that threshold.

My 36-month base case is $8 million to $15 million in ARR, with more than half of revenue still tied to embedded implementation and the company narrowing toward retail and product-operations workflows. That could become a good business, but today I am being asked to fund excellent operators before they have exposed the repeatable machine. I would not write the personal check yet. One verifiable fact flips me: three named customers deploying the same Marker-built workflow module into production, with the second and third deployments requiring less than half the implementation labor of the first.
