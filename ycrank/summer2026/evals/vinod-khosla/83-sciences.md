# 83 Sciences -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence could be enormous, but 83 Sciences has not quantified it. Recovering the 83% of experimental data stranded in notebooks and instrument exports could collapse the number of wet-lab iterations required to discover batteries, catalysts, or PFAS replacements. Yet the dossier supplies neither today’s experimental cost pool nor a measured reduction in time, cost, or resource use. A $170.4 million materials-informatics market growing to $410.4 million is not the relevant upside; it may instead reveal that the company is selling research software into a modest category rather than rewriting materials discovery.

There is an important technical signal. Dalton used an anonymous battery lab’s historical experiments to propose synthesis conditions that exceeded the lab’s prior capacity plateau, producing a manuscript in under two months. The company also reports one novel material. This is more than an interface wrapped around an open model, but it is not yet a 5–10x discontinuity: there is no disclosed baseline for experiments avoided, discovery time, capacity improvement, or cost, and no independent validation. MatterGen, OMat24, Citrine, MaterialsZone, CuspAI, and Orbital make the model layer plentiful. Unpublished failure data becomes defensible only if access rights, provenance, and cross-partner learning survive contracting.

The founders are the strongest part of the option. Ian Naccarella combines chemical engineering with battery-material commercialization at Sila; Eric Riesel brings an MIT chemistry PhD and materials-model experience; Yankang Yang has deployed enterprise AI across a 30,000-person firm. That is complementary talent around an expensive scientific workflow, not three generic AI résumés. Their years of collaboration and advisors in materials science strengthen the architecture. What is absent is an observed instance of belief revision or evidence that they recruited specifically around the largest remaining weakness—data rights, experimental reproducibility, or industrial deployment.

The attractive unstated pattern is a scarce scientific workflow becoming a repeatable production platform: every engagement could convert private failed experiments into better structured priors and validated outcomes. The painful countercase is that each partner’s data and foreground IP remain isolated, turning Dalton into a bespoke discovery consultancy. Grants of up to $50,000, in-person onboarding, and a Head of Sales search before the technical benchmark is public increase that risk. Spending on distribution before resolving whether heterogeneous lab records produce reproducible advantage is the wrong dollars-versus-risk graph.

The exception case does not rescue the investment today. A modest initial product can be tolerated when exceptional founders are attacking a quantified giant value pool with room to mutate the route. Here, the dossier documents neither that underlying cost pool nor an instigator-level founder action that overrides missing technical and economic proof. Nor is there a scale model: no pricing, gross margin, experimental cost per contract, utilization assumption, or path from discovery services to software economics. A system that needs subsidized onboarding and continued expert labor has not reached a globally adoptable price.

I would pass, while keeping the company close. The option is intellectually credible but not yet priced by evidence: strong team, real experiment, unbounded claim. One verifiable result would flip me—a named industrial partner publishing a controlled comparison showing Dalton reaches a material-performance target with at least 5x fewer wet-lab runs than its existing workflow, under contractual terms permitting learning across engagements. That would simultaneously establish technical discontinuity, proprietary compounding, and the beginnings of an unsubsidized cost curve.
