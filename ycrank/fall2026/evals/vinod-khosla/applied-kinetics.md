# Applied Kinetics -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Applied Kinetics claims seven weeks to build and test a transformer against up to 65 weeks on the open market. That is a potentially consequential 9.3x compression. But the economic consequence depends on whether equipment actually reaches the customer, passes acceptance, and advances project energization. Accelerating one component does little if utility interconnection remains the binding constraint. The adjacent $25.8 billion data center power market establishes an existing spending pool; it does not establish how much infrastructure this company can unlock or how radically its cost changes.

The technical question is where those 58 weeks disappear. AI agents review specifications and allocate work across factories, while the company manages approvals, testing, customs, freight, and service. That could expose usable capacity hidden behind fragmented procurement. It could also move the queue from manufacturing into qualification and delivery. The dossier supplies neither a named incumbent comparison nor a completed shipment supporting the advertised improvement. I give the numerical claim limited credit. I cannot yet underwrite a repeatable technical discontinuity.

The strongest argument against passing is the manufacturing network. Forty-plus partner plants could let two founders aggregate supply without financing factories themselves. Rohit Naras’s GPU telemetry work at Crusoe provides relevant exposure to AI infrastructure; Adhik Durga’s IBM agent experience supplies software capability. My actual investment pattern includes execution-heavy businesses and technologies that work through installed infrastructure. Proprietary transformer physics is not a prerequisite. If qualified capacity already exists and coordination is the constraint, this approach could beat a much more expensive invention.

But the network’s reported 10+ GW belongs to the manufacturing base, not Applied Kinetics deliveries. Exclusivity and contractual capacity rights are unestablished. My exceptions for an evolving platform or an extraordinary mission require affirmative evidence of the platform mechanism, founder instigation, or quantified consequence. This dossier does not yet supply that override. Nor does it establish the AI red flag of copying a standard model; architecture is undisclosed. The narrower concern is that operational labor may remain the source of value while software contributes little accumulating advantage.

The first capital should attack qualified delivery, not expand the catalog. Applied Kinetics describes inspections and acceptance checks, but no disclosed experiment specifies a pass threshold, budget, timing, or response to failure. That receives the missing-evidence default. The founders’ backgrounds likewise establish relevant skills, without an observed belief revision or difficult complementary recruit. Economics receives its default too: project quotations disclose no landed-cost comparison, warranty burden, working-capital requirement, or contribution margin. Missing revenue at this stage does not bother me. An untested route from factory access to reliable, economical delivery determines the decision.

I pass at 31/100. Applied Kinetics has a credible industrial bottleneck and an interesting speed claim, with insufficient evidence that its intervention changes the underlying economics. One verifiable result would reopen my decision: an independently documented, customer-accepted transformer order delivered within seven weeks against a comparable qualified incumbent’s 65-week quote, at no higher landed price and positive contribution after inspection, freight, financing, and warranty provision. That would turn advertised manufacturing speed into evidence of a commercially useful discontinuity.
