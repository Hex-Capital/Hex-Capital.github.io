# Aucert -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Aucert’s founders have earned the right to work on this problem. Vivek Soneja helped build PhonePe’s mobile architecture; Rajesh Kumar spent 11 years at PayPal, including work on patented automated testing. Mobile release failures are something they have encountered through action. That is specific knowledge. PhonePe’s 500M+ users belong to the prior employer, however; they establish operating context, not Aucert’s distribution or traction.

The decisive question is what survives when another team reproduces the agents. Aucert describes organization-specific screen graphs, approved expectations, remembered paths, and historical verdicts. Those could become valuable accumulated knowledge. Customer ownership does not prevent switching costs, but the dossier supplies no evidence that rebuilding this knowledge is difficult or that it improves verification measurably. BrowserStack already offers overlapping mobile automation, accessibility checks, and testing agents. Autonomous exploration is a useful product distinction; its durability remains unproved.

The strongest case against passing is that these founders know which expectations cannot be reconstructed from a screenshot. Installed apps have costly release cycles, and functionality, accessibility, design, and store policy create several reasons to reuse the same verification system. If Aucert captures the tacit knowledge behind release decisions, each deployment could deepen an embedded asset. But the public evidence currently describes that possibility through draft terms. It does not demonstrate it through repeat usage or a technical comparison. My exceptions for extraordinary founder behavior or an unusually developed early prototype require evidence beyond relevant employment and a launch.

There is money in the problem: the dossier cites an $11.45B mobile-testing market for 2026. Aucert names mobile engineering and QA buyers, but it has not established how its initial scan expands into a larger transaction or budget beyond testing. Its free scan and founder demonstrations provide acquisition paths; neither demonstrates users creating utility or distribution for other users. That misses my recurring preference for participant contributions that compound across a system. Draft monthly subscriptions establish an intended payer relationship, while undisclosed prices and absent realized revenue leave direct value capture unproved. October’s Launch YC publication and 317 LinkedIn followers do not resolve either question.

I would pass with my own money today. The issue is whether unusually relevant founders can turn their knowledge into an asset competitors cannot cheaply reproduce. Nothing in the dossier establishes an integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-persistence red flag. The concrete concern is substitution, compounded by unredacted screen content reaching AI providers when sensitive applications may be the most demanding buyers. My flip condition is a measured migration test at a renewing customer showing that Aucert’s accumulated expectations preserve verification coverage that a competing system cannot recover without substantial rebuilding. That would turn the proposed switching cost into evidence.
