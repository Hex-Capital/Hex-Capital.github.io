# Hubble -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Aaron Leon built EHR and AI infrastructure serving 1M+ patients, but Hubble has no mechanism by which the millionth user improves the product for the first. That is Hubble’s signature: unusually strong founder-specific knowledge trapped inside a sales-led interoperability utility. Hubble can automate labor with code; Hubble has not yet turned that code into permissionless leverage or a compounding network.

**Stack Overflow** is the high rung for specific knowledge: developers built for developers after years of public immersion in the problem. Hubble approaches that rung because Leon led 50+ engineers at Amazon One Medical while Prabha Dublish launched telehealth, marketplace, integrations, and AI-care products as Grow Therapy’s first product hire. The analogy is load-bearing—Hubble’s founders lived inside healthcare infrastructure before founding Hubble—but it breaks where Stack Overflow’s shipped knowledge was publicly legible and organically adopted. Hubble reports 40+ live tools, yet no public repositories, performance benchmarks, customer names, or repeat-usage evidence show that its knowledge has become an externally validated artifact.

**Perplexity’s** low-rung weakness on network effects is that each customer can receive value without making the next customer’s product materially better. Hubble inherits the same weakness without Perplexity’s permissionless distribution override. Epic, Cerner, Aetna, and UnitedHealthcare coverage may improve Hubble’s breadth, but Hubble has not shown that customers contribute reusable integrations, data, or workflows that compound value for prior users. Forty connectors are inventory. A network begins when participation creates value automatically.

Hubble’s 40+ tools can replace fax, portal, and telephone labor over the next 18 months, but automating permissioned work is not the same as permissionless leverage. Hubble still needs patient authorization, provider cooperation, payer access, regulated credentials, and inferred founder-led demonstrations. Code makes each retrieval cheaper; gatekeepers still determine whether retrieval occurs. Hubble therefore compounds operational efficiency, not distribution—the architecture sits closer to enterprise infrastructure than to Twitter’s media replication.

The $8.57B global interoperability market gives Hubble room beyond one narrow clinical workflow: legal histories, life sciences, research, digital health, payer access, and provider read/write are credible adjacent vectors. Hubble would move up a tier if one normalized patient record became a programmable substrate on which outside developers could launch multiple products without Hubble selling each workflow. Today, Health Gorilla’s QHIN network, 1upHealth’s 75+ enterprise customers and 35M+ patients, and Zus Health’s shared patient profile suggest that Hubble is entering a large market whose obvious platform positions are already occupied.

**Notion** is the useful founder parallel: long-lived product obsession created enough specific knowledge and trust to survive repeated rebuilds. Hubble’s founders met in college, built Womentum together, and later accumulated complementary healthcare experience before starting Hubble; that reads as intelligence, energy, and a relationship tested across time. Hubble clears the pre-seed partnership bar, but Hubble does not yet clear the stronger 10-year test: no prior venture outcome, public technical corpus, or evidence about how Dublish and Leon behave under regulatory failure and customer pressure makes integrity directly observable.

CMS-0057-F begins forcing affected payers toward expanded FHIR APIs in 2027, so my 36-month forecast is that Hubble reaches $2M–$6M ARR selling retrieval and workflow infrastructure to digital-health and life-sciences customers, but remains a demo-led vendor rather than becoming a compounding healthcare-data network. Hubble’s domain knowledge and 40+ live tools are sufficient to win contracts; Hubble’s permissioned access model and entrenched competitors keep the upside geometry closer to a useful acquisition target than a 10,000x platform.

Health Gorilla’s regulatory moat supplies the strongest bull case: as with Anchorage Digital, permission can become leverage when everyone else finds compliance too difficult. Hubble could use browser, voice, API, provenance, and audit tooling to become the universal last-mile layer that QHIN-only competitors cannot match. The case stings because Hubble is already live across EHR and payer systems, but it loses today because Hubble discloses no revenue, named customer, coverage percentage, proprietary dataset, or reliability benchmark while Health Gorilla has raised $80M+ and holds QHIN/QHIO designations. I would flip to Invest if Hubble demonstrates that customer-added workflows or connectors drive at least 30% of new deployments without founder-led sales; that would convert integration labor into permissionless network leverage.
