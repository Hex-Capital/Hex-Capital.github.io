# Routant -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Routant’s critical missing asset is evidence of what its founders have learned by doing. Deploying agents into private systems means encountering the difference between a permission that looks correct and one a security team will actually approve. Someone who has repeatedly crossed that boundary could possess valuable specific knowledge. The dossier identifies neither the founders nor a deployment they completed. I assign the founder default; the reported team size of zero supplies no reliable evidence about the actual team.

The product promises one vendor integration, customer-controlled permissions, human review, and audit trails. What survives if a competitor reproduces those features? No deployed policy base, proprietary model, exclusive access relationship, or demonstrated switching cost is documented. Keycard already describes delegation chains and policy-bound credentials; Arcade describes authorization and approvals at each action. This overlap makes differentiation consequential, but it does not establish that Routant’s unseen implementation is technically trivial. Defensibility receives the missing-evidence default.

The strongest argument for Routant is that authorization could become the distribution bottleneck for agent vendors. In the January 2026 Okta-commissioned survey, 69% of 150 decision-makers reported security concerns slowing adoption. A product that lets customers approve additional vendors through an existing trusted connection could control access to demand. That possibility deserves attention: the winning infrastructure company might emerge from owning the approval relationship rather than offering the most authorization features. But Routant has not demonstrated that relationship. The adjacent category estimate—$210 million in 2026, projected to reach $1.02 billion in 2031—does not establish its path into additional buyers or transactions. Market expansion remains at default.

My preference for systems that enable independent participants is relevant here. Routant could help outside developers reach enterprise customers. Yet enabling a vendor is only the beginning: does adding that vendor make the system more useful for another vendor or customer? There is no evidence of reusable approvals, contributed integrations, referrals, or improving onboarding as participation grows. Embedded policies might eventually create replacement costs, but the dossier labels that possibility as inferred. Neither a network effect nor an accumulated asset has been established.

Direct monetization is equally unresolved: no payer, price, contract, or realized revenue is verified. Those omissions are acceptable at pre-seed, but they earn no affirmative economics credit. I also find no documented integrity problem, artificial financing deadline, or founder behavior that warrants a veto. My exceptions for exceptional founders, unusual working prototypes, or an emerging network do not rescue this case because none of those overriding signals is documented. Missing information remains uncertainty throughout the evaluation.

I pass on investing my own money today. Routant identifies a credible obstacle, but the evidence does not yet show why value will compound inside this company. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: a referenceable production cohort showing that customer-approved access relationships are reused across independent agent vendors, with onboarding time falling as participation grows. That would begin turning the proposed access layer into a system whose participants create value for one another.
