# Grip -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: compost and biogas plants should receive mixed organic material, identify contaminants continuously, and remove them without people standing beside filthy conveyor belts. Grip owns a plausible hand for that world—a custom gripper whose successes and failures become training data—but the dossier does not map the intervening transitions from prototype to reliable facility infrastructure. The EU’s 70% packaging-waste recycling target is pressure, not proof that this particular machine is leaking back from the future.

Jonas Gruetter is the strongest evidence here. Before Grip, he worked on reinforcement learning, perception, and irregular-object manipulation at Gravis Robotics, ETH Zürich’s Robotic Systems Lab, and Ursa Mining. A 12-ton excavator completing field tasks at 70% success is real operating tuition, not a fashionable “physical AI” biography assembled for fundraising. It demonstrates technical obsession and contact with hostile environments. It does not yet demonstrate recruitment magnetism, commercial command, or that Grip’s four-person team can maintain hardware inside wet waste facilities.

The system wound is also real but incompletely decoded. Plastic contamination can spoil compost entering processing facilities or fields, operators still employ people to remove it, and European regulation is tightening. But Grip has not quantified the labor bill, contamination loss, regulatory penalty, or disposal cost it eliminates. More importantly for me, the dossier does not identify whose agency increases: this may improve operator economics, but it does not obviously create occupational control for workers or break a gatekeeper’s rent.

The bull case stings. Organic waste is a nastier manipulation problem than the dry recyclables targeted by suction systems, so a dexterous gripper trained on failed grasps could become the concealed enabling layer beneath composting, recycling, and biogas. Gruetter’s excavator work suggests he may be unusually equipped to solve exactly the variability that keeps AMP, Glacier, Recycleye, and EverestLabs from owning this niche. If Grip succeeds there first, every dirty deployment could deepen a dataset that cleaner-line competitors cannot cheaply reproduce.

I cannot underwrite that flywheel from the public evidence. Grip reports no customer, pilot, pick rate, contaminant-removal rate, uptime, deployment footprint, or retrofit process, while four funded competitors already disclose facility deployments or operating systems. This is not a penalty for being pre-seed; it is the absence of the one costly artifact that would distinguish a difficult machine from a compelling robotics description. The closest known exception is CosmicBrain AI: both pair pre-revenue physical AI with relevant founder depth, but CosmicBrain had a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept. Grip has disclosed neither.

My 36-month base case is that Grip remains below $1 million ARR and is still running facility-specific pilots rather than repeatable deployments. I pass today, narrowly: the founder and lower-stack wedge are good enough to keep watching, but my own money needs evidence that the machine survives the waste stream. The flip condition is one named, paid compost or biogas deployment operating for 90 days with disclosed uptime, contaminant-removal performance, and customer economics superior to manual or suction-based sorting.
