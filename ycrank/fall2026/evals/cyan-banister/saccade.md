# Saccade -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I would like a routine visit to catch neurological change while someone still has years to plan their life. Saccade’s fifteen-minute eye-movement assessment offers a physical place to begin. But work backward: the signal must track confirmed pathology, distinguish useful early warning from ordinary variation, and lead to a decision that helps the patient. The dossier does not establish those transitions. A headset can measure something precisely while leaving us uncertain about what it means.

The most consequential detail is the distance between Saccade’s launch claim—detecting biomarkers up to fifteen years before symptoms—and its homepage disclosure that diagnostic use and individual risk prediction remain unvalidated. That does not establish deception; it does determine what I can underwrite. Earlier information returns agency only when people can interpret and use it. An asymptomatic result would not itself establish eligibility for Kisunla, which requires confirmed amyloid pathology and treatment initiation at mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia. Why should someone learn this result today? What changes tomorrow? Those questions sit inside the product.

Shreyas Bhardwaj gives me a reason to keep looking. His medical-device work and Hawkes Lab soft-robotics experience relate directly to building the instrument, and UCSB documents actual camera, illumination, chassis, and thermal-management engineering. This is tangible technical work. His biography supplies relevant preparation, although it does not yet establish the long-running obsession, repeated independent builds, or recruitment of complementary clinical talent that would make me his first believer. I would not confuse a short public biography with an absence of those qualities.

The strongest case against my pass is that Saccade could start in longevity clinics and build the evidence that makes broader neurological screening useful. Its October 2026 launch reports San Francisco clinic deployment; UCSB supplies an engineering record and a $7,500 competition award. My pre-revenue investment in **CosmicBrain AI** shows why missing revenue need not stop a physical-technology bet: a working prototype can precede commercial proof. The material difference was signed industrial proofs of concept and a documented lifelong building history. Saccade’s unnamed clinics and undisclosed assessment volumes do not yet provide equivalent evidence of committed use.

Saccade also touches my pattern of finding infrastructure beneath a visible beachhead. But the proposed longitudinal, pathology-linked dataset remains hypothetical, and a patent-pending attachment does not establish control of a scarce diagnostic bottleneck. ViewMind already offers overlapping eye-movement assessments. Nor does the dossier identify a particular fee, restriction, or gatekeeper whose removal transfers control to patients. The privacy gate matters here because neurological recordings and inferred risk are sensitive; the dossier establishes neither a severe privacy violation nor enough architecture to clear that gate. It names a CEO and supplies no evidence of the governance shell game I avoid.

I pass with my own money today. The issue is the missing bridge from an engineered measurement to useful knowledge, rather than the absence of pre-seed revenue. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently run, prospectively registered study showing that Saccade adds clinically useful discrimination against confirmed pathology beyond an appropriate existing assessment, at prespecified thresholds. That would give this promising instrument a demonstrated job.
