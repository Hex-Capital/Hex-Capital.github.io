# DeepReach Inc. -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Robots are learning thousands of occupations in the places where those occupations actually happen, and the scarce input is not another simulation—it is permissioned access to the long tail of human skill. DeepReach has found a leak from that future: 475 deployed stereo devices, more than 1,000 local experts across seven countries, and 150,000 professional-skill demonstrations collected in three months. NVIDIA’s GR00T N1 shows that egocentric human video already feeds robot policies. The missing piece is a clearer chain from today’s demonstrations to tomorrow’s production deployments, but this is more than fashionable “physical AI” language. The artifact exists.

The founder fit is unusually exact. Tim Li previously built HireIO to a reported $15 million in annual revenue and Talex.ai into a 70,000-person annotation network across ten countries. Chris Liu brings a computer-vision PhD, Meta ML-systems experience, and founding-engineer work at Aven. One founder understands how to recruit and operate distributed labor; the other understands whether its output is technically useful. Their ten-year relationship and explicit CEO/CTO split reduce, although do not eliminate, my governance concern. This looks like accumulated operating tuition rather than a category pivot invented for Demo Day.

The weird behavior is already visible. One hundred fifty entrepreneur-run data businesses recruited local experts, gained workplace access, operated DeepReach hardware, and generated 500,000 operation clips. Customers then signed several million dollars of contracts while imposing environment-diversity constraints and rejecting concentrated batches. Those are costly actions on both sides of a functioning network. The discrepancy between “nearly 150,000 clips” in the company description and “500,000 operation clips” in the launch materials needs reconciliation, but the underlying behavioral signal remains far stronger than a waitlist or laboratory prototype.

The system wound is real but incompletely priced. Centralized capture produces controlled environments, while robot developers need demonstrations distributed across occupations and geography. DeepReach replaces direct hiring and laboratory access with locally operated collection businesses, returning some occupational control to entrepreneurs and experts. But the dossier does not disclose operator economics, workplace-access costs, or DeepReach’s margin. I cannot yet tell whether local operators own durable customer relationships or merely absorb collection complexity while the platform and model laboratories retain the rent.

The strongest case against my investment is not Scale AI’s size or synthetic trajectories by themselves. It is that DeepReach may be building a surveillance-heavy services network with no durable custody advantage. Warehouses, kitchens, farms, and repair shops contain bystanders, proprietary processes, and customer information; “compliance-verified” is not a consent, retention, deletion, or audit architecture. Meanwhile Scale AI already collects more than 1,000 demonstration hours daily, several startups offer overlapping egocentric data, and NVIDIA is explicitly reducing dependence on human demonstrations. Without exclusive access, repeat purchasing, or controlled rights, 475 devices could become fleet overhead rather than infrastructure.

I still invest because the team has already coordinated hardware, distributed businesses, skilled labor, quality constraints, and paying demand—the hidden operating stack competitors must reproduce. This is an enabling layer beneath robotics rather than a thin interface to one model provider, but the moat is execution and network access today, not yet proven data compounding. My verifiable flip condition is narrow: I pass if diligence cannot produce a documented, auditable consent-and-data-custody framework covering workers, bystanders, workplaces, retention, and deletion across all seven operating countries.
