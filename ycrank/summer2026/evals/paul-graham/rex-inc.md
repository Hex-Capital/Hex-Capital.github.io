# Rex -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Merlin Kafka and Lewis Blackwood worked together for four years at Sequence, but neither leaves the public hacker trail I usually find before I find the startup. That is Rex’s signature: an unusually credible pair of enterprise operators with a thinner technical genealogy than their product would suggest. Rex is not two consultants who discovered AI receivables on a market map. Kafka and Blackwood previously handled billions in receivables and more than 50 ERP integrations. But Rex gives me evidence of practiced competence before it gives me the unmistakable spoor of animals.

**Stripe** is the high rung for cofounder configuration: two brothers who had been building together since Auctomatic, with trust old enough to survive disagreement and complementary enough to ship. Rex approaches that rung because Kafka and Blackwood spent four years together at Sequence, one leading product and the other engineering. Rex diverges because working together inside someone else’s organization is not the same as failing, improvising, and rebuilding together. The relationship is unusually strong for a pre-seed pair, but Rex has not yet shown the Collison-style history where separating the founders from the artifact becomes impossible.

**Loopt** is the low rung for hacker genealogy: Sam Altman’s force of personality overrode the absence of the sort of named pre-company technical artifact that made Stripe or Reddit obvious hacker bets. Rex partly repeats that mechanism. Blackwood was an early GoCardless engineer, founded Personably, and led engineering at Sequence, which is much better evidence than a CS credential. But Rex shows zero public repositories on his GitHub, no public GitHub for Kafka, and no demo or open-source project predating Rex. Blackwood may be an excellent hacker whose work was private. I merely cannot award Rex the tribal signal I can actually see.

More than $500 million in receivables managed is Rex’s strongest evidence that the founders ship instead of merely pitch. Rex put Synthesia live in under three weeks, then completed 22,000 autonomous actions in four months across 30–40 procurement portals. Over the next 18 months, that resourcefulness can compound because every broken portal, disputed invoice, and missing purchase order becomes another procedure the founders must first solve manually and then encode. The deduction is limited: Rex provides no cereal-box stunt, rejection reversal, or pre-funding improvisation showing Kafka and Blackwood doing something unreasonable to stay alive. The artifact is real; the animal story remains unproved.

Sequence processed billions in receivables across more than 50 ERP integrations before Kafka and Blackwood started Rex. That makes Rex unusually organic: the founders spent four years inside the exact mess they now automate. The score would fall a tier if Rex were merely an AI layer chosen after surveying fashionable enterprise categories, or if Sequence’s work had kept the founders far from collections exceptions. It would rise to the very top if Kafka or Blackwood had built Rex first for a recurring problem they personally handled before deciding to form Rex. The dossier establishes lived proximity, not yet the Dropbox-style moment of building the thing because one founder could no longer tolerate the pain.

**Coinbase** is the closest single-anchor parallel for Rex’s schlep. Armstrong ran toward compliance; Rex runs toward invoice disputes, changing procurement portals, customer emails, ledger updates, and the possibility of sending the wrong collection message to a Fortune 100 customer. Rex’s SOC 2 Type II status inconsistency and Synthesia’s 30–40 portal workflows are not peripheral execution risks. They are the work. Fazeshift and Monk can reproduce an agent demo, but maintaining hundreds of account-specific procedures while taking responsibility under outcome- and SLA-based pricing is where Rex may turn schlep blindness into an advantage.

Synthesia’s 22,000 actions in four months lead me to predict that by July 2029 Rex will remain independent at $15–30 million in annual recurring revenue, with managed execution generating more revenue than the customer-operated platform. Rex’s experienced founder pair should keep winning complex deployments, while Rex’s weaker visible hacker genealogy and crowded agent market make a Stripe-like technical default less likely. The likely outcome is a valuable order-to-cash operator disguised as software, not a universal financial operating system.

HighRadius launched more than 180 finance agents, while Fazeshift and Monk have raised $22 million and $29 million for overlapping workflows. The strongest bear mechanism is bundling: Rex’s procedures become features, HighRadius discounts them into a wider suite, and Rex remains responsible for every portal failure without owning a durable technical advantage. Rex survives that objection today because Synthesia went live in under three weeks, executed 22,000 actions, and retrieved purchase orders worth more than $40 million—production evidence stronger than an agent catalog. I would reverse the verdict if Rex cannot produce, by July 2027, a second named customer with at least 10,000 autonomous actions and deployment in under four weeks. That would show Synthesia was an impressive custom project rather than the start of a compounding operation.
