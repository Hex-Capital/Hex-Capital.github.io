# Async -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Ben Smith dropped out at 20 and has already moved from nonprofit fundraising agents to Skope billing, legal agents, and now Async’s cross-industry workflow service. Async is an unusually adaptive founder story attached to an unusually ordinary capital profile. Learning rate is Async’s strongest signal; the absence of proprietary science, infrastructure, or a roadmap requiring more than $50M is its weakest. That pairing usually produces a competent vertical software business, not a consequence-first investment.

**Slash** (2024) is the high rung for learning rate because a documented pivot exposed founders capable of discarding their original thesis and earned a $100M Series C. Async shares the willingness to move: Skope billing became legal agents, while Async now targets law firms, healthcare clinics, and real-estate companies. The divergence is decisive. Slash’s pivot had a documented causal insight; Async’s dossier supplies no explanation of what Ben Smith and Connor Park learned at each transition. Async shows motion, but motion without an articulated thesis update can be wandering rather than learning.

**DoorDash** (2013) sits at the low rung on required-capital fit because capital-light logistics did not require a Khosla-sized science check; extraordinary distribution and ambition overrode that mismatch. Async has no equivalent override yet. Async scopes one or two workflows through a consultative audit, charges for outcomes, and publishes no capital-intensive technical milestone. Custom integrations and model bills may consume cash, but labor-intensive delivery is not capital-hostile innovation. I am over-resourced for Async unless the architecture becomes something materially deeper.

The $7.6B global AI-agent market compounds for Async only if each deployment converts customer-specific work into reusable infrastructure. Over the next 18 months, company memory, approval logic, and action history could turn Async’s two law-firm deployments into a repeatable legal operating layer. The opposite loop is more likely from the dossier: every new agreement requires another audit, integration, and maintenance burden. Async then scales implementation labor rather than displacing it. The economic mechanism does not break; consulting margins merely improve.

Anthropic’s 15 ready-to-run small-business workflows make the technical-risk score brutally simple. Async would flip a tier if long-horizon execution required a proprietary reliability breakthrough—auditable memory, permissioning, recovery, and autonomous action across hundreds of systems at error rates Claude or Gumloop could not match. Async has published no such benchmark, moat, or proprietary-data evidence. External models perform the cognition while Async supplies deployment and service. That is useful. It is not the **Commonwealth Fusion** pattern where technical risk itself repels consensus capital.

**KiOR** defied petroleum orthodoxy with falsifiable bets on catalyst yield, feedstock economics, and refinery integration. Async’s founder parallel stops at outsider energy: Smith sold shoes and collectibles before dropping out, while Park studied computer science at Michigan. Async has no public position that contradicts AI-agent consensus, no named expert forecast it intends to break, and no controversial technical claim. Avoiding career-domain-expert continuity earns some credit; entering one of the most crowded AI categories does not make Async contrarian.

Two named law firms—Clifton Law and the Law Office of Joshua Lowenthal—make my 36-month forecast concrete: by July 2029, Async will have narrowed into legal intake, conflict-check, and engagement automation, will remain independent at $1M–$4M ARR, and will no longer market equally to healthcare and real estate. Async’s rapid pivots make verticalization likely, while Async’s low-capital, customer-specific delivery model limits the probability of a category-defining outcome.

Clifton Law also supplies the strongest bull case: Async may be learning through paid deployments, outcome pricing may align incentives better than seats, and the billing-to-legal-to-broader-agents sequence could be the **Slash** exception unfolding before public metrics reveal it. Async could turn action history into a proprietary context layer and replace administrative labor across millions of small businesses. The dossier still loses that argument: Anthropic overlaps directly, Relevance AI and Gumloop have raised $37M and $70M, no moat is documented, and the SOC 2 Type I claim conflicts with Async’s own security page. I would flip only if Async reaches a verifiable milestone of at least $10M ARR from 100 or more customers at 80% or better gross margin by July 2027.
