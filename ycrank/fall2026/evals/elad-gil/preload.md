# Preload -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Preload sits at a plausible market opening: contact-aware learning could let robotics teams buy human demonstrations instead of collecting everything on robots. The controlling variable is **cost per successfully learned robot task**, including collection, retargeting, calibration, and validation. Cheaper human recordings matter only if they reduce that complete cost. The dossier identifies the transfer problem but supplies no Preload benchmark establishing the economic advantage. I can formulate the investment hypothesis; I cannot yet underwrite it.

The strongest affirmative evidence is the September 2026 Touch2Robot research showing improved robot replay completion from contact-aware feedback. That creates a credible technical reason to collect information absent from video. Preload’s combination of egocentric video, tactile gloves, and EMG addresses that opening. But the cited result belongs to an academic system, and the dossier provides neither a numerical improvement nor Preload-specific transfer results. This earns some credit for a newly feasible approach, without establishing a step change in customer economics.

Market structure makes that distinction consequential. Axonnic already offers EMG collection, while Human Archive collects synchronized multimodal demonstrations and announced an $8.2 million seed in May 2026. Having three modalities is therefore insufficient differentiation. Preload needs to deliver more useful training outcomes per dollar or accumulate something customers cannot cheaply reproduce. Dataset licensing is an inferred business model; exclusive rights, collection scale, and switching costs remain unverified. The physical-business red flag is a possibility here: custom instrumentation could retain service economics. The dossier does not establish that outcome, but it also does not demonstrate the margin reconstruction needed to overcome it.

The strongest case against passing is the team’s unusually relevant technical background. Li’s computational-neuroscience and Neuralink experience, Pandiaraju’s neural-interface work, and Wang’s EMG and motion-tracking research map directly onto the sensing problem. This fits my unstated pattern of entering physical markets where specialized expertise can change the economics. It also warrants checking the research-stage exception. However, the dossier attributes the transfer research to outside teams and documents no comparable founder-produced technical result supporting that override. Relevant expertise is real; costly founder commitment or prior startup learning is undocumented, so I keep those separate.

Preload’s October 1 launch attracted 246,186 tracker-reported video views by October 15. That is visibility, not evidence that robotics teams repeatedly train on its data. There is no documented customer usage or payment, so demand receives the absent-evidence default rather than an adverse score. Similarly, an integrated collection stack does not yet establish ownership of the recurring training workflow. Better models could increase the value of sensor-rich demonstrations, but Preload has not documented retained evaluation data, robot integrations, or feedback loops that let it capture that value. October’s TACROSS work also suggests that cross-sensor alignment methods are becoming more accessible.

I would pass on a personal check today. The technical opening is credible, but the market’s decisive economic proposition remains untested, and the primary market gates do not clear. My single flip condition is **a paid repeat deployment with a named robotics customer demonstrating at least 30% lower fully loaded cost per successfully learned task than its existing robot-demonstration process, on held-out contact-rich tasks**. That is my proposed investment threshold, not a claimed result. It would connect Preload’s sensing expertise to customer economics and repeat demand—the mechanism this company needs to prove.
