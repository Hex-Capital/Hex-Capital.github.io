# Greypoint Industries -- Elad Gil Evaluation

$55.25 billion of projected counter-UAS spend by 2034 makes **Greypoint Industries** a catalyst-rich but structurally non-index defense-hardware entrant: the market window is Greypoint Industries’s strongest dimension, while its need to displace deployed interceptor platforms is its weakest. Canada’s July 2026 Defence Drone Initiative explicitly requests low-cost, low-collateral interceptors, and the first ARROW contract suggests real buying behavior rather than hackathon tourism. The problem is that Greypoint Industries currently sells a product into the wave; it does not yet own the layer through which the wave must pass.

**Anduril** is the high rung on market re-opening: Ukraine exposed a procurement failure, and Anduril converted that catalyst into scaled contracts from a position already supported by Lattice and shipped hardware. Greypoint Industries shares the load-bearing mechanism—a recent conflict-driven realization that million-dollar missiles cannot economically answer mass-produced drones—and Canada’s new competitive supplier process makes the timing unusually explicit. Greypoint Industries diverges where it matters: Anduril entered its 2022 Series D with compounding contracts and founders who had shipped Oculus and Palantir, while Greypoint Industries discloses one ARROW contract and no range, accuracy, deployment, or production data.

**Lyft** exposed the low-rung failure mechanism on index position: entering behind a scaled leader in a market whose economics favor the existing network is not contrarian; it is buying the weaker version of the obvious bet. Greypoint Industries is not in a pure winner-take-all network market, but Anduril already couples Anvil to Lattice, Fortem reports more than 4,500 captures, and Airobotics has an operational airport deployment. Greypoint Industries could become an index on distributed counter-UAS only if LEGION becomes the interoperable sensing-and-cueing layer across multiple interceptors; today Greypoint Industries appears to be another integrated interceptor vendor fighting qualification competitions product by product.

The first ARROW contract is informative pre-seed traction, but Greypoint Industries provides no contract value, repeat order, revenue, or usage curve. Over the next 18 months, revenue velocity will compound only if that initial contract produces validated kill-rate data, a qualification record, and follow-on procurement; otherwise every military buyer restarts the diligence cycle and Greypoint Industries accumulates pilots rather than revenue. A signed defense contract is better than an LOI, but without conversion evidence Greypoint Industries remains between “false signal” and “just working,” well below the Harvey pattern where the dollars remove ambiguity.

Jordan Khoo’s General Dynamics and TerraSense controls work, Steven Xu’s Sailbot controls leadership, Nic Chu’s General Dynamics experience, and Darcy Zhang’s infantry service give Greypoint Industries unusually coherent founder-market fit. The score flips into the top founder-shipping tier if Greypoint Industries can show that the founders iterated ARROW through multiple field tests—specific versions, measured failure modes, and improvements delivered between customer reviews. The dossier instead establishes relevant credentials and a built system, but not the Perplexity-style cadence of discussing an issue on Monday and shipping the fix a week later.

**Color Genomics** worked because the hard scientific capability—commodity sequencing—already existed, leaving workflow, distribution, and operations as the primary execution problem. Greypoint Industries has not isolated the miracle as cleanly: LEGION must passively geolocate emitters, ARROW must autonomously intercept them, manufacturing must reach defense-grade reliability, and Greypoint Industries must clear competitive qualification plus controlled-goods and export requirements. None requires new physics, which prevents a science-project rejection, but the dependencies compound; Greypoint Industries is skating for the puck with several execution miracles still coupled together.

Canada’s July 2026 Defence Drone Initiative makes my 36-month forecast falsifiable: by July 2029, **Greypoint Industries** will have pivoted its center of gravity from autonomous ARROW interception toward LEGION sensing and C2 integration for Canadian Arctic surveillance, will remain below $5 million ARR, and will have no scaled ARROW deployment across a NATO force. The catalyst can keep Greypoint Industries alive and funded, but the low index position favors selling an interoperable software-and-sensor layer rather than financing a head-on interceptor manufacturing race against Anduril and Fortem.

Fortem’s 4,500 reported captures make the strongest opposing case sting: counter-UAS demand may be expanding so quickly that procurement becomes oligopolistic, and Greypoint Industries’s first contract, hackathon win, infantry insight, and General Dynamics-linked engineering could reproduce the **Anduril** known exception—shipped operators exploiting a defense re-opening despite hardware and procurement risk. Greypoint Industries has not yet earned that exception because no public performance metric, deployment volume, production yield, or repeat purchase separates ARROW from a qualified prototype. Three named NATO customers, at least $5 million ARR, and greater than 20% quarter-over-quarter revenue growth would demonstrate that Greypoint Industries is “just working” and flip my verdict to Invest.
