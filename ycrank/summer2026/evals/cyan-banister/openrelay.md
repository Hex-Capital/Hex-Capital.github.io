# OpenRelay -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. AI workloads do not belong to one GPU family or one cloud; they flow toward whatever combination of silicon, geography, price, and latency fits the job. OpenRelay’s 100 billion-plus weekly tokens across 22 locations and 11 accelerator SKUs are a real leak from that world. What is missing is the complete backward map: which workloads become portable first, which provider contracts and software abstractions must change, and why OpenRelay—not a hyperscaler or existing inference cloud—controls the resulting network.

The present system is irritating in a wonderfully concrete way. AI teams navigate quotas, drivers, consoles, contracts, and idle reservations, while usable accelerators sit fragmented across providers. OpenRelay turns that mess into a container upload and one OpenAI-compatible endpoint, and one production B300 deployment reportedly reduced costs by more than 25%. That returns infrastructure choice to AI teams and gives hardware owners another route to utilization. I want clearer unit economics showing who captures the current rent, but this is more than “cloud is expensive.”

The founders have paid unusually relevant operating tuition. Prashant Patel helped build Amazon Bedrock’s Custom Model Import into a multi-million-dollar-ARR product, then supported 10,000-plus-GPU clusters and 10 million-plus monthly serverless executions at Voltage Park. Jaden Wang built a half-megawatt data center at twenty, founded Heaviside Compute, and worked across TensorDock and Voltage Park. One founder understands the inference control plane; the other has physically assembled the capacity beneath it. That is pre-company obsession expressed through difficult artifacts, not résumé decoration.

The working product also clears my immersion test better than most pre-seed infrastructure companies. Processing 100 billion-plus tokens every week requires customers to entrust OpenRelay with production workloads, latency, and data—not merely sign an LOI. Operating across four continents and eleven accelerator types suggests the routing problem has survived contact with heterogeneous hardware. The missing evidence is customer concentration, repeat cohorts, revenue, and whether usage persists without promotional pricing. I see costly behavior; I cannot yet see durable customer captivity.

Here is the case that stings: OpenRelay may be a skilled brokerage layer in a market where RunPod already reports $120 million ARR, Together AI has immense capital, and hyperscalers can copy routing features. Its compatible endpoint makes adoption easy and departure easy. Dedicated VMs do not receive the automatic migration implied by the broader failover story, third-party hardware expands the consent and custody surface, and SOC 2 Type II is unfinished. Accumulated benchmarks could become a controlled asset, but the dossier does not establish a structural barrier or explain why providers will not expose the same telemetry elsewhere.

I would invest my own money because the founders, operating footprint, and production usage make this an enabling layer rather than a fashionable interface. My 36-month forecast is that OpenRelay reaches $15 million to $30 million ARR as an operator-neutral inference network, but remains in a hard fight to prove that routing data and provider density compound faster than switching. The one flip condition is governance: if the corporate documents reveal equal founder control without a contractual tiebreaker and one accountable leader, I pass.
