# Melty -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Melty has shipped something, but I cannot yet see what David Miller does when it breaks. The Windows launcher downloads mashups, installs dependencies, and starts games. Those are concrete accomplishments. The dossier contains no specific episode in which Miller encountered an obstacle, built a workaround, and changed the outcome within days or weeks. That leaves my most important founder test unanswered. It earns the missing-evidence default, without implying that Miller is passive.

The product is the strongest evidence here. Getting different games, dependencies, and multiplayer connections to cooperate involves real work. Melty fits my recurring interest in programmable tools: creators can use external coding agents to build and publish experiences. But the evidence stops at an available launcher and described capabilities. There is no reproducible compatibility benchmark, measured installation success rate, or documented creator building an unexpected workflow. I give the shipped software intermediate technical credit. I cannot infer the reliability of the underlying system from the convenience of its interface.

The strongest argument against passing is that Melty may already have assembled the beginnings of its market. A third-party snapshot reported 10,611 Discord members on October 26. Miller’s background coaching gamers gives him relevant exposure, and multiplayer invitations offer a plausible way for one delighted player to recruit another. A free product can spread before anyone knows how to charge for it. Yet Discord membership does not tell me whether ten friends repeatedly return to one mashup. The app and current mashups are free; paid content and tips exist only as possibilities in the terms. Neither attention nor prospective monetization establishes that narrow well of need.

The interesting schlep is making somebody else’s game modifications work on an ordinary player’s PC. Existing products such as Nexus Vortex establish that installation work has value. Melty could find its opening in the extra difficulty of cross-game experiences. Miller’s gaming background supplies context, but the dossier never connects a particular problem he experienced to this product’s origin. Similarly, the archived names LookLoot and Deployed Telemetry establish no evidence-driven learning sequence. I cannot award either an organic origin story or a good pivot by filling in the missing events.

There are also choices worth questioning. Requiring marketplace exclusivity for twelve months after removal could make recruiting the first good creators harder. Contractual control does not demonstrate that creators want to stay. Publisher restrictions and compatibility failures are concrete obstacles against which founder resourcefulness could become visible. The privacy allegations remain unverified; they do not establish misconduct. None of my known exceptions supplies an override here: the available product lacks the demonstrated user pull or independently validated proof needed to replace the missing founder evidence. The dossier also provides no basis for alleging partner dependence, premature delegation, or founder obstinacy.

I would pass on investing my own money today. My single flip condition is a documented four-week recovery from a real compatibility failure: Miller ships the fix, and the affected player cohort resumes weekly play through Melty. That would connect the working artifact to both resourcefulness and actual need. It would tell me much more than another ten thousand community members.
