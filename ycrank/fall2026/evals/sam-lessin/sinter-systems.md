# Sinter -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Sinter’s proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and need for a first scarce check are all unknown. YC membership establishes none of them. I cannot call this an auction, but I cannot underwrite a cheap, non-consensus entry either. The more interesting question is what my money would prove: that polymer tooling can close the gap between a printed prototype and repeatable production parts, at economics that make customers reorder.

There is a plausible venture moment inside that question. Sinter currently offers FDM production; polymer mold fabrication and fill, pack, and warp simulation are still described as “coming soon.” A finite experiment could measure tool life, dimensional repeatability, delivery time, and cost per accepted part against conventional tooling. But that is an experiment I am proposing. The dossier supplies no round-funded test, deadline, or pass/fail threshold. The $362.5 billion plastics market does not resolve whether this particular manufacturing process works.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ actual operating exposure. Oliver Johnson has operated a print farm and makerspace. Justin Kwon has worked on robotic fish processing at Shinkei and distributed model training at Manifold. Together, they have shipped a marketplace that reported onboarding more than 150 printers nationally, including more than 20 around NYC. This fits my unstated preference for people translating software into neglected physical operations. Moving from coordinating suppliers to controlling production could also put quality, delivery, and process learning within their control. That is the attractive possibility here. Their experience earns credit; it does not establish production-grade polymer tooling.

What Sinter owns that already causes a customer to buy remains unproven. Those 150 printers represent supply, while the current business presents in-house manufacturing. Neither the earlier network nor REST/MCP ordering interfaces establish repeat demand for today’s product. The 138 LinkedIn followers add no purchasing evidence. Meanwhile, Xometry already offers automated pricing and molding procurement. Sinter needs its manufacturing process to create a measurable customer advantage; a convenient ordering interface alone leaves it competing on capabilities an established supplier already provides.

Delete the next financing from the model and the dossier cannot yet tell me what survives. Order-specific quotes establish an offer, but realized revenue, contribution margins, burn, and runway are undisclosed. I am not treating a two-person pre-seed team’s missing financial history as evidence of bad economics. I am assigning the stated default because there is no demonstrated route from this check to self-funded operations. My exceptions for missing financing gates require unusually concrete conversion, cash economics, or proprietary access. The founders’ relevant experience is the strongest evidence here, but it does not supply those receipts.

I pass on the available evidence. Sinter could become a useful manufacturing business, and I would not force it into a global-monopoly story to make that worthwhile. My one verifiable condition for reopening underwriting is a completed, customer-paid polymer-tooling pilot with repeat orders and measured contribution sufficient to support a documented break-even plan without another financing. That would connect the technical claim, customer behavior, and cash outcome. Today those connections remain prospective.
