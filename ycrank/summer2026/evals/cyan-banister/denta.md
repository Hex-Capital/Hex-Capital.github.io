# Denta -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A dentist opens a practice without assembling an accounting department, training a front desk to reconcile insurers, or stitching together five systems; software observes the practice and completes the administrative work. I can work backward from that world: Denta first reads fragmented systems, then executes workflows across them, then replaces selected functions with its own practice-management layer. That is a plausible chain, but the dossier does not show which workflows have crossed from assistance into autonomous completion, how errors are resolved, or what becomes possible after administration disappears. “The practice runs itself” is still more destination than map.

Jack Beecher found the problem inside his father’s ten-practice Iowa dental group. That matters more to me than the Dartmouth line. Insurance posting and cross-location reporting are operating wounds he encountered before founding Denta, not inconveniences selected from an AI market map. It is the same useful mechanism that can turn operator pain into infrastructure: the material difference from Carta is that Denta has not yet shown that its family-business wound generalizes into a system customers cannot remove. There are no dated pre-company builds, recruited complementary leaders, or technical artifacts establishing the full Rizz–Tizz–Biz case, but the first-believer proof is real enough to keep me curious.

The broken system is visible, though not yet measured. Practices pay accounting firms and front-desk labor to move insurance payments manually while their operational truth remains divided among incompatible products. Denta can return attention and operating control to dentists, especially across multiple locations. But I cannot identify the rent precisely: no labor hours eliminated, posting delay reduced, denied claims recovered, or fee displaced. Without that number, automation may be valuable while remaining an optional efficiency product.

A DSO client and “multiple practices on Denta” are better evidence than a waitlist. Someone has apparently entrusted a working product with live administrative data, which is costly behavior in healthcare. The evidence stops just before it becomes strange or compelling: there is no customer count, cohort, revenue, repeat-workflow volume, or documented action completed without human intervention. I make exceptions to missing revenue when a functioning artifact, signed proof, and unusually deep builder history reinforce one another. Here, the founder reports usage, but the public record cannot yet distinguish production dependence from founder-intensive implementation.

The strongest bull case is that these implementations are quietly teaching Denta every ugly mapping between practice systems. A solo founder with access to ten friendly practices and an outside DSO could accumulate the workflow rules needed to move from integration layer to native operating system. That would be the concealed infrastructure underneath the “AI receptionist” wave. Today the opposite evidence is stronger: Archy already offers native payment posting, notes, analytics, communications, and eligibility; CareStack serves more than 3,000 practices; Denta controls no disclosed dataset or technical bottleneck and depends on third-party practice software. Anthropic may also process health, financial, input, and output data, while the public privacy policy does not describe the limiting architecture. For me, that consent-and-custody gap is a gate, not paperwork.

I expect Denta to reach roughly $1–3 million in ARR within 36 months through implementation-heavy DSO contracts, but not yet displace a practice-management system. That could be a respectable business; it is not presently the six-years-ahead enabling layer I invest my own money behind. I would not write the check today. One verifiable condition flips me: an unaffiliated cohort of at least three DSOs using Denta to complete a named insurance or accounting workflow autonomously, with measured labor removal and no founder-managed exception handling.
