# Mnemonic Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Mnemonic Labs built its training pipeline and architecture in under six weeks. That is the most useful fact in the application. Two founders have made something run. But I cannot find the next fact I want: what broke, and what did they do about it? Building quickly and being resourceful under resistance are different observations. The dossier establishes the first, provisionally. It leaves the second unanswered.

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders may know something their former employers cannot afford to pursue. Videh worked in quantitative research at Citadel and Walleye; Kalki worked at Tower Research, where the company attributes $20 million in annual PnL to his work. Their argument about trading desks’ short profitability horizons could explain why capable insiders would leave to attempt longer-horizon research. That makes this substantially more interesting than two programmers discovering that financial markets contain money. But the $20 million belongs to prior work, and the dossier does not identify a particular problem they encountered there that forced them to invent this approach. Relevant experience earns attention. It does not establish the origin story or the neglected operational burden.

The current run exceeds 25TB of tokens. That measures an input. The proposition being tested is whether more training produces useful new strategies, so the important output is performance against a fixed baseline after trading costs. Mnemonic has disclosed neither that comparison nor a scaling curve. I give the reported pipeline partial technical credit; I cannot give it the credit due a demonstrated trading advantage. This is also where the strongest bull case stops short of my unstated preference for concrete proof before the round. The founders have assembled research machinery, but have not yet disclosed the result that makes the machinery valuable.

There are two possible businesses here, and they require different evidence. A proprietary trading operation can be its own first user; it need not acquire software customers to establish demand. Licensing requires a desk that repeatedly uses or pays for the product. Mnemonic has disclosed neither current trading results nor repeat external usage, and its commercial model remains unspecified. The broader $26.3 billion algorithmic-trading estimate cannot answer either question. Prodigy’s overlapping foundation-model work also means the category description supplies little differentiation. The useful distinction will have to appear in what the model can do.

The brothers previously researched robotics, but changing fields does not by itself demonstrate learning. There is no documented observation that rejected the earlier route while preserving an underlying goal. Nor does the dossier establish the active red flags that would make me reject them outright: dependence on a major partner, premature delegation, or waiting for funding before beginning. The AI-written coverage is a third party’s article, not evidence that the founders outsource their own thinking. Still, the known exceptions require a convincing working product, unmistakable pull, or validated technical results. A running training pipeline does not yet supply that override.

I would pass on Mnemonic Labs today. The decisive gap is evidence of founder action under a real constraint, coupled with proof that the research produces something useful. One verifiable result could change my decision: an independently auditable six-week live-trading trial, using a frozen evaluation protocol, that beats a prespecified baseline after costs and documents a material execution blocker the founders themselves overcame during the trial. That would connect the people, their response to resistance, and the technical result. At present those connections are still hypotheses.
