# Keres -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Christopher Palazzolo’s experience is the strongest reason to take Keres seriously. More than four years across Boom’s aircraft maintenance, avionics, flight testing, and XB-1 production operations put him close to the physical consequences of engineering decisions. That is relevant specific knowledge. It earns substantial founder credit, but not the highest band: the dossier does not establish that either founder has built an interceptor or sold into military air defense. Parker Jamieson’s missing biography remains uncertainty.

The decisive question is what survives the engagement. Garmur proposes to destroy the target and recover the aircraft. If that works repeatedly, Keres could spread expensive propulsion, sensing, and control hardware across multiple kills. But reuse compounds economically only when inspection, repairs, and turnaround preserve the savings. The public description provides neither a demonstrated interception-and-recovery sequence nor operating-cost evidence. A proposed aircraft supplies no established hardware advantage merely because copying software would be insufficient to reproduce it.

This is the strongest argument against passing: Palazzolo has worked inside aircraft operations, and the product attacks an actual physical bottleneck—interceptor inventory and engagement cost. Keres fits my recent preference for solving constraints outside pure software. Anduril’s Roadrunner-M preserves the aircraft when an engagement does not require destruction; successful recovery after a kill would therefore be a meaningful distinction. Yet the difference currently resides in the claim. Fortem already offers autonomous capture and has disclosed military contracts. Competitors’ deployment evidence makes Keres’s missing demonstration consequential without proving its approach cannot work.

The broader counter-UAS spending forecast—$12.6 billion in 2026—establishes context, not Keres’s accessible market. The dossier does not map the initial buyer and capability into independently supported adjacent demand. Nor does it establish my preferred compounding mechanism: one customer making the product or distribution better for another. Shared mission learning could eventually help, but proprietary data rights and cross-customer improvement are hypotheses. Aircraft sales and maintenance are also inferred; the October 12 media profile demonstrates neither payment nor direct value capture. These dimensions receive their absent-evidence defaults.

I find no documented integrity problem, artificial financing deadline, or founder instability. My known exceptions do not resolve the remaining gap: the dossier establishes neither a self-reinforcing network that warrants overlooking monetization nor the demonstrated prototype and unusual capital efficiency that can justify a prelaunch investment. Keres has credible operating experience and an important problem. Neither establishes that this particular machine works.

I pass with my own money today. My flip condition is one paid military pilot that independently documents repeated target destruction, aircraft recovery, and return to service at a lower fully burdened cost per kill than the buyer’s existing alternative. That single result would turn the proposed mechanism into evidence across execution, hardware capability, and value capture.
