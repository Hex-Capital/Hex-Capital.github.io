# UFO.ai -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

UFO.ai has evidence that its founders can build consequential software. It has no disclosed evidence that the round is a good purchase. Post-money valuation, competing term sheets, and the capital-funded experiment are all unknown. I will not infer an auction from YC membership or founder pedigree. But I cannot underwrite scarce capital without knowing the price or what truth my check buys. Those two dimensions receive their missing-evidence defaults.

The strongest argument for writing that check is Alex Graveley’s combination of GitHub Copilot, Comet, and Hackpad, which Dropbox acquired. Those are shipped receipts across AI execution and collaborative work—the two systems UFO.ai is joining. Marshall Kiely adds founding AI engineering experience at Notable Health and finance experience at Perplexity. This fits my pattern of backing people who can translate between ecosystems. The published runtime’s separate authorization, identity, execution, and accounting layers make that experience tangible. Founder capability earns substantial credit; it cannot establish customer ownership or attractive financing terms by itself.

Self-hosting is the most interesting economic possibility here. An Apache-licensed runtime, customer-supplied model keys, and unlimited workspace members could help businesses own their automation rather than accumulate another set of software seats. But who captures the value? Customers can retain their workflows, competitors can reuse the core, and UFO.ai must earn its hosted-service premium. Glean and Dust already combine company context with agent execution. The September 27 hackathon and repository’s 99 stars and 13 forks establish public distribution activity; they do not establish that proprietary access caused a purchase. There is no documented buyer replacing an external vendor with UFO.ai, so the owned-distribution dimension stays at default.

The prepaid billing model collects cash before consumption, but a billing mechanism is not demonstrated economics. Task rates, revenue, gross margin, burn, and runway are undisclosed. More concretely, the September 23 engineering account describes an agent reusing an archived repository despite a stored correction and requiring manual recovery. That makes human recovery cost part of the underwriting: repeated paid tasks must remain profitable after errors and intervention. The disclosure does not prove negative margins, and I give no penalty for an invented financing dependency. It does prevent me from treating autonomy as an established source of operating leverage.

I pass at **33/100**, with considerable respect for the builders. The objection that stings is straightforward: this team has already helped create major changes in how people use software, and customer-controlled deployment could become the purchasing requirement that distinguishes UFO.ai from hosted alternatives. That is a credible bull case. It still lacks the measured conversion, retained usage, or cash economics that justify overriding my financing gates. The prior-exit exception also requires an explicit capital plan and suitable terms; an exit alone does not qualify. My one verifiable flip condition is a signed sub-$10 million post-money financing agreement funding a customer-committed, six-month experiment with a predefined positive contribution-profit threshold for recurring tasks, including model, infrastructure, and human recovery costs. That would give this check a price and a specific card to flip.
