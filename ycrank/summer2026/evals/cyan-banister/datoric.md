# Datoric -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Models no longer learn mainly from whatever the open web happened to preserve; robots, computer-use agents, and synthetic people require commissioned human behavior with a defensible chain of consent. Datoric’s 250,000 computer-use traces, approximately 15,000 hours of screen activity, and sample-level rights records are a real leak from that world. What is missing is the map between here and there: the dossier does not explain which model capabilities make each new modality necessary, how collection economics change, or why Datoric becomes the permanent system of record rather than this year’s supplier.

The present artifact is unusually loud for pre-seed. Nearly seven figures of revenue in 30 days, more than 300,000 active contributors, and a commercial dataset already containing substantial human labor are not waitlist theater. Customers are apparently paying for difficult-to-source behavior, contributors are performing it, and Datoric is packaging it through private, modality-separated systems. I discount the revenue because customer count, concentration, and repeat purchasing are undisclosed, but I do not explain it away merely because the company is young.

The hidden system problem is also real: manipulated submissions, uncertain consent, and broken provenance can make training data unusable or legally dangerous. Datoric responds with contributor-linked samples, engagement-specific licenses, isolated projects, and consent before capture. That is good custody architecture. It is not yet a fully exposed rent: the dossier does not quantify the cost of rejected data, rights remediation, model retraining, or the share currently captured by an incumbent gatekeeper. The agency returned is clearest for buyers seeking auditable training rights, less so for contributors whose compensation and continuing control remain unspecified.

Reddy’s 2023 automation of more than 15,000 hours of annotation work by bypassing platform QA is wonderfully uncomfortable evidence. He learned where contributor systems break by becoming the attack. Lin adds robotics and ML exposure plus a procedural-video benchmark. But I cannot confuse knowledge of an exploit with proof that I should entrust these founders with the consent and licensing records of 300,000 people. Their rapid transition from Arzule’s Winter 2026 partnership-intelligence pitch to Datoric’s Summer 2026 data business also weakens the pre-company-obsession case. There is technical tuition here, but no documented long arc of builds, customer work, or recruiting that shows this was the destination rather than the available wave.

The bear case stings because Datoric may be a fast-growing services operation sitting between giant customers and interchangeable labor. Scale AI, Turing, Mercor, and Prolific already possess larger networks and operating resources; Datoric discloses no contributor exclusivity, retention, structural barrier, or repeat-customer data. Nearly seven figures could be one urgent collection rather than durable demand. The company nevertheless controls more than a thin model wrapper: its contributor network, private capture systems, multimodal QA, and rights chain can support voice, video, computer-use, and robotics customers. That lower-stack position fits my pattern, while the explicit consent architecture keeps the privacy red flag from firing today.

I would invest, but with eyes open: the working system and costly user behavior outrun the founder-history and durability evidence. None of my known exceptions excuses a custody failure here; Datoric already has enough commercial proof that integrity must be a gate, not a promise. One verifiable condition flips me to a pass: customer-level invoices showing that more than 60% of trailing-90-day revenue came from a single, non-renewing collection project.
