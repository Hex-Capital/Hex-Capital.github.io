# OpenVector -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequential version of OpenVector is not better video analytics. It is a low-cost perception-and-action layer for physical businesses: existing cameras observe work, detect exceptions, and initiate responses without a human watching every feed. That could replace a meaningful expert-service and monitoring cost pool across manufacturing, logistics, retail, and security. But the dossier quantifies only a $37.84 billion video-analytics market—not monitoring labor, operational losses, affected workers, or a 10–100x resource consequence. The mechanism could be large; the consequence remains asserted rather than calculated.

The strongest evidence is technical. Andrey Gizdov’s CVPR 2025 Spotlight work samples roughly 3% of pixels and claims 5–10x lower bandwidth. Combined with Vishal Urlam’s embedded-systems and camera-infrastructure background, this is more than a generic vision-language-model wrapper. But bandwidth is only a supporting constraint. OpenVector has not shown 5–10x better event accuracy, latency, false-positive rates, or cost per monitored camera against Spot AI, Ambient.ai, or human monitoring. A published compression mechanism does not yet prove a superior autonomous worker.

The team is unusually aligned with the problem for four people: multimodal research, embedded systems, camera networks, and a collaboration originating in the MIT Grand Hack project. That earns attention. It does not yet demonstrate the founder property I care about most after technical ambition: evidence-driven belief revision and recruiting specifically against identified weaknesses. The unexplained coexistence of Foveal Vision and OpenVector might reflect healthy tactical evolution, but without a documented pivot and the evidence that caused it, I will not award learning-rate credit for ambiguity.

The largest kill risk is long-horizon reliability, not bandwidth. A system applying POS discounts or altering customer profiles can destroy trust with a small number of false actions. Human review and configurable confidence thresholds contain damage but also reduce the claimed labor substitution. OpenVector has named the risk, yet there is no decisive experiment: no hours observed, event classes tested, precision threshold, false-action budget, customer acceptance criterion, or stop condition. A fall 2026 station delivery is a shipping milestone, not risk removal.

The bull case stings. OpenVector reports live use cases in four sectors, works with installed cameras, offers offline inference, and already exposes actions through calls, email, POS/CRM updates, and webhooks. That compatibility with existing infrastructure could make a horizontal workflow platform more valuable than a narrowly packaged safety or security product. Competitors raising $61–93 million also confirms that customers spend on the problem. If deployment corrections compound into proprietary data, the present technical wedge could become a learning system. Public evidence, however, provides no named customer, deployment count, repeat usage, reservation count, or data-rights advantage. Breadth may currently be demos rather than a platform.

The $449 monthly or $6,499 upfront station price is plausibly below dedicated human monitoring, but there is no bottom-up inference, hardware, support, utilization, or gross-margin model. Nor is there evidence that the optional station beats a competitor’s per-camera economics without subsidized deployment labor. I would not invest my own money today. One verifiable condition flips the decision: three named industrial customers running at least 50 cameras each for six months, showing at least a 5x reduction in monitoring cost while maintaining over 95% precision on customer-defined events and fewer than one harmful autonomous action per 10,000 interventions.
