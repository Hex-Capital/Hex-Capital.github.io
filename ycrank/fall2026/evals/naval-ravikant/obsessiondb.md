# ObsessionDB -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

ObsessionDB’s founders have earned knowledge of this problem. All three worked at Numia; their launch describes turning infrastructure built there into a product. A former Numia CTO building a warehouse that Numia actually uses is stronger evidence than a database résumé. The June 2026 case study reports a 200 TB migration and monthly workload costs falling from roughly $8,000 to $2,000. The founders have encountered the constraints they now sell relief from. That earns substantial founder credit.

The difficulty is identifying what compounds beyond their expertise. SharedMergeTree, distributed NVMe caching, and locality-aware routing are concrete engineering work. But proprietary implementation does not establish an advantage that survives someone reproducing the implementation. The dossier identifies no exclusive workload data, proprietary model, or demonstrated switching cost. ClickHouse controls the upstream engine and competes in the managed service market. If it matches ObsessionDB’s economics, what remains that customers cannot buy elsewhere? Production experience might become the answer. It has not yet been shown to accumulate into a protected asset.

There is room to maneuver. ObsessionDB already describes workloads migrating from BigQuery and Elasticsearch, spanning analytics and customer-facing APIs. Shared storage and separately provisioned compute can serve those buyers without requiring the agent category to develop exactly as advertised. That is a credible expansion mechanism, stronger than attaching the company to a $14.1 billion parent-market estimate. The market is large enough; the harder question is how much economic value an independent operator can retain beside its upstream supplier.

The strongest case for writing a check is that these founders can discover the durable advantage through deployment. Paying production customers, millions of API requests, and a largest customer operating over 50 TB give them real environments in which to learn. I can accept an absent user network when unusually capable founders have built a working product. I can also accept incomplete monetization evidence when adoption supplies another powerful mechanism. Here, direct charging is already present—monthly compute and storage, plus annual BYOC fees—but customer cost savings do not reveal ObsessionDB’s gross profit. Numia’s shared founder history also makes its testimonial less informative about repeatable acquisition outside that relationship.

ObsessionDB fits my preference for infrastructure bottlenecks more closely than fashionable application software. It does not yet fit my preference for customers creating value for other customers. Neither the developer tier nor 42 GitHub stars establishes that loop. Migration assistance and workload audits can win business, but the dossier does not show acquisition or utility improving as participation grows. The founders’ operating knowledge is the strongest asset; it still appears concentrated in the team rather than embedded in a system that improves without proportional labor.

I would hold my money today. The security disclosure mismatch deserves a direct answer: the homepage advertises encryption at rest while the June security page describes it as forthcoming. That is unresolved evidence about accuracy, not proof of dishonesty; there is no documented unfair dealing or artificial financing pressure. My investment flip condition is a verifiable production benchmark showing that accumulated deployment learning gives ObsessionDB a sustained cost-performance advantage over current ClickHouse Cloud across independently acquired customer workloads, at positive contribution margins. That would connect specific knowledge to something customers keep paying for after competitors respond.
