# Insurf -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The first thing I want to know is what happened when Insurf met a payer, practice, or security requirement that prevented a case from moving. The dossier does not answer that. It describes selected specialty-practice pilots and a sophisticated workflow, but names no blocker, founder-built workaround, elapsed time, or resulting change in user behavior. That is fatal to my initial test. Chang Lu and Bryan Chung may be resourceful, but Brown credentials, medical research, and IEEE publications are evidence of ability, not resourcefulness under resistance.

There is nevertheless a real artifact here. Inveto combines intake, source-cited drafting, physician review, submission, follow-up, payer decisions, and posted-payment reporting. The versioned policy snapshots, submitted text, attestation events, and payment evidence form a sensible audit trail. Deterministic matching and staff-controlled submission also show that the founders noticed where a generic language model is unsafe. But production PHI is still disabled, SOC 2 has not been issued, and AWS monitoring and branch protection remain incomplete. This is working-system evidence, not yet a measured technical proof: there is no case count, accuracy result, time reduction, appeal recovery rate, or independently observed threshold.

The narrow well is plausible but not demonstrated. Providers reportedly spend 13 staff-hours on roughly 40 prior-authorizations each week, and specialty practices are a more useful starting niche than “healthcare.” Yet Insurf discloses neither the practices nor what they repeatedly use. “Pilot” can mean anything from daily dependence to a polite meeting. The 442 reactions to Bryan’s YC announcement are investor-legible attention, not demand. No revenue, retention, recovered payments, or pilot outcomes show that users pull this crude product back after encountering its limitations.

The strongest bull case is that the founders have chosen exactly the sort of ugly work that creates an opening. They must reconcile medical records, payer policies, insurer portals, physician attestations, BAAs, appeals, and payment outcomes. Lu supplies clinical context and Chung supplies technical depth. If those pilots are already moving real cases, the public dossier could lag the company substantially. But this is also enterprise healthcare software built by young founders whose experience persuading practice administrators and compliance officers is unproven. Dependence on CoverMyMeds and athenaOne interfaces compounds that risk. The known exception for unusually young founders requires numerical technical proof and direct problem exposure; Insurf currently has the resemblance, but not the proof.

The market will not wait for them to acquire it. Silna reports 50,000 patients served, Anterior supports organizations covering 50 million lives, and Cohere processes more than 12 million requests annually. Insurf’s provider-side authorization-and-appeal loop is differentiated, but its future payer-specific dataset is still hypothetical and the cited denial-management market is only $1.32 billion globally. My 36-month base case is that the team narrows into an appeal-drafting or workflow component sold through larger revenue-cycle vendors rather than becoming independent decision infrastructure.

I would pass today because the operating gate fails: there is neither documented resourcefulness nor enough measured product or user evidence to override it. One result would flip me: a named specialty practice publishing a cohort of at least 100 real cases showing repeated weekly use, materially reduced staff time or recovered payments, and the specific compliance or payer-interface blocker the founders themselves shipped around. That would turn three vague claims—resourcefulness, technical proof, and user pull—into observable facts.
