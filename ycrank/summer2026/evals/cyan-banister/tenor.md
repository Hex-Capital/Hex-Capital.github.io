# Tenor -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: companies employ fleets of software workers, and every worker carries a budget, an accountable owner, a success definition, and a ledger connecting its actions to business results. Tenor has the right nouns for that world. The 280-fold decline in fixed-capability inference costs is a present-day leak because cheap execution permits agent proliferation, which eventually makes economic governance unavoidable. But the dossier does not map the intervening steps or show that Tenor’s allocation loop already works. This is a plausible future assembled from the category, not yet a future disclosed by the product.

The ordinary object I keep poking is the token bill. Why is purported labor still purchased and monitored like undifferentiated cloud plumbing? Tenor’s answer—assign responsibility, measure outcomes, and redirect tokens toward productive work—is more interesting than “build another agent.” It could return allocation control to COOs, CIOs, and AI leaders. Yet the company has not quantified the rent, identified who captures it, or demonstrated that cross-system outcomes can be attributed reliably. Without that, the economic identity is a label attached to spend rather than a working accountability system.

The team is the strongest signal. Muhtasham Oblokulov worked on production ML at Munich Re and co-authored StarCoder and StarCoder 2; Amgad Al-Zamkan researched optimization objectives and adaptive decision systems after studying at TUM and Berkeley; Hamze Al-Zamkan operated inside appliedAI, startups, and TUM.ai. Those are real technical artifacts and unusually relevant ingredients for a three-person pre-seed team. What is missing is evidence of pre-company obsession: no dated sequence of attribution experiments, customer wounds, independently shipped systems, or enterprise users that persuaded the founders before AI workers became a crowded category.

The product evidence is much thinner. Tenor’s support material describes Slack scopes, administrator approval, configurable access, escalation paths, and deletion requests, but documentation proves a contemplated operating surface, not costly user behavior. The public record contains no customer, revenue, repeat deployment, attribution benchmark, or verified outcome. Its 392 LinkedIn followers and Summer 2026 YC launch are attention signals; they do not show anyone entrusting Tenor with a budget or changing how work is assigned.

The sharp bull case is that Tenor becomes the economic control plane above many agent vendors. Falling inference prices can increase aggregate spending, and neither an agent builder nor a knowledge graph automatically answers which worker created value. The bear case stings more today: Relevance AI already sells agent teams, Artisan has reached $5 million ARR, and Glean has reached $200 million ARR, while Tenor controls no demonstrated data asset, distribution channel, or technical bottleneck. Persistent worker histories might eventually create switching costs, but that is inferred. Processing Slack data and durable correction histories also creates a consent-and-custody burden that administrator approval and deletion requests do not fully resolve.

My 36-month forecast is that Tenor narrows from “AI workers” into AI FinOps and agent observability while remaining below $1 million ARR. I pass today—not because a Summer 2026 company lacks mature revenue, but because its strongest evidence is team capability while its defining economic loop has produced no observable behavior. One result would flip me: a named 90-day enterprise deployment in which a finance team audits Tenor’s attribution and uses it to reallocate at least $100,000 of annualized AI spend.
