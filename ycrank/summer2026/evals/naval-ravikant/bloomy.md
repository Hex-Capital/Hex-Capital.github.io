# Bloomy -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Alex Southmayd has specific knowledge earned through action. He taught seventh-grade ELA, tutored test-preparation students, built acquisition systems at Lyft, and has now placed Bloomy in six schools serving 500 students. The strongest evidence is not the Amherst, Stanford, or McKinsey résumé. It is that a former teacher assembled a 1,200-plus-skill curriculum, integrated school diagnostics, sold deployments, and observed voluntary after-hours use within months. This is unusually dense founder-problem fit for pre-seed.

The weakness appears when I copy the code. Bloomy’s knowledge graph, standards-aligned content, assessment telemetry, and i-Ready and NWEA MAP integrations are real product assets, but the dossier does not show that they are scarce. IXL already offers more than 20,000 skills, Khan Academy has distribution through over 600 districts, and Southmayd says coding-agent fleets generate much of Bloomy’s code and curriculum. Agents demonstrate his leverage; they also give competitors the same leverage. The future proprietary asset could be longitudinal skill-level data tied to outcomes, but a future moat is not a present moat.

The early behavior is better than the architecture. Five hundred students across six schools, roughly 90% of surveyed pilot students wanting to continue, and nearly one in five students using Bloomy voluntarily after hours are meaningful signals. Reported ARR doubling every two weeks is directionally encouraging, and the company names both its payer and price: families pay $279 annually per subject, while schools buy customized contracts. But without absolute ARR, renewals, or contribution economics, growth rates can describe a very small base. The reported 2× NWEA MAP growth is promising rather than causal proof.

The market is broad enough to support a business: Bloomy can move from remediation into core ELA, mathematics, writing, family subscriptions, schools, districts, and ESA-funded purchasing. The estimated $5.13 billion adaptive-learning market is growing, but this is an established market, not an unexplored one. More importantly, Bloomy has no demonstrated participant loop. Students consume lessons; they do not create reusable supply, distribution, or utility for the next student. Each school still appears to require institutional selling, implementation, and trust. This resembles conventional educational software more than a permissionless learning network.

The strongest bull case is that direct user behavior should override an incomplete theory. A former teacher has already produced deployments, reported learning gains, repeat demand, and after-hours usage before conventional financing validation. That is the kind of strange early evidence I do not want to explain away. It is not yet a known-exception case, however: the behavioral sample is small, the efficacy evidence is company-reported, and the underlying product remains readily substitutable by larger incumbents. I see no integrity, artificial-deadline, or reassurance-seeking red flag. The conflict is structural, not personal.

My 36-month base case is Bloomy reaching $2 million to $6 million in ARR as a credible school-and-family mastery product while remaining vulnerable to incumbent bundling. That can be a good company without becoming a nonlinear angel outcome. I would watch rather than invest today. One condition flips the decision: an independently evaluated longitudinal cohort showing that Bloomy’s learning gains improve materially as its proprietary cross-school telemetry accumulates. That would turn usage data from a possibility into a compounding asset.
