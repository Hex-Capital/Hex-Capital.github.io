# GUILD -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Kaya Celebi brings machine learning from Hartree Partners and Morgan Stanley, but no aerospace-manufacturing or government-contracting scar tissue. That gives GUILD a peculiar signature: GUILD is highest on the ugly, capital-hungry, regulated-defense swing and lowest on the founder-origin test. The idea has mud on its boots; the founders do not yet. GUILD looks like people visiting a hard place with excellent tools, not people forced to build because they already lived there.

**SpaceX** is the high rung because the rocket was both the frontier artifact and the capital problem; the difficulty itself became the moat. GUILD shares the load-bearing mechanism: GUILD accepts compliance, materials, inspection, packaging, logistics, and delivery liability on government aerospace contracts. The divergence is ownership. SpaceX built the machine, while GUILD coordinates third-party factories with $500,000 against Hadrian’s $500 million. I like the audacity, but GUILD is one supplier failure away from discovering that accountability without control is merely concentrated pain.

**The consensus YC AI-agent darling** is the low rung because credentialed founders discover a market in a memo and wrap somebody else’s model around it. That failure pattern appears uncomfortably inside GUILD: Celebi is a Columbia-and-Duke machine-learning engineer, Gurlemis is a former Morgan Stanley associate, and neither biography documents making aerospace parts or fulfilling a government contract. Celebi’s 183-star flight-analysis repository is a rebuttal, not a cure. GUILD supplies no physical moment when either founder felt the procurement problem, so the absent-evidence penalty applies.

**The $500,000 seed financing** may preserve GUILD’s magical weirdness because becoming the accountable prime contractor is much stranger than selling procurement software. Over 18 months, every completed GUILD contract could compound a private map among technical requirements, capable factories, realized prices, quality outcomes, and delivery times. That is the Crusoe-shaped possibility: an awkward operating business secretly becoming infrastructure. If GUILD merely extracts PDFs and routes jobs, however, Xometry can absorb the workflow and GUILD’s weirdness collapses into brokerage.

**Kaya Celebi’s CEO title** gives GUILD a visible captain but not a documented tiebreaker. GUILD would move up a tier on governance if Celebi holds explicit operating authority, founder vesting, and a written deadlock protocol with Gurlemis. Without those facts, “Co-Founder and CEO” is typography rather than constitutional law. GUILD avoids the worst HQ Trivia signal—two people sharing command—but silence about equity and conflict resolution earns only partial credit.

**Flock Safety** was physically legible before the spreadsheet: a camera, a Wi-Fi signal, an artifact sitting in the world. GUILD can eventually point to an aerospace part delivered under a named contract, which is more tangible than an API, but GUILD’s present product remains an invisible coordination layer between drawings, suppliers, and compliance documents. The factory touches the metal; the government touches the finished part; GUILD touches the workflow. Until one completed delivery can be inspected and traced through GUILD’s system, the physical-verifiability signal remains below midpoint.

**Hadrian’s $500 million manufacturing buildout** makes my 36-month forecast falsifiable: GUILD will abandon—or sharply narrow—the accountable-prime model and pivot toward defense-supplier quoting and compliance software, reaching less than $2 million ARR by July 2029 rather than becoming a scaled aerospace manufacturer. GUILD’s strongest attribute creates the pressure: regulated execution is attractive precisely because it is brutal. With no disclosed working-capital facility and no owned production capacity, GUILD must finance materials while absorbing supplier quality and schedule risk, making software margins the likely escape hatch.

**The February 2026 America First Arms Transfer Strategy** is the strongest case against that forecast: government demand is expanding, GUILD has a named CEO, and each successful contract could create a data flywheel unavailable to pure software vendors. Celebi’s commodities systems work and flight-analysis repository also raise the **DeepMind** exception—cross-domain technical range can occasionally substitute for lived origin. The case stings because GUILD may be a new kind of prime contractor, not a marketplace. The dossier still shows no revenue, completed contract, qualified-factory performance, or founder government-contracting history. Three on-time, conforming government deliveries with positive gross margin and a disclosed working-capital facility would flip my verdict to Invest.
