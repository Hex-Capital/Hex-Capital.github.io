# Tokenless -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Tokenless has earned attention through work. The founders published EnvCheck on September 30 and report 110 reward hacks and grading bugs across DeepSWE, Terminal-Bench, and BFCL, documented in 66 write-ups. Reproducible findings matter more than university affiliations. Agarwal’s alignment research and Wang’s infrastructure experience give that work context; the artifacts demonstrate that the team can find defects in the exact machinery it proposes to audit. This is substantial evidence of specific knowledge ([EnvCheck](https://usetokenless.com/blog/envcheck/), [findings](https://github.com/tokenless-ai/envcheck-findings)).

The investment question is what Tokenless owns after everyone reads those findings. Its published methodology and MIT-licensed grader help buyers understand the problem, but the dossier establishes no exclusive corpus, proprietary model, or accumulated switching cost. The specialized red-teaming model remains future work. Open research can create a market while leaving its economics available to everyone. I give the founders credit for discovering defects; I cannot count those same discoveries again as a defensible asset without evidence that subsequent deployments compound something competitors cannot obtain.

There is a plausible route from public benchmark audits into proprietary RL environments. Unnamed early partners exploring domain-specific reward hacking suggest someone wants that extension. But the adjacent buyers, budgets, and repeatable purchasing motion remain unspecified. Vals AI and Gray Swan establish nearby competition, not Tokenless’s addressable revenue. Nor does publishing useful findings establish a user-to-user loop: the dossier shows no customer contributions that improve another customer’s audit. Current-product pricing and receipts are also absent. Touchy’s $14.99 purchase option belongs to the previous product and earns no monetization credit here.

The strongest argument against passing is that Tokenless could occupy the point where expensive training runs become trustworthy. Its concrete defects already challenge established evaluation systems. If private engagements generate exclusive failure trajectories, the founders could turn today’s research advantage into tomorrow’s specialized auditor. My willingness to back demonstrated prototypes before distribution is established applies here. It keeps this company interesting. But the other exceptions—extraordinary recruiting evidence or an already compounding user network—are not established. The shift from Touchy alone shows neither unhealthy abandonment nor useful persistence. There is no documented integrity or financing-pressure veto; there is simply insufficient evidence that the valuable work becomes an owned, compounding business.

I would pass with my own money today. Tokenless fits my preference for technical infrastructure and work published without institutional permission, but it has not demonstrated the contribution mechanism that makes permissionless systems compound. My verifiable flip condition is a repeat paid deployment showing that an exclusive corpus from earlier audits materially improves defect detection in a new customer’s environment against the same auditor without that corpus. That would connect customer activity, durable advantage, and direct value capture. Today those connections remain hypotheses.
