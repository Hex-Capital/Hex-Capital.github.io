# Nines -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Idle compute is the consequential part of Nines’s proposition. A robot that connects fiber sooner could recover productive capacity as well as replace technician hours. That could make the economics unusually attractive. But the dossier gives no commissioning-delay baseline, value of stranded compute, or fraction of the bottleneck Nines can remove. The $16.2 billion data-center robotics estimate includes cleaning, inspection, and handling. It does not quantify this opportunity. I see a potentially important consequence, with insufficient evidence to price the option.

The immediate technical question is whether Nines can manipulate dense fiber reliably enough that the intervention creates less disruption than it removes. Its website sequence is identified as simulated inspection; there are no disclosed physical task-success, recovery, or endurance results. Microsoft’s published transceiver work establishes technical precedent while identifying adjacent-cable disturbance as a cascading-failure mechanism. Exclaim already targets optics, cabling, and drive replacement. These are specific engineering concerns, not reasons to surrender to incumbents. Nines nevertheless supplies no measured discontinuity against technicians or competing systems.

The strongest argument for investing now is the commissioning wedge. Working before a rack enters service could offer a more forgiving environment for collecting manipulation data before attempting live maintenance. Li brings Meta experience, Xerox PARC exposure, and autonomous-underwater-vehicle work; Eom brings software delivery experience from Patreon. That combination makes the attempt credible. It also fits my preference for AI that performs physical work and for industrial systems compatible with installed infrastructure. But the proprietary learning loop remains hypothetical, and these biographies do not document rapid belief revision or recruiting against a named weakness. The approximately 252,000 launch views establish attention, not operator validation.

I would spend the next dollar on a physical commissioning experiment. The dossier identifies reliability as an existential uncertainty but provides no company-owned test threshold, budget, timing, or stop condition. Likewise, site-specific adaptation could become repeatable deployment engineering or remain expensive custom work; pricing and operating-cost evidence do not distinguish those paths. Missing revenue is ordinary at this stage. Missing robot utilization, intervention burden, and deployment economics leaves unsubsidized cost parity unproven. My concern about interface-only AI is not established here, and there is no evidence of premature organizational scaling. The actual gap is evidence that the physical mechanism works economically.

I pass with my own money today. My execution-heavy investments and exceptions for extraordinary founders prevent a rigid requirement for novel science or completed milestones. They still require affirmative evidence of an exceptional instigator or a sufficiently demonstrated consequence; Nines’s current record supplies neither override. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an operator-witnessed commissioning trial across 10,000 representative fiber connections, achieving at least 99.9% autonomous completion, zero adjacent-connection disruptions, and fully loaded cost below one-fifth of the human baseline, with avoided compute-idle time measured. Those are proposed investment thresholds, not claimed results. Such a trial would turn the central promise into evidence worth underwriting.
