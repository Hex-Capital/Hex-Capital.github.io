# Sutrix -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Sutrix’s possible secret is that the durable asset in clinical research is the accumulated record of approved reconciliation decisions. Automating chart extraction would then be the entry point; controlling how institutions establish study-ready truth would be the business. The dossier does not establish this proposition, identify peers who reject it, or report an experiment testing it. The claimed 80% preparation-time reduction demonstrates potential usefulness. It does not establish why an important company must exist independently of other abstraction vendors.

The founding structure is substantially more convincing. Ritvik and Supratik Panuganti are brothers who report four years of building together, including work at HSS. That is affirmative evidence of alignment and shared execution, rather than a convenient partnership assembled for fundraising. Their clinical and engineering backgrounds explain how the product emerged from actual research work. Sutrix fits my unstated preference for companies formed through operating experience. Current ownership and full-time status remain undisclosed; I would neither assume perfect economic alignment nor invent a part-time-founder red flag.

What, precisely, does Sutrix get to own? Its initial customer is identifiable: research teams preparing studies from disconnected clinical records. Reusable definitions, source-linked fields, and approved exclusions could become valuable institutional memory. But the dossier establishes that these features exist, not that they create proprietary technology or measurable switching costs. Layer Health, Dyania, and Carta already address overlapping work. Their presence does not prove Sutrix is interchangeable, but study-specific positioning alone does not establish a monopoly mechanism. Requiring customers to validate outputs could deepen dependence on accumulated approvals—or preserve a labor-intensive service relationship. The distinction determines the investment.

The strongest objection to passing is that customers have already answered a question investors often leave theoretical. Sutrix reports paying teams at more than 10 institutions, work across 15-plus labs and 50-plus studies, and more than $150,000 in cumulative revenue before broader software access. That is meaningful commercial evidence at this stage. It supports a founder-led distribution route to clinical researchers and biostatisticians, although the LinkedIn demonstrations and access requests do not establish repeatable acquisition economics. Individually negotiated engagements also leave the revenue engine unresolved: the dossier does not separate software from services or disclose prices and margins. Paid delivery weakens the coordination-risk objection. It does not supply the independent technical breakthrough or realized power-law economics that would justify an exception to an unproved monopoly.

I would pass on a personal check today. The founders have demonstrated that they can organize and sell useful work; the evidence has not yet shown that delivering more work creates an increasingly exclusive position. My 36-month expectation is that individually scoped research engagements still generate most revenue. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: a documented renewal cohort showing institutions reject a cheaper, functionally comparable alternative because transferring their accumulated Sutrix definitions and reconciliation histories would require substantial revalidation. That would turn institutional memory from a plausible product benefit into evidence of value capture.
