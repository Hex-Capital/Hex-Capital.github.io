# Adalat AI -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

A witness should not lose another working day because a courtroom cannot get testimony onto the page. Six years forward, I can imagine Indian courts producing usable, multilingual records during hearings, with judges spending their attention on disputes rather than dictation. Adalat AI attacks an actual step along that path: stenographer shortages and handwritten records. But the dossier establishes transcription and workflow deployment; it does not establish the intervening steps from faster records to faster justice. I would not quietly award the company that entire future.

The most convincing artifact is people changing their working day. An external Oxford overview documented deployment in more than 4,000 courts across nine states by March 2026. A magistrate reports handling five to six witnesses daily instead of two to three. That is much more interesting than downloads or followers. Kerala’s November 2025 recording directive also demonstrates institutional acceptance, although mandated adoption cannot tell me how many judges would choose the product voluntarily. I want to sit beside the stenographer, watch the corrections, and see whether the hearing actually finishes sooner.

Why does this bottleneck exist? Courts depend on scarce transcription labor and manual documentation; litigants bear the waiting cost. Adalat’s free delivery removes a software purchasing expense, while its tools could return time to court staff and people seeking justice. That fits my preference for repairing an irritating institution and returning agency. The limit is important: the dossier identifies an operational shortage, but neither quantifies the resulting delay nor identifies an artificial restriction or rent recipient. Faster testimony also leaves scheduling, adjournments, and judicial capacity unresolved.

The founders have relevant operating tuition. Saxena has practiced inside the legal system; Bhattacharya brings multilingual NLP research and founding engineering experience. Their February 2024 connection produced an MVP for court demonstrations, giving me more than two impressive biographies. This fits my quieter pattern of backing mundane workflow repair. Still, the record does not establish years of independently pursued experiments or unusually strong recruiting evidence. Likewise, court integrations and implementation experience are useful assets, but they do not yet demonstrate control of a scarce technical dependency. TERES and jhana already overlap the transcription and workflow layers.

The strongest argument against my hesitation is that Adalat has already persuaded institutions to reorganize consequential work around its software. A $500,000 grant in October 2025 supports a model in which public benefit can expand without charging courts. Working products and domain depth can justify a pre-revenue investment exception; missing receipts are not my objection. The material difference is that Adalat is a nonprofit with documented pro bono agreements. This dossier supplies no instrument through which my angel capital participates in its success. A valuable public institution can grow substantially without becoming an equity investment.

There is also a concrete privacy concern to resolve. The September 8, 2026 investigation demonstrated credential-enabled access to production administration. Adalat reports no confidential court-user data access and describes credential rotation and access restrictions; that is meaningful remediation, not independent proof that custody is now sound. I would not infer founder-control problems or dependence on one closed model supplier—the dossier establishes neither. My assessment is **Neutral**, and I would not write a personal angel investment check today. One verifiable condition would reopen that decision: a legally reviewed financing agreement establishing a credible investor return mechanism compatible with Adalat’s nonprofit status and existing pro bono commitments. Security verification would remain essential diligence.
