# COACH -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders acquired their knowledge by operating the problem. Mathieu and Thomas Perez previously ran a 44-rep field-sales business that produced €3.5 million in 14 months; they experienced the managerial blindness, travel, and uneven coaching that COACH now addresses. Nicolas Sebag adds a decade of software work and mobile experience. This is unusually strong founder evidence because the product encodes an operating system they needed themselves, not a market thesis assembled from interviews.

Execution is already translating into money. COACH reports reaching €1 million ARR within eight months, after reporting €500,000 ARR and 120% monthly growth, with 100% pilot conversion and no churn. Thousands of reps, 74,473 analyzed meetings, and claimed conversion-rate improvement suggest repeat usage rather than a disposable demonstration. The numbers are company-reported, the stated lift varies between 27% and 32%, and pricing and gross margins remain undisclosed. Those gaps weaken precision, but they do not erase direct value capture.

The hard question is what remains after a competitor copies the application. Transcription and language-model analysis come from Anthropic, while Rilla and Siro already sell substantially the same mobile recording and coaching workflow. The corpus of 74,473 meetings is a real potential asset, but COACH has not shown that it produces better recommendations than a competitor using commodity models and its own customer data. Data becomes a moat only when it changes outcomes; possession alone is storage.

There is nevertheless an attractive compounding mechanism hiding inside the product. COACH moves fragmented offline conversations into a searchable body of sales behavior, then compares each rep against the strongest performers. That can turn private operating knowledge into organizational leverage. But it is not yet a user network: the dossier provides no evidence that one customer improves acquisition or utility for another, and forward-deployed pilots and customer-success work still imply proportional company labor.

The initial wedge—organizations with at least five field reps—can expand into the broader $6.9 billion sales-enablement market, projected to reach $21.2 billion by 2033. The shared capability is plausible: capture conversations, connect behavior to outcomes, and prescribe improvement across more sales channels. Competition validates the demand while limiting the option. COACH can become a valuable vertical software company without becoming a defining network.

My base case is that COACH reaches $12–25 million ARR within 36 months through strong selling and expansion, but remains an execution-led sales platform rather than a self-reinforcing network. The strongest objection is that the present metrics may be an artifact of founder-led pilots: two heavily funded direct competitors, consent friction in markets such as California, an external model dependency, and inconsistent efficacy claims could compress retention once novelty fades. I still invest because exact-problem founder knowledge and unusually fast monetization preserve meaningful upside. I flip to Pass if a customer-level holdout test cannot show that COACH’s accumulated meeting corpus produces materially better conversion outcomes than the same workflow using commodity models alone.
