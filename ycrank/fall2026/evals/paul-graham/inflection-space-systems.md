# Inflection Space Systems -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Inflection Space Systems has founders who have built spacecraft. What I want to see next is what happened when building this spacecraft became difficult. Alex Lisso’s experience spans Millennium, Rocket Lab, Planet, and Interlune. Matt Hanley automated satellite integration and testing at Loft Orbital and worked on Lunar Photon at Rocket Lab. These are relevant accomplishments. But the dossier contains no specific Inflection obstacle followed by a founder-built workaround and a dated result. I cannot yet tell how quickly these founders turn resistance into progress. That is the central uncertainty behind my pass.

Their choice of problem makes sense. Hanley has worked on precisely the integration and testing work that spacecraft standardization must simplify. Qualification, payload interfaces, and production tooling are substantial schleps. This gives Inflection a credible connection to the problem, although the dossier does not identify the particular experience that caused the founders to start it. The strongest bull case is that they have seen enough bespoke spacecraft work to know which complexity can be eliminated. One standard platform could then let each production run improve the next.

Inflection reports that more than half its internally designed stack is built, including flight and ground software. That earns provisional technical credit. It does not establish that an integrated spacecraft works or that robotic assembly produces it cheaply. My willingness to back unfamiliar industrial technology depends on a measured artifact; the dossier supplies development progress without a disclosed acceptance test, environmental qualification result, or production-cycle measurement. The first flight booked for Q1 2028 need not prevent useful proof today. A ground test could answer an important question much sooner.

The commercial question is whether enough customers can use the same spacecraft without turning it back into a custom engineering project. Exponent’s 100 kg payload capacity and 1.5 kW orbit-average payload power define a specific envelope. Published prices fall from $1.5 million for one spacecraft to $700,000 each for one hundred. That makes standardization consequential, but those are offered prices, with neither realized margins nor named signed customers disclosed. Apex already pursues productized spacecraft manufacturing. Inflection needs evidence that a particular payload customer accepts its constraints and benefits enough to buy. LinkedIn followers and investor-event selection cannot answer that question.

There is also no documented product revision tied to customer or technical feedback. I give that the missing-evidence default; it does not show obstinacy. Similarly, hiring a Head of Business Development does not establish that the founders are avoiding early sales, and the dossier shows no dependence on an unnamed large partner. The explicit red flags are largely unproven. But the known exceptions do not supply an override either: there is no demonstrated integrated prototype, independently validated technical result, or unmistakable user pull strong enough to replace the missing founder evidence. Relevant experience makes the bull case plausible. It does not yet clear my investment gate.

I would pass today. One verifiable event would make me reconsider: a dated customer acceptance record showing that the founders encountered a specific payload-integration failure, shipped a working correction within weeks, and secured acceptance from the paying customer. That would connect their experience to resourcefulness, technical performance, and actual need. Inflection’s ambition is clear. I want to see how the founders behave when a real customer makes that ambition inconvenient.
