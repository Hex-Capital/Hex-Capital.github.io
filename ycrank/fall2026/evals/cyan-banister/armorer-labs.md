# Armorer Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a hospital allowing an agent to act because its authority is narrowly bounded, every consequential action is checked, and a security fix cannot silently break patient intake. Armorer Labs is building something that future needs: the connection between permission to act and proof that the action stays inside the fence. But that is my extrapolation. The dossier establishes present security capabilities; it does not establish the intervening steps from wrapping tool calls to institutions delegating consequential work. I want to distinguish a useful security product from evidence that a different world has begun.

The founders give me a reason to keep looking. Daniel Begimher and Cristian Leo report three years building AI security systems together at AWS, with concrete work spanning ASH, ThreatForest, and SIR-Bench. That is operating tuition in the actual problem, beyond an employer logo. Their complementary development and security-science backgrounds matter more than the reported 100,000-plus customers reached by an AWS agent, which belongs to their former employer. The record supports technical commitment and an established working relationship. It supplies less evidence of independently pursued obsession before those jobs, recruiting ability, or command of enterprise selling.

Why does a security finding still become an engineer’s homework assignment? Armorer identifies a real division of labor: tools discover vulnerabilities, while engineers must repair them and determine whether the repair broke the business. Generating fixes and checking preserved workflows could return time and control to those engineers. That is a specific operating wound. The dossier does not quantify the labor burden or identify a licensing rule, institutional gatekeeper, or rent recipient that Armorer displaces. I give it credit for the mechanism it describes, without turning ordinary workflow improvement into a claim of institutional liberation.

The strongest case for my money is underneath the interface. Armorer retains its runtime, model assets, policies, and evaluation data; its documented one-use authorization tokens address a concrete enforcement problem. This fits my pattern of looking for concealed infrastructure beneath a visible wave. The founders have also shipped public integration SDKs. Yet proprietary ownership does not establish a scarce dependency. The reported 1.9-millisecond checks and 98% accuracy come from a small, 50-document comparison, while protection requires removing direct credential paths. HiddenLayer, Zenity, and Noma already occupy adjacent enforcement territory. Armorer must make verified remediation consequential enough that customers choose its layer.

The Just Eat Takeaway engineering-director testimonial is the most useful behavioral clue: someone endorses issue resolution. It does not establish repeated production use, payment, or engineers trusting Armorer with consequential changes. The Guard repository’s 45 stars and 10 forks cannot supply that evidence. My known exception for technically committed, pre-revenue teams keeps this opportunity alive, but the dossier lacks comparable signed deployment commitments. Customer-environment deployment offers a useful custody option; it does not resolve consent and data-handling questions. Nor do two founders establish equal-control dysfunction. Those gates remain unverified, rather than failed.

I land at Neutral. Armorer Labs has credible builders and an enabling product, but my strongest signal is the team’s accumulated technical work; my weakest is evidence connecting today’s artifact to the future I would underwrite. Missing revenue or a completed SOC 2 report does not drive that judgment. **My flip condition is one named regulated customer documenting repeated production use in which Armorer’s verified fixes safely enabled an agent workflow previously blocked by security review.** That would show the product expanding what people can entrust to machines—and give me a reason to reach for my own checkbook.
