# Control Seat -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI-assisted development now lets a two-person team attack industrial-control software, but the customer is still a risk-sensitive plant operator, and the controlling variable is whether Control Seat can deploy above an installed SCADA system quickly enough to win recurring software revenue without becoming a custom integrator.

The overlay strategy is the right wedge. Years of configuration make wholesale SCADA replacement unattractive, while OPC UA, MQTT, SQL, and REST support let Control Seat enter through a monitoring or intelligence project and potentially expand into control, history, forecasting, and investigation. The Battex Chile deployment proves that the system can remotely monitor and control a real battery installation. It does not yet establish the threshold that matters: repeatable deployment time, recurring contract value, or expansion across plants. FactoryTalk and Siemens WinCC support also remain unfinished, constraining access to a substantial portion of the installed base.

The product is more durable than an AI dashboard. It touches field devices, retains operational history, integrates with existing systems, and can assume responsibility for an end-to-end operational loop. Better models should improve investigation and forecasting while leaving Control Seat’s permissions, integrations, and control state intact. This is the strongest part of the company. The opposing risk is architectural commoditization: Seeq, Litmus, and Augury have collectively raised at least $567.6 million and already cover adjacent analytics, OT-data, and predictive-maintenance functions. Control Seat therefore has to own deployment and control, not merely add natural-language analysis to data that incumbents already reach.

The economic discontinuity is less convincing. Warren Shepard says AI reduces the effort required to develop complex control systems, but the dossier does not quantify lower deployment cost, faster commissioning, higher customer margins, or a change from services economics to software economics. Custom pricing, integration work, support, and SLA options may be sensible initially, yet they also create the principal red flag: a software-aware systems integrator can grow revenue while retaining labor-intensive delivery and weak margins. The company’s broad product surface—PLC programming, networking, SCADA, historians, analytics, and commissioning—intensifies that risk.

Shepard and Grodnick have unusually direct technical context for a pre-seed team. Their SpaceX, Tesla, Microsoft, and Amazon experience covers manufacturing networks, SCADA, controls, security, flight software, and large-scale GenAI. They also encountered the incumbent systems before choosing the problem. That supports technical credibility, but it does not demonstrate second-time founder learning, unusual sacrifice, or the desperation signal I look for. Employer quality cannot substitute for evidence about motivation or execution under startup constraints.

My strongest bull case is that one live deployment plus deep controls experience is exactly what early evidence should look like in a slow-moving industrial market: start above legacy SCADA, earn trust through monitoring, then expand into the control plane and adjacent plants. I am not ready to write a check because neither the primary market gate nor the physical-business economics has cleared. The verifiable flip condition is three independent customers deploying Control Seat above existing SCADA in under 30 days each, paying at least $100,000 in recurring annual software fees excluding integration services. That would show the wedge is repeatable and that software, rather than project labor, captures the value.
