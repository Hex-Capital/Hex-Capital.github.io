# Qlo -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Commercial underwriters should spend their time deciding which risks deserve capital, while software continuously turns broker email, attachments, approved pricing logic, and renewal history into auditable decisions. Qlo has found a small leak from that world: one enterprise customer uses it for daily quoting, and Distinguished Programs reports roughly 3× primary-quote throughput. But the dossier does not show the path from that useful workstation to the future institution. It names no intermediate unlocks, no remaining technical distance, and no reason Qlo—rather than Federato, Sixfold, Kalepa, or a policy-administration vendor—owns the transition.

The founder story is more persuasive. Ryan Hu spent approximately 2.5 years inside MGA, carrier, and broker workflows through PinchLabs, built that business to roughly $500,000 ARR, and received acquisition term sheets before Qlo. That looks like operating tuition, not a recent category pivot. Liv Huang adds self-taught engineering and prior work at CuboAi. The team has recruited underwriting advisers with 22-plus and 14-plus years of experience, although the dossier does not establish how the founders learned to work together or whether Huang’s technical contribution creates a capability competitors cannot easily buy.

The product mechanism is exact. Qlo does not merely summarize submissions; it compiles macro-enabled Excel raters, preserves source references, applies the customer’s approved pricing logic, and generates quote-through-renewal artifacts without demanding PAS replacement. Daily use and 3× throughput are stronger evidence than a pile of unexercised LOIs. The paid pilot, parity check, and refund promise also force the artifact into contact with the ugly part of insurance: if the spreadsheet logic or policy documents are wrong, Qlo pays for the failure.

The bull case stings because this could become infrastructure through accumulation rather than invention. Every implementation captures another customer’s rating rules and decision history, while a narrow one-line-of-business entry point reduces deployment risk. My concern is that the dossier currently describes those switching costs as inferred. Two-week setups may encode consulting labor rather than a scalable compiler, and three heavily funded competitors already cover overlapping ingestion, rating, and quote-and-bind workflows. My 36-month base case is therefore a useful vertical SaaS business reaching $3 million to $8 million ARR among MGAs and program administrators, but not yet the underwriting system of record.

There is also a broken workflow but not yet a fully decoded broken rule. Qlo identifies manual movement among email, spreadsheets, raters, and policy systems, and the throughput result suggests real labor is being recovered. It does not quantify the wait, error cost, staffing constraint, or economic rent, nor show that underwriters gain new pricing or occupational agency rather than simply processing more submissions for the same institution. The distinction matters: workflow efficiency can produce a good company without revealing a system-level opening.

I would not write the personal check today. The product touches sensitive submissions, pricing logic, and policy records, yet the dossier supplies no explicit data-custody, consent, model-dependency, or surveillance-limiting architecture. That unresolved gate sits beside an undifferentiated six-year map and an unproven lower-stack moat. One verifiable result would flip me: ten unrelated MGAs renewing after twelve months, with new programs deployed within two weeks, independently measured rating parity, and evidence that configuration time falls rather than rises with each customer. That would show Qlo is compiling the hidden insurance operating layer, not staffing it with software-shaped services.
