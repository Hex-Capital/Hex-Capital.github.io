# OpenVector -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first check is $500,000 from YC. The post-money valuation and competing-term-sheet count are missing. That establishes “first check,” but not “scarce check”: YC’s standard capital says nothing about whether the next dollar is cheap or already being auctioned. I cannot underwrite convexity without the price, and the absence matters because OpenVector operates in an already fashionable intersection of AI, computer vision, and robotics.

The more important omission is what this capital is supposed to prove. Fall 2026 delivery of the OpenVector Station is a roadmap date, not a venture moment. The dossier names no deployment target, accuracy threshold, customer-conversion milestone, or finite experiment connecting the $500,000 to a dramatic change in value. “Turn any camera into an autonomous worker” actually contains several simultaneous tests: long-horizon visual reasoning, reliable event detection, safe downstream actions, legacy-camera integration, and cross-industry sales. Capital that advances all five without deciding which truth matters first becomes a product-development budget, not a discrete wager.

The technical team is the real asset. Andrey Gizdov co-authored CVPR 2025 Spotlight work behind a foveated system that samples roughly 3% of pixels and claims 5–10× lower bandwidth. Vishal Urlam brings embedded systems plus camera and network-protocol work at Tata. That is considerably more than résumé theater: they have shipped research and infrastructure on the machine side of the bridge. What is not yet demonstrated is the other side—operating authority inside a warehouse, restaurant, retailer, or security organization where false positives create discounts, customer-record changes, or physical interventions. OpenVector has built the translation technology; it has not shown that the founders own the workflow being translated.

That missing ownership is the decisive problem. “Live use cases” across four sectors are reported without a named customer, deployment count, reservation count, paid conversion, repeat usage, or data-rights evidence. The public distribution footprint—13 YC Launch votes, one GitHub follower, and no public repositories—does not itself matter, but it confirms that the founders’ combined LinkedIn reach has not been shown to cause a purchase. A refundable $100 reservation and a $449 monthly price are offers, not demand. Nor is deployment-generated data yet a dark pool; without contractual rights, retention, or demonstrated improvement from customer corrections, it is merely a possible future asset.

The strongest bull case is that the market is real and the system is unusually cheap to deploy: existing-camera support plus an offline Jetson station could let OpenVector reach operational environments that $61–93 million competitors have approached with narrower products. This resembles the mechanism that justified **Ando** despite AI risk—software embedded in a measurable enterprise workflow. The material difference is that Ando had quantified accuracy, paid conversion, and retention; OpenVector presently has none of the three. Here, model reliability is not an abstract risk because a detection can trigger a POS discount or modify a customer profile. Human review and confidence thresholds reduce harm, but they also weaken the claim that the camera has become an autonomous worker.

The $449 monthly price could support a good business, but a price sheet is not economics. There is no revenue, hardware cost, gross margin, burn, or runway showing that the company can survive without another round. My 36-month base case is that OpenVector narrows from horizontal camera automation into one security or industrial-monitoring vertical and remains below $2 million ARR; the founders are strong enough to produce useful technology, but the current product spans too many workflows without owned distribution. I am passing. The one condition that flips me is a named multi-site operator deploying at least 25 paid stations and renewing them after six months with published detection accuracy and gross margin—one compact result proving purchase, reliability, workflow ownership, and viable cash economics.
