# Obia -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI research could let industrial tenants evaluate more properties while letting Obia’s brokers close more leases per employee. That is the market opening. The controlling variable is **net commission contribution per broker after research, verification, tours, and negotiation costs**. Obia describes automating the research, but the dossier does not establish how much of total transaction labor that removes. Saving hours on screening matters only if it changes the economics of completing a lease. I would pass on a personal check today.

The fee structure makes that distinction consequential. Tenants pay nothing; landlords pay the brokerage fee, whose rate is undisclosed. Faster recommendations therefore do not directly produce revenue. They must increase completed transactions or reduce delivery costs enough to improve contribution margins. The $292.6 billion market estimate includes property management and tells me little about this opportunity. Crexi’s May 2026 AI release establishes that research automation is becoming available to existing brokers; it supplies no evidence that Obia can capture a disproportionate share of the resulting savings. The economic discontinuity remains unmeasured.

The strongest bull case is the company-reported logistics engagement on October 23, 2026. Zoning screening, site-combination analysis, and throughput-per-rent comparisons address an operational decision with potentially substantial consequences. An industrial tenant could value that work much more than a generic property search. Obia also provides representation, with brokers handling tours and negotiations, so it has a route from research to a completed outcome. This fits my interest in AI performing institutional work. But the unnamed engagement establishes some actual use, without establishing repeat demand, payment, or a completed lease. The CoreNet sponsorship provides access to prospective customers; it does not resolve that distinction.

Owning representation is Obia’s strongest architectural signal. Better models could improve its research while the brokerage retains responsibility for advancing the transaction. However, the dossier documents no persistent approval workflow, exclusive property access, or completed-deal feedback system. Accumulated search requirements might become useful proprietary state, but there is no dataset size or evidence that a competitor would find it expensive to reproduce. Tandem already combines AI with office brokerage, and Crexi equips incumbent brokers with overlapping tools. Obia needs an advantage that survives both stronger models and competitors buying the same capabilities.

Samuel Cho’s Amazon and research background supports engineering competence; Jason Henkel’s conversational-AI work is relevant to intake. Neither establishes prior startup learning or costly personal commitment, so I leave that dimension at its missing-evidence default. I also find no basis for an exception: the dossier supplies neither frontier research and scarce model-building talent nor demonstrated reconstruction of service economics. The physical-service red flag is unresolved rather than proven—brokers still perform local work, and their cost has not been measured. **My flip condition would be a reconciled cohort of at least ten completed industrial leases showing twice the net commission contribution per broker-hour of comparable manually researched transactions, including all human verification and local execution time.** That would turn Obia’s promising workflow into evidence that technology changes this market’s economics.
