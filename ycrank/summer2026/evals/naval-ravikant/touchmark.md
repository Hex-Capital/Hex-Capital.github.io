# Touchmark -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have built something adjacent to what they know, but the dossier does not yet show specific knowledge earned inside inference procurement. Ilia Bolgov has mathematics, finance, and Revolut trading-product exposure; Roman Yanushevskyi has Citadel Securities quantitative research, Lovable AI engineering, and an IOI gold medal. These are strong indicators of raw ability. The live GLM 5.2 and MiniMax-M3 order books are evidence of action. But no completed trade, provider relationship, customer outcome, or prior system sold into this exact market establishes that the team understands the operational problem better than capable competitors do.

Touchmark’s interesting idea is not merely cheaper tokens. It is turning future named-model inference into a transferable contract: buyers prepay, receive capacity through an OpenAI-compatible key, and may resell unused blocks. If outsiders actually supply and trade these contracts, fragmented capacity becomes price-discoverable and participants create liquidity for one another. That matches the permissionless-market pattern. Today, however, it is a diagram rather than a loop. There are no disclosed participants, executed volumes, completed resales, bid-ask spreads, or evidence that another user makes the market more useful.

The fatal asymmetry is that Touchmark needs liquidity before liquidity becomes its defense. The website and order-book software can be copied; Ornn already has $33 million for compute spot markets and derivatives, SF Compute has raised $40 million and reportedly manages more than $100 million of hardware, and OpenRouter has approximately $50 million in annualized revenue plus existing inference distribution. If any of them adds forward token reservations, Touchmark presently owns no scarce supply, proprietary pricing data, exclusive model relationship, or accumulated customer network that survives the copied code.

The market is large enough to maneuver in: global inference was estimated at $106.15 billion in 2025 and projected to reach $254.98 billion by 2030. But that top-down market does not prove that forward contracts are a durable wedge. Token prices fell roughly 600-fold from 2020 to 2026, so buyers may rationally avoid locking capacity unless Touchmark solves basis risk, counterparty reliability, and resale liquidity simultaneously. The July 2026 shift from quality-based output pricing to forward-capacity trading may be intelligent iteration, but without customer migration or usage evidence it is not yet persistence validated by reality.

The strongest bull case is that two unusually technical founders have launched a strange financial primitive before the market has learned to ask for it. Listed blocks at $3,800 per billion GLM 5.2 tokens and $870 per billion MiniMax-M3 tokens at least make the product concrete, and a successful exchange could compound proprietary transaction history. The known exceptions for immature monetization or distribution require an already-forming network, unusually revealing founder action, or demonstrated capital efficiency. Touchmark has not shown one of those overrides. Nor is there a disclosed integrity, morale, or artificial-deadline red flag; the pass is structural, not personal.

I would pass now. In 36 months, I expect Touchmark either to be inactive or to have pivoted from a transferable token exchange into conventional inference procurement and routing, because bilateral customer service can generate revenue before a thin order book generates liquidity. One condition flips the decision: verified execution of at least $10 million in annualized token-contract volume across three independent model providers and ten repeat buyers, including completed secondary resales. That would prove both direct value capture and the participant-created market that could survive copied software.
