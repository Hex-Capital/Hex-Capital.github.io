# Markov -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence-of-success case is potentially large but presently unquantified. Reliable computer-use agents could replace expensive human effort across CAD, coding, design, and spreadsheet work, yet Markov gives me neither the labor cost pool nor the improvement its data could produce. A $3.2 billion training-dataset market growing toward $16.3 billion is a vendor TAM, not evidence of a 100x societal consequence. The dossier establishes demand for better trajectories; it does not establish that Markov changes the economics of computer work.

The technical artifact is more credible than the ambition statement. Two founders have assembled 48,478 videos, roughly 12,300 hours of professional-software activity, and an auditable AutoCAD environment retaining screenshots, files, model turns, and input events. Distribution is real: 150,000 cumulative Hugging Face downloads and 17,814 in the preceding month. But downloads measure curiosity, not model improvement. Markov has not shown a 5–10x task-success, sample-efficiency, or cost advantage over Scale AI, Invisible, or synthetic augmentation. Its own 50-task AutoCAD benchmark can pass visually similar but semantically wrong geometry. That is an important scientific admission—and evidence that the evaluator is not yet trustworthy.

The strongest bull case is that this two-person team has created more public infrastructure than many funded data companies. Dev Mandal brings experience from Sarvam AI and GUI-agent projects; Harish Ashok previously built a voice-operated robotics assistant. Their public corpus could be the wedge into proprietary environments, evaluators, and application-specific learning loops. The funded competitors also prove that laboratories spend heavily on data. But Markov has disclosed no customer, renewal, revenue, or buyer-generated workflow showing that free dataset attention becomes a compounding commercial asset rather than lead generation for labor-intensive projects.

Capital should now attack two kill risks: whether licensed human trajectories outperform cheap synthetic alternatives, and whether the underlying recordings have commercial chain of title. The dossier identifies both risks but supplies no decisive experiment, threshold, budget, or stop condition. Publishing 856 GB is an execution milestone; it is not risk removal when research already reports useful augmentation from only 312 human trajectories. My verifiable flip condition is an independent model laboratory demonstrating at least a 5x improvement in held-out task success per training dollar from Markov data versus a synthetic-trajectory baseline across 100 or more workflows.

The founders’ learning rate remains unknown. Prior AI and robotics work supports problem familiarity, but there is no documented belief revision, complementary magnet recruit, or assignment of ownership to provenance, evaluation, and enterprise-data risks. Economics are even less developed: no price, revenue, annotation yield, acquisition cost, or gross-margin model distinguishes scalable environment software from a bespoke data-services business. Pre-seed companies need not have revenue, but a company selling data must know which part of production gets radically cheaper with scale.

I pass today. The exception case would treat the 150,000 downloads and unusually large corpus as sufficient evidence that a tiny team can evolve a free-data wedge into infrastructure. That is plausible, but the override requires either a quantified giant consequence or a proprietary learning loop; Markov has demonstrated neither. The downside is one check, but an undefined upside is not the same as an uncapped one.
