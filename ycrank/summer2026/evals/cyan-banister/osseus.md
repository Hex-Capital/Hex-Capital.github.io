# Osseus -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A robot team changes a motor, and the design system automatically finds the affected board, control code, safety requirement, supplier constraint, and simulation before anyone builds the wrong machine. That future is coherent. Osseus also has a plausible present-day leak from it: a permissioned dependency graph spanning CAD, ECAD, GitHub, Jira, tests, decisions, and component availability. What is missing is the path between those two points. The dossier does not show which dependencies the product already infers reliably, which still require manual mapping, or which successive technical unlocks turn reviewable drafts into trusted engineering actions.

The founders have real ingredients rather than fashionable biographies. Anish Mariathasan worked on embedded medical devices, cancer computer vision, and a prior compliance-AI startup; Ayush Patel built an agentic QA system at Apple and a self-driving Raspberry Pi robot. Those artifacts cross several domains Osseus must connect. But I cannot yet see a long-running obsession with robotics development infrastructure, a recruited team beyond two Imperial classmates, or evidence that either founder has personally lived through a costly cross-domain hardware failure. The dossier also names both simply as founders, leaving the accountable leader and any governance tiebreaker unidentified. I would investigate that before signing, not infer the worst from silence.

The broken system is specific but its economics remain blurry. Mechanical designs, electronics, code, requirements, tests, and supplier feedback do live in separate tools; losing the reason behind a change can create expensive rework. Yet Osseus does not quantify that rework, identify who captures the present rent, or show whose agency materially changes. The marketplace further muddies the wedge: Builder, Team, Enterprise, and transaction commissions describe two businesses before either has demonstrated demand. Component liquidity is not a natural consequence of dependency traceability.

The strongest bull case is that “onboarding R&D teams now” conceals something unusually valuable. If teams are already connecting live CAD, source code, tests, and controlled documents, every approved dependency could compound into an engineering-history asset that Altium, Duro, Trace.Space, and PhysicsX do not possess across domains. Customer-managed keys, permission mirroring, VPC deployment, and default-deny sharing are unusually appropriate foundations for that enterprise data. This could become concealed infrastructure beneath the visible robotics wave.

But the dossier gives me 418 LinkedIn followers, a waitlist, and no customer count, revenue, signed proof of concept, repeat-use cohort, or completed marketplace transaction. Pre-revenue is not the problem; a working artifact that survives contact with industrial users could compensate for it. Here I cannot tell whether an engineer has trusted Osseus with a real revision, accepted one proposed dependency, or rerun one linked test. Meanwhile Altium already owns Valispace, and adjacent vendors have credible routes into the workflow. A graph promised by a website is not yet a controlled bottleneck.

My verifiable flip condition is three named robotics teams using Osseus weekly for 90 days, each documenting at least one downstream engineering failure the dependency graph caught before build or release. Until then, I see an intelligent enabling-layer hypothesis with credible technical founders, but not the strange, costly user behavior that tells me the future has actually leaked into the room. I would stay close rather than write the check today.
