# Viraj Aero -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: a computing company chooses where to build because it can bring reliable power with it. Viraj Aero could help make that possible. The path runs through a condenser that survives continuous operation, a turbine whose added output exceeds the recovery system’s own energy demands, and customers willing to trust that package with expensive equipment. The company’s steam-injection architecture and dated aviation milestones—a 2027 demonstrator and 2029 commercialization target—give me something concrete to work backward from. They establish an engineering path; they leave the continuous-duty power application to prove.

Why should a developer’s construction schedule depend on a grid connection it cannot control? The IEA estimates that grid bottlenecks could delay approximately 20% of planned global data-center capacity through 2030. That is a specific institutional constraint, and on-site generation could return control over timing and location to the developer. But Viraj has not established the price of that independence. Fuel supply, permitting, maintenance, and backup requirements still shape the economics. The dossier identifies the queue more clearly than the financial benefit of escaping it.

The part I find most attractive is the little piece of plumbing beneath the enormous AI story. Viraj reports a patent on its onboard condenser and offers upgrades to existing turbine-genset builders. That fits my recurring preference for concealed physical infrastructure: one component could serve aviation, new generators, and installed turbines. Steam injection already has decades of terrestrial use, however. The valuable asset would be a durable, economical recovery implementation. The unpublished patent scope and unverified operating performance leave that control only partly established.

Risson and Bouilloux have relevant operating tuition. His propulsion work at Ascendance and Beyond Aero complements her experience developing six power projects totaling more than 120 MW. Recruiting a CTO with Price Induction/Wingtra experience and a COO with GE Aerospace France leadership experience strengthens the execution case. This is more substantial than impressive degrees. Still, the biography does not document years of independent experiments before formation. The differing CSO and managing-director descriptions warrant understanding actual responsibilities; they do not establish a leadership shell game or founder deadlock.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that hardware customers begin committing engineering effort long before revenue appears. Viraj reports full-scale aircraft-prototype integration and work with Safran, alongside €13 million in aviation LOIs. My exception for pre-revenue industrial companies absolutely matters here. But the dossier does not establish signed industrial proofs of concept, repeated customer operation, or the scope of Safran’s involvement. Aviation purchase interest cannot establish data-center adoption. I give the team credit for its engineering direction without turning those disclosures into demonstrated user behavior.

The objection that stings is that Viraj may have found an elegant way to move heat around while adding machinery customers cannot afford to maintain. Hot-site condensation, recovery-system power consumption, and continuous-duty reliability could consume the advertised benefit. The unexplained 30% versus 40% additional-power claims make a common baseline especially necessary; they do not establish deception. I see enough physical substance to keep looking, but my own money stays put today. My flip condition is one paid data-center pilot completing 1,000 continuous operating hours at specified hot-site conditions, with independently measured net efficiency and water balance meeting its contracted specifications. That would connect the enabling component I like to behavior a customer has actually paid to sustain.
