# Alkera AI -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Data agents are not merely suggesting SQL; they are changing production pipelines, and the scarce thing is permission to act without quietly poisoning every dashboard downstream. Alkera AI has one small leak from that future: whole-stack column lineage paired with graph planning and approval controls for destructive SQL and shell actions. But the dossier never works backward through the transitions required to make autonomous operation normal. Its “why now” is simply that agentic analytics became a category in 2025–2026. That describes today’s crowded wave, not what the wave enables next.

The technical mechanism is real. Alkera’s 83.28% Pass@1 leads the six displayed DataAgentBench agents, and its persistent knowledge base could make each action more organization-specific. Yet the margin over SCRIBE is only 1.43 percentage points on 54 queries, while PromptQL already has shared context and deterministic permissions. Looker, Tableau, Power BI, Metabase, Sigma, and Hex integrations are still “coming soon.” A benchmark-winning agent is useful; a trusted control plane spanning the actual enterprise data graph would be infrastructure. Alkera has not crossed that line, and incumbents can presently assemble the same ingredients.

The founders are the strongest part of the case. Tony Li built a multi-agent Python framework at ByteDance; Andrew Tran worked on data infrastructure at Hudson River Trading and Ramp; Rick Gao built quantitative models at Verition. They have also worked together for four years across research, projects, and a trading competition. That is credible technical tuition and complementary fluency. What I cannot find is the pre-company wound or first-believer act: no dated sequence of independently built data tools, production incident that became the specification, recruited design partners, or customer institution that trusted them before the category became fashionable. The dossier also names three founders but no CEO or ultimate decision-maker. I would not infer equal control, but I would require the governance answer before signing.

The product is available through IDE, CLI, and browser interfaces, with published prices of $50 and $250 per month. Those facts establish a functioning commercial artifact, not user behavior. Five hundred forty-two LinkedIn followers do not show that anyone repeatedly entrusted Alkera with production data, paid for it, or allowed it to execute a consequential change. The pricing page names revenue possibilities; it supplies no revenue signal. For a trust product, the revealing behavior is not downloading it. It is granting write access and returning after the first frightening moment.

The bull case stings: this is exactly the sort of pre-revenue technical team whose prototype can matter before conventional traction appears. The relevant precedent is CosmicBrain AI, where missing revenue was overridden by a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a founder’s lifelong robotics record. Alkera has the prototype-like benchmark, but not the signed production proofs or the pre-market obsession. Pending compliance certifications and incomplete integrations make that difference material because enterprise trust is the product, not administrative cleanup.

My 36-month base case is that Alkera pivots toward an enterprise lineage-and-approval control layer and reaches $1 million–$3 million ARR by August 2029, rather than becoming the defining autonomous data agent. I pass today. One verifiable condition flips me: five named production customers each permit Alkera to execute write operations weekly for 90 consecutive days without a material data incident. That would turn safety from a benchmark claim into strange, costly trust.
