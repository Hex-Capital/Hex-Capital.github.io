# Insurf -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

$32.17 billion in claims-management spending gives Insurf room to move, but Insurf is a large-market workflow company with a small-network architecture. That pairing is the signature. Insurf can expand from appeals into prior authorization, coding, payment tracking, and payer intelligence, yet each hospital currently receives value independently. A big market creates optionality. It does not create compounding.

Uber is the high rung for pivot-room: black cars became UberX because the underlying market was transportation, not one workflow. Insurf shares the broad substrate—$67.78 billion in projected global claims-management revenue by 2031—and Insurf already touches intake, drafting, submission, follow-up, and posted payments. The divergence is capital efficiency. Uber could redirect software and supply; Insurf must renegotiate services agreements, BAAs, PHI controls, and hospital integrations. The market is wide, but each pivot may require fresh permission.

Perplexity is the low rung on user-added value: one user does not directly improve the next user’s experience, so product quality must substitute for network effects. Insurf has the same weakness today. Insurf calls its coverage-decision graph proprietary, but publishes neither its record count nor evidence that one customer’s resolved claim improves another customer’s appeal. Versioned payer-policy and outcome snapshots could become shared intelligence. Until cross-customer learning is demonstrated, the “graph” is a database with an ambitious noun.

Signed services agreements and one-workflow hospital pilots put Insurf on labor-and-permission leverage, despite a code-native product. Over 18 months, Insurf’s software can make each physician reviewer more productive, but distribution still compounds through procurement, security review, BAAs, integrations, and staff-controlled submission. Production PHI remains disabled until technical gates and founder approval are complete. Code scales after permission arrives; Insurf does not yet scale permissionlessly.

Bryan Chung’s two IEEE publications and Chang Lu’s Brown BS/MD path show intelligence adjacent to the problem, not yet non-trainable knowledge of revenue-cycle operations. Insurf would flip this dimension into the top tier if Chung had shipped payer infrastructure at scale or Lu had personally managed hundreds of denials inside a hospital. Applied mathematics, physics, neuroscience, and zero-shot cancer modeling can build Insurf’s engine. They do not prove the founders know which claims-workflow exceptions will kill it.

Stack Overflow began with developers who had spent years living the exact problem and whose public artifacts exposed intelligence, energy, and judgment before the check. Insurf’s live demo and detailed security methodology show unusual execution for two Brown students, while Insurf’s decision to require physician review and staff-controlled submission shows epistemic restraint rather than reckless automation. Integrity remains unreadable, however, and neither founder has a prior venture, long operating history, or public body of work establishing the 10-year-partner test. Quiet is not guilt. It is simply unpriced information.

Amperos has raised $20.2 million to automate overlapping portal, call, collection, and appeal workflows, so my 36-month forecast is that Insurf reaches $2 million to $5 million ARR by July 2029 as a specialty-practice appeals vendor, while Insurf’s payer-behavior graph remains customer-isolated rather than becoming industry decision infrastructure. The large market can support a useful software business. The absent network makes a category-defining outcome less likely because every new account must still be sold, secured, integrated, and serviced.

CMS prior-authorization requirements beginning January 1, 2026 create the strongest bull case: standardized decision data could turn Insurf’s attested source snapshots into a regulatory data flywheel, much as Anchorage converted permission into a moat. Insurf could then learn across payers while competitors merely automate clicks. The bull case stings because 80.7% of appealed Medicare Advantage denials were overturned, leaving an enormous labeled outcome stream. It loses today because Insurf reports no graph size, production outcomes, customer count, or right to pool data across customers, while PHI production is still disabled. I would flip to Invest if Insurf verifies three named health-system deployments and shows that cross-customer graph learning improves appeal overturn rates by at least ten percentage points. Until then, I watch rather than write the check.
