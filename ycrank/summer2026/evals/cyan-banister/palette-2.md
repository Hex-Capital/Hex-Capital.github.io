# Palette -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the plausible future is clear: a creative team describes a campaign to one persistent production system, and that system remembers the brand, preserves characters, chooses among disposable generation models, clears rights, and moves work through approval. Palette has one small artifact from that world—a public studio and API routing image, video, music, and editing requests across several models. But the dossier does not map the intervening steps from today’s model switchboard to that trusted production system. It gives me Veo, Runway, and Kling release dates—why now—when I need to know what comes next.

The working artifact is Palette’s strongest evidence. The company reports 35% faster generation, 50% lower cost, task-capable routing across its internal benchmark, and more than 130 regression tests. Those are real engineering choices, not a rendering. Yet there is no dated cohort, repeat workflow, paying customer, or strange expenditure of user time. Pricing at $0.01 per credit tells me how Palette hopes to charge; it does not tell me that anyone repeatedly buys the roughly 1,000 credits required for a character sheet, storyboard, and 15 seconds of consistent video.

Josephine Lee and Serena Pei are technically credible. USACO Platinum, MIT computer science, diffusion and multimodal research across CSAIL, the Media Lab, and LIDS, plus production exposure at Scale AI, Datadog, Amazon, and MIT laboratories form a strong skills base. Meeting through an art program is suggestive. What is missing is the first-believer act: a dated pre-company obsession, an independently shipped creative artifact, or evidence that either founder lived inside a production team’s ugly approval and rights workflow. This dossier shows preparation for building AI software, not yet the operating wound that specified Palette.

The bull case is that model proliferation makes neutral orchestration increasingly valuable. Palette could accumulate customer-specific brand constraints and routing-performance data while model vendors fight beneath it. My pre-revenue exception for CosmicBrain AI does not carry over: both had functioning prototypes, but CosmicBrain paired its artifact with a founder’s lifelong build record and signed industrial proofs of concept. Palette has neither documented user commitment nor a controlled technical asset that downstream products must use.

Today the layer is vulnerable from both directions. Runway and Luma already aggregate models while controlling proprietary ones, and Synthesia has passed $100 million ARR with a narrow enterprise use case. Palette depends on models that its own terms say may be substituted or retired. Its routing tests are know-how, but not yet a scarce enabling constraint. The legal posture worries me more: customer media may implicate copyright and likeness rights, responsibility is shifted to customers, and the privacy policy and terms still identify themselves as starter templates. Encryption, tenant isolation, SSO, and audit logging help, but consent, custody, and rights cannot remain paperwork for later when the product’s memory is supposed to become the moat.

I pass with curiosity. Palette has built a competent horizontal tool inside today’s crowded wave, but it has not shown the six-year chain, the broken rule and captured rent, or the weird user behavior that would make this more than model aggregation. One verifiable fact would flip me: twenty enterprise creative teams using persistent Palette workflows every week for three consecutive months, with measured retention of brand or character state across at least three interchangeable model providers.
