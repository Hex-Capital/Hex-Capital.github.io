# Forward -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine a borrower describing a business once and receiving financing options based on what lenders actually fund, rather than whom an advisor happens to know. Forward’s conversion of borrower information into structured deals could be an early piece of that world. But two transitions matter: lenders must expose their changing appetite, and completed transactions must teach the system which matches work. The dossier establishes neither transition. I can imagine the destination; I cannot credit Forward with having demonstrated the path.

Why does the money sit undeployed while analysts shuffle documents? Forward identifies manual outreach, email, and spreadsheets as the obstruction. Removing that work could return time to advisors and broaden borrowers’ access to capital. That is useful. But there is no quantified search cost, identified fee collector, or demonstrated transfer of bargaining power. With pricing undisclosed, I cannot tell whether the company makes capital access more independent or becomes another intermediary charging for introductions.

The founders give me a better reason to pay attention than the AI label. Daniel Yoon worked in private credit at Kain Lawyers; Riley Janjis worked on more than $1 billion in credit diligence at Rixon Capital and Longreach Alternatives. Their previous tutoring business reportedly reached six-figure annual revenue and more than 100 clients. That is evidence of two people building and selling together before this company, while Darryl Luo brings prior CTO experience. It establishes commercial initiative and relevant exposure. It does not yet establish a long-running obsession with this particular problem or a sequence of independently built credit-market artifacts. ([YC founder profiles](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/useforward))

The strongest argument against passing is that these founders may already be discovering the market through transactions. An investor reported $180,000 in annualised revenue within the first two months, with currency unspecified; the founder reports more than 50 onboarded funds and $300 million in processed deals. Those are meaningful early signals. I would happily back a company before substantial revenue when a working artifact and exceptional founder evidence make the future tangible. Here, however, “processed” does not establish funded transactions, and onboarding does not establish repeated lender behavior. The November 6 launch placeholder could coexist with a private operating product; it does not prove the earlier claims false. It does leave the actual customer workflow difficult to inspect.

Forward also resembles a pattern I like: an ordinary business concealing infrastructure underneath. Its announced Fidelity Bank, Hudson Cove, and iPartners relationships could become the beginnings of a useful network. But relationships without demonstrated exclusivity or proprietary execution data do not establish control of a bottleneck. Finitive already addresses private-credit matching, while adjacent document and underwriting vendors could move toward it. The decisive question is whether each completed deal makes Forward harder to replace. Nothing supplied demonstrates that compounding effect. Nor does the dossier establish a severe privacy violation, confused leadership, or dependence on one closed model supplier; those red flags should not be invented to justify a pass.

I pass with my own money today. My exceptions for extraordinary consumer behavior, technical recognition, or a compelling pre-revenue prototype do not bridge the specific gap here: useful origination activity has not yet become evidence of a learning network. **One verifiable flip condition:** a six-month transaction audit showing that repeat lenders fund matches selected using Forward’s accumulated execution data, with materially better close rates than those same advisors’ manual outreach. That would show the hidden infrastructure arriving.
