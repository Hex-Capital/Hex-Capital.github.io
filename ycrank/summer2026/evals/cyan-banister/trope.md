# Trope -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Trope CUA’s 366 commits make Trope a magically weird services business with software hiding inside it, while the absence of a named CEO makes Trope a two-pilot aircraft with no disclosed controls. The highest signal is the unsexy inversion: Trope is implementing Microsoft Business Central in weeks instead of joining DualEntry, Campfire, and Rillet in the fashionable race to replace the ERP. The lowest signal is authority. Matthew Chow and Victor Vannara are both “Co-Founder,” and the dossier supplies no tiebreaker. I like the game Trope may secretly be playing; I do not yet know who gets the final move.

Crusoe is the high rung for magically weird: an ugly operational problem, unfashionable incumbency, and technology disguised as a strange services proposition. Trope shares that mechanism by selling fixed-price ERP migrations and managed agents into a market where traditional partners bill hourly for six-to-eighteen-month projects. Trope diverges where the analogy becomes load-bearing: Crusoe’s founders possessed social license on oilfields and attracted rejection on aesthetic grounds, while Trope has disclosed neither sophisticated investor passes nor an underpriced round. “AI-native” is consensus language in Summer 2026, even if ERP implementation is delightfully unsexy beneath it.

HQ Trivia exposed the low-rung governance failure: tactile product pull temporarily concealed two founders who lacked durable tiebreaker authority. Trope’s dossier names Chow and Vannara as co-founders but names no CEO, majority operator, vesting arrangement, or conflict protocol. Trope cannot claim the HQ Trivia exception because 27 GitHub stars, four forks, and 547 LinkedIn followers are not explosive consumer pull. Multiple founders are a rebuttal when authority is explicit; Trope’s silence earns the absent-evidence default because the dangerous conversation may not have happened.

Matthew Chow built production firmware at Tesla, Zipline, Generac, and Schneider Electric, while Victor Vannara worked on LLM/RAG, MCP, and distributed systems at Amazon. That technical adjacency gives Trope real Tiz, and the prior workflow product’s paid pilots give Trope a little Biz. The hidden pattern fails to compound, though: no founder is publicly shown living through a GP/NAV migration or personally running a Business Central implementation. Over 18 months, unnamed principal consultants with 200-plus implementations could generate the exception data while Chow and Vannara merely encode it; unless the founders themselves absorb the customer scar tissue, the consultants—not Trope’s founders—own the lived relationship to the problem.

The $16.1 billion Microsoft Dynamics services market is unsexy enough to attract me, and Trope’s agents touching financial data, reconciliations, postings, and collections carry more consequence than another meeting-note bot. Trope still sits below the SpaceX-and-Anduril frontier rung because Trope is software-only, runs inside the customer’s Microsoft environment, and routes ambiguous actions to humans. The capital-intensive or regulated score would flip upward if Trope assumed auditable operational responsibility for financial workflows that incumbents could not safely automate, rather than supplying a harness atop permissions and run logs Microsoft increasingly provides itself.

Flock Safety produced an artifact I could encounter before meeting the founder: a camera and even a Wi-Fi name existing in the physical world. Trope has a weaker but observable parallel because a customer can watch a migration reconcile, measure workflow completion becoming two-to-three times faster, and verify the prior product’s reported 90% error reduction. Trope remains far from Flock’s tactile immediacy: a normal human cannot install or photograph Trope within 48 hours, and the cited nonprofit results belong to the prior workflow-guidance product rather than the current ERP implementation service.

Microsoft’s December 31, 2029 Dynamics GP support deadline gives Trope a real migration clock. My 36-month forecast is that Trope pivots decisively toward white-label migration and workflow agents for Microsoft SIs and VARs, reaches $1 million–$3 million in annual recurring revenue, and remains a specialized partner platform rather than becoming an independent ERP. Trope’s weirdest insight is preserving Business Central while funded competitors replace it; Trope’s unresolved founder authority makes a focused channel business more probable than a category-defining platform.

The nonprofit client’s 400-plus staff, paid pilots, 366 repository commits, and reported 90% error reduction form the strongest case that Trope could outrun my caution: Chow and Vannara may be polymaths whose prior product was the lived apprenticeship, making Trope closer to the DeepMind exception than a credentialed AI wrapper. The bear mechanism still stings—Microsoft offers the same agent runtime, an in-product designer, MCP connectivity, and an AI Development Toolkit, while Trope has no disclosed current-product revenue and no named CEO. Trope’s prior shipping record defeats the claim that nothing exists, but it does not defeat platform substitution or founder deadlock. I remain Neutral; one verifiable condition would flip me to Invest: Trope reaching $1 million ARR from at least three partner-referred Business Central deployments by July 2027.
