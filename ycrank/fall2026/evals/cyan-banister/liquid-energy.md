# Liquid Energy -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine a business ordering inference capacity the way it orders equipment: a truck arrives, panels unfold, and computing begins without waiting for a utility connection. Liquid Energy’s advertised eight-week delivery would make that world feel wonderfully ordinary. But getting there requires dependable power through changing sunlight, economical GPU utilization, and repeatable factory deployment. The dossier describes the destination; it does not yet show a working NanoDC leaking out of that future.

The four-year grid queue is the part that grabs me. Why should buying computation require waiting for permission to obtain electricity? JLL’s January 2026 research identifies a concrete institutional bottleneck, and carrying generation could return control over deployment timing to the buyer. That earns meaningful credit. But this is a quantified delay, not yet a demonstrated economic escape: Liquid Energy publishes neither capacity pricing nor storage sizing. A shorter queue can still lead to more expensive tokens.

The strongest case against passing is that this team is attacking the physical constraint beneath the fashionable application layer. Integrating power, cooling, networking, and compute could make deployment itself the product. That fits my unstated preference for concealed infrastructure. Rune’s use of existing solar facilities also makes the distinction tangible: carrying the array could expand where capacity can go. Yet proprietary-module descriptions do not establish control over a scarce enabling asset. The company’s terms explicitly leave capabilities under development, and no measured advantage establishes that integration survives real operating conditions.

The founders supply some first-believer evidence, particularly commercial initiative. Sajan Khosa previously signed three brokerages at AJMER and raised over $100,000 through his charity; those show an ability to get people to act. Sid Ravikumar brings founding-engineer experience, and Arsh Sekhon has worked at Carbonified. What I cannot recover is a dated history of obsession with power electronics, cooling, storage, or factory production. Velocity participation since 2024 establishes continuity, but the older storage-and-waste-heat description leaves the technical journey unresolved. It does not establish a trend-driven pivot, either.

I would happily fund this before revenue if the right evidence existed. **CosmicBrain AI** is a relevant exception because physical infrastructure preceded paying customers there, too; the material difference was a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a founder’s longstanding robotics record. Liquid Energy’s YC and NVIDIA affiliations do not substitute for that evidence. No completed customer deployment or repeated prototype use appears here. Nor does the dossier establish a privacy violation, concealed leadership arrangement, or dependence on one closed model supplier. My hesitation comes from missing operating proof, rather than an affirmative red flag.

I pass with my own money today. Liquid Energy has identified a consequential queue, but the evidence does not yet connect its proposed escape to dependable customer behavior. My single flip condition is an independently verified, paying-customer NanoDC deployment delivered within eight weeks, with a 90-day operating record showing power sources, availability, GPU utilization, and fully loaded cost per delivered token. That would let me inspect the future instead of supplying it myself.
