# Vernius Systems, Inc. -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: jet-powered attack drones have made visual-only interception inadequate, military customers are buying low-cost interceptor platforms, and Vernius becomes large only if its seeker materially lowers the field-proven cost per successful intercept. That is the controlling variable—not stated TAM, detection range, or LOI volume. The 45 units sold to special forces, one frontline test, and eight-week development cycle provide real evidence, but pricing, combat success rates, false alarms, jamming performance, and repeat orders remain undisclosed. I therefore believe the variable is identifiable, not yet validated.

The technology shift is credible. A 24 GHz module that accepts external coordinates, guides beyond visual range, and hands off to terminal vision could replace six-figure seekers in a rapidly scaling class of inexpensive interceptors. Ukraine’s move toward mass-produced interceptors and faster Shaheds supplies the institutional and technical discontinuity. Vernius has also demonstrated unusually rapid hardware iteration. What is missing is the economic bridge: no disclosed unit price, gross margin, integration cost, or cost-per-kill comparison proves that the module actually reconstructs incumbent economics.

Product pull is the strongest evidence. Selling 45 units within eight weeks to special forces is substantially more informative than a generic pilot or waitlist, particularly alongside the reported STING integration and frontline test. I assign little present value to the $500 million of 2027 LOIs; the gap between 45 units and that figure is too large, and neither contracted pricing nor delivery commitments are public. The important next signal is a reorder from an operator after deployment, not another manufacturer’s expression of interest.

The bear case is that Vernius has built a valuable component without securing the value-capture layer. Autonomous operation depends on an external cueing network and terminal vision system, while Cambridge Aerospace and Mara are developing integrated interceptors and sensors. Those companies can internalize seeker development, pressure module margins, or dictate interfaces. Claims about proprietary engagement data do not help until Vernius can quantify the dataset and its reproduction cost. This is also where the physical-defense exception has to be applied carefully: Vernius shares Anduril’s productized response to a strategic opening, but it has not yet shown the product margins or integrated ownership that made that exception compelling.

The team earns measured credit rather than founder-first underwriting. Arthur Nguyen-cao brings six years of RF PCB work and direct Ukraine hardware experience; Joe Gonzalez designed secure Tomahawk-fleet communications; Arthur and Hisham El-Halabi have already co-founded a company together. That is strong domain alignment and some second-time learning, but the dossier does not document a prior exit, costly personal commitment, or the desperation signal required for top-band founder credit.

I would make a small personal investment because Vernius passes both market gates and already has unusually concrete pre-seed demand. The thesis remains narrow: modular seekers must become a repeat-purchased layer across multiple interceptor platforms while preserving attractive hardware margins. I would flip to pass if the special-forces buyer does not place a repeat order after operational deployment; that single event would sharply weaken both the cost-per-intercept thesis and the claimed product pull.
