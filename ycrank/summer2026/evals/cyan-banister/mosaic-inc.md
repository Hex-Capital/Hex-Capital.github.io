# Mosaic -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Engineering organizations may have dozens of agents writing, testing, and operating software while humans enter only for judgment, permissions, and exceptions. In that world, a shared terminal is not the destination; it is the first awkward window into a new coordination problem. Mosaic has one credible leak from that future: its July 2026 demonstration showed multiple people controlling a terminal while Claude Code agents communicated and preserved context. What is missing is the path between that artifact and the future institution—who delegates authority, how decisions become durable organizational memory, and why Mosaic owns that layer rather than merely displaying it.

The founders are more convincing than the current market story. Dorsa Rohani worked on distributed inference at Tenstorrent and conducted ML research at NVIDIA, Vector Institute, and Deep Genomics; her public Machine-Learning repository has roughly 2,500 stars. Shubham Patil has built collaboration interfaces for developer teams. That is real technical adjacency and shipped-artifact evidence, not two people who discovered “multi-agent” after reading a market report. But the dossier does not show a dated pre-company obsession with this exact wound, a long co-founder history, or commercial command sufficient to explain a $200-per-seat product. I see Tizz; Rizz and Biz remain mostly unproven.

The irritating system is legible but not yet decoded. Teams lose decision history across agent sessions and manually reconstruct context during handoffs. Mosaic returns continuity and some control to developers, but it has not named the rent: no measured hours lost, failed deployment cost, permission bottleneck, or institutional gatekeeper benefiting from fragmentation. Without that economic anatomy, “multiplayer AI” risks being a delightful interaction model attached to a problem that Conductor, Superset, Pentagon, or the underlying agent vendors can absorb.

There is a working artifact. The Neuron covered the multiplayer Claude Code demonstration on July 9, and Oflight documented Claude Code, Codex, and OpenCode sessions, worktree isolation, and shared control two days later. That proves Mosaic built something specific enough for outsiders to inspect. It does not prove costly or weird behavior: there are no named paying teams, repeat cohorts, retained sessions, or developers rearranging their workflows around the product. The CosmicBrain exception does not carry Mosaic over that gap: both had functioning prototypes, but CosmicBrain also had signed industrial proofs of concept and a founder’s lifelong record in the problem.

The strongest bull case is that the terminal is only the beachhead. Persistent histories, permissions, and cross-agent state could become the control plane beneath every coding model, and the founders’ distributed-systems background gives them a plausible route there. The bear case stings more today: Mosaic depends on upstream CLIs and authentication policies, publishes no security architecture despite shared-agent credential exposure, controls no scarce asset, and competes against products already moving toward persistent memory and human multiplayer. A severe custody failure would not be an ordinary pre-seed blemish; it would violate the trust required for the entire product.

My forecast is that Mosaic pivots within 36 months from shared terminals toward enterprise agent permissions, audit trails, and session governance, reaching roughly $1–3 million ARR if the founders make that transition before multiplayer becomes a bundled feature. I am neutral today. One verifiable condition flips me to invest: ten named teams paying the listed $200 per seat, with at least six months of retention and weekly multi-human agent handoffs that would break without Mosaic’s preserved state.
