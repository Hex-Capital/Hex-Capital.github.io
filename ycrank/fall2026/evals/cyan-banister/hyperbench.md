# HyperBench -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

I can picture the 2032 workshop: an engineer describes a failure, leaves the bench running overnight, and returns to a repair tested against the actual device. HyperBench has a small artifact from that world. Its September FPGA demonstration connected compiler infrastructure to a physical board over SSH and ran roughly one million model parameters at approximately 10,500 decode tokens per second. That is a narrow internal result, but it crosses the boundary that interests me: software reaching into physical reality and checking what happened. The remaining transitions are substantial—reproducing customer environments, making repairs safely, and repeating the process across different systems.

Why does an engineer still have to carry the system around in their head? HyperBench links repositories, datasheets, layouts, and interfaces because a failure can live between those boundaries. Its custom Rust retrieval pipeline and evidence-linked system graph are a concrete technical asset beneath the agent interface. This fits my pattern of looking for concealed infrastructure under a visible application. But the dossier establishes an implementation, not control of a scarce dependency. Nominal already exposes engineering context through APIs and MCP; HyperBench must demonstrate why its understanding of a system remains valuable when other platforms supply agents with context.

Justin Leong’s work on Anduril’s Roadrunner GNC firmware matters because physical systems punish mistakes that software-only demonstrations can hide. Ivan Ge’s OpenAI infrastructure experience supplies a complementary technical background. Those are relevant working histories, not merely impressive logos. I still cannot establish the pre-company obsession, recruiting ability, or commercial command that would make me believe this particular team will endure years of integration work. Nor does the dossier identify a quantified institutional rent: manual diagnosis consumes engineering time, but there is no demonstrated fee, gatekeeper, or restriction whose removal transfers economic control to the engineer.

The strongest argument for investing now is that customer adoption could lag genuine technical progress. HyperBench reports a debugging comparison of 117.7 versus 237.2 seconds, with both attempts passing. At pre-seed, I do not need revenue to recognize a working experiment. My pre-revenue exception applies when the prototype accompanies unusually strong founder conviction and external commitment. Here, the experiments establish technical activity; private-beta enrollment establishes an invitation. Neither shows an outside engineer repeatedly entrusting a difficult bench to the product. That missing behavior leaves the exception incomplete.

The bear case stings because the very work that makes HyperBench useful could keep it small. Every customer might require a different collection of permissions, instruments, configurations, and recovery procedures. If integrations remain bespoke, the team could spend its time maintaining customer environments while a broader platform owns the relationship. Local execution is a useful architectural choice, but it does not establish data-custody safeguards. Unverified executive titles also leave accountability unresolved; they do not establish a governance violation. There is no documented founder shell game, severe privacy problem, or dependence on one closed model supplier to justify treating those red flags as present.

I am **Neutral** on HyperBench with my own money. The physical execution is interesting enough to pursue, and the enabling layer is more substantial than a narrated agent demo. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently referenceable customer using HyperBench weekly for eight consecutive weeks to complete hardware-verified repairs across two repositories without founder intervention. That would show the system knowledge becoming reusable—and an engineer changing their behavior because it works.
