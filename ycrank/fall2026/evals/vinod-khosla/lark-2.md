# Lark -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Sixty hours saved each month across 60,000 inventory items is useful. The investment question is how much purchasing expertise, working capital, and inventory waste Lark could ultimately eliminate. Interline Hardware supplies an actual customer result, but neither its original labor requirement nor the effect on stockouts and inventory investment is disclosed. I cannot turn hours saved into an orders-of-magnitude consequence without those denominators. The consequence remains unquantified; that earns the missing-evidence default, rather than a penalty for being early.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that the founders have already crossed from demonstration into customer operations. Interline reports first use within a month. Jason’s supply-chain agent work at Traba supplies relevant experience, and his reported 400K ARR achievement at Datost suggests commercial execution—although that revenue belongs to his previous company. Michael’s reported recruitment of more than 10,000 volunteers suggests organizational reach. Neither establishes the particular founder behavior I am underwriting: revising an important belief after evidence and recruiting complementary people to remove a named risk. Those remain unknown.

Lark also fits my less-publicized willingness to back execution-heavy businesses. **Block** began with a modest operational wedge that could expand into financial infrastructure; Lark’s purchasing wedge could similarly accumulate responsibility across distributor operations. The material difference is that this dossier establishes neither the size of the value pool Lark can capture nor a mechanism for escaping customer-specific implementation. That prevents the exception from carrying the decision. There is also no evidence here for the scientist-led, quantified-consequence exceptions that justify investing before commercial proof.

The product deserves limited technical credit for deployed ERP workflows and reported labor savings. The separate claim of 50 hours saved weekly at an unnamed customer strengthens the possibility of substantial automation, but it cannot be combined with Interline’s result or converted into a 5–10x benchmark. Didero already executes overlapping procurement workflows. Purchasing, forecasting, and analytics agents therefore do not by themselves establish a discontinuity. My concern about conventional AI products competing through integration applies, but the dossier does not establish that Lark is merely a wrapper. Equally, customer mappings are not yet evidence of a proprietary learning loop.

The next dollar should test whether reliable automation survives deployment beyond the first customers. Approval limits, logs, and pause controls address operational exposure; they do not establish an error threshold or a capital-efficient sequence of decisive experiments. The dossier provides no such milestone plan. Economics are similarly unresolved: setup generally takes one to two months, while subscription prices, implementation effort, and delivery margins are undisclosed. Missing revenue is ordinary at this stage. Missing evidence about repeatable delivery leaves the cost-parity criterion at its default.

I pass with my own money. Lark has demonstrated useful work, but the evidence does not yet connect that work to extraordinary leverage. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independently audited deployment cohort showing at least 5x more purchasing throughput per total human hour—including implementation, approvals, and exception handling—at lower fully loaded cost and without worse stockouts or excess inventory. That would show whether the founders are removing an expertise bottleneck at scale.
