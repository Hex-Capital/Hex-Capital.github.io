# Assemble -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Enterprise systems no longer have “implementations”; software agents continuously inspect dependencies, propose changes, test them in shadows, and leave reversible trails. Assemble has one artifact leaking from that future: a Salesforce agent that completed 30 Superbadges and 629 badges in 6.4 hours, passing 93% on the first attempt. The causal path is plausible—governed connectors enable access, dependency maps provide context, shadow environments constrain mistakes, and action traces make changes reversible—but the dossier has not shown the final transition from controlled Trailhead exercises to an institution trusting the agent with a live system of record.

The team can build. Aliyan Ishfaq worked at LangChain, contributed to a repository with 10,400 stars, and previously reported 100,000 users and more than $50,000 ARR for ParaAI. Shaurnav Ghosh brings SAIL, AWS, and Apple experience; Shrish Janarthanan adds cybersecurity exposure from KPMG. That is credible first-believer proof in agents, evaluation, and software execution. It is not yet pre-company obsession with the ugly economics of ERP migrations. I see excellent technical biographies, but no dated operating wound inside Salesforce, SAP, Oracle, Workday, or the system-integrator machinery they intend to replace.

Why do these implementations consume months or years? The dossier points to undocumented processes, distributed business logic, internal specialists, and system integrators, but it does not quantify the rent or show who regains control when Assemble removes it. Does a business-systems leader deploy changes without a consultant? Does an administrator complete in one afternoon what previously required a six-week statement of work? The agency transfer is still narrated rather than observed. Two open go-to-market roles before any disclosed customer behavior make me wonder whether the team is staffing around a hypothesized buyer instead of living beside the first desperate user.

The sharpest bull case is that the benchmark is not a toy: API version control, scoped branches, recorded mutations, generated tests, shadow validation, and deterministic rollback attack the exact trust bottleneck that prevents enterprise agents from acting. CosmicBrain AI established that a working prototype can outweigh missing revenue at pre-seed, but its exception also included signed industrial proofs of concept and a founder’s lifelong domain record. Assemble has neither disclosed production use nor equivalent enterprise-operations tuition. Thirty Superbadges prove machine competence; they do not prove that a human will risk a quarter-end Salesforce configuration.

There is also a stack-position tension. Assemble is deeper than a chat wrapper because it controls change traces, tests, and rollback infrastructure. Yet its strongest proof sits on Salesforce, while Salesforce, SAP, Oracle, Workday, Workato, and general agent vendors control the access surfaces and can absorb overlapping functions. Customer-specific dependency maps could become valuable infrastructure, but “could” is doing the work until deployments show that Assemble learns something durable the platform owners cannot readily reproduce. Privileged-system access also creates a custody and surveillance gate: action recording is useful, but the dossier does not specify data retention, permission boundaries, or who can inspect sensitive traces.

My 36-month base case is that Assemble narrows into Salesforce change management and reaches roughly $1–3 million ARR rather than becoming a cross-platform autonomous IT layer. I would not write the check from my own money today. One condition flips me: three named enterprises must each complete at least 100 production configuration changes over 90 days—with disclosed error and rollback rates—and at least one must run on a non-Salesforce system. That would transform the Superbadge artifact into the strange institutional behavior I need to see: companies repeatedly handing consequential work to the machine.
