# Vouch -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Making competent financial advice affordable to Indian middle-class households could matter enormously. Vouch has identified fragmented holdings, investment selection, and tax planning as the work to replace. But the dossier does not quantify what households currently pay, how many are excluded, or how much expert labor disappears if Vouch succeeds. The $2.3 billion wealth-platform estimate includes brokerage and distribution beyond its segment. I cannot turn that number into a demonstrated societal multiplier.

The immediate technical question is whether Vouch can deliver trustworthy advice with radically less expert involvement. Connecting accounts and generating recommendations does not establish that mechanism. Novelty Wealth already offers AI advisory, aggregation, and tax planning; Kuvera supplies portfolio analysis and execution. Vouch provides no benchmark against either. Human experts participating in key decisions could be sensible engineering, but the amount of human work remaining determines whether this becomes broadly accessible expertise or another advisory service. Neither a technical discontinuity nor an off-the-shelf wrapper is established by the evidence.

Arnav Ghatiwala is the strongest reason to challenge my skepticism. Building Robinhood’s Advisor Network and working on trading-engine optimization and operations at Jane Street provide relevant exposure to financial products and their operational constraints. My actual investment pattern includes execution-heavy fintech. **Block** is a relevant exception: a narrow financial-product wedge could expand into much larger infrastructure. The material difference is that Vouch has not yet demonstrated the expansion mechanism or quantified the value pool it could capture. Ghatiwala’s background makes that possibility credible enough to investigate, but it supplies no observed belief revision or difficult complementary recruit—the specific founder behaviors this framework rewards.

Vouch’s reported November 2026 launch depends on licensing. Regulatory difficulty alone does not bother me; I repeatedly accept it when permission enables a consequential capability. Here, however, an intended launch is not an achieved authorization or a decisive experiment. The reported $500,000 YC investment establishes financing, not risk removed. The dossier supplies no costed test, pass/fail threshold, or response to failure for licensing or advice reliability. It also gives no evidence of premature spending on distribution, founder rigidity, or exit optimization. Those red flags are unproven, and I will not manufacture them.

I pass on the available evidence. Pricing, delivery costs, and expert-review requirements are undisclosed, so affordable economics remain untested rather than disproven. Missing revenue at pre-seed is not the objection. The objection is that neither a measurable expertise multiplier nor an exception strong enough to override its absence is documented. One result would reopen my decision: an independently audited pilot on 100 real household portfolios showing advice quality comparable to qualified human advisers, including tax correctness and suitability, at one-tenth their fully loaded delivery cost with all required human review included. That would establish a mechanism worth underwriting.
