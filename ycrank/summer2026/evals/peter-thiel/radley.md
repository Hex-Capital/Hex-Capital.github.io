# Radley -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Radley’s contested truth is that radiology AI creates more value when its builder owns the medical practice than when it sells software to one. What would prove this wrong? If the acquired practice cannot produce materially better throughput, turnaround time, or margins after deployment, then “AI-native” describes an ownership structure, not a secret. The prior software business signed $2.2 million in contracts and went live, but that validates demand for software—not the stronger claim that acquiring providers is the superior business.

The founders are the best evidence for the thesis. Maryam Jahed and Seak Meng Lay worked together while Carbon Health expanded from 31 to more than 100 clinics; she led healthcare products through commercial revenue, an insurer acquisition, and FDA breakthrough designation, while he built clinical infrastructure as a founding engineer and VP of Engineering. This is a long-aligned operating team rebuilding a system it has already encountered, not two people assembled around a fashionable category. Yet the dossier does not establish full-time status, ownership, compensation, or whether clinical AI remains entirely in-house. Strong foundation, incomplete economic alignment.

The weakest point is monopoly. An acquired practice is a beachhead, but a beachhead is not yet a mechanism for owning a market. Radiology Partners already combines practices with proprietary technology across more than 25 million annual examinations; Rad AI reaches more than 40% of U.S. health systems; Sirona has 12 million annual studies under contract. Radley discloses no exclusive data, tenfold productivity result, regulatory asset, network effect, or integration advantage. The future moat—workflow data, tuned agents, credentialing infrastructure, and an acquisition playbook—is inferred. Competition has arrived before defensibility.

The pending acquisition nevertheless gives the economic case substance unusual at pre-seed: it would bring $4.3 million in annualized revenue, while the prior product’s $2.2 million of signed contracts demonstrates that this team can sell into healthcare. The bull case is sharp. Owning revenue, clinical workflow, and software deployment could let Radley improve one practice, acquire others using demonstrated economics, and compound operational knowledge faster than software vendors can obtain it. This fits the unstated pattern of manufacturing a company around experienced operators rather than waiting for a conventional software wedge.

But the acquisition has not closed, and the return engine remains underdetermined. There are no purchase terms, financing costs, gross margins, physician-retention figures, payer concentration, or measured AI productivity gains. Expansion must coordinate practice financing, clinical validation, multistate licensing, facility credentialing, and integration while competing against a national consolidator with vastly greater scale. The $149.54 billion imaging market cannot rescue weak value capture. A large market plus acquired revenue may produce a respectable services company; it does not yet show how one angel check produces a power-law outcome.

The strongest exception would be independent technical or operating proof powerful enough to override the crowded structure. None is present: unlike a verified zero-to-one event, the prior contracts concern the abandoned software model and the practice revenue remains prospective. I pass today. The single flip condition is a closed acquisition followed by twelve months of independently documented results showing at least a 30% improvement in radiologist throughput or contribution margin, with retained facility contracts and no proportional increase in clinician labor. That would convert the secret from assertion to test, and the practice from purchased revenue into proprietary technology expressed through operations.
