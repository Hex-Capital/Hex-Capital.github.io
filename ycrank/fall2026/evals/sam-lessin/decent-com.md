# Decent -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Decent can save employers money and still be the wrong investment for me. Serif Health reports $60,000 in annual savings; Locate reports more than $100,000 against its renewal proposal. Those are useful customer outcomes. They do not tell me what I pay for the equity, what commissions Decent collects, or what scarce capital would prove. Post-money valuation, competing offers, and the current round’s experiment are all undisclosed. I cannot underwrite the deal by substituting employer savings for investor economics.

The strongest reason to keep looking is the team’s operating history. Soman previously operated health plans and a PEO and sold the earlier Decent; Budker led production AI work at Meta. That is substantive experience on both sides of the proposed bridge between insurance operations and automation. Their relationship goes back roughly 16 years to Kindle. This fits my preference for people who can translate between ecosystems. The prior company’s acquisition belongs in founder underwriting, however; its customers and financing do not belong on this new entity’s balance sheet.

There is also more here than an audience waiting for a product. The named testimonials show customers using Decent’s comparison workflow to obtain meaningful savings. That earns partial credit for access producing action. But “67 companies working with us” does not establish 67 commission-producing accounts, and inherited LinkedIn followers establish no acquisition advantage. The potentially valuable asset is a continuing employer relationship with usable claims and renewal history. Its ownership and durability remain unproved: claims review excludes fully insured and PEO arrangements, while Nava already offers overlapping brokerage, audits, and year-round support.

The bull case stings because this could become a real, capital-efficient brokerage quickly. Two experienced founders, existing customer outcomes, and recurring commissions could support a business that finances its own growth. I am comfortable with operational complexity when software improves a neglected workflow. But the reported automation of 89% of brokerage work has no disclosed methodology, and it cannot stand in for contribution profit after human review and servicing. Provider-dependent commissions also create a question about whose economics the recommendations optimize. No disclosed revenue, margin, burn, or runway resolves whether Decent can survive without another round.

I checked the exception case before passing: unusually strong conversion and retention, demonstrated cash economics, or a financing structure that removes repeated fundraising could outweigh missing gate evidence. Decent’s testimonials establish customer value, but not those overrides. Nor does the dossier establish an auction, an incoherent founder committee, or dependence on error-free AI; I will not invent adverse evidence. My decision is **Pass on the documented deal**. One verifiable condition would reopen it: a documented financing proposal below $10 million post-money that funds a dated, 12-month test of reaching cash break-even from retained brokerage accounts without another raise.
