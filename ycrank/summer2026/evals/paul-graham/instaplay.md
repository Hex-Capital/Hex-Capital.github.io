# Instaplay -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most revealing fact is not that Instaplay has 100,000 monthly users. It is that FunBench found games with inverted controls, broken interfaces, incomplete progression, and median engaged play of only 3.0–7.9 seconds. These are excellent obstacles to encounter early. But the dossier does not say what the founders changed after encountering them, how quickly they shipped it, or whether the change improved play. I see the collision but not yet the running back. That leaves the central founder test unanswered.

The technical proof is much stronger. Three people have built a deployed system that turns English descriptions into playable solo and multiplayer browser games, distributes them by link, and records prompts, edits, preferences, and engagement from 20,000 creators. FunBench adds more than a diagram: 3,000 blinded votes and measured generation costs of $0.86–$7.11 per delivered game. The product is flawed, but it exists at meaningful scale. This is the dossier’s clearest evidence of fierce nerd behavior.

There is also real pull, though it is shallower than the headline suggests. Instaplay reports 100,000 monthly active users acquired organically through group chats, Discord, ChatGPT, and shared browser links. That is considerably better than a waitlist. But “people who want AI games” is not yet a narrow well, and seconds of engaged play suggest many users inspect the novelty and leave. I want to know which type of creator returns, what they repeatedly make, and whether their friends come back without being prompted. The network could be starting, but the current figures do not distinguish a social product from a large collection of disposable demos.

The founders have plausible ingredients: Gary Wu previously built an LLM-evaluation startup, Frank Liu worked at Instagram and is a top-1,000 Teamfight Tactics player, and Akhil Aggarwal built credential-threat detection at AWS. What is missing is the causal story. The dossier does not establish that one of them repeatedly needed this product, nor that they have already changed routes after a falsified hypothesis. Personal gaming experience is not the same as discovering a neglected problem. Their cofounder history is also unproven, though absence of public evidence is not evidence that the relationship is weak.

The strongest bull case is that Instaplay already matches an important pattern: technical users are composing new artifacts on a programmable substrate, and usage is generating a proprietary feedback stream before the business model is settled. Twenty thousand creators and 100,000 monthly users at pre-seed are difficult to dismiss. But the bear case still wins today. Competitors report 3.5 million and 20 million users, the product is free, generation can cost $7.11 per game, and no evidence shows that Instaplay’s telemetry improves output quality or retention. Influencer and consumer partnerships are outreach, not distribution. A network whose objects are abandoned after seven seconds does not become defensible merely by recording the abandonment.

I would not write the check yet. The technical artifact clears one half of my gate, but the dossier supplies only default evidence that these founders turn resistance into rapid shipped change. One verifiable fact would flip me: show a dated generation or retention blocker, a founder-built workaround shipped within two weeks, and a subsequent four-week creator cohort whose repeat creation rate at least doubled. That would connect the impressive machine to the kind of founders who can keep steering it.
