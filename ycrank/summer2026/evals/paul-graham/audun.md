# Audun -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The first question is what broke. Audun entered a business full of things that break—licensing, debtor disputes, GDPR, payment-plan negotiation—but the dossier never shows one breaking for these founders and what they shipped in response. Registering Audun Collection AS under Norway’s collection-agency code in June 2026 proves commitment, not resourcefulness. The unverified Finanstilsynet licence is still an obstacle, not a workaround. With no named blocker, dated action, or observable result, the people-first gate remains unmet.

There is more substance in the product than in the evidence around it. Audun says it can coordinate reminders across channels, negotiate payment plans, retain interaction histories, and escalate vulnerable debtors to humans. That is a plausible architecture for the new Debt Collection Act and automated-decision constraints. But the debtor portal is still “coming soon,” and a creditor walkthrough is not a production system. Peter Flo’s Midjourney and data-science experience makes the technical claims more credible; it does not convert them into a measured proof. I give modest credit for a seemingly built creditor-facing workflow, but there is no benchmark, case volume, or recovered payment.

Who wants the crude version now? The dossier names no creditor, debtor cohort, pilot, revenue, repeat usage, or payment outcome. A $5.98 billion market and three funded competitors show that debt collection is a software category. They do not show that Audun has found a narrow well. AgentCollect, Altur, and Domu also automate overlapping calls, messages, negotiations, and plans, so the missing usage evidence matters more here than it would in an empty market. “Book a walkthrough” describes a sales funnel, not founders personally recruiting and learning from the first ten users.

The strongest bull case is that four capable founders have deliberately chosen an ugly regulated market. Two have McKinsey backgrounds, one led special projects at Midjourney, and the product design anticipates disputes, vulnerability, and auditability. Regulation could become the moat after they do the work. But this does not match the unstated pattern behind my strongest recent bets: a concrete proof spike before the round. Nor does a known exception apply; those require a working artifact, unmistakable pull, or independent validation strong enough to replace the missing prior. There is no explicit press-launch, partnership, or premature-sales-hire red flag, but there is also no evidence of founder-led selling or a route changed after rejection.

The origin is equally unproven. None of the founders is documented as having worked in collections or encountered this problem personally. The registered agency and compliance-shaped workflow suggest willingness to approach the schlep, but unresolved licensing and GDPR questions are not accomplishments. Within 36 months, I expect Audun either to pivot from third-party collection into workflow software for creditors’ licensed in-house teams or remain a small implementation business, because that route removes the hardest regulatory liability without requiring proprietary collection performance.

I would pass today. The score is low because the decisive facts are missing, not because pre-seed companies should already be large. One result would flip me: a named creditor processing at least 1,000 real overdue accounts through Audun for eight weeks, with repeat usage, collected payments, and a documented founder-built response to a licensing or debtor-safety blocker. That single trial would test resourcefulness, technical reality, and user pull at once.
