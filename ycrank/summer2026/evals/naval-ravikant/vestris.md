# Vestris -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Specific knowledge is visible before credentials. Aahil Valliani and Joshua Tang processed real closing files at the same title shop, then built software around the work they had personally endured. Vestris is not two students discovering title processing through market research. One founder had already built a software product and held three cited patents; the other had built live telemetry systems. More importantly, they converted that experience into a working pilot rather than merely describing the problem.

The product attacks expensive coordination: emails, county portals, title records, prorations, commitments, and closing disclosures. In one title shop, Vestris reports compressing eight to ten hours of processing into one hour and doubling capacity. That is meaningful pre-seed technical proof. The human-approval gate is also sensible because a seven-hour saving is valuable only if the final title output remains trustworthy.

But execution is not durability. Vestris says its Schedule B workflow was trained on decades of approved and rejected titles, yet the dossier does not establish who owns that data, whether competitors can access it, or whether reviewed outcomes compound into an exclusive model. Copy the browser-agent code and the remaining assets are jurisdiction knowledge and workflow integration—useful, but Qualia, Flueid, and Stavvy already possess industry relationships, integrations, or adjacent automation. The current advantage is faster labor, not yet proprietary leverage.

The $18.5 billion of 2025 title-insurance premiums proves that money moves through the market; it does not prove an equally large software opportunity or a path into adjacent transactions. Coverage is publicly limited to Washington, D.C., and Montgomery County, while each new county introduces different portals and conventions. There is also no disclosed payer, subscription price, per-file fee, or revenue. My 36-month base case is that Vestris reaches $1 million to $3 million in ARR as a useful title-processing vendor but remains a vertical workflow product rather than a compounding network.

The strongest objection is that this underrates a capital-efficient prototype simply because its user loop and business model are immature. The one pilot settles millions of dollars monthly, reported capacity doubled, and the founders have unusually direct domain experience. That partially resembles the exception I made for **Notion**: a demonstrated product can justify patience before distribution becomes observable. The material difference is that Vestris enters a legible incumbent category using increasingly commodity computer-use capabilities; the dossier has not yet shown a new product language or asset that becomes harder to reproduce with every customer.

I see no evidence of the founder-integrity, artificial-deadline, or reassurance-seeking red flags that would veto the option. I also do not yet see the unstated pattern I want: customers are not contributing supply, distribution, or utility for one another, and automating fragmented offline work is not the same as turning that fragmentation into a network. I would not invest today. The flip condition is ten paying agencies across at least five jurisdictions showing that their reviewed closing outcomes produce a measurable accuracy advantage unavailable to incumbents. That would convert capable automation into a proprietary learning system.
