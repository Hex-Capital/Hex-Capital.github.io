# Lyon -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence case is not yet large enough. Lyon points to an $18.9 billion predictive-analytics market growing toward $82.3 billion, but TAM is not consequence. The dossier does not quantify how much fraud, credit loss, underwriting labor, or excluded lending its model eliminates. Four-times precision in identifying premium-card converters is commercially useful; it is not evidence that a societal cost pool changes by 100x. This currently looks like better financial optimization, not rewritten financial infrastructure.

The technical evidence is much better than the ambition statement. A two-person company trained on 28 billion transactions for a fintech serving tens of millions of users and beat its existing rules by 4x precision. That is a real customer constraint, not a laboratory benchmark or an interface wrapped around a standard API. Training inside each customer’s VPC, producing reusable embeddings, and adapting them across tasks could replace months of separate feature engineering. But the public result covers one cross-sell task. Credit is merely entering deployment; fraud, collections, income, churn, and lifetime value remain claims. Four-times on one task does not yet establish a multi-task foundation model.

The strongest case for investing is the known small-team financial-platform exception. Like Block at its beginning, Lyon has a tiny technical team, an enormous existing financial value pool, and a narrow product that could expand across many decisions. The material difference is that Lyon’s current intelligence is retrained separately on customer-owned data. There is no demonstrated shared learning loop, structural data advantage, or repeatable deployment across institutions. Without those, the apparent platform may remain high-end implementation work that Taktile, Feedzai, H2O.ai, or an internal bank team can reproduce.

The next dollars should test that distinction, not fund sales scale. The largest kill risk is whether one representation transfers across institutions and tasks with materially less labeled data, engineering time, and compute than incumbent methods. Lyon has not disclosed a threshold, experiment cost, timing, or stop condition. The credit deployment is directionally relevant, but it is not an explicit risk-removal plan. A decisive test would compare three tasks across three institutions and require at least a 3x reduction in implementation time while preserving a substantial predictive advantage.

Gabriel Noya has credible model-systems ability: Stanford EE/CS, NVIDIA experience, and an edge-AI optimization win. Those facts help explain how the prototype was built, but they do not demonstrate fast belief revision, financial-domain judgment, or complementary recruiting. The dossier does not even clarify the second founder’s identity and history. SOC 2 Type 1, 35 monitored controls, and an insurer engagement show unusually mature procurement work for pre-seed, yet they do not answer whether the team can recruit owners for regulated-model validation, enterprise deployment, and financial risk.

The economic gate is also unproven. Customer-specific training inside every VPC may create trust, but it may also produce consulting-like deployment cost and weak software margins. No contract value, training cost, deployment time, inference cost, or revenue is disclosed. My verifiable flip condition is three paying financial institutions running at least three production tasks each, with deployment completed in under 30 days and documented total cost at least 5x below separate feature-engineered models. Until then, I would pass. One check buys a technically interesting experiment, but the dossier has not shown an uncapped consequence or a repeatable economic machine.
