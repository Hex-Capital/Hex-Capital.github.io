# Pennant -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Institutional voting policies could operate like executable code: every proposal arrives already mapped to precedent, conflicts go to humans, and the rationale survives audits and personnel changes. Pennant has pieces of that world—policy-grounded recommendations, approvals, overrides, and source-linked records—but the dossier does not map the intervening transitions or show why this becomes a “world model” rather than excellent governance software. The December 2025 executive order explains urgency today, not what becomes newly possible six years out. That leaves my primary future test at its missing-evidence prior.

The present system is more legible. ISS and Glass Lewis reportedly control 97% of proxy advice while institutions still push thousands of filing pages through PDFs, spreadsheets, and notes. Pennant gives an asset owner the ability to apply its own policy instead of inheriting standardized advice. That returns genuine decision agency, but the dossier never quantifies the rent: no advisory fees displaced, analyst hours eliminated, voting delays shortened, or switching economics exposed. I can see the gatekeeper and beneficiary; I cannot yet see the full economic wound.

The founders are the strongest evidence. Ryan Stewart spent more than ten years inside the exact machinery—State Street proxy voting, BlackRock Aladdin, and PJT board advice. Tomas Taylor brings the complementary institutional-product and agent experience, including Mindware and AutoGPT contributions. They met fifteen years ago and worked on the same Aladdin desk, while the CEO and CTO responsibilities are explicit. This is not a fashionable AI category discovered last winter. It is accumulated operating tuition, although the dossier lacks a dated sequence of pre-company prototypes or recruiting feats that would make the obsession fully undeniable.

The artifact is also real. A large U.S. public pension plan, a Top AmLaw 100 firm, and governance advisors are paying; a global asset manager is a design partner; S&P 500 companies are engaged. Enterprise buyers do not casually introduce an unknown system into voting and board-advisory work. That is costly behavior and a serious pre-seed signal. But Pennant provides no contract values, renewals, completed proxy seasons, recommendation volumes, or examples of customers repeatedly overriding the system. I know institutions bought it; I do not yet know whether the product has entered their muscle memory.

The painful counterargument is that these customers, combined with unusually exact founder-market fit, may be the first leak from a much larger governance operating system. Yet the proposed moat remains inferred. SEC filings are public, general-purpose models are available to competitors, JPMorgan is building internally, and Tumelo, Minerva, Proxymity, ISS, and Glass Lewis already own adjacent workflows or relationships. Pennant depends on sources such as Bloomberg, Broadridge, FactSet, and Diligent rather than controlling a scarce lower-stack asset. Its policy mappings and override history could become infrastructure, but today they are potential residue from using the application—not demonstrated control of an enabling layer.

My 36-month expectation is a credible specialist business at roughly $3 million to $8 million in ARR, concentrated among governance advisors and asset owners, rather than the world model its title promises. I am neutral and would not write the personal check yet. The single flip condition is three institutional customers renewing after a complete proxy season while collectively processing at least 10,000 proposals through Pennant, with documented analyst-time savings and retained policy precedents driving the renewals. That would turn workflow adoption into the mundane artifact showing the larger future has actually begun.
