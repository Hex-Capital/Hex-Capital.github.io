# Async -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Async’s contested truth is that small professional firms do not want tools for constructing AI agents; they will pay an outside operator to own specific outcomes, and those embedded workflows will eventually compound into software rather than consulting. The falsifier is equally clear: deployments remain bespoke projects that require fresh auditing, integration, and maintenance without customer expansion or improving delivery economics. Four named law-firm deployments and “thousands of hours” automated constitute a shipped experiment, but Async names neither the prevailing belief it disproves nor independent retention, cost, or accuracy results. There may be a secret here, but the dossier has not separated it from the fashionable claim that every small business needs an AI workforce.

The narrow market is hiding inside the company’s broad positioning. Async advertises law, healthcare, real estate, and childcare, yet every published customer story is a law firm: Martinez Legal, Clemente Mueller, Joshua Lowenthal, and Clifton Law. Small law firms could be a coherent beachhead, especially because intake, conflict checks, engagement letters, and task creation form a connected operational system. But the company has not committed to dominating that market. It instead describes four verticals while competing with well-financed agent platforms and legal specialists. Customer-specific memory, integrations, tenant isolation, and SOC 2 certification can create switching friction; they are not yet proprietary technology, network effects, or scale economics. This is customization, not monopoly.

The founders are the strongest part of the case. Ben Smith and Connor Park worked together before Async, built Skope, and carried the product from outcome-based billing infrastructure into legal agents. Connor owns the technical function, while Ben’s prior selling experience complements it. That history is materially better than two strangers assembled for YC. The repeated repositioning can also be read as persistence rather than drift because the current product has live deployments. Still, the dossier does not establish founder ownership, compensation, full-time status, or whether all essential implementation work remains internal. Foundation risk is reduced, not eliminated.

Distribution exists, but its economics remain obscure. Async obtains introductions, audits a firm, scopes one or two workflows, deploys them, and seeks expansion under a new agreement. The four legal customers show that this route can close real accounts; this is more informative than an untested “enterprise sales” label. Yet no public price, sales cycle, conversion rate, renewal rate, or implementation cost shows that outcome pricing covers forward-deployed labor. A two-person company can make custom deployments look efficient because the founders absorb the work. The power-law question begins when Async can add the twentieth firm without operating like twenty small consultancies.

The strongest opposing case is that the service burden is the mechanism rather than the defect. By entering customer systems manually, Async could learn repeatable legal workflows that self-service competitors never observe; outcome pricing could drive expansion, and accumulated memory could make removal progressively painful. The named firms and thousands of automated hours give this theory real substance. But no known exception rescues the current evidence: there is no independently verified zero-to-one technical result, dominant user behavior, or proprietary asset strong enough to override crowded agent competition. The dossier documents useful work, not yet an unrepeatable company.

My 36-month base case is that Async abandons its four-vertical framing, becomes a legal-operations automation vendor at roughly $2 million to $6 million ARR, and retains a service-heavy delivery model rather than becoming the dominant SMB agent platform. I would not write the personal check today. I would flip to Invest on one verifiable condition: a cohort of at least 20 law firms achieves more than 130% net revenue retention after twelve months without services headcount growing as fast as revenue. That would convert the current ambiguity into evidence of compounding distribution, durable workflow ownership, and software economics.
