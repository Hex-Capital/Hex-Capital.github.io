# Cosmic Robotics -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Tens of thousands of panel installations make Cosmic Robotics a frontier-hardware company whose machine I can watch work, but whose founder compact I cannot yet inspect. **SpaceX** is the high rung here: capital-intensive hardware became the moat because flight cycles accumulated knowledge that software tourists could not buy. Cosmic Robotics shares that mechanism through millimeter-level placement, tens of thousands of field cycles, and a NASA lunar-construction contract. Cosmic Robotics diverges because solar robotics already has Charge Robotics, Terabase, and Gritt in the field; the hardware is real, but the category is no longer lonely. This is a 14/15 frontier swing, not a rocket-sized act of disbelief.

**HQ Trivia** is the low governance rung because an intoxicating product temporarily concealed unresolved authority between its founders. Cosmic Robotics names James Emerick CEO and Lewis Jones CTO, which is better than shared titles, but Cosmic Robotics discloses neither their prior relationship nor a tiebreaker, equity structure, vesting agreement, or conflict protocol. Eight people and two technically formidable founders can move beautifully until the first argument over solar deployments versus lunar construction. “CEO” on LinkedIn is a suggestion; authority under pressure is the test. I apply nearly the absent-evidence default.

James Emerick deployed autonomous earthmoving equipment at Built Robotics before Cosmic Robotics, while Lewis Jones built propulsion and space systems at Relativity Space, SpaceX, NASA JPL, and NASA JSC. That is lived technical adjacency, not two tourists discovering construction labor in a market memo. Over the next 18 months, Cosmic Robotics compounds this advantage whenever a dusty jobsite failure becomes a mechanical redesign, a perception edge case, and another contractor relationship. The missing piece is autobiographical ownership: Cosmic Robotics has excellent résumés, but the dossier does not show the physical moment when either founder personally felt the panel-installation problem. Biz and Tiz are visible; Rizz outside the pitch is not.

Gritt has raised $32 million and contracted 2.8 GW, while Terabase has raised over $200 million and installed 40 MW. Cosmic Robotics therefore sits closer to an emerging robotics race than to **Crusoe** burning stranded gas before anyone understood the game. Cosmic Robotics would flip into my highest “magically weird” tier if named EPCs were refusing the machine for some apparently absurd reason while repeatedly calling it back after crews doubled productivity. The NASA contract and lunar-construction ambition are weird in the good way, but a $4 million round led by Giant Ventures with HCVC and MaC Ventures is already credible institutional validation, not evidence that the room is laughing.

One panel every 30–40 seconds gives Cosmic Robotics the **Niantic** quality I care about: I do not need a spreadsheet to understand movement in the physical world. Cosmic Robotics can be photographed lifting a 90-pound panel, observed working beside a crew, and judged against the claimed doubling of labor productivity. The deduction is access rather than tangibility: unlike downloading Ingress, a normal person cannot deploy Cosmic Robotics within 48 hours without reaching a remote utility-scale project. Still, the artifact exists outside founder testimony, and that matters enormously to me.

A NASA lunar-construction contract plus demonstrated solar deployment leads to a falsifiable 36-month prediction: by July 2029, Cosmic Robotics will remain independent and generate $12–25 million in annualized deployment or leasing revenue, with solar-panel placement still contributing more revenue than data-center piping or lunar work. Cosmic Robotics gets there because repeated physical cycles improve reliability and contractor trust together; the machine becomes less fragile while the deployment dataset becomes harder to reproduce. The moon is the wrapper. The terrestrial construction platform is the game Cosmic Robotics is really playing.

Terabase’s 40 MW installed and Gritt’s 2.8 GW pipeline make the bear case sting: Cosmic Robotics may be a well-funded feature robot caught between competitors with factory-level throughput and larger contracted fleets, while workers still supervise placement and fasten every panel. Cosmic Robotics could burn hardware capital pursuing solar, cooling pipes, data centers, and lunar construction before owning one workflow, and an undisclosed founder disagreement could turn that strategic sprawl into the **HQ Trivia** failure mechanism. The evidence that wins today is tens of thousands of completed installations with more than doubled labor productivity—not a laboratory prototype or an LOI. I invest, but one verifiable condition flips me: if customer-paid deployments fail to exceed $5 million annualized revenue by July 2028 despite those installation cycles, the field data are activity without commercial pull.
