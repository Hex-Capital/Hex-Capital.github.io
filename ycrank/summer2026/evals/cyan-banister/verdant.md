# Verdant -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A property owner submits plans once; local rules are machine-readable; obvious conflicts disappear before filing; planners spend their time on judgment rather than document ping-pong. Verdant could participate in that world, but the dossier does not map the transitions required to reach it. San Jose’s pilot and more than $3 million in SwiftGov contracts show that governments are buying AI-assisted permitting now. They are evidence of a current market, not yet a leak proving Verdant owns what comes next.

The broken system is more legible. Interviews with over 200 local-government employees found incomplete applications bouncing repeatedly between applicants and understaffed departments before manual review even begins. Verdant attacks that exact loop with jurisdiction-specific intake, document checks, staff review, and drafted reports while preserving government approval authority. I can see whose time is returned. I cannot yet see the magnitude: there is no quantified delay, resubmission rate, staffing cost, or completed permit cycle showing that the software removes the rent rather than adding another review surface.

Aidan Ng and Jason Yi have credible proximity to the institution. Ng worked across DOJ, USDA, Capitol Hill, Deloitte’s government practice, and commercial-real-estate underwriting; Yi built government HR and legislative-tracking software and researched public-benefits automation and privacy. The 200 interviews show disciplined curiosity. But this is still biography plus discovery, not pre-company obsession proved through dated artifacts, independently built permitting tools, or a customer who followed them before YC. The dossier tells me they understand government. It does not yet show the first-believer act that makes me believe they must build this company.

The sharpest bull case is that tedious jurisdiction configuration becomes the asset. Every ordinance, GIS source, form, hearing process, and exception Verdant encodes could make later deployments faster and turn an irritating service burden into infrastructure. The trouble is that this asset remains hypothetical while GovWell already offers completeness and code checks, Clariti owns CivCheck, and PermitFlow claims 12 million permitting datapoints. Verdant currently appears above the bottleneck, assembling workflows around third-party cloud, document, and model providers, rather than controlling the scarce layer beneath the wave.

There is also a custody question I cannot wave away. Permit files can contain identities, addresses, zoning records, and detailed building plans, and Verdant routes data through AWS, GCP, Supabase, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Reducto. Encryption, MFA, role-based access, testing, and human approval are sensible controls, but they do not yet establish explicit retention boundaries, model-training restrictions, or jurisdiction-controlled custody. The CEO is clearly identified, and there is no disclosed governance shell game; the unresolved red flag is the surveillance architecture, not founder accountability.

I pass today. There is a real institutional wound and a plausible team, but no disclosed working deployment, revenue, signed jurisdiction, repeated user behavior, or controlled technical asset. None of the exception logic for backing a pre-revenue company applies yet because the dossier supplies neither a functioning prototype used in the field nor signed proofs of concept. One result would flip me: three named jurisdictions completing live permit cycles with Verdant, each documenting materially fewer resubmissions or shorter review times while retaining reusable jurisdiction configurations and explicit data-custody controls.
