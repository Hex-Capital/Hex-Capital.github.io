# SubVysion -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Maxwell Stone’s on-device inference SDKs reached more than 10,000 pharmacies, while SubVysion has no mechanism by which its next contractor automatically benefits its previous contractor. SubVysion is a specific-knowledge-heavy, network-light field-robotics business. The founders know how to make machines perceive the physical world, but SubVysion currently sells that knowledge one construction site at a time. Intelligence scales. Site work does not.

Replit is the high rung for specific knowledge: Amjad Masad spent years building code environments before the market supplied a narrative. SubVysion shares the artifact-first mechanism—Stone worked on railyard mapping and Roboflow inference, while Wenson Tang built a $500 LiDAR search-and-rescue drone. SubVysion diverges because neither founder has documented immersion in GPR interpretation, underground-utility operations, or excavation liability. Adjacent technical knowledge is real, but specific knowledge includes the ugly edge cases: wet soil, nonconductive pipes, bad elevation models, and the consequences of a missed gas line.

Perplexity is the low rung on user-added network value: one user’s query does not directly improve the product for the previous user, so the network criterion had to be waived through permissionless distribution, exceptional ML knowledge, and pre-consensus AI timing. SubVysion has the same missing network mechanism without those three overrides. SubVysion acquires contractors through cold calls, deploys its own rover, and appears to contract by project or site; one customer’s excavation does not attract another customer or create new supply. A hypothetical sensor-signature library is not yet a network. Data becomes leverage only when accumulation is automatic and improvement is demonstrated.

Founder-led cold calling produced SubVysion’s first customer and investor, but the next 18 months compound labor and capital more readily than code. Every SubVysion deployment appears to require transporting hardware, collecting site data, validating GPR output, and accepting workflow liability before the mobile AR layer delivers value. The processing software may scale, but code inside a service is not code as distribution. Permissionless leverage means the product replicates while the founders sleep; SubVysion’s rover still has to arrive at the job site.

The $1.45 billion underground-utility-mapping market, forecast to reach $2.29 billion by 2030, is large enough for a useful business but narrow for angel-power-law geometry. SubVysion would move up a tier if underground mapping were merely the entry point into contractor-operated infrastructure capture, utility digital twins, insurance underwriting, or a shared subsurface-data layer. SubVysion currently shows no adjacent customer motion, no proprietary dataset scale, and no structural barrier against Exodigo, which already reports 86.1 million square feet mapped. The first idea usually fails. A market must leave room for the second and third ideas.

Twitter had Jack Dorsey shipping and Ev Williams carrying a completed founder history; the intelligence, energy, and long-term-partner evidence could be read from prior behavior. SubVysion shows intelligence through Stone’s production ML work and energy through nationwide job-site operations only months after Stone and Tang joined forces in January 2026. SubVysion’s first customer investing is a meaningful trust signal, but the co-founder relationship is seven months old and the dossier offers no long-duration evidence about integrity, conflict handling, or treating prior collaborators well. Integrity is difficult to signal in a dossier, so absence is neutral. It is not proof.

Contracts on U.S. job sites make my 36-month forecast falsifiable: by July 2029, SubVysion will have pivoted from operating autonomous rover deployments to selling GPR-processing and AR-mapping software to utility-locating contractors, reaching $1 million–$3 million in ARR. SubVysion’s strong computer-vision knowledge makes the software pivot plausible; its absent network effects and site-by-site economics make the present architecture difficult to compound. Capable founders often survive a weak architecture by changing the architecture.

Exodigo’s $200 million of funding and 100-plus customers form the strongest bull case: the category is validated, SubVysion already has live contracts, and a first customer investing suggests unusually strong early trust. Anchorage Digital supplies the known-exception precedent—permission-heavy infrastructure can become attractive when regulation, validation, and accumulated data turn permission into a moat. The case fails today because SubVysion discloses no accuracy-validation standard, proprietary-data scale, insurance structure, or evidence that additional scans reduce miss rates; “100% visibility” instead collides with published GPR limitations. I pass with my own money. I would flip if SubVysion demonstrated at least 10 contractor-operated crews uploading scans into a shared model that measurably lowers miss rates while sustaining greater than 70% gross margins.
