# Torus -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Marcus Lima has led more than $100M of capital projects, while Rahul Thayil previously built an AI threat-intelligence startup that raised $3.5M. That makes Torus a schlep-rich engineering company with an oddly thin public hacker trail. Torus is strongest where physical documents, liability, security, and procurement make the work unpleasant, and weakest where I would normally expect to find code, demos, or open-source artifacts. The signature is promising but incomplete: experienced founders walking into a real mess, without yet showing the animal artifact that makes the bet easy.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness because compliance was not adjacent to the product; compliance became the product’s advantage. Torus shares that mechanism. Torus must reconcile drawings, specifications, datasheets, RFIs, and procurement approvals while supporting isolated managed tenants or customer-controlled networks. Engineering liability and unfinished SOC 2 certification will repel founders who merely want to wrap a model in a chat interface. Torus diverges from Coinbase because more than 50 consistency-check categories are still a product claim, not a publicly benchmarked system whose painful accuracy work has already compounded into trust. I put Torus below Coinbase but closer to it than to a pure-software workflow tool: 13/15.

**Loopt** (2005) exposed what happens when force of personality outruns technical timing and visible hacker evidence. Torus has a related weakness. Rahul’s prior AI company and autonomous-systems work suggest technical competence, but Torus shows no public repositories, competitive-programming history, recognized hacker-community presence, or named technical artifact built before fundraising. Marcus’s Heimdal history and $100M of capital-project delivery do not substitute for code. Torus may contain an animal programmer, but hackers usually leave tracks, and Torus has not shown them publicly. That puts Torus only slightly above the absent-evidence default: 7/15.

Multiple Fortune 500 companies and major EPCs are reportedly deploying Torus, which is evidence of resourcefulness even without names or contract values. Over the next 18 months, founder-led ingestion of five or six customer documents can compound into something important: every ugly pilot teaches Torus another document relationship, approval convention, and failure mode. But Torus has not disclosed an Airbnb-style stunt, a Dropbox-style demo, or even revenue improvised through manual work. The **Airbnb** exception therefore does not yet apply; there is no cereal-box moment strong enough to override every other uncertainty. Repeat founders getting infrastructure buyers into deployments is meaningful, but it is not yet animal proof: 23/35.

Marcus Lima’s capital-project history makes the Torus idea plausibly organic, but the dossier never says Marcus personally encountered one of the illustrated $500K rework orders or built Torus to repair his own workflow. The score would flip upward if Torus showed that Marcus first assembled the cross-document graph for a project he was personally delivering, before customers or investors asked for it. The score would flip downward if “Legora for physical engineering firms” came from market comparison rather than lived frustration. Torus currently sits between those stories: substantial founder-problem proximity, no explicit lost-USB-stick origin. That is 18/25.

**Stripe** (2010) is the useful co-founder parallel because two repeat builders brought complementary hacking and commercial experience to an organic problem. Torus also has two repeat founders: Marcus brings physical-project delivery, while Rahul brings AI systems. But the Collison brothers had a relationship stronger than any contract, whereas Torus discloses nothing about how long Marcus and Rahul have known or built together. A five-person Torus staff does not answer whether the two founders operate as a durable pair. I will credit the complementary configuration without inventing chemistry: 6/10.

Customer deployments already underway lead me to predict that by July 2029 Torus will reach $6M–$12M in ARR from EPC and project-owner consistency workflows, with much of the product still delivered through unusually hands-on implementations. Torus will get there because security reviews, document mapping, and engineering-package customization are precisely the schleps better-funded horizontal AI vendors avoid. Torus will not reach it merely by having a better model; Torus will reach it by accumulating hundreds of firm-specific rules that began as manual founder work.

**Trunk Tools**, with $70M raised and a Cortex product already spanning drawings, RFIs, revisions, bids, and submittals, is the strongest bear case. Torus could be an enterprise AI wrapper whose five-or-six-document demos convert into expensive pilots but not repeatable usage; unfinished SOC 2 work, no published accuracy benchmark, no revenue disclosure, no GitHub trail, and no documented founder history make that mechanism sting. Torus still earns an Invest because Marcus has actually delivered capital projects, Rahul has already founded an AI startup, and Torus reports deployments inside Fortune 500 companies and major EPCs rather than merely a waitlist. I would flip to Pass if Torus cannot produce one named reference customer with repeated project usage by July 2027.
