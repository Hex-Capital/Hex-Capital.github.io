# Frontier Computing -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Michael Domarkas has acquired specific knowledge through action unusually early. Four years in neuromorphic computing, two years culturing neuronal tissue, an Emergent Ventures grant at 17, and a live substrate that learned Frogger are stronger evidence than his Cambridge affiliation or the prestige of a $10 million round. The 25-game demonstration is small, but it is an artifact produced inside the exact biological, electrical, and machine-learning problem Frontier Computing proposes to solve. That earns founder credit; it does not yet prove the system generalizes.

The durable asset is biological process knowledge, not software. Copying Frontier’s interface would not reproduce its tissue-culture method, and reaching 100 million or 500 million viable neurons could create compounding expertise in media chemistry, electrode control, and biological training. But the company itself says reproducibility, survival at scale, structured-circuit generalization, and credit assignment remain unresolved. The current proof is a 92% peak Frogger crossing rate after one hour—not a repeatable training substrate. A proprietary process that cannot yet be reproduced internally is a hypothesis about a moat, not the moat itself.

The strongest bull case is that these unresolved questions are precisely why the upside is nonlinear. Improved iPSC differentiation, longer-lived microfluidic cultures, and better multi-electrode arrays may have opened a new computing substrate, while conventional GPUs remain constrained by memory movement. If Frontier’s large cultures work, the company could sell infrastructure into both biological research and machine-learning compute. The absence of a published TAM matters less than the breadth of those adjacent markets; what matters more is whether the same substrate can graduate from games to commercially useful learning.

The bear case stings harder today. Cortical Labs already sells $35,000 systems and cloud access, while another competitor has reported a paying biological-compute customer. Frontier has no published price, revenue, customer workload, or independent replication. Its $10 million financing and press coverage can become fashionable validation substituting for adoption—the exact error to avoid in an unproven new category. A one-person company simultaneously attempting cell-culture scaling, electrophysiology, learning algorithms, cloud infrastructure, and ethics governance also contains several coordination and technical failure modes before it reaches a customer.

Frontier does not match my permissionless-system pattern yet. Customers would consume centrally grown biological capacity; they do not improve the substrate, distribution, or utility for one another. There is also no evidence that usage generates exclusive data or a proprietary model. A strange product can deserve an exception from ordinary revenue expectations, but that exception requires either exceptional capital-efficient learning or behavior that compounds. Raising $10 million to build the next two systems is not evidence of either.

My 36-month base case is that by September 2029 Frontier has become a hosted organoid-research infrastructure company generating $1 million–$5 million in ARR, rather than a competitive substrate for training general machine-learning models. I would not invest yet. One verifiable condition flips the decision: three unaffiliated customers renewing paid workloads on a reproducible 100-million-neuron system after independently measured learning gains. That would convert scientific possibility into durable process knowledge and direct value capture.
