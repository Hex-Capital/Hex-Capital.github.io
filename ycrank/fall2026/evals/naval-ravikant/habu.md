# HABU -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

HABU’s strongest asset is Bryan Tharalson’s experience doing the work it wants to automate. Managing development projects teaches things a software team cannot learn from spreadsheets alone. His reported $80 million-plus of project experience matters; the separate $310 million-plus figure needs reconciliation, not an accusation. Neither account identifies completed projects or attributable outcomes. I give the founders credit for relevant operating knowledge, while stopping short of treating their biographies as demonstrated exceptional execution.

The decisive question is what HABU retains after another developer copies its operating system. Performing development creates an opportunity to accumulate contractor relationships, execution knowledge, and underwriting outcomes. It does not automatically create an exclusive asset. The dossier establishes no proprietary project dataset or contractual control over supply. TestFit already sells overlapping feasibility capabilities, and Mosaic combines software with construction operations. HABU’s physical responsibilities make this more substantial than an interface over an API, but responsibility itself is not defensibility.

The strongest case for investing is that the team understands both sides of the transformation. Tharalson combines development experience with being Mutiny’s first sales hire; Junhao Chen brings AI operations and Navy logistics experience. Selling completed development services could capture savings that software vendors surrender to their customers. That fits my pattern of moving fragmented offline activity into more efficient systems. But HABU has not yet shown the next step: participants making the system more valuable for other participants. A second investor does not demonstrably improve the first investor’s project. Permits, builders, and architects still require coordination, and the claimed fivefold increase in projects per employee has no published project-level validation.

There is substantial economic activity around the wedge, but capital deployed is not revenue available to HABU. The cited $160 billion of multifamily investment in 2025 establishes transaction volume, not addressable development fees. Phoenix investors writing $5 million–$20 million equity checks are a specific initial customer; expansion into other markets remains unproven. Fee development identifies a payer and a direct way to collect value, earning modest credit despite undisclosed pricing and revenue. The important uncertainty is whether fees compensate for the labor and risk assumed. Phoenix’s 10.7% vacancy and declining rents make that distinction consequential: processing an underwriting model faster cannot improve the underlying rent.

I pass at this stage. My exceptions for an unusually capable founder or a capital-efficient prototype deserve consideration here, but the dossier supplies neither exceptional founder artifacts nor measured operating efficiency sufficient to override the structural concern. There is no documented integrity or financing-pressure red flag; unsupported automation claims remain unverified rather than disproven. My verifiable condition for reconsideration is a repeat investor signing a paid multi-project mandate after completed-project accounts demonstrate rising gross profit per employee without increased guarantees or construction exposure. That would show HABU turning specific knowledge into scalable economics.
