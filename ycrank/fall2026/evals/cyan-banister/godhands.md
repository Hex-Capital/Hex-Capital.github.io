# GodHands -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine a new support employee handling an unfamiliar escalation without hunting down the person who remembers why an exception exists. GodHands wants to make that possible by watching work and turning it into sourced playbooks. But there are two bridges here: capturing what someone did, then understanding when someone else should do it. The dossier describes the first and advertises the second. It does not establish the intervening steps from recorded activity to dependable judgment. That future is my extrapolation; I cannot give the company credit for having demonstrated it.

The present-day wound is more convincing. The founders describe an employee departure that disrupted their previous employer for months ([YC launch](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/Vn7-godhands-agents-that-learn-by-watching-your-best-people)). Why should an ordinary departure scramble a company? Because the institution stores its operating knowledge inside people, then asks them to reconstruct it during a handover. GodHands could return time to departing employees, newcomers, and the expert everyone interrupts. This fits my recurring attraction to mundane operating pain. Still, the dossier identifies a coordination failure, with no quantified extraction by a gatekeeper, and it leaves open whether workers gain control or management simply gains visibility.

Sai Vamshi Batchu and Aditya Bommakanti have credible technical preparation: both worked at Graviton, and Aditya has low-latency systems experience, NeurIPS research, and a linked implementation repository ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/godhands); [Fugal](https://github.com/idea-iitd/Fugal)). Those are artifacts beyond a polished biography. They do not yet show years spent pursuing this particular problem, exceptional recruiting, or command of the buying process. The August engineering-memory positioning and October operations positioning could reflect useful discovery; the dossier does not establish the reason for that change. I would neither reward it as insight nor punish it as opportunism.

The strongest argument against passing is the support leader’s claim that GodHands documented previously undocumented escalations within two weeks ([GodHands, indexed excerpt](https://godhands.ai/)). Exceptions are exactly where valuable institutional knowledge hides. If that result is repeatable, these founders may have found something considerably better than screenshot documentation. I can back a company before revenue when a working artifact and unusually deep founder conviction carry the bet. Here, the anonymous testimonial establishes limited product evidence, without showing repeated employee participation or successful use by a second person. Meanwhile, Scribe already markets workflow context and agents and reports $100 million ARR; Mimica also trains agents on observed workflows. Their success validates spending in the category, while making the claim to a scarce enabling layer harder to establish. GodHands has not demonstrated control of that bottleneck.

Employee trust could decide whether the whole mechanism works. If people conceal the difficult cases because observation feels like replacement training, GodHands captures the tidy procedure and misses the judgment it needs. Advertised device-side redaction, application controls, and configurable retention are encouraging architectural choices; they do not establish informed employee consent or actual custody practices. I see an unresolved privacy gate, rather than evidence of a severe violation. CEO and CTO roles are identified, and nothing establishes equal-control deadlock or dependence on one closed model supplier. My pass rests on the thin future bridge and limited behavioral proof. **One result would reopen it:** a referenceable, paid 90-day support deployment in which consenting employees repeatedly contribute exceptions and new hires successfully resolve those cases using the resulting playbooks. That would show the knowledge actually changing hands.
