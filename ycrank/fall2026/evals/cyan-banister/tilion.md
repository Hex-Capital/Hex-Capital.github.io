# Tilion -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: my agent renews subscriptions, reconciles invoices, and collects information across websites while I do something else. Tilion could supply the browser underneath those errands. But getting there requires reliable execution, delegated credentials, and websites accepting the agent’s authority. Fortress addresses the browser-detection obstacle. The dossier does not establish how that capability carries Tilion through the remaining transitions. I can imagine the future; I cannot give the company credit for steps I supplied myself.

The engine work is what catches my attention. Fortress patches Chromium’s C++, generates coherent device identities, and accepts existing Playwright and Puppeteer code through CDP. That is a concrete enabling asset beneath several possible applications, fitting my pattern of looking underneath the visible product wave. Yet Tilion controls only part of the obstruction: customers still need suitable network egress, competing browsers also patch their engines, and earlier BSD releases remain available to fork. The scarce asset would have to become the team’s continuing ability to keep access working. No proprietary detection-feedback advantage is established today.

Why does the browser need a disguise? Cloudflare and website operators can withhold automated access; developers absorb the interruptions and maintenance. Tilion gives those developers some operational control back. But the dossier identifies no quantified toll or artificial restriction whose removal clearly transfers economic agency to an individual. Some blocking protects another person’s data or choices. Generated identities therefore raise my consent question: whose authority does the agent carry? This dossier establishes neither a severe privacy violation nor an explicit architecture limiting misuse. I would leave that gate unresolved rather than declare either guilt or safety.

There is a working object here, and that matters. BrowserArena recorded 156 ms median lifecycle latency and 99 successful runs out of 100 on October 22. Engineers have reportedly installed Fortress more than 100,000 times across 170-plus companies. Those are reasons to take the product seriously, although downloads and company counts do not establish repeated, costly dependence. The $2,000 monthly organization subscription is an asking price, not disclosed revenue. Tilion’s own broader test achieved 86.4% successful loads across 92 protected sites; the different test scopes make that a useful boundary on “unblockable,” not evidence of fabricated results.

The founders have also done more than collect credentials. Arham maintains Fortress releases, Luthra previously shipped Scribia to a reported 5,000 installs in two weeks, and members of the team jointly authored AI research. Those artifacts earn real founder credit. They do not yet reveal the dated, pre-company obsession or recruitment of first believers that makes me comfortable financing a difficult journey. Luthra is identified as CEO; four college friends are not evidence of equal-control deadlock or a leadership shell game.

The strongest argument against my pass is uncomfortable: developer installation can be an expensive act of belief, and a team maintaining native browser patches may learn faster than better-funded competitors. My exceptions allow missing revenue when technical proof and founder depth are compelling; Tilion’s implementation makes that argument credible. Still, the combination here is stronger on enabling technology than on the distinctive future or founder history I would underwrite with my own money. I pass today. One verifiable flip condition would be an independently documented customer running a recurring, permissioned workflow through Fortress for 90 days, showing both paid renewal and why competing infrastructure failed. That would turn browser evasion into evidence of durable agency.
