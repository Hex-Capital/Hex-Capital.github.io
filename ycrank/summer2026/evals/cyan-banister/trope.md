# Trope -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. ERP implementation should no longer be a six-to-eighteen-month ceremony: agents continuously translate operating decisions into configuration, reconcile migrations, test release changes, and escalate exceptions to a human principal. Trope has pieces of that world—an agent harness, field-level Business Central APIs, native side panels, and release-wave testing—but the dossier does not map the intervening transitions or show that its current ERP product is already changing customer behavior. The clearest leak comes from the predecessor workflow product: paid pilots, a contract covering more than 400 staff, two-to-three-times faster completion, and 90% fewer errors. That artifact is encouraging but cannot fully validate the July 2026 product transition.

The system economics are sharper. Hourly consultants benefit when implementations stretch, while customers absorb missed requirements, configuration drift, and late data failures. Trope reverses that incentive with fixed-fee delivery targeted at weeks rather than months, returning control over schedule and budget to the buyer. I like this mechanism because automation becomes margin, not merely a feature. But no named regulation or structural gate keeps incumbents from adopting the same contract, and the dossier supplies neither pricing nor implementation outcomes showing that Trope can bear fixed-price risk profitably.

Matthew Chow and Victor Vannara have real technical range: production work spanning Tesla firmware, Zipline drones, Amazon AI agents, MCP tooling, and distributed systems. More importantly, they shipped the earlier workflow system far enough to win paid pilots. That is first-believer behavior, not résumé theater. What I cannot yet see is a dated pre-company obsession with ERP itself, or who among the unnamed principal consultants credited with 200-plus implementations actually supplies the commercial judgment when a migration becomes ugly. The two founders’ ultimate decision authority and any contractual tiebreaker are also undisclosed; that is a diligence question, not evidence of a governance failure.

The strongest bull case is that services are camouflage for infrastructure. Fixed-price projects could finance reusable migration mappings, exception histories, tests, and managed agents until Trope owns the evaluation layer for ERP automation. The prior contract and measured error reduction make that more credible than a generic AI consultancy. But today this remains an inferred corpus, not a controlled asset. Trope depends on Microsoft’s APIs, infrastructure, commercial policy, and system of record, while Microsoft and established Dynamics partners already possess customer distribution. Campfire, Rillet, and DualEntry are attacking the same budget with more than $100 million raised apiece. A company sitting beneath the wave should gain leverage as the wave grows; Trope currently rents its foundation from one closed platform.

The working-artifact evidence therefore lands in an awkward place. Someone paid for the earlier product and entrusted it with workflows across a 400-plus-person organization, which is costly behavior. No current customer is publicly shown trusting Trope with a Business Central migration, reconciliation, posting workflow, or managed agent. The pre-revenue exception I sometimes make requires a functioning current prototype, institutional commitments, and founder obsession that predates the category. Trope has adjacent proof, but not that full combination.

I am neutral today. My 36-month base case is a capable Microsoft Dynamics implementation boutique generating roughly $2 million to $5 million in annual revenue, with software improving delivery margins but not yet creating an independent platform. One condition flips me to invest: three named customers must complete fixed-fee implementations, at least half must adopt recurring managed agents, and the same harness must be operating against a second ERP system. That would prove the services wedge is accumulating portable control rather than decorating Microsoft’s stack.
