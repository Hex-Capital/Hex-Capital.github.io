# Tilo -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Tilo’s interesting bet is that agents should operate on application state directly. Screenshot-and-click automation makes a machine reconstruct intent from pixels, then manipulate an interface designed for humans. Typed actions could eliminate much of that work. But a claimed “up to 10,000×” reduction in computer-use cost does not establish an equivalent reduction in the cost of completing useful work. The dossier supplies neither a measured spending baseline nor a quantified consequence for the people ultimately using these agents. The $16.7 billion AI operating-system category estimate does not fill that gap.

There is actual engineering here. Shared state, permissions, history, and undo give the action framework more substance than an interface around a model API. That matters against my red flag for undifferentiated AI products. Yet Tilo has no reproduced comparison against Simular or Browser Use on equivalent tasks. Its architecture also changes the comparison: native applications must describe their resources and actions, while competitors work with existing software. Eliminating interaction cost by requiring application integration may move the expense into implementation and maintenance. I give the mechanism some credit; I do not underwrite the advertised multiplier.

The strongest bull case is that this integration becomes the platform. An application developer exposes actions once, and humans and agents can collaborate through the same permissioned, reversible state. Tilo’s public SDK and MIT-licensed framework create a plausible route to that outcome. This matches my unstated preference for a narrow technical workflow that can become reusable infrastructure. But the ecosystem is still a hypothesis. Three stars and zero forks on the GitHub mirror establish neither a committed technical community nor its absence elsewhere. My exceptions for an extraordinary instigator addressing a giant existing value pool, or visible exponential progress with enormous consequences, are not established by the present evidence.

The founders have relevant building experience: Quinten Wolff developed patent-workflow automation at BDPE during 2025–2026, and both founders entered TUM through its early-admission program. That supports problem familiarity. It does not document fast belief revision or recruiting complementary people to own specific risks. Tilo’s response to the October 21 catalogue authorization expiry—renewal procedures, monitoring, and clearer messages—is useful operational evidence, but it does not establish a program for retiring the largest uncertainty. Application adoption, offline revocation, and unimplemented hosted-operator isolation remain unresolved. There is no disclosed decisive experiment, budget, threshold, or stop condition. I also see no evidence that the team is spending aggressively on distribution before resolving those risks.

I pass with my own money today. Missing revenue and a two-person team are ordinary pre-seed conditions; the missing evidence concerns the central investment mechanism. Tilo has not shown that native integration produces a durable, substantial reduction in delivered workflow cost, and there is no operating-cost model covering hosting, integration, and support. My 36-month forecast is that the commercial product narrows into an agent-action SDK for existing application environments rather than achieving broad adoption as a replacement desktop. I would flip to Invest on one independently audited paid deployment showing at least 10× lower fully loaded cost per successfully completed task against a named existing automation alternative, including application integration, maintenance, and equivalent permission enforcement. That would turn an architectural possibility into evidence of an economic discontinuity.
