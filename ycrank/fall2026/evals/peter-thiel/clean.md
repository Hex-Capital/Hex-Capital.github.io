# Clean -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Clean’s possible secret is that dated operational records reveal a company’s buying window before conventional prospecting tools do. That is a testable proposition: matched prospects selected through shipments, permits, loans, and contracts should produce more purchases than prospects selected through ordinary enrichment. But the dossier names no peer rejecting that proposition and supplies no comparative test isolating it. Vivameda’s improvement from approximately 4% to 19% replies is encouraging; it cannot distinguish better timing from fresher contacts or better outreach. I leave the secret at its absent-evidence default.

The decisive question is who keeps the value if this works. Clean identifies a useful workflow, but no bounded customer segment it can dominate. Its claimed datasets and record provenance do not establish exclusive access, proprietary predictive performance, or network effects. Meanwhile, Clay’s September 2026 announcement already combines company context, external signals, and research agents; Apollo also combines research and signal-based outreach. This is affirmative competitive overlap. Clean could make sellers more productive while the incumbents capture most of the economic benefit. That is the failed primary gate.

The strongest objection to passing is substantial: the CEO reports **$240K ARR before YC**, and Vivameda reports **$60K in contracts within two weeks**, separately from another $120K of pipeline. These are unusually concrete early signals. Clean also fits my less-public interest in information systems that make previously obscured activity legible. The known-exception tests therefore matter: demonstrated customer behavior or an independently established technical breakthrough could outweigh an initially uncertain monopoly. Here, the customer evidence establishes usefulness, while neither the testimonial nor the founder’s revenue claim establishes a mechanism competitors cannot reproduce. The exception does not remove the gate’s risk.

Pavan’s Cisco enterprise-sales experience has a practical expression: handwritten letters, personalized outbound, founder events, and a demo-led buying process. There is an identifiable sales owner doing identifiable work. That earns distribution more than its default, although no channel-level conversion, sales cycle, or contract value establishes repeatability. The revenue claim also gives the subscription engine some substance beyond a business-model hypothesis. Public pricing, customer counts, margins, and expansion economics remain unavailable, so the arithmetic supporting a power-law outcome is still thin. The broader $4.75 billion intent-data market does not supply that arithmetic.

The founding structure remains unresolved rather than adverse. Pratham and Tejas met at HackHarvard and reconnected at NexHacks; that does not establish convenience matching specifically to form a startup. Clarissa’s student description and Pratham’s separate product do not establish part-time commitment either. Conversely, the dossier does not document all four founders’ full-time status, ownership, or long shared operating history. I use the stated default rather than manufacture either alignment or a red flag.

I pass on Clean today. The team has evidence that it can sell a useful product; the unresolved investment question is whether it can own the resulting market. My single verifiable flip condition is a customer-audited longitudinal study in one defined vertical showing that Clean’s proprietary purchase-outcome data produces a persistent predictive advantage over Clay and Apollo that those competitors cannot reproduce from the same available records. That would connect the proposed secret to an actual monopoly mechanism.
