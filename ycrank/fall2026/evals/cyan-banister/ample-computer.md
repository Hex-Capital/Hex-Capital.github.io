# Ample Computer -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

The interesting object here is an application that exists before its owner has claimed it. Move the clock to 2032: someone asks an agent for a useful tool, receives a working service, and assumes responsibility only when it becomes worth keeping. Ample Computer’s agent-created, human-claimed deployment workflow points toward that world. Its reported October 17, 2026 production implementation of the Personal Agent Protocol is a small present-day artifact. But the remaining transitions matter: agents must manage ongoing operating costs, and ownership must transfer without losing credentials, data, or accountability. The dossier establishes the beginning of that path, with little evidence about traversing it.

Why does shipping still require someone to assemble compute, databases, networking, storage, and release tooling? Ample attacks a real coordination burden. Removing it could give people without cloud expertise more control over what they can build. Yet the dossier identifies neither a quantified delay nor an artificial restriction whose removal changes who gets to participate. Per-second billing with a one-minute minimum describes how Ample charges; it does not establish savings for users. Railway and Render already expose deployment operations to agents. My concern is that the proposed liberation becomes a convenient feature available from every host.

The strongest argument against passing is that infrastructure can look ordinary while quietly becoming indispensable. Ample runs code in isolated microVMs and provides managed infrastructure rather than merely generating deployment instructions. An Anthropic-verified connector added in October 2026 and a Hermes plugin put it within reach of different agent ecosystems. That fits my pattern of backing concealed infrastructure beneath a visible application wave. If human claiming becomes the natural ownership model for agent-created software, Ample could occupy an unusually valuable junction. But the dossier shows no scarce technical asset or demonstrated dependency that lets the company hold that junction. Being underneath an application is a useful starting position; ownership of the bottleneck still needs proof.

Ravi Mishra brings more than a freshly acquired interest in AI: he previously founded Athleague and Awaken, and an Awaken release reports an earlier startup acquisition in 2012. Ishan’s published integration work adds concrete evidence of building. Those facts earn founder credit, although neither establishes a longstanding obsession with deployment infrastructure or the ability to recruit around it. The six showcased applications, including Yard Bros and Saltless Surf, provide limited evidence of completed use; ownership, payment, and repeat behavior remain undisclosed. A prototype can justify an exception to missing revenue when independent founder depth and committed customer demand carry the bet. Here, those supporting facts are still thin. The missing formal title for Ishan also does not establish a leadership shell game, and the dossier supplies no evidence of severe privacy abuse or dependence on one closed model provider.

I would pass at this point. Ample Computer fits the unglamorous workflow-repair side of my investing, but its strongest possibility—software appearing before a human becomes its operator—has not yet produced distinctive, costly user behavior. I would reverse that decision on one verifiable result: a cohort of ten independent customers repeatedly paying to run agent-created, subsequently claimed applications for three consecutive months, with deployment records and interviews showing that this ownership handoff determined their choice over Railway or Render. That would connect the unusual workflow to an actual reason this company should exist.
