# Hubble -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Hubble’s possible secret is that the patient’s legal right of access—not another national exchange—is the most reliable universal interface to medical records. The falsifier is straightforward: if browser and voice agents cannot deliver materially more complete records, at lower cost and latency, than API-based competitors once request volume scales, the secret collapses into an expensive integration service. Hubble has advanced beyond a slogan: 40+ production tools cover Epic, Cerner, athenahealth, UnitedHealthcare, and Aetna, and a legal customer reportedly received complete records within one week. But the dossier never shows that competitors reject this proposition, nor does it disclose retrieval completion, accuracy, or volume. This is a plausible secret with experiments behind it, not yet a demonstrated zero-to-one result.

The customer outcomes are unusually concrete for pre-seed. Hubble attributes $340,000 in denied-claim recoveries during 90 days to one dental deployment, an improvement in patient cost-estimate accuracy from 50% to 94% to another, and a reduction of paralegal work from hours to minutes. These results demonstrate that fragmented records create real economic waste. They do not yet prove a scalable product: every customer is anonymous, pricing is undisclosed, and the one-week legal turnaround may conceal manual exception handling. Software that automates bureaucracy can look excellent at low volume while quietly becoming bureaucracy at high volume.

The foundation is much stronger than the market position. Prabha Dublish and Aaron Leon have known each other for more than a decade and previously built Womentum together. Dublish was Grow Therapy’s first product hire; Leon built data infrastructure at Amazon Care and One Medical serving more than one million patients. Their histories cover both the institutional workflow and the technical substrate, and there is no sign of a part-time founder, convenience-matched team, or outsourced core engineering. Ownership, salary, and secondary liquidity remain undisclosed, but those are missing facts rather than adverse ones.

What small market does Hubble dominate? The dossier offers legal technology, dental revenue recovery, cost estimation, digital health, life sciences, and care delivery. That is evidence of versatility but not a beachhead. Particle Health, 1upHealth, Health Gorilla, and PicnicHealth have each raised at least $49 million, while browser automation and voice agents can become features inside their existing networks. Hubble resembles the information systems I favor because deterministic identifiers, source traceability, and audit logs make contested records legible. Yet resemblance is not a monopoly mechanism. Without exclusive retrieval data or measured workflow compounding, its 40 connectors are an integration inventory that competitors can reproduce.

The coordination risk is also real but not fatal. Hubble depends on APIs, brittle portals, phone systems, provider release queues, identity verification, and record-specific consent rules it does not control. Its patient service is free, while B2B price, gross margin, sales cycle, and implementation burden are unknown. Direct demos have evidently produced deployments, so distribution is more than a label; it is not yet repeatable economics. An $8.57 billion projected interoperability market can support a large company, but no arithmetic shows that one Hubble revenue engine can capture enough of it to create a power-law outcome.

The strongest objection to my caution is the customer behavior itself: three distinct workflows produced measurable economic improvements, and the founders have already built across systems that better-funded companies have struggled to unify. Persistent demand or an independently verified technical breakthrough can override an initially weak monopoly. Hubble has indications of both, but not enough evidence of either: no paid-customer count, retention, retrieval success rate, or proprietary data advantage. I would flip to Invest on one verifiable result—at least $1 million ARR from a single vertical with gross margin above 80% and net revenue retention above 120% after twelve months. Until then, this is an excellent founding team building a useful integration company, not yet a last mover.
