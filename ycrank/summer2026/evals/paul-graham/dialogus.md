# Dialogus -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important fact about Dialogus is missing. I can see what the founders built, but not what broke, what they did next, or how quickly the result changed. Thousands of production calls are evidence of execution, not necessarily resourcefulness. The dossier never names a rejected enterprise integration, failed deployment, or operational incident followed by a founder-built workaround. I cannot substitute Google and Meta internships for that evidence.

The product itself is much stronger. This is not merely a voice demo. Dialogus recognizes callers, reads live systems, performs policy-limited actions, transfers exceptions with context, and records auditable tool calls. SOC 2 compliance and replayable regression scenarios imply that the team has already confronted production complexity. Serving thousands of calls for organizations displayed as Papa Johns, KFC, and Totalplay is a functioning technical proof before a pre-seed company could reasonably expect institutional permission. But the proof remains partly opaque: there is no resolution rate, error rate, latency benchmark, or independently measured threshold.

There is also a real narrow well. Enterprise contact centers will tolerate a crude product if it removes enough labor while preserving safe escalation, and these are named organizations rather than anonymous waitlist entries. Thousands of calls are much better evidence than eight YC launch votes. Yet Dialogus has not disclosed whether any customer pays, renews, expands call volume, or continues using it after an initial pilot. The product has entered production, but the users have not yet visibly pulled it back hard enough to distinguish adoption from a carefully managed deployment.

The schlep is substantial. Telephony, CRM integrations, bilingual Mexican Spanish and English, audit trails, compliance, and action errors are exactly the ugly parts that a thin model wrapper would rather ignore. The weakness is origin. Nothing shows that Ibarra, Rodriguez, or Terán Hernández encountered this problem through prior contact-center work or built Dialogus to solve a need they personally had. Applied-AI experience helps them implement the system, but it does not explain why they noticed the right problem. Nor is there evidence yet that they changed routes after customers falsified an earlier plan.

The strongest case for investing is that Dialogus matches one of my less explicit patterns: a small technical team assembled a difficult substrate before fundraising and obtained concrete enterprise usage. The usual red flags are mostly absent. Distribution is direct rather than dependent on one promised partner; there is no press-centered launch plan, premature sales hiring, or evidence that the founders have delegated product details. Even the young-enterprise-founder concern is weakened by actual Fortune 500 production. But no known exception cures the operating gate. A working product can override several uncertainties, but here it does not yet replace the missing evidence of rapid correction or unmistakable customer pull.

I would not write the check today. My base case is that Dialogus becomes a capable bilingual implementation vendor with roughly $5–12 million ARR within 36 months, but struggles to become the defining platform as OpenAI, Salesforce, Sierra, and better-funded voice competitors commoditize the components around it. One fact would flip me: a dated production failure followed within weeks by a founder-shipped change that measurably improves resolution or error rate and causes a named customer to renew or expand. That would simultaneously reveal the running back and the narrow well.
