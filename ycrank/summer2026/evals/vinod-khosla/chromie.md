# Chromie -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence case is too small and too vague. Government contractors waste expensive hours searching portals, qualifying opportunities, and coordinating bids, but Chromie quantifies neither that labor pool nor a mechanism for changing government purchasing by an order of magnitude. An $8.1 billion software TAM growing 9.2% annually describes a market, not a societal consequence. This is potentially useful workflow compression, not yet an option on a radically different cost curve.

The product does not clear the technical gate. A reusable Company Brain, source-traced requirements, recompete prediction, and audit logs may improve usability, but the dossier supplies no accuracy, time-saved, win-rate, or cost benchmark. GovDash already serves nearly 200 customers, Procurement Sciences more than 300 organizations, and at least four funded competitors cover overlapping discovery and capture workflows. Without a measured discontinuity or proprietary learning loop, Chromie is competing through implementation and interface choices around broadly available models.

The strongest bull case is the founders. Ananth Sankaralingam previously built an acquired product with 40,000-plus users; both founders have credible AI and engineering backgrounds; and their one-way trip to Washington, D.C., with visits to 50 GovCon offices demonstrates doer behavior. Three named customer logos and Snovation’s report that Chromie found an RFI missed by its existing pipeline provide more signal than ordinary pre-seed website claims. The repeated pivots—from browser extensions to browser-agent infrastructure, insurance, and now government contracting—could represent unusually fast belief revision.

But flexibility without accumulating advantage is wandering. The dossier does not show what evidence triggered each pivot, which belief changed, or which complementary recruit now owns procurement, security, or federal compliance risk. Chromie ingests sensitive past-performance documents while disclosing no security certification, whereas established competitors advertise FedRAMP High, SOC 2 Type II, or other federal controls. Nor is there a milestone plan specifying whether matching accuracy, portal coverage, security approval, or measurable bid improvement is the first kill risk. Capital currently appears directed toward finding a market rather than buying decisive technical evidence.

My 36-month base case is that Chromie pivots again into a narrower regulated-industry workflow or services-assisted automation product and remains below $1 million ARR. The painful counterargument is that the founders’ prior product velocity, direct fieldwork, and three early customers could let them discover a platform-shaped wedge before larger competitors respond. I would reverse my decision on one verifiable condition: a cohort of at least 20 contractors must demonstrate a independently auditable 5x improvement in qualified opportunities per employee, with the advantage attributable to proprietary customer-outcome data rather than generic model access.

I pass. There is limited check downside, but the upside presently looks like another GovCon software vendor in an already financed field—not a low-probability mechanism capable of producing a 100x consequence. No known-exception override applies: the founders are adaptable, but the dossier shows neither a giant infrastructure-scale value pool nor a technical trajectory whose consequence makes missing economics acceptable.
