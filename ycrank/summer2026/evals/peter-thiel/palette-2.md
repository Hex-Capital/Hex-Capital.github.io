# Palette -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Palette’s proposed secret is that an independent routing layer can make fragmented generative-media models cheaper, faster, and more coherent than either using one model or working inside a model owner’s studio. The falsification test is straightforward: if Runway, Luma, or another upstream provider can match Palette’s routing and persistent creative context without sacrificing economics, the company is an interface, not a monopoly. The dossier already supplies uncomfortable evidence: Runway and Luma aggregate models inside unified workspaces, while Palette has not named a belief those peers reject. This is closer to a measurable product hypothesis than a secret.

The measurements are nevertheless real enough to investigate. Palette reports 35% faster generation, 50% lower cost, complete task-capable routing on its internal benchmark, and more than 130 regression tests. That demonstrates engineering substance, but not proprietary technology: the benchmarks are self-reported, the models come from ByteDance, Google, Kling, MiniMax, and LTX, and Palette’s own terms contemplate substituting or retiring them. Customer-specific brand constraints and routing histories could eventually create switching costs, but future data is not present data. Today the company competes through better orchestration in a market where orchestration is already becoming standard.

The strongest evidence is the founding team. Josephine Lee and Serena Pei met through an art program before studying computer science at MIT, rather than meeting merely to manufacture a YC application. Pei’s diffusion and multimodal research across CSAIL, the Media Lab, and LIDS directly supports the in-house routing work; Lee adds competitive-programming and AI-music experience. Their CEO and CTO roles indicate commitment, but the dossier does not establish full-time status, ownership, salaries, or whether they previously shipped a substantial product together. There is no affirmative founding red flag, yet economic exposure remains unproved.

Distribution is indefinite where the technology is definite. A public studio, API, and $0.01-per-credit price are concrete, but “creators, marketing teams, operations leaders, and learning-and-development leaders” describes four markets rather than one beachhead. Enterprise custom quotes provide no contract value, sales cycle, implementation cost, customer count, retention, or revenue. The roadmap status of SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001—and legal documents still identifying themselves as starter templates—makes the enterprise route slower precisely when self-service economics remain unknown. A forecasted $3.44 billion AI-video market cannot establish that one revenue engine will produce a power-law outcome.

The bull case is that two technically credible founders have shipped a functioning studio and API just as rapid model proliferation makes neutral routing valuable. If their workflow context compounds into proprietary performance data, Palette could become the control plane rather than another generation surface. The known exception for an uncertain monopoly, however, requires a verified zero-to-one technical event. Internal speed and cost comparisons do not clear that threshold, and neither YC nor OpenAI backing can substitute for customer behavior. The portfolio pattern toward the complete AI production stack also cuts against Palette: it controls neither models nor compute and presently captures only the aggregation layer.

I pass. My 36-month base case is that Palette either pivots toward services-heavy enterprise creative workflows or remains below $2 million ARR because model vendors absorb its visible advantages. One condition would flip the decision: at least $1 million ARR concentrated in one named customer segment, with six-month retention demonstrating that proprietary workflow or routing data—not subsidized credits or services—prevents customers from switching to Runway or Luma. Until then: capable founders, fashionable category, unowned position.
