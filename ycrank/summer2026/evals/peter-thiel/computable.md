# Computable -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Computable’s proposed secret is that GPU capacity should trade as standardized, transferable weekly inventory rather than remain trapped in private leases. What would prove this wrong? If AI developers prefer hourly flexibility, direct supplier contracts, or established marketplaces even when weekly reservations are resellable, then Computable has created a financial instrument without corresponding demand. The live August 2026–January 2027 H100 listings show that the mechanism can be offered; they do not show a matched trade, a repeat participant, or a buyback at a meaningful price. This remains a testable thesis, but not yet a tested secret.

The more serious problem is competition, not technology. SF Compute already supports bids, offers, time-block purchases, and resale while managing more than $100 million of third-party hardware. Runpod and Vast.ai have substantial revenue and existing developer distribution. Computable’s calendar-week grid is a product distinction, but a distinction is not a monopoly mechanism. Ownership of its own order and price data becomes valuable only after liquidity exists; before then, it is an empty ledger. The standing-buyback claim is further weakened by terms imposing no obligation to repurchase or guarantee a price. Instant liquidity is therefore an aspiration contradicted by the contractual reality.

The strongest bull case is that the founders understand electronic markets unusually well. Years building trading infrastructure at Jump Trading and Coinbase could let them standardize fragmented compute contracts, build credible market plumbing, and turn forward-price discovery into a network effect. The 2% fee on each side gives one coherent revenue engine, and an $8.21 billion GPU-services market supplies ample underlying value. But this is an unstated-pattern fit only at the margin: Computable touches the AI production stack without owning compute, energy, or a technical bottleneck. It is presently a broker sitting between capacity suppliers and developers, where the incumbent with the deepest liquidity can copy the weekly contract.

The founding structure is unknowable from the dossier. The two founders’ names, roles, full-time status, shared history, ownership, and responsibility for core engineering are not public. Their relevant employment backgrounds support competence, but competence cannot substitute for alignment. Missing evidence is not a red flag at pre-seed; it is also not evidence that the foundation is sound.

Distribution is similarly embryonic. A self-service market and a Show HN launch producing 45 points and 26 comments identify a plausible developer channel, but no conversion, transaction volume, repeat usage, sales cycle, or acquisition economics are reported. At a 4% combined fee, $1 million of marketplace volume yields only $40,000 before infrastructure, credit, support, and capacity-delivery costs. A fund-scale outcome therefore requires enormous recurring volume and durable liquidity precisely where SF Compute already has capital, hardware relationships, and the same resale primitive.

My forecast is that within 36 months Computable either pivots into managed GPU procurement for a narrow class of AI companies or shuts down the open exchange after failing to establish independent liquidity. The known exception for a contested financial market requires persistent transactions despite adversity; here there is no reported transaction behavior to supply that override. I pass. One condition flips the decision: independently verifiable monthly trading volume above $10 million, with a majority from repeat third-party buyers and sellers and without Computable acting as the economically material market maker. That would convert the secret from slogan to observed market structure.
