# Amulet -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have relevant infrastructure knowledge, but the dossier does not yet prove exact-problem knowledge acquired through repeated deployment. Nithik Bala worked in Meta web infrastructure and at Rockset; Varun Puru worked on backend and cloud systems at Meta and Amazon. Amulet itself—sub-100-ms mounts, copy-on-write forks, content-addressed commits, and conflict-checked publishing—is meaningful evidence that they can build. The missing evidence is what broke in real agent workloads, who experienced it, and what the founders learned that Archil, E2B, and Daytona have not.

The product is technically substantial but the advantage is still mostly code. A competitor can reproduce workspace forks, provenance, and compare-and-swap publishing. Amulet has no demonstrated proprietary model, scarce data, hardware, network, or exclusive distribution; its accumulated workspace history may eventually create switching costs, but private-beta history is not yet a compounding asset. Self-published p99 reads of 1.9 ms and 8.4 GB/s per mount establish an engineering claim, not a durable substrate.

The strongest case is that this is an infrastructure bottleneck arriving behind a real usage wave. E2B reported hundreds of millions of sandbox sessions, Daytona reached a $2 million forward revenue run rate, and ephemeral agent compute plausibly needs durable, provider-independent state. Amulet’s isolation model also addresses a concrete coordination failure: parallel agents can fork a dataset, preserve provenance, and publish without silently overwriting one another. The wedge could expand from automated-research labs into training jobs, agent-compute platforms, and general data-intensive AI workflows.

The Notion exception is relevant because a working, capital-efficient prototype can justify investing before a user-distribution loop appears. The material difference is that Amulet enters an already financed field and has not demonstrated a product language or asset competitors cannot absorb. Archil has raised $18 million, E2B $32 million, and Daytona at least $29 million. Amulet may be the cleaner architecture, but cleaner architecture without proprietary accumulation often becomes a feature.

The $500-per-month Growth plan and usage pricing of $0.20 per GiB-month show direct value-capture intent, but no payer, completed transaction, or recurring revenue is reported. Nor does one customer improve the product or distribution for another customer; every account presently appears to require ordinary integration and sales effort. The founders’ recent pivot from a post-sales product is not itself a red flag—updating can be strength—but the dossier does not show the integrity, persistence, or unusually obsessive user behavior that would override the structural gaps.

My base case is that within 36 months Amulet reaches $1 million–$3 million ARR selling durable agent workspaces, but remains an integration layer rather than becoming a self-reinforcing platform. I would not write the check today. One condition flips the decision: three named production customers showing that accumulated workspace history or cross-provider integrations produce rising retention and make replacement materially harder after the code is copied.
