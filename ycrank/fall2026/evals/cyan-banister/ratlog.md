# RATLOG -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I want a satellite operator to choose an orbit and a delivery date without building the business around somebody else’s launch queue. RATLOG could help create that world. Its proposed 20-tonne reusable rocket would serve satellites and potentially space stations and orbital data centers. Work backward, though: dependable launch availability requires repeated flights; repeated flights require recoverable hardware and reliable propulsion. The company reports an engine hot-fire, targets flight-ready propulsion in 2027, and orbital launch in 2029–2030. There is a path here, with one small piece already burning. The distance between that flame and dependable transportation remains substantial.

The founders give me more conviction than the reservation headline. Charlie Weise and Paul Kunhart worked on propulsion through Innuendo before RATLOG; their experience includes Argonaut research and engine-nozzle design. Felix Kelm brings electronics leadership from the same project, while Matty Driesnack adds startup operating experience from Lio. These are complementary people who gathered around an actual machine. That is meaningful first-believer evidence. The dossier does not establish a long, dated sequence of earlier builds or commercial execution, so I cannot give them the highest founder band. But this is considerably more informative than four aerospace degrees and an attractive rendering.

Why does a satellite operator wait three or four years? RATLOG reports those delays and prices reaching $15,000/kg. The customer loses operating time and postpones revenue while launch providers control scarce scheduling capacity. Returning control over that calendar matters. But the dossier does not establish how much of the price comes from engine complexity, how much comes from other launch costs, or whether RATLOG’s proposed shared flights would resolve the relevant scheduling constraint. SpaceX already operates reusable rockets. Removing a turbopump only changes the customer’s life if it changes the economics and availability of the complete vehicle.

That makes the propulsion architecture the center of this bet. RATLOG targets 90% fewer engine parts, but its CEO also acknowledges increased bearing demands from physically rotating the engine. Where did the complexity go? Into bearings, pressurization hardware, maintenance, or nowhere expensive? I want the whole assembly on the scale. This fits my unstated preference for concealed physical infrastructure beneath several downstream markets: the company is attempting to build transportation those markets would need. Yet control of a proposed engine cycle does not establish control of a scarce, economical launch capability. Launch-site arrangements in Oman or the UAE also remain unresolved.

The strongest argument against waiting is that this team reports building and hot-firing its first engine in four months for under $30,000. Dismissing that because customers cannot yet buy an orbital flight would miss how early hardware companies begin. My known willingness to fund pre-revenue industrial prototypes applies here. Still, a cheap first test does not establish cheap qualification or reuse. Nor do $400 million of signed reservations establish costly customer behavior when payment and cancellation terms are undisclosed. I assign those agreements no revenue and no demonstrated customer sacrifice. The named CEO provides an accountable leader on paper; the dossier supplies no affirmative evidence of the governance shell game or dismissive attitude toward operating complexity that would make me stop immediately.

I am **Neutral** on RATLOG today. The founders and physical enabling layer deserve serious attention; the customer behavior evidence remains at the absent-evidence default. There is also no demonstrated commercial beachhead financing the harder destination, so the inexpensive first engine cannot stand in for the capital path to orbit. My verifiable flip condition is an independently witnessed, full-duration test of the flight-configuration propulsion assembly that meets its predeclared thrust and total propulsion-system mass budgets, including pressurization hardware and bearings. That would show whether the missing turbopump removed a bottleneck or merely moved it.
