# Risklytics -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Risklytics’s contested truth is precise enough to test: frontier-technology companies are unknowingly buying insurance made porous by new AI exclusions, and a specialist broker can identify and close those gaps better than a generalist. What would prove this wrong? Initial placements would reveal that exclusions rarely change usable coverage, customers will not pay or switch brokers, or carriers treat Risklytics’s analysis no differently from ordinary submissions. The 2026 ISO exclusions make the proposition timely, but not contrarian: Alton Risk already markets substantially the same policy-gap analysis and carrier matching. This is an informed hypothesis, not yet a demonstrated secret.

The beachhead—AI agents, robotics, and autonomous systems—is narrow and real. But a narrow market is not automatically a monopoly. Risklytics claims a continuously updated database of forms, endorsements, filings, and carrier treatment, yet discloses neither its size nor any proprietary placement outcomes. Alton directly overlaps; Vouch owns broader startup relationships; Corgi combines distribution with underwriting and has raised more than $268 million. Risklytics currently has a specialization inside a competitive brokerage market, not a mechanism that lets it preserve the value it creates.

The foundation is more promising. Samuel Gold and Alexander Risio both moved full-time onto Risklytics in May 2026, and Risio brings technical experience plus an insurance licensing examination. But “both attended Harvard” is weak evidence of long alignment, and there is no ownership or prior joint-shipping history. Their abandoned Ember catastrophe model shows willingness to reject a non-starter, but also means the present company was assembled around a recent pivot rather than a long-lived operating thesis. Full-time founders deserve credit; founder-company identity remains unproved.

The economic stack is still circular. Risklytics cannot place coverage until it receives producer licenses, needs wholesalers and carriers to establish placement history, and needs placement history to become valuable to those same counterparties. Wholesale brokers, MGAs, YC, and Harvard are named sources of relationships, but there is no identified buyer funnel, sales cycle, conversion rate, commission schedule, or bound policy. The $335.87 billion global brokerage TAM contributes nothing to the power-law question. Without pricing, margins, retention, or an expansion path beyond a labor-intensive specialty brokerage, there is no arithmetic showing that one revenue engine can produce a multibillion-dollar outcome.

The strongest bull case is that ISO created a discontinuity in 2026 and incumbents will be slow to understand it. Each placement could teach Risklytics which exclusions matter, which carriers actually accept frontier risks, and which submission evidence changes underwriting decisions; that learning could compound into proprietary carrier-appetite data. The founders are full-time, the product handles a genuinely technical workflow, and wholesale relationships could solve early access. But none of the exceptions that justify overlooking competition is present: there is no independent technical result, persistent customer behavior, bound production, or realized economics. A possible data flywheel is not yet a data flywheel.

I expect that by August 2029 Risklytics will have pivoted from direct brokerage into policy-analysis software for wholesalers and MGAs and will remain below $2 million in ARR. I would reverse this verdict upon one verifiable milestone: a named carrier grants Risklytics an exclusive frontier-AI insurance program after the company has bound at least 50 policies. That would simultaneously demonstrate distribution, proprietary underwriting knowledge, and a defensible beachhead. Today the judgment is simpler: specialized broker, not last mover; useful analysis, not monopoly.
