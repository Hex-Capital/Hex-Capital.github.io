# Prized -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI coding now lets operations, support, finance, and RevOps employees create software, the economic buyer is the administrator responsible for company data, and the controlling variable is the percentage of employee-built tools that reach recurring production use without consuming engineering time or creating unacceptable security incidents. Prized addresses that variable indirectly, but the dossier never measures it or states a threshold. Ten hand-delivered toolboxes demonstrate outbound effort, not that administrators approve deployments or employees keep using them. The primary market gate therefore remains unproven.

The technology shift is real but the economic step change is incomplete. Retool reports that 35% of surveyed teams replaced at least one SaaS product and 78% expect to build more custom tools in 2026. That supports increased adoption of custom software, but it does not establish Prized’s effect on deployment time, engineering cost, gross margin, or SaaS replacement spend. Multi-stakeholder adoption also matters: an employee wants speed, while an administrator must approve connectors and accept production-data risk. Unless reusable approvals make the second deployment materially easier than the first, natural-language generation may accelerate prototypes without changing enterprise adoption.

The strongest evidence is architectural. Per-tool database roles, brokered credentials kept outside code and agent context, scoped integrations, outbound-host allowlists, audit logs, company sign-in, read-only defaults, and approval for risky migrations form a governed execution workflow rather than a thin prompt interface. Better models should improve the generated applications while Prized retains permissions, credentials, deployment state, and responsibility for actions. This is the right product shape for durability.

The problem is market crystallization. Superblocks already combines natural-language application generation with centralized permissions, auditing, policy enforcement, private-cloud deployment, and more than 50 integrations. Retool has an estimated $120 million in ARR, while Lovable reports $500 million in annualized revenue. Prized’s per-tool isolation may be better implementation, but no customer evidence shows that buyers perceive it as a category-defining difference. The dossier also supplies no structural barrier preventing a funded incumbent from reproducing these controls.

The best opposing case is that governance, not generation, becomes the scarce layer as employee-built software proliferates. Prized has already built unusually concrete security primitives, and the founders previously worked together at Gander before its acquisition; Griffith was its founding engineer, while Eliades also co-founded Loophole. If early customers begin by approving one connector and then rapidly fork many tools across departments, Prized could turn its first product into a reusable distribution channel. But this is not a frontier-research or rare-technical-talent exception to missing pull: the core category is established, the closest competitor is funded, and no deployment, revenue, retention, or named pilot establishes that compounding loop.

I pass today. My 36-month base case is that Prized pivots from general internal-tool generation toward a governance and execution-control layer for enterprise AI agents and remains below $3 million ARR. The verifiable condition that flips me is five paid companies each sustaining at least 20 monthly non-engineer builders for three consecutive months, with most new tools reaching production through previously approved connectors. That would identify the controlling variable, demonstrate product-market pull, and show that governance compounds distribution rather than merely adding friction.
