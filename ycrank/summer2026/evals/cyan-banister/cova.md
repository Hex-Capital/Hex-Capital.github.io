# Cova -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Cova is licensed and live in Michigan, yet Jordan Ibe and Ansh Tandon are both merely labeled “Founder,” with no named CEO or tiebreaker. That pairing is Cova’s signature: an unsexy, regulated care operation with real-world consequences and unresolved authority at its center. I like the first half because Medicaid enrollment, electronic-visit verification, and caregiver management repel tourists. I cannot ignore the second half because two people can share a difficult journey, but they cannot share the steering wheel when the road disappears.

SpaceX made capital intensity and institutional hostility part of the moat; the obstacle was not decoration but the reason conventional investors stayed away. Cova shares that mechanism in miniature: operating a licensed provider under Michigan Home Help rules is harder than selling another AI dashboard, and the federal requirement that at least 80% of certain Medicaid HCBS payments reach direct-care workers forces genuine operational invention. Cova diverges sharply because Michigan permits caregivers to enroll directly, no structural barrier has been identified, and the difficult technology appears to be workflow automation rather than a rocket-class breakthrough. Cova is ugly-regulated, which I enjoy, but not yet magically defensible.

HQ Trivia failed after unresolved authority between its co-founders metastasized into a feud. Cova presents the opening frame of that movie: Jordan Ibe and Ansh Tandon spent a year building together, but Cova names neither a CEO nor a documented decision protocol. “Founder” is not an operating role. The provider pivot adds hiring, payer compliance, clinical-service obligations, and caregiver crises—the exact environment where a polite 50/50 ambiguity becomes an emergency. No evidence means I apply my governance default, and here the dossier’s explicit two-founder structure makes the silence louder.

Jordan Ibe worked at a VA medical center, while Ansh Tandon studied bioengineering and data science; both then spent a year building AI for home-care agencies and hospitals. Cova therefore did not materialize from a weekend TAM exercise, but Cova’s founder relationship to caregiving still looks adjacent rather than autobiographical. Over 18 months, genuine lived knowledge would compound through caregiver trust, payer judgment, and knowing which enrollment problem needs a phone call instead of an agent. Technical workflow familiarity compounds more slowly because Aidaly, Entyre, or an incumbent agency can acquire it. I see some Tiz and Biz. The dossier has not shown me the physical moment either founder felt the problem.

Honor has raised $325 million, Aidaly has raised $8.5 million, and “AI-native healthcare” is hardly a deserted carnival midway. Cova earns non-consensus credit for abandoning the cleaner software-vendor position and becoming the care agency itself; Cova is never quite playing the game it seems to be playing. The score would flip upward if sophisticated investors were passing specifically because Medicaid home care was too operationally ugly, or if Cova showed proprietary case data proving agents could administer care inside the 80% compensation constraint. Without either, the provider pivot is intriguingly strange but not yet Crusoe-level “three uninvestable things stapled together.”

Flock Safety could be encountered in the physical environment before the spreadsheet explained it. Cova has a weaker but legitimate version of that tactile test: a family can call Cova, enroll a caregiver, receive care at home, and observe whether payment and service actually happen. Cova’s AI agents remain invisible abstractions, however, and no case count, repeat usage, revenue, or measured enrollment time is public. A licensed service is more touchable than a developer API, but a license without observed families is a camera mounted on no street.

The Medicaid 80% direct-care compensation rule will push Cova toward the part of the stack where software margins can breathe. My 36-month forecast is that Cova pivots back toward selling Medicaid enrollment, billing, and EVV-compliance software to Michigan home-care agencies and reaches $1 million–$3 million in ARR, rather than becoming a scaled national care provider. Cova’s regulated operating experience can produce a valuable workflow product; Cova’s unclear authority and absence of a structural provider moat make the sprawling agency path less durable.

Cova’s strongest bull mechanism is the HandUp exception: a modest-looking, unsexy service can deserve the check when human need and founder proximity matter more than a pristine software moat. Jordan Ibe’s VA exposure, Michigan licensure, separate caregiver and patient intake paths, and hands-on training could let Cova learn faster than Honor, Aidaly, or an abstract software vendor. That case stings because real care delivery could generate the proprietary workflow data the dossier currently lacks. It does not win today: Cova shows no revenue, case volume, repeat usage, autobiographical caregiving origin, or named CEO, while Michigan permits direct caregiver enrollment. I would flip to Invest if Cova names one founder as final operating authority and documents at least 100 paid, recurring weekly care cases with improving enrollment or billing economics.
