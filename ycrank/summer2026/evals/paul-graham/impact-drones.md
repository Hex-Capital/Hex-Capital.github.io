# IMPACT Drones -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The decisive fact is missing. IMPACT Drones names hard obstacles—GPS jamming, rapid launch, and the absurd economics of using a million-dollar effector against a sub-$50,000 drone—but does not show what broke during development, what the founders built in response, or how quickly the result improved. The sealed cell, frangible lid, and visually guided interceptor are specific design choices. They are not yet evidence of relentlessly resourceful behavior under resistance. With no dated blocker-to-workaround sequence, I have to use the founder default.

The strongest evidence is where the idea came from. Jasper Lortije previously built GNSS-denied navigation for long-range ISR drones at AeroIntel and helped secure the 2025 NATO Summit. Alessandro Fiume led sales there, so this is a two-person team with prior shared execution in the same difficult domain. The idea was not produced by combining “AI” with a large market. These founders encountered the technical and procurement world themselves. They have also chosen a magnificent schlep: flight safety, site integration, reload logistics, government authorization, false-positive control, and debris over civilian infrastructure.

But hardware becomes evidence only when it crosses a threshold. IMPACT describes HUNTER and turbine-powered SABLE, yet publishes no intercept success rate, environmental result, false-positive rate, or GPS-denied test. Q3 2026 pilots are prospective. Meanwhile, Fortem reports more than 5,500 captures and Origin’s BLAZE is deployed by four European NATO states. Credentials and architecture earn less credit than one independently witnessed interception. The pre-round proof spike that often distinguishes unusually technical founders is absent, and none of the known exceptions applies without a working, measured artifact strong enough to replace it.

The user evidence is weaker still. Data centers, airports, energy facilities, and bases are categories, not users. The 875 LinkedIn followers, two press mentions, and 27 applicants for an FPV pilot job tell me almost nothing about whether anyone needs the crude product now. There is no named pilot customer, signed LOI, revenue, repeat usage, or procurement owner. The subscription model could turn reloads and monitoring into recurring revenue, but without a disclosed price or engagement rate it could just as easily conceal unattractive hardware and service costs.

The bull case is that the dossier is catching IMPACT immediately before the evidence arrives. The founders already worked together, the navigation experience maps directly to the product, and forthcoming Middle East pilots may offer a faster regulatory route than private deployment in the United States. A successful pilot could generate flight data, customer pull, and a regulatory template at once. But the present distribution language leans on “partnerships, pilots, and procurement,” while U.S. law does not clearly authorize private infrastructure owners to perform kinetic mitigation. That creates the red-flag version of the story: the product can work technically and still spend years waiting for a powerful partner’s permission.

I am passing today. My 36-month base case is that IMPACT abandons direct civilian-site subscriptions as its primary route and sells government-authorized deployments through a Middle Eastern defense buyer or prime contractor, while remaining below $3 million ARR. The single condition that would flip me is a named civilian-infrastructure pilot converting into a legally authorized paid deployment after an independently witnessed GPS-denied test achieves at least a 90% intercept rate. That would turn the current collection of plausible claims into a logical proof.
