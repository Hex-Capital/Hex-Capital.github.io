# Shire Intelligence -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Better vision models let full-service restaurants use existing cameras to coordinate service, but Shire Intelligence becomes a large company only if that coordination produces recurring software profit at each location. That is the controlling variable. Faster table turns can create customer value without creating an attractive supplier business. The dossier does not establish deployment costs, inference costs, support requirements, or realized subscription pricing. At the advertised starting price of $40/month, each restaurant contributes just $480 in annual revenue before those costs.

The strongest case for Shire is that the technology now connects observation to work. Its disclosed use of DINOv3 and SAM3 follows their August and November 2025 releases, respectively. The company reports seven-minute faster table turns and 14% more guests served monthly. Those are economically relevant outcomes: increasing covers through an existing dining room could improve restaurant economics without adding floor space. I give this evidence meaningful, discounted credit for the technology shift. The results lack sample sizes and measurement methods, however, and they do not establish Shire’s own margins. The broader $7.6 billion restaurant-software market cannot answer that question.

The distinction between customer value and product pull matters particularly here. Mathews Restaurant’s reported $65,000 in processing savings is a named customer signal, but it validates payment economics more directly than camera-driven service coordination. It does not establish paid Intelligence adoption, repeat usage, or retention. The bull case is that payments and POS create distribution through which Shire can sell dining-room automation without acquiring the restaurant again. That would be a useful product-to-distribution mechanism. The dossier shows the products offered together, but not evidence that this cross-sell already works.

Shire’s product description earns more architectural credit than a standalone vision dashboard would. Updating table states, balancing seating assignments, and triggering staff tasks connects perception to an operational loop. Better models should improve that loop. What remains undocumented is whether Shire retains the integrations, staff feedback, and workflow state that make the relationship durable. PreciTaste already has adjacent vision and staff-guidance capabilities. Publicly available model components help Shire enter the market while also reducing competitors’ implementation costs; proprietary footage alone would not resolve that exposure.

Kuperman and Tabaku’s restaurant work gives the team relevant operating context, and their longstanding relationship is useful. Neither their academic credentials nor the prototype’s hackathon win establishes the costly commitment or prior startup learning my founder criterion requires. This also does not qualify for a research-stage exception: the dossier documents an application of available models, rather than scarce frontier research that could justify underwriting ahead of demand. The physical-market exception would require demonstrated changes in delivery economics, which remain unproven. I would pass today, while recognizing a credible automation opportunity. One result would reopen the decision: a ten-restaurant cohort renewing paid Intelligence subscriptions after six months and generating positive contribution profit after camera setup, inference, and support costs.
