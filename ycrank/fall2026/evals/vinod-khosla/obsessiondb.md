# ObsessionDB -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

ObsessionDB has demonstrated a useful cost reduction: Numia’s reported warehouse bill fell from approximately $8,000 to $2,000 per month after migrating 200 TB from BigQuery. My first question is what becomes possible when that reduction compounds across machine-generated workloads. The dossier identifies a $14.1 billion parent market, but leaves the consequential step unquantified: how much expensive expertise or computation becomes accessible, to whom, and at what scale? I can underwrite improbable success. I still need to understand what success changes.

The engineering earns attention. ObsessionDB retains ClickHouse’s execution engine while replacing storage with shared object storage, stateless compute, distributed NVMe caching, and locality-aware routing. That is substantive infrastructure work. The named customer result establishes a reported 4x cost improvement against BigQuery, supported by an architectural explanation and production deployment. It does not establish a 5–10x advantage against a tuned ClickHouse Cloud deployment. Provisioned-capacity pricing can also change a bill without producing an equivalent improvement in resource efficiency. Separating those effects determines whether the company owns a durable technical advantage.

The strongest argument against passing is that these founders have already encountered the problem at meaningful scale. All three worked at Numia; Höffl was its CTO. ObsessionDB reports paying production customers, monthly petabyte processing, and a largest customer serving APIs over 50 TB while ingesting hundreds of thousands of events per minute. My investing pattern includes execution-heavy businesses that turn an existing workflow into a platform. This team has more evidence for that route than an ordinary pre-seed infrastructure company. But the founder record supplied here establishes domain experience; it contains no documented belief revision or difficult complementary recruit. I will credit the experience without inventing the learning behavior.

The next dollar should resolve the largest survival uncertainty. The dossier supplies no ranked kill risk, costed experiment, numerical threshold, or stop condition, so risk-removal discipline receives its absent-evidence default. Reliability deserves particular attention: native backup and restore are unimplemented, time travel is the recovery alternative, and security disclosures differ between the homepage and the June security page. Those differences require verification; they do not establish misconduct. Likewise, the indicative $4,090 monthly configuration establishes a selling price. Without cache utilization, infrastructure expense, support burden, and gross margins, it cannot establish sustainable cost parity. The $3,000 conversion offer supplies no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence.

I pass at 33. ObsessionDB avoids the superficial AI-wrapper red flag through proprietary storage engineering. I have also accepted missing early milestones or economics when a documented instigator and an extraordinary consequence justified the option; this dossier does not establish that override. The bull case remains credible: experienced operators could build a valuable warehouse business from production demand. My verifiable flip condition is an independently reproducible production study showing at least 10x lower fully loaded cost per successful agent task than tuned ClickHouse Cloud, at matched freshness, latency, and recovery guarantees. That would connect the engineering to a capability expansion large enough to reopen the investment decision.
