# Fabraix -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Ahmed Aly and Ibrahim Abdu worked together for nearly four years before building Nyx, but neither is described as building Nyx to solve a problem he personally suffered. That makes Fabraix a strong-pair, weakly organic company. Fabraix has the configuration I want and a working artifact that found vulnerabilities across dozens of Fortune 500 agents. But the idea currently reads as adjacent to the founders’ experience rather than emerging irresistibly from their own lives.

**Stripe** is the high rung for co-founder configuration: two brothers with years of shared hacking history and complementary ability, building something neither could have built as quickly alone. Fabraix has a meaningful version of that mechanism because Aly and Abdu worked together at Two and had known each other for almost four years. Fabraix diverges from Stripe because the dossier establishes duration, not the texture of the collaboration—no earlier joint artifact, crisis, or failed startup shows how the pair behaves when the code breaks at 3 a.m. Still, this is a real pair rather than two strangers assembled for an application.

**Kiko** exposed the danger of building a plausible product without an origin story strong enough to pull the founders through bad timing. Aly ran fraud systems processing more than $1 billion annually, and Abdu built production-debugging agents at Meta, so Fabraix is not a market-research invention. But Fabraix never says either founder was attacked through an agent, repeatedly performed red-team assessments by hand, or first built Nyx for his own workflow. Domain adjacency is useful. It is not the same as Drew Houston forgetting his USB stick. Fabraix therefore sits above a manufactured AI-security pitch but below a genuinely organic idea.

Fabraix’s 50,000-plus Playground attempts, 10,000 attack strategies, and vulnerabilities across dozens of Fortune 500 agents are tracks left by resourceful founders. Over the next 18 months, that behavior can compound: every successful attack becomes a variant, every variant improves the next assessment, and every public challenge attracts more adversarial input. Fabraix has already shipped more than a deck. The missing animal signal is an unscalable act—personally winning a resistant customer, manually securing an agent overnight, or turning a rejection into revenue. The current evidence resembles a very good technical launch, not yet the cereal boxes.

Lakera’s acquisition by Check Point shows both the schlep and the counterfactual. Fabraix reaches the top of this dimension only if Aly and Abdu personally grind through enterprise permissions, reproduce production failures, guide remediation, and survive security procurement repeatedly. Fabraix currently performs black-box attacks across chat, voice, browser, and coding agents, which is technically unpleasant, but competitors such as Mindgard already automate similar testing and open-source frameworks reproduce parts of the workflow. If Nyx remains a remotely executed software benchmark, the schlep is copyable. If Fabraix becomes the people customers call during live agent incidents, the score flips upward.

Ahmed Aly’s published research and Ibrahim Abdu’s compiler, database, and Meta-agent work put Fabraix on the same axis as **Stripe**: technically serious founders building infrastructure that ordinary application programmers would rather avoid. Fabraix falls short of that anchor’s hacker genealogy because neither founder has a public repository or a named pre-company artifact, while the Playground has only 71 GitHub stars. Hackers usually leave trails. Fabraix has strong professional evidence and a working system, but the unstated pattern is only partially satisfied.

Prompt Security’s 2025 acquisition by SentinelOne suggests the likely outcome: by July 2029, Fabraix will reach $2 million to $5 million in ARR and be acquired by Palo Alto Networks for less than $100 million rather than becoming an independent security platform. Fabraix’s longstanding founder pair can build valuable attack technology, while Fabraix’s weak organic origin and dependence on enterprise distribution make absorption more likely than category ownership. The platform vendors already have the customer channel; Nyx is the capability they will want to buy.

Dozens of Fortune 500 agents, a 78% AgentHarm success rate, and 50,000 challenge attempts make the strongest opposing case: Fabraix may be discovering an attack corpus that compounds like Coinbase’s compliance knowledge, and the absence of revenue at pre-seed should not matter. Fabraix also avoids the obvious red flags—no solo founder, no MBA-first pair, no hiring-before-shipping plan—and Airbnb is the known exception proving that a strange idea plus animals can outrun weak initial evidence. The sting is real. But “vulnerabilities found” does not mean paying customers, the Fortune 500 companies are unnamed, and Nyx faces Mindgard plus freely available frameworks. Ten named enterprise customers, more than $1 million ARR, and net retention above 120% within twelve months would flip my verdict from a measured check to Strong Invest.
