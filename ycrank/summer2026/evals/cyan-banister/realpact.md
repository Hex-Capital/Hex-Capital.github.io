# RealPact -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Paying New Hampshire brokerages and a firm-wide Four Seasons Sotheby’s pilot make RealPact an observable workflow product with invisible founder authority. That is RealPact’s signature: brokers can watch records become contracts and deadlines become actions, but Ranvir Deshmukh and Erik Peterson are both merely “Co-founder,” with no named CEO or tiebreaker. I can see RealPact working; I cannot see who wins the argument when RealPact breaks. Biz and Tiz are present. Rizz and authority remain off-camera.

**Niantic** is the high rung because I played Ingress before I pursued John Hanke; the artifact made the founder’s strange map of the world tangible. RealPact shares that load-bearing mechanism: a Four Seasons Sotheby’s office can test whether RealPact retrieves a deed, cross-references a permit, and populates the right contract. RealPact diverges because a normal human cannot install it and feel the magic within 48 hours; access is gated through brokerage pilots, and no repeat-usage or error-rate evidence is public. The product can be observed, but not yet loved in the wild.

**HQ Trivia** is the low-rung warning: explosive tactile pull temporarily concealed unresolved co-founder authority, and the Yusupov-Kroll fracture eventually collected its debt. RealPact shows the same structural blank without HQ Trivia’s exceptional override. Deshmukh and Peterson previously built CourseMe together, yet RealPact names neither a CEO nor a documented tiebreaker, and RealPact reports no viral usage signal powerful enough to excuse that omission. “Two founders” is a rebuttal; “nobody has final authority” is close to a hard no.

CourseMe reached 4,000 monthly users, one million page hits, and roughly 90% of Dartmouth before acquisition, so Deshmukh and Peterson know how to ship and distribute a useful product. RealPact’s founder-origin mechanism is weaker: no brokerage employment, real-estate operating history, or autobiographical encounter with transaction paperwork appears in the dossier. Over 18 months, RealPact’s jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction integrations will compound only if the founders possess the social knowledge to distinguish a New Hampshire broker’s real pain from a clever automation demo. Right now they are visiting the place, not obviously *of* it.

SkySlope already serves more than 900,000 professionals and offers AI-assisted transactions, compliance, forms, voice assistance, and back-office workflows. For RealPact to become magically weird rather than another consensus AI wrapper, one surprising fact would need to emerge: brokers must be choosing RealPact specifically because its external-record retrieval solves a failure incumbents structurally cannot touch. RealPact would move into Invest territory on this dimension if named brokerages rejected SkySlope yet paid RealPact for that capability; the current evidence—paying customers with undisclosed revenue and one pilot—does not establish the rebellion.

SOC 2 was unfinished in May 2026, while RealPact’s model output can enter legally consequential contracts using fragmented city, county, and state records. That gives RealPact a faint **Vigil Labs** parallel: regulated ugliness can become a moat because every assurance, correction, and jurisdictional integration makes the next entrant’s work less pleasant. RealPact is still software-only, however, with no certification, proprietary dataset, or structural barrier disclosed. I like an unsexy compliance thicket, but a thicket is not a moat until somebody has bled building the path.

$500,000 from Y Combinator gives RealPact enough runway to discover whether the external-record wedge is a product or a feature. My 36-month forecast is that RealPact narrows from an “AI-native OS” into property-record intake and compliance automation, remains below $3 million ARR, and is acquired by SkySlope for under $30 million by July 2029. RealPact’s observable workflow earns adoption; RealPact’s incumbent overlap, absent moat, and unresolved authority prevent an independent platform outcome.

Four Seasons Sotheby’s is the bull case that stings: combine that firm-wide pilot with paying New Hampshire brokerages and founders who already distributed CourseMe to 90% of Dartmouth, and RealPact could be another HQ Trivia exception where product pull outruns my governance anxiety. RealPact has not yet shown the exceptional ingredient that made that exception rational—usage intensity—and SkySlope already processes more than three million transactions annually. A signed Four Seasons Sotheby’s rollout worth at least $500,000 in annual recurring revenue would flip my verdict, because then RealPact’s tactile pull would be strong enough to treat the governance defect as a rebuttal rather than a hard no.
