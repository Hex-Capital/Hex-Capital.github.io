# Superserve -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Superserve has shipped something substantial. What I cannot find is the episode that would tell me most about Pavitra Bhalla: what stopped him, what he did about it, and how quickly that changed the situation. His staff engineering role at Dosu and founding-engineer experience at LTSE make technical competence credible. They do not establish resourcefulness under resistance. That part of the investment case remains unanswered.

The product gives me a reason to pursue the question. Superserve offers persistent Firecracker VMs, memory snapshots, forks, and credential brokering outside the guest. Public SDKs and documented lifecycle behavior provide concrete evidence of an operating system people can build on. I give substantial credit for that artifact. The advertised sub-50ms startup time receives less weight because the dossier supplies no independent benchmark. Documenting failed resumes and recovery controls also shows that the team has encountered operational complexity, although it does not establish how effectively they resolved it.

The strongest evidence of demand is the reported 100,000 machines run weekly, with Lindy and YC named as users. Coding-agent builders are a sufficiently specific niche, and persistent execution solves an intelligible problem for them. But machine count is sensitive to workload design: one customer can create many short-lived machines. I want to know whether the same builders return and expand their workloads. The published $0.0504/vCPU-hour price establishes a way to charge; introductory credits mean usage alone cannot establish payment. This is meaningful early demand evidence with an unresolved connection to durable revenue.

The earlier RayAI orchestration platform makes the product history potentially revealing. OCV launched it on November 5, 2025; Superserve now sells execution infrastructure. Perhaps customers repeatedly struggled with the computers underneath their agents, and Bhalla followed that observation down the stack. That would be a good reason to change direction. The dossier does not establish it. Likewise, Dosu experience puts him near the problem without documenting a personal collision that originated this product. I cannot award either discovery or adaptability credit for a plausible story I supplied myself.

The strongest argument for investing is that Superserve fits a pattern I like: technical users adopting a programmable primitive before its boundaries are settled. Its operating product and reported workload volume could matter much more than an incomplete founder biography. Dropbox supplies a relevant exception: a working prototype and rapid founder correction outweighed uncertainty about the founding team. Superserve shares the working-product evidence; the rapid correction and unusually strong founder behavior remain undocumented. The usage figures warrant investigation, but do not yet justify overriding my resourcefulness gate.

Competition makes that distinction consequential. E2B already preserves memory across pauses, and Modal offers VM sandboxes. Unlimited continuous runtime gives Superserve a concrete opening, but competitors can narrow it. Winning could require repeatedly doing difficult installation, reliability, and customer-service work faster than larger teams. Claude and LangChain integrations provide distribution paths; there is no evidence that the operating plan depends on a promised major partnership. Nor does missing cofounder history establish a bad relationship. The central uncertainty is affirmative evidence of how these founders behave when the product stops being enough.

I would keep talking, but hold my check. My assessment is neutral because the shipped infrastructure and named usage are substantial, while the founder evidence needed for my decision is missing. One verifiable event could change that: Lindy confirming that Bhalla personally solved a blocking production problem within a week, restoring a workload that subsequently remained in paid use. That would connect technical ability to resourcefulness and customer necessity.
