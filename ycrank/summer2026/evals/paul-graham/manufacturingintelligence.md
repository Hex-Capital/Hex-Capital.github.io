# HERA -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when HERA first failed. Patel and So interviewed more than 200 manufacturing workers and put a demo into three production plants by May 2026. That shows motion. But the dossier never names a rejected installation, an inaccurate check, or a confidentiality obstacle followed by a founder-built workaround. I cannot infer resourcefulness merely from activity. The founders may be running backs, but I have not yet seen them change direction under pressure.

The strongest evidence is the artifact. Two recent industrial-engineering graduates have built software that reviews GD&T, tolerance stacks, datums, assemblies, and company-specific rules in minutes, with demonstrations inside actual plants. Keeping drawings on customer premises also addresses a real constraint rather than pretending it away. This is more than a diagram, but less than measured technical proof: the published 94.77% precision and 5.23% hallucination figures belong to another research system. HERA supplies no accuracy benchmark, adjudicated violation count, or documented scrap prevented.

The users are plausible but not yet pulling. Aerospace and medical-device engineers can lose $40,000 on a bad part, so the pain is specific and expensive. Yet 200 interviews prove learning, not demand; three live demos do not establish repeated use; and the current offer is a free, NDA-covered review of 20 drawings. There is no paid deployment, named design partner, retention figure, or engineer who changed a workflow around the product. Who wants this crude version enough to depend on it next week? The dossier does not answer.

The founders did not invent the problem from a generic market map. Patel worked on manufacturing projects at Pfizer and TE Connectivity, while So built industrial systems and automated workflows. That gives them legitimate proximity to the work. The wedge is also full of useful schlep: confidential drawings, on-premises deployment, internal standards, tolerance mathematics, and engineer sign-off. But proximity is not the same as a founder-originated need. There is no dated account of either founder personally encountering a drawing error and building HERA to remove it.

The bull case is that customer-specific rules and adjudicated violations become a difficult proprietary substrate. A working product inside three plants could therefore be much more informative than the absence of revenue at pre-seed. The bear case stings more: CoLab, Axial, Redprint, and Glypad already offer overlapping reviews, with Redprint charging as little as $1.98 per drawing. HERA’s unstated-pattern test is not merely whether it shipped, but whether it produced an unusually concrete proof spike before the round. It has not. Nor does a known exception apply: there is neither independently validated technical performance nor unmistakable user pull sufficient to override the missing founder-under-resistance evidence. I do not see the listed red flags of delegated sales or dependence on a large partner, but on-premises procurement could still turn every deployment into a slow custom engagement.

I would not write the check yet. The single flip condition is a named, paid plant case study documenting a specific failed check or deployment blocker, the founders’ dated product response, and repeated use that prevented at least one five-figure manufacturing error. That would simultaneously show what the founders do when blocked and whether the narrow well is real.
