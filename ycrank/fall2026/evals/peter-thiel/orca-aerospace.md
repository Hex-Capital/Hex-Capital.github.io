# Orca Aerospace -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Orca Aerospace’s possible secret is that mission-level judgment can move onboard a spacecraft while deterministic planning and command checks preserve safety. That proposition is testable: sequences of individually permitted commands could still produce an unsafe mission outcome. But the dossier supplies neither Orca’s validation results nor a prevailing belief that named peers reject. Indeed, several peers are pursuing similar architectures. The company has identified an important engineering problem; its particular discovery remains unestablished.

The decisive difficulty is ownership of the solution. Shield AI and Sedaro reported executing **189 approved commands aboard a NOVI satellite in August 2026**, using an onboard validation framework. In Orbit Aerospace markets onboard reasoning and fault management. These are concrete overlaps with Orca’s proposed differentiation; Quindar’s ground-based mission control is a less direct comparison. Customer-avionics integration and hardware-in-the-loop testing establish necessary engineering work. They do not establish proprietary technology, exclusive data, or a bounded market Orca can dominate. Integration could eventually create switching costs, but today the same work could become a recurring delivery expense.

The strongest objection to passing is the founders. Jacob Ososke spent more than a decade in Lockheed Martin GNC and founded its first AI-GNC team; Hakan Chunton brings MIT trajectory research and Lockheed GNC experience. Their backgrounds make the separation between probabilistic objectives and deterministic execution credible as an engineering choice. Orca fits my unstated preference for extending computation into physical systems, and its founders come from the relevant operating environment. However, shared employment does not establish that they previously shipped together. Full-time status, ownership, and economic exposure remain undocumented. I assign the founding structure its stated default, without inventing either alignment or a red flag.

The joint Lyra rendezvous-and-proximity-operations study is the most useful early external engagement. It also exposes the deployment constraint: model memory determines compute-board requirements, and board power affects spacecraft bus sizing. This is substantive customer-context work for a pre-seed company. It remains a concept-level assessment, with no disclosed purchase commitment. The briefing form and **CAGE 1YYL8** registration identify avenues toward customers, but establish neither a repeatable acquisition channel nor contract economics. Software licensing is inferred; pricing and margins are unknown. Consequently, there is no grounded arithmetic connecting one revenue engine to a power-law outcome.

Could this warrant an exception? My known exceptions require evidence such as an independently demonstrated technical breakthrough, persistent transaction demand, or a concrete deployment path tied to urgent demand. Orca has not documented those conditions. The competitor’s orbital demonstration proves that the category is becoming technically credible; it cannot serve as Orca’s technical proof. Conversely, the dossier shows no founder liquidity problem, regulatory wind-down order, or retracted technical claim. The coordination red flag is relevant through avionics, safety validation, compute, and power, but it would be an overreach to impute an entire factory-and-licensing failure stack to this software company.

I pass with my own money. Orca Aerospace has credible engineers addressing a consequential problem, but the documented competitive overlap leaves its monopoly gate below threshold. Missing revenue is ordinary at this stage; the investment turns on whether this team owns a capability others cannot reproduce. **One verifiable flip condition would reopen diligence: an independently verified, paid orbital deployment demonstrating a mission capability that named competing systems cannot match within the same avionics and power budget.** That would give the proposed secret commercial and technical substance.
