# Petrarch -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Three completed transaction categories and zero public founder repositories make Petrarch a schlep-heavy brokerage with operator tracks but faint hacker tracks. Petrarch has already moved construction plans, autonomous-driving recordings, and codebases through a process involving sourcing, cleaning, and legal clearance. That is real work, not a market-size slide. But Petrarch’s public record says more about LinkedIn networks than code, so the analytical signature is unusually high appetite for unpleasant operations paired with unusually weak visible hacker genealogy.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness: compliance was not incidental to the product; enduring compliance became the asset. Petrarch shares that mechanism because every dataset requires Petrarch to establish ownership, permitted uses, privacy constraints, formatting, and a willing buyer. The analogy is load-bearing. Petrarch diverges from Coinbase because Brian Armstrong had a working Android wallet, while Petrarch discloses only three transaction categories—not named buyers, transaction values, repeat purchases, or a demonstrated legal-clearance system. Petrarch has entered the schlep zone, but has not yet shown that it can turn the schlep into machinery.

**Loopt** (2005) is the low rung on hacker genealogy because Sam Altman’s animal force, rather than a public technical artifact, justified making an exception while the product infrastructure remained premature. Petrarch shows a trace of the same pattern: Samuel Hahn’s record is GTM and venture sourcing, Ian Lee’s is employment at Scale AI and Meridian AI, and none of Petrarch’s three founders has a public repository. Sudhish Swain’s hospital-deployed computer-vision software prevents Petrarch from landing at the bottom, but one cited engineering artifact is not the pre-company trail left by the Collisons or Aaron Swartz. Petrarch may contain hackers; the dossier does not yet prove hacker genealogy.

Thirty-six Constellation Fellow applicants reveal Petrarch’s resourcefulness mechanism. Over the next 18 months, Petrarch’s commissioned student network could compound into a map of obscure data owners and live buyer demand, provided Petrarch converts introductions into repeatable transactions. The darker version is a swarm of lightly trained brokers producing leads that require founders to rescue every deal manually. Petrarch’s completed transactions show more animal behavior than credentials alone, but Petrarch has not disclosed an Airbnb-like stunt, a rejected-then-rebuilt artifact, or even transaction revenue. The founders have left tracks, though not yet claw marks.

Samuel Hahn led GTM at a data-compliance company, Ian Lee worked with Scale AI, and Sudhish Swain handled computer-vision and biological datasets. Those facts give Petrarch adjacency to the problem, but adjacency is not an organic idea. For Petrarch’s score to flip upward, one founder would need to show that a needed industrial dataset was impossible to license in a prior job, that Petrarch began as the internal tool built to solve that blockage, and that another buyer independently demanded it. Until then, Petrarch reads as three capable people who noticed a market discontinuity, not as Drew Houston repeatedly forgetting his USB stick.

Three Harvard founders left university to build Petrarch, which is a better configuration than a solo founder and provides plausible coverage across sales, AI data, and engineering. **Stripe** (2010) remains the useful single-anchor parallel: two brothers with years of shared history could move quickly because coordination had already been debugged. Petrarch supplies only the fact that all three attended Harvard; Petrarch does not establish how long they built together, whether they survived prior failure together, or who owns the final technical decision. The trio shape is right. The relationship evidence is incomplete.

Petrarch’s three disclosed transaction categories imply that by July 2029 Petrarch will have become a managed enterprise data-rights brokerage—not a self-serve marketplace—with $2 million to $5 million in annual revenue from bespoke sourcing, clearance, and preparation. Petrarch’s high tolerance for legal and operational grind can sustain that business, while Petrarch’s thin hacker trail makes a rapidly compounding software marketplace less likely. The schlep will generate revenue before it generates scale.

Defined.ai’s $85 million in funding and Datarade’s catalog of more than 500 providers form the strongest bear case: Petrarch could be a biz-dev-first intermediary recruiting fellows because Petrarch has neither proprietary software nor exclusive supply. An unreadable public product site, no disclosed revenue, and no public repositories make that objection sting. But Petrarch has completed transactions across three genuinely different data types, and the **Airbnb** (2009) exception says not to dismiss founders who sell the awkward thing before the infrastructure looks respectable. I would write a small check, barely. If Petrarch cannot show by July 2027 that at least one buyer made a repeat purchase and that a completed transaction produced positive gross margin after clearance and preparation costs, I would flip to Pass.
