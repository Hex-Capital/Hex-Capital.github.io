# Stoa -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Kaan Yigit’s commodities-desk experience at Uniper is Stoa’s strongest signal; Stoa’s weakest is the absence of any definite plan beyond aggregating RFQs. Stoa is a finance-literate founding group building market infrastructure without yet demonstrating that it knows the market’s predetermined destination. Pedigree without a definite plan produces a capable broker, not necessarily a monopoly.

**Affirm** is the high rung on founder lineage because Max Levchin had already shipped PayPal to scale before attacking credit. Stoa shares the domain-adjacency mechanism: Yigit built quantitative systems for commodities, Eren Berke Saglam holds Series 7 and 63 registrations, and Berat Celik combines computer science with fintech experience. Stoa diverges where the analogy becomes decisive: none of Stoa’s founders has operated an exchange, settled physical-hardware trades at scale, or built a prior power-law company. Credentials are evidence of competence; they are not the Levchin override.

**Quora** is the low rung on definite planning because an indefinite consumer roadmap could survive only through Adam D’Angelo’s Facebook pedigree and a speculative knowledge-graph network effect. Stoa exhibits the same missing-plan mechanism without that exception: Stoa names no 24-month liquidity milestone, clearing architecture, inspection capability, geographic expansion, or category sequence beyond routing RFQs and recording settlement. “More transactions produce better data” is a hope about emergence, not a definite plan. Stoa receives the absent-evidence default here.

**Compute Exchange** already offers verified counterparties, physical GPU inventory, and quotes within 24 hours, so Stoa has not named an incumbent blind spot that the obvious competitor rejects. Stoa’s secret could become real if Compute Exchange’s inventory model structurally cannot produce firm, auditable clearing prices across dealers, lenders, liquidators, and OEM channels while Stoa’s RFQ architecture can. The dossier does not establish that counterfactual; Stoa currently looks like another participant in the July 2026 rush to standardize AI-hardware trading. Mimetic markets often mistake simultaneity for inevitability.

Stoa’s 0% fee for eligible data partners can bootstrap proprietary clearing-price and counterparty-performance data, but the mechanism compounds only after transactions settle. Over the next 18 months, Stoa must attract liquidity with waived fees, which weakens pricing power precisely while Compute Exchange draws procurement from more than 100 compute providers. Without disclosed RFQ volume or completed transactions, Stoa’s data flywheel is circular: liquidity creates data, but anticipated data does not create liquidity. Monopoly is the condition of success; Stoa currently has a marketplace hypothesis, not monopoly economics.

**Anduril** used physical systems and regulated procurement as mutually reinforcing barriers: hardware ownership, integration depth, and government trust made imitation expensive. Stoa touches atoms and compliance—physical NVIDIA GPUs, KYB verification, U.S.-only institutional access, and export-law obligations—but Stoa does not inspect hardware, take title, guarantee participants, or assume dispute risk. Stoa therefore captures the coordination layer while counterparties retain the difficult atoms-heavy work. “We wanted flying cars” does not mean financing the spreadsheet through which flying cars are resold.

Compute Exchange’s July 2026 physical-market launch makes the 36-month forecast falsifiable: by July 2029, Stoa will have pivoted from a neutral exchange into a GPU brokerage and price-data service producing $1 million to $3 million in annual recurring revenue, without establishing a liquid independent marketplace. Stoa’s finance-oriented founders can build a useful intermediary, but Stoa’s missing definite plan and unproven liquidity moat point toward services revenue rather than zero-to-one market ownership.

Ornn’s $33 million financing is the strongest bull case: AI compute may be becoming a commodity fast enough that several exchanges can grow, while Stoa’s RFQ records and data-partner program could compound into a benchmark analogous to a young financial price index. **Lyft** supplies the known-exception precedent—duopoly can be investable when network effects stabilize two winners. The objection fails today because Stoa discloses no settled volume while Compute Exchange already aggregates more than 100 providers; unlike Lyft, Stoa has not demonstrated one side of the network. I would reverse the pass if Stoa verifies $50 million in annualized settled GMV with more than half of counterparties completing repeat transactions.
