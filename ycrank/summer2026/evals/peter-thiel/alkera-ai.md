# Alkera AI -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Alkera AI’s proposed secret is that a data agent becomes trustworthy only when it understands column-level consequences across the entire stack and operates within explicit permissions. The falsifying event is straightforward: if general coding agents or incumbent data platforms achieve comparable production reliability using their existing metadata and permission layers, Alkera has built a feature, not a company. Its 83.28% Pass@1 on DataAgentBench is technical evidence, but the 1.43-point lead over SCRIBE is too narrow—and the 54-query benchmark too constrained—to establish a zero-to-one result.

The strongest fact is the founding structure. Rick Gao, Tony Li, and Andrew Tran met at Yale four years before launch and worked together across research, coursework, projects, and a trading competition. Their experience spans data infrastructure at Ramp and Hudson River Trading, multi-agent systems at ByteDance, quantitative software, and computational biology. This is a coherent technical group formed through repeated work, not three strangers assembled around the current AI fashion. Full-time status and founder ownership are not documented, however, so commitment is strongly suggested rather than economically proved.

The central problem is competition. PromptQL already combines shared context with deterministic data permissions; Altimate sells AI teammates for SQL and dbt; Prophecy generates production Spark and SQL. Alkera adds whole-stack lineage, downstream impact mapping, verified organizational knowledge, and destructive-action approvals, but no retention, proprietary-data, patent, or dominant-beachhead evidence shows that it can keep the value those features create. The inferred switching costs from accumulated lineage graphs may eventually matter. Today they are a resemblance to a monopoly mechanism, not evidence of one.

The bull case is sharper than a generic “AI for data” pitch. A capable, long-aligned team has shipped a measurable system, leads a relevant benchmark, and offers a product architecture in which customer-specific knowledge could compound. The OpenAI exception would permit uncertain monopoly if a verified technical event were sufficiently discontinuous. Alkera’s material difference is that 83.28% versus 81.85% is incremental and readily contestable, not a threshold event that reorganizes the market. The dossier’s own competitor evidence therefore outweighs the exception.

The revenue design is coherent but unvalidated: free usage can feed $50 Plus and $250 Pro subscriptions, while enterprise contracts add VPC or on-premises deployment, SSO, SCIM, audit logs, and SLAs. That is one engine with plausible expansion rather than several unrelated businesses. Yet no paying customer, gross margin, conversion rate, sales cycle, or deployment duration demonstrates that the self-service and enterprise motions reinforce each other. Pending compliance certifications and missing Looker, Tableau, Power BI, Metabase, Sigma, and Hex integrations also raise the implementation burden before Alkera can credibly own “whole-stack” lineage.

I am neutral, not because the founders are ordinary, but because unusually coherent founders are currently pursuing an unusually imitable position. My one flip condition is verifiable: demonstrate that at least ten production customers retained Alkera for twelve months while expanding paid usage, specifically because accumulated lineage and verified organizational knowledge produced materially higher reliability than PromptQL, Altimate, and general coding agents. That would convert the central mechanism from commodity software into a narrow monopoly. Until then, this is a strong team searching inside a crowded category for the secret that would justify the company.
