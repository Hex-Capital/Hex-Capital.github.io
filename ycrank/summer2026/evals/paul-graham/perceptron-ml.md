# Perceptron ML -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The first question is not whether lawyers need better AI. It is what broke at a law firm, what the founders did next, and how quickly the fix changed the workflow. The dossier never answers this. Three systems are described as deployed, but there is no named blocker, dated workaround, or observable result. Michael and Peyton Marcotte may be resourceful, but their credentials and finished case-study pages do not prove it. I have to leave the controlling founder test at its missing-evidence default.

The strongest evidence is technical. Michael previously built production agents for hardware debugging and formal verification at NVIDIA, and Perceptron says it has deployed timekeeping, monitoring, and research systems inside firms. Source-gating generated claims against primary authorities is also a real architectural choice, not merely an AI wrapper. But the company supplies no citation-error benchmark, customer-controlled test, or independently measured threshold. “Every fact is checked” is still a claim about a machine, not a measurement from one.

Law firms form a narrow user niche, and three deployments are more meaningful than a waitlist. But the firms are anonymous, and the dossier gives no revenue, repeated usage, retention, referrals, or evidence that lawyers tolerated a crude product because they urgently needed it. The adoption gap—69% personal generative-AI use versus 34% firm-level legal-AI adoption—could create a valuable implementation schlep. It could equally mean Perceptron spends months selling and customizing systems that users do not repeatedly use.

The July-to-August change from a cross-industry event engine to “AI-native” law firms is potentially evidence of running-back behavior. The missing part is what the founders learned. There is no rejected hypothesis, customer observation, or indication that prior traction carried into the legal product. Neither founder has documented legal-practice experience, so there is also no evidence that this idea grew from a problem they encountered themselves. A fast change of noun without the causal story is not yet adaptability; it may simply be category selection.

The bull case stings. The founders are brothers, Michael has unusually relevant technical experience, and bespoke private deployments may be the manual work through which they learn what repeatable legal infrastructure should become. Harvey’s estimated $195 million ARR and more than 25,000 customer-created agents show that the market is real. But they also show that customization is not an unoccupied wedge. Perceptron has disclosed neither the concrete proof spike nor unmistakable user pull that could override missing founder evidence. None of the documented exceptions applies without an independently validated result, a striking working prototype, or user behavior too strong to ignore.

I would pass on investing my own money today. The likely failure mode is not that the software does nothing; it is that a technically capable team becomes a small legal-AI implementation consultancy while larger platforms absorb the reusable product surface. One verifiable condition would flip me: a named law firm documents a specific failed workflow, a founder-built fix shipped within two weeks, eight consecutive weeks of lawyer use, and an independently audited citation-error rate. That single case would reveal the founders, the machine, and the users at once.
