# RealPact -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Ranvir Deshmukh’s prior CourseMe exit and RealPact’s lack of real-estate operating history produce a recognizable signature: RealPact is an execution-capable founder bet trapped inside my least attractive sleeve, consensus vertical-AI SaaS. RealPact has paying New Hampshire brokerages and a Four Seasons Sotheby’s pilot, so the founders can clearly ship and sell; the problem is that the asset being financed remains a standard software cap table with no visible price dislocation, owned distribution, or illegible structure that gives me an underwriting edge.

Drop.io is the high rung for asymmetric conviction: I kept turning the crank on file sharing against free incumbents because the product expressed a belief formed before the financing story. RealPact sits below that rung but above the generic opportunist: Deshmukh and Erik Peterson previously built CourseMe to 4,000 monthly users and roughly 90% Dartmouth penetration, then moved from SignPact’s $9.99 e-signatures into RealPact’s brokerage workflow. That sequence shows operating persistence, but RealPact lacks the domain scar tissue or opinionated public writing that would prove the founders arrived at property-record automation through missionary conviction rather than discovering a sellable AI wedge.

Fin’s 2019 workplace-analytics pivot exposes the low-rung failure mechanism here: an AI assistant becomes ordinary workflow software when the Wizard-of-Oz learning never hardens into a proprietary asset. RealPact retrieves fragmented government records, but RealPact reports no exclusive data, retention advantage, or defensibility while SkySlope already serves more than 900,000 professionals with AI-assisted transactions, compliance, forms, voice, and back-office tools. Software is worthless—fine, provocative overstatement—but vertical workflow software without a structurally scarce input is especially easy to strand in the SaaS factory.

The $500,000 YC financing gives RealPact eighteen months to turn a New Hampshire wedge into national evidence, yet RealPact’s value-seed mechanics deteriorate as soon as that story works. Each Sotheby’s-style pilot, AI-agent label, and enterprise contract makes the next SAFE more legible and therefore more consensus-priced, while county-by-county integrations and unfinished SOC 2 work consume the runway before the curve resolves. No round price, competing investors, or visible institutional passes are disclosed, so I apply the absent-evidence default rather than inventing a bargain.

Paying New Hampshire brokerages could make RealPact a capital-efficient implementation business, but RealPact would flip into an Invest structure only if those deployments fund expansion without repeated equity tacks—or if the financing were tied to transaction revenue rather than another generic SaaS SAFE. Today the jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction maintenance burden, legally consequential contract population, and firm-wide sales motion imply expensive compliance and integration work before default-alive economics. Show me positive contribution margin from three states and a financing instrument that shares cash flow instead of pretending every brokerage workflow deserves a $1 billion software outcome, and the structure score changes materially.

John Fish and Bookshelved are the clean single-anchor parallel for founder-owned distribution: the audience existed before the financed asset and reduced acquisition risk from day one. RealPact has useful reach—Deshmukh has roughly 6,000 LinkedIn followers, Peterson roughly 1,000, and RealPact 1,039—but RealPact has not shown that those followers constitute a brokerage community, customer pipeline, or identity-bound brand. CourseMe’s adoption by approximately 90% of Dartmouth is evidence that these founders understand concentrated distribution; it is not evidence that distribution crossed with them into real estate.

SkySlope’s existing scale makes my 36-month forecast concrete: RealPact reaches roughly $1 million to $3 million ARR in regional brokerage deployments, then sells to SkySlope or another transaction-management incumbent rather than financing an independent national platform. RealPact’s strongest capability appears to be packaging fragmented property records into a usable workflow, while RealPact’s weakest position is owning any scarce data or distribution that prevents an incumbent from buying the product instead of losing the market to it. That is a respectable outcome, but at an undisclosed YC follow-on price it is not automatically a venture-shaped angel return.

Four Seasons Sotheby’s is the bull case that stings: RealPact already has a firm-wide pilot, paying customers, a prior founder exit, and a manual geographic integration burden that could become the moat once RealPact accumulates jurisdiction-specific corrections faster than SkySlope. Robinhood is the Known Exception to my consensus aversion—an early enough entry can absorb later heat—and CourseMe suggests these founders know how to dominate a bounded network before expanding. I still pass because no dossier evidence shows proprietary correction data, repeat usage, or capital-efficient deployment economics; verified renewal of the Sotheby’s pilot plus paying multi-state expansion with gross margins above 70% would falsify my acquisition forecast and flip the verdict.
