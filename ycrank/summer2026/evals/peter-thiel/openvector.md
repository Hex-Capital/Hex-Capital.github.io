# OpenVector -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

OpenVector’s proposed secret is that a vision system can turn ordinary cameras into reliable autonomous workers by examining roughly 3% of the available pixels. The claim is unusually concrete: the founder-authored foveated-vision research reports 5–10× lower bandwidth and received a CVPR 2025 Spotlight. But the falsification test is not bandwidth. It is whether this compressed representation can monitor long-duration physical processes and trigger consequential actions without accumulating false positives. The dossier establishes an efficient vision technique; it does not yet establish the larger secret that physical work can be automated reliably through it.

The more immediate problem is monopoly. OpenVector simultaneously advertises gyms, car washes, offices, warehouses, restaurants, retail, manufacturing, logistics, and security. That is not a beachhead; it is a list of markets. Spot AI already supports existing cameras and video agents, while Ambient.ai and Voxel occupy security and industrial safety. The foveated system may be proprietary technology, but no deployment data shows that its bandwidth advantage creates materially better accuracy, cost, or retention in one bounded segment. A technical distinction without a market it can dominate remains a feature inside a competitive category.

The founders are better than the market positioning. Andrey Gizdov has genuine computer-vision depth across Harvard, MIT, and Weizmann; Vishal Urlam has embedded-systems and camera-infrastructure experience from CMU and Tata. They also built together after meeting at MIT Grand Hack in 2025. Yet one year of collaboration is not long alignment, and the dossier provides no cap-table, salary, or founder-ownership evidence. There is no affirmative sign of part-time work or outsourced engineering, so the founding structure is incomplete rather than adverse. Founder or professional manager? The evidence leans founder, but has not proved durable economic exposure.

The $449 monthly station and 20-minute live-stream demonstration make distribution more definite than the usual “enterprise sales” slogan. They still leave the decisive economics unanswered: no named buyer, conversion rate, sales cycle, installation burden, gross margin, deployment count, or nonrefundable order exists publicly. At that price, demo-led selling and hardware support can become uneconomic quickly. The refundable $100 reservation tests curiosity, not willingness to absorb autonomous-action risk. One product and a $37.84 billion market permit power-law upside in theory, but without a repeatable customer unit there is no arithmetic connecting this price to a multibillion-dollar company.

The strongest bull case is the known exception for a verified technical event preceding a clear monopoly—the OpenAI pattern. OpenVector has the beginnings of that argument: published research, edge deployment, aligned technical founders, and a product that acts rather than merely classifies. The material difference is that the CVPR result verifies sampling efficiency, not the operational breakthrough customers purchase. Nor does the company yet match the unstated pattern of controlling a complete AI production stack or emerging from a concentrated operator network. Its red-flag stack—long-horizon reliability, consequential false actions, pre-order hardware, and an unproven channel—still requires several components to synchronize, although compatibility with existing cameras prevents this from becoming a fatal hardware-coordination case.

My 36-month base case is that OpenVector narrows into industrial safety or physical-security monitoring and remains below $3 million ARR rather than becoming a horizontal autonomous-worker platform. I would investigate, not invest today. The single condition that flips the decision is ten named customers in one vertical paying at least $449 per month for six months, accompanied by an independently auditable false-action rate below one per 1,000 monitored events. That would simultaneously convert the secret from research into operating truth and the market from horizontal competition into a plausible beachhead.
