# Silica -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Silica has a plausible component business and an undocumented investment proposition. Proposed post-money: unknown. Competing term sheets: unknown. Whether my money supplies a genuinely scarce check: unknown. YC, Founders Inc., and UP.Partners backing establishes that investors have participated; it establishes neither an auction nor a bargain. I would pass on the evidence presented. That is an underwriting decision, not a claim that a pre-seed hardware company should already have revenue.

The interesting hypothesis is concrete: measuring torque where the actuator meets the load could give robot developers better physical-interaction data and reduce software workarounds. But HEBI and Sensodrive already sell torque-sensing joints. Silica therefore needs to prove an economically meaningful improvement in sensitivity, cost, or integration. Its specifications remain design targets. December 2026 prototypes and January 2027 production are dated deliverables, but neither tells me what measurable result this round purchases or why achieving it dramatically changes the business’s value. A production date alone is not a venture moment.

Enrico Milletti is the strongest reason to keep paying attention. Developing materials and manufacturing processes at Apple, together with founding-engineer experience at Artefact, supplies relevant operating substance beyond academic credentials. This fits my unstated preference for founders who can translate between difficult physical systems and a new customer requirement. The limitation is specific: the dossier documents manufacturing experience, but not deployments into robot developers’ workflows or operating access on that side of the bridge. Manufacturing yield and calibration knowledge could eventually become proprietary assets; today they are possibilities.

The bull case is that Silica makes force-aware robotics affordable enough to unlock purchases that existing suppliers cannot serve economically. That would put value in accumulated manufacturing knowledge rather than a temporarily superior AI model. It is a serious counterargument. However, an open preorder page establishes a purchasing channel, not an observed purchase, and “affordable” has no published price or comparative benchmark behind it. There is also no margin, burn, or working-capital evidence from which to delete the next financing. I assign the specified defaults to those unknowns. The relevant exception precedents require demonstrated conversion, customer economics, or an explicit alternative financing structure; none is documented here. Nor does this dossier establish an AI-wrapper, financing-pressure, or founder-control red flag.

My money would need to reveal whether Silica can repeatedly manufacture a differentiated joint that customers will pay for. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: a robot developer places a paid repeat order after qualifying delivered Q Series units against a named incumbent on a documented performance-and-price requirement. That would turn the strongest story into evidence of customer action. It would still leave the round price to underwrite. Until then, Silica offers relevant manufacturing experience without proof that its proposed advantage causes a purchase.
