# OpenVector -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Andrey Gizdov’s CVPR 2025 research and OpenVector’s $449-per-month Station reveal OpenVector’s signature: consequence-rich physical automation with a capital profile too ordinary for the ambition. If every passive camera becomes an active operator, human observation ceases to be the control layer for restaurants, warehouses, gyms, and offices. That is potentially important. Yet OpenVector currently sells a modest appliance and integrations, not the foundational infrastructure required to break that economic mechanism.

**OpenAI** (2019) is the high rung on consequence because machine intelligence threatened to reorganize all knowledge labor before revenue or moat were legible. OpenVector shares the load-bearing mechanism: OpenVector wants ubiquitous sensors to initiate real-world actions rather than merely record reality, potentially removing human monitoring from millions of locations. OpenVector diverges because the dossier identifies a $71.30 billion video-analytics market and examples such as POS discounts and car-wash operations, not a named labor pool or incumbent regime that disappears. Consequence over probability applies only after consequence is demonstrated.

**DoorDash** (2013) is the low rung on required capital: capital-light execution did not need a scientific-scale Khosla check, and only extraordinary distribution and ambition justified the exception. OpenVector exhibits the same mismatch without DoorDash’s evidence of a compounding network. OpenVector asks for a refundable $100 reservation, charges $6,499 upfront, and discloses no roadmap requiring more than $50 million before a commercial milestone. I am over-resourced for an OpenVector deployment plan that currently resembles edge hardware plus SaaS.

A 92% confidence threshold before OpenVector applies a POS discount exposes the technical compounding mechanism over the next 18 months. If Gizdov’s predictive coding, continuous-time dynamics, and attention-driven spatial sampling produce materially lower latency and error rates on customer video, OpenVector can accumulate corrections and deployment-specific data that commodity vision agents cannot reproduce. If OpenVector is instead a general vision model connected to webhooks, every incorrect action raises supervision costs while Spot AI, Coram AI, and Ambient.ai copy the workflow. The science signal is real; proof that the science powers the product is not.

Andrey Gizdov withdrew from Harvard after research at Harvard, MIT, and the Weizmann Institute, but OpenVector has not documented which expert consensus Gizdov believes is wrong. OpenVector would move into my top founder-defiance tier if Gizdov demonstrated that dense frame-by-frame vision is an obsolete architecture and published a reproducible benchmark showing a fivefold compute reduction without sacrificing action accuracy. A dropout label is not contrarianism. Experts often leave institutions to commercialize consensus; the important act is invalidating the consensus.

**Slash** (2024) earned its exception through a documented pivot that exposed unusually high learning rate. OpenVector’s movement from the Foveal Vision message of compute and bandwidth reduction to OpenVector’s camera-to-action automation may be an intelligent thesis update, but the dossier provides no stated reasoning or “we were wrong” moment. OpenVector’s MIT Grand Hack win and continued founder collaboration show execution continuity, not yet adaptive learning. Product-positioning divergence without a causal account can represent learning—or unresolved strategy.

The $449 monthly Station price leads to a falsifiable prediction: by July 2029, OpenVector will have narrowed from a universal active-operator platform into car-wash or restaurant workflow automation and reached roughly $3 million to $8 million in ARR, without becoming foundational vision infrastructure. OpenVector’s mechanism favors that outcome because existing-camera compatibility and POS or DRB SiteWatch integrations can sell quickly, while autonomous-action errors force OpenVector to constrain workflows to one repeatable vertical. A vertical business could be good. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself” does not mean every good vertical business deserves my money.

Spot AI’s $93 million funding and Gizdov’s CVPR publication form the strongest bull case against that forecast: OpenVector could use bio-inspired edge vision to leapfrog richly funded incumbents, and DoorDash proves that a capital-light YC company can become a defining platform without science-scale burn. OpenVector’s undisclosed design partners and planned fall-2026 Station deliveries provide the beginnings of that path. The evidence that wins today is harsher: OpenVector discloses no proprietary dataset, no deployment scale, no measured advantage over Ambient.ai, and no proof that predictive coding drives production decisions. I would flip to Invest if OpenVector verifies ten named multi-site customers and demonstrates at least a fivefold compute-cost advantage at equal or better action-error rates against Spot AI-class systems.
