# Cova -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Cova is licensed and live in Michigan, which makes Cova a healthcare-rail operator with no owned distribution—the highest/lowest pairing that defines the bet. Cova fits my underwritten healthcare-services sleeve because Jordan Ibe and Ansh Tandon abandoned selling software to agencies and became the agency, but 227 LinkedIn followers and no demonstrated caregiver community leave Cova renting every relationship it needs. Healthcare SaaS is worthless; more precisely, the interesting asset is the regulated operation underneath the software, and Cova has begun building that asset.

**Ro** is the high rung on category fit: regulatory complexity across prescription rails made an expensive healthcare operation venture-shaped because the licensing schleps became the moat. Cova shares the willingness to internalize the ugly work—Michigan provider licensure, Home Help enrollment, electronic-visit-verification compliance, and Medicaid billing—but Cova diverges where the analogy becomes load-bearing. Ro proved demand across a national payer surface; Cova discloses no patients, revenue, case volume, or measured cost advantage, while Michigan explicitly lets caregivers enroll directly. Cova sits below Ro because it has entered the right kind of swamp without showing that crossing it creates scarce land.

**Fin** is the low rung on owned community: a clever Wizard-of-Oz operation can still spend years searching for distribution when users attach to the service rather than a founder or community. That pattern is present at Cova. Jordan Ibe has 2,000 LinkedIn followers and Ansh Tandon has 3,000, but Cova has only 227 company followers, no identified newsletter, channel, caregiver network, or opinionated public writing, and no evidence that those personal graphs convert into households. Cova is not a creator disguised as a startup; Cova is a local care agency that must acquire both caregivers and patients one relationship at a time.

Cova’s dossier gives no round price, named investors, visible tier-one passes, or unusual financing terms—no evidence, so I apply the absent-evidence default. The counterfactual that changes the round score is straightforward: Cova would need a clearly sub-batch-median valuation or a structure tied to reimbursement cash flows rather than a generic YC SAFE. Without that, “AI-Native Home Care Agency” reads dangerously close to the hot vertical-AI deck, even though Cova’s provider pivot is more contrarian than another agent shell. A good business at the wrong SAFE price is still a bad investment.

Federal rules directing at least 80% of certain Medicaid HCBS payments to direct-care workers make Cova’s capital structure a regatta, not a software sprint. Over the next 18 months, Cova must compound small administrative savings across intake, eligibility, scheduling, EVV, and billing while recruiting enough caregivers to cover fixed compliance costs; if AI removes labor from the remaining 20%, each additional case improves the operation. If caregiver churn, denied claims, or telephone support absorb those savings, Cova becomes a thin-margin agency requiring repeated equity tacks. Cova is potentially bootstrappable and venture-acceleratable, but the dossier supplies no agency margin or default-alive plan proving which one it is.

Jordan Ibe’s VA medical-center experience and the founders’ year building home-care AI place Cova closer to **Drop.io** on stubborn execution than a consultancy-generated idea. Drop.io’s conviction was visible in years of building against free incumbents; Cova’s conviction is visible in a harder move—taking provider, caregiver, and payer responsibility instead of merely selling software—but not yet in public category writing or a three-year obsession. Cova gets real credit for choosing Type 2 fun, while one year of adjacent work and a July 2026 pivot remain insufficient evidence of missionary temperament through multiple reimbursement cycles.

Michigan’s direct-enrollment option and Cova’s absence of disclosed case volume lead to a falsifiable 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Cova will remain primarily a Michigan home-care operator generating $3 million–$8 million in annualized gross reimbursements, rather than becoming a national software platform or completing a conventional venture-scale Series A. Cova can reach that range through licensure, caregiver recruitment, and administrative automation, but its weakest asset—owned distribution—will constrain geographic replication before its healthcare-rail knowledge becomes software-like leverage.

**Ro** is the strongest objection to that forecast: Cova’s provider pivot, Michigan license, mixed Medicaid/LTCI/private-pay model, and AI-controlled overhead could make regulatory complexity itself the venture moment, just as Ro’s capital intensity became a moat rather than a defect. The objection stings because Cova has already taken the operational step most AI founders avoid. It does not yet win because Michigan permits direct caregiver enrollment, the 80% payment rule compresses administrative economics, and Cova reports neither repeat usage nor a measured cost advantage. I would flip to Invest if Cova verifies at least 15% contribution margin after caregiver compensation and claims losses while reaching 1,000 active households primarily through organic caregiver or family referrals.
