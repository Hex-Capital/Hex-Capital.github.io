# Plover Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine an engineer writing the rules under which software may change, then leaving agents to make those changes overnight. Plover Labs has a plausible path into that world: capture company conventions, turn observed failures into regression cases, and make passing those checks sufficient for release. But the little detail I keep coming back to is that Fieldguide still requires a person at the end. Plover has reached the doorstep of its promised future. The customer has not yet handed it the keys.

Why does that person still need to be there? Because checking what we remembered to specify is different from recognizing what we forgot. Fieldguide’s August 2026 evaluation produced merge-ready results on 37 of 40 historical tasks, but excluded production review and some browser and document checks. That is useful technical evidence, with a clearly bounded meaning. The decisive transition is whether Plover can expand those boundaries without transferring an unreasonable burden to whoever writes the specifications. Its future mechanism is concrete enough to interest me; autonomous shipping remains unproven.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that customers are already doing something expensive. Envoy and Fieldguide reportedly have five-figure monthly contracts, and Fieldguide devoted engineering effort to co-development and a production quarter. Its customer-authored account reports 40% greater total merged-PR throughput. I would use that observation carefully: Fieldguide calls it non-causal, and it does not establish the separate company-reported claim of tripled PRs per engineer. Still, money, integration work, and sustained production use are substantial pre-seed signals. Plover does not need another impressive demo to establish that somebody cares.

What interests me beneath the coding-agent excitement is the accumulation of Blueprints, regression cases, and environment definitions. This fits my pattern of finding consequential infrastructure inside apparently mundane workflow repair. Those assets could become the machinery through which an enterprise permits many different agents to act. But customer-specific configuration is not automatically a bottleneck Plover controls. Sonar already offers deterministic semantic and data-flow checks, while Harness is combining delivery infrastructure with acquired Augment technology. The uncomfortable counterfactual is that Plover teaches enterprises how to specify their requirements, then their existing platforms absorb the execution. The dossier does not establish how difficult those assets would be to reproduce or migrate.

Edward Ahn brings relevant preparation: safe-reinforcement-learning research at Carnegie Mellon, AI-agent work for Apple Vision Pro, and experience founding InPerson. BrailleWare adds a concrete building artifact. That earns more confidence than a résumé assembled around this month’s fashionable category, but it does not establish a dated, sustained obsession with software verification or show how he recruited this team. The system being repaired is also identifiable—engineers absorb the review burden created by agent output—but the dossier names no artificial restriction or quantified rent. Returning engineering attention matters; eliminating human approval only returns meaningful control if engineers can trust and inspect the replacement.

I am **Neutral** with my own money today. My willingness to back an early prototype before revenue does not resolve this particular gap: Plover already has commercial proof, while the consequential technical claim remains ahead of its demonstrated deployment. A CEO is identified, and the dossier establishes no governance violation, severe privacy problem, or dependence on one closed model supplier; those red flags should not be invented. My expectation is a useful verification business that retains human approval longer than its headline suggests. One result would flip me: a customer-verified, prospective 90-day deployment in which Plover ships a defined class of material production changes without human approval, with a change-failure rate no worse than that customer’s human-reviewed baseline.
