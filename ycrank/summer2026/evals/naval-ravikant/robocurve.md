# Robocurve -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have already touched both sides of the exact problem. Jay Chooi contributed to Inspect Evals at the UK AI Security Institute; Aris Zhu worked at Amazon Robotics, Amazon AGI Labs, and Yondu Robotics. Together they shipped roughly 400 commits across a cross-embodiment framework and completed a frontier-model pilot on real hardware. That is specific knowledge acquired through action, not résumé adjacency. The strongest asset today is the team’s ability to connect model evaluation with physical execution.

But capability is not yet leverage. Inspect Robots is MIT-licensed, laboratories can reproduce evaluation pipelines internally, and Positronic, Instance, and ReSim already attack overlapping workflows. Thirteen repositories, 134 GitHub stars, 19 forks, and multiple hardware adapters show execution and early distribution; they do not yet show an asset that improves whenever a competitor copies the code. The inferred cross-model, cross-robot dataset could become that asset, but one undisclosed pilot is the beginning of a dataset, not a moat.

The bull case is that Robocurve becomes neutral measurement infrastructure for physical AI. Open source is permissionless distribution, robotics evaluation is a growing infrastructure bottleneck, and every contributed benchmark or adapter could broaden the system’s usefulness. This matches two revealed patterns I care about: outsiders contributing before institutional approval, and value migrating from pure software into the physical world. But the loop remains hypothetical. Forks do not prove that users are producing reusable benchmarks, routing customers to Robocurve, or making later evaluations materially better.

The market may be enormous while this company’s capture layer remains narrow. Cross-embodiment evaluation could expand from frontier labs into manufacturers, insurers, enterprise buyers, and robot operators, but the dossier establishes neither that path nor the associated spend. Reproducibility also requires hardware, resets, controlled environments, and domain labor. Physical work can create defensibility, or it can turn software margins into a testing consultancy. Without pricing, completed paid engagements, or sound unit economics, I cannot tell which business Robocurve is building.

The early-prototype exception does not resolve this. A capital-efficient team can deserve financing before revenue or a network appears, but only when the product language is unusually singular. Here, three overlapping evaluation companies appeared before Robocurve established ownership of the category. There is no disclosed integrity problem, artificial deadline, founder instability, or other red-flag veto; the problem is structural uncertainty, not adverse founder evidence.

My 36-month base case is that Robocurve pivots toward operated certification and evaluation services for robotics developers, reaching roughly $1–3 million in ARR but remaining labor- and hardware-intensive rather than becoming the default benchmark network. I am neutral today. The single flip condition is verifiable: at least ten paying robotics organizations repeatedly submit models or robots to a shared benchmark system, with contributed evaluations producing proprietary longitudinal data and measurable reuse by other customers. Then the service becomes a compounding network, and I would invest.
