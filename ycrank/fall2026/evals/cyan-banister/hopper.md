# Hopper -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

A voice agent says “Let me check that for you” because several models are still waiting on one another. Why should the person on the telephone have to accommodate the plumbing? Hopper is working on something real here. But move the clock to 2032: conversation with software should feel ordinary, and developers should be able to change the machinery underneath without rebuilding the conversation. That is a future I can imagine. The dossier does not yet establish which intervening changes Hopper will own, or why its current optimizations lead to that position.

The founders give me more reason to pay attention than the market label does. Pavan Katta led post-training at Salient; Jashwanth Pedapudi worked on inference kernels at Meta and payments and streaming infrastructure at Uber. Those are useful places to discover that a seemingly simple interaction conceals a nasty operating problem. Their thirteen-year relationship also predates this company. But relevant employment is only part of my founder test. The dossier provides little dated evidence of independently pursued experiments before Hopper, or of these founders recruiting others into an initially improbable belief. I see technical preparation; I cannot yet see the depth of obsession.

Hopper’s strongest argument against my hesitation is that it has shipped plumbing people can actually connect. Its Dograh integration merged on October 1, 2026. The reusable HTTP/2 client addresses a concrete source of overhead, and the company reports 80ms time to first token for Gemma 4 and 150ms time to first audio for Qwen 3 TTS. This fits my pattern of looking underneath the visible application for an enabling business. Yet Fireworks already combines specialization with serving, and Hopper’s OpenAI-compatible interface makes substitution straightforward. The team controls implementation work, but the dossier does not demonstrate control of a scarce dependency.

Why do developers pay today’s speech vendors? Some of that payment buys capabilities and operational reliability, rather than an artificial restriction Hopper can remove. Open-weight deployment could give builders more control over their models, but the claimed cost advantage lacks a comparable workload-level accounting. The advertised $0.30 per million input tokens is a price, not evidence of customer savings or Hopper’s margins. Likewise, prepaid credits establish a billing mechanism, not paying usage. The merged integration represents real engineering effort; it does not establish that customers repeatedly entrust production calls to Hopper.

I would not reject this simply because revenue is undisclosed. My exceptions allow a working prototype and unusually deep founder conviction to precede commercial proof. Hopper has enough technical substance to make that counterargument sting. Its separation of logging permission from prior written training consent also addresses an important privacy concern, although it means customer calls do not automatically become a shared training asset. The dossier identifies CEO and CTO responsibilities and supplies no affirmative evidence of a governance shell game or dependence on one closed model supplier. Those red flags do not decide this pass. The missing link is between competent infrastructure and a future that specifically needs this company.

I would pass with my own money today. My flip condition is one documented 90-day production cohort of three unrelated voice-agent customers that renew after demonstrating lower total cost at equivalent conversational quality and latency under load. That would show Hopper becoming an operating dependency customers choose to keep. Today, the strongest evidence supports founders who understand the machinery; it does not yet show the machinery changing anyone’s behavior enough to earn that dependence.
