# OpenRelay -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have already lived inside both halves of this problem. Prashant Patel built Amazon Bedrock’s Custom Model Import into a multi-million-dollar ARR product, then supported 10,000-plus-GPU clusters and 10 million-plus monthly serverless executions at Voltage Park. Jaden Wang built a half-megawatt data center at 20 and later worked on HPC deployment and virtualization at TensorDock and Voltage Park. This is specific knowledge acquired through operation, not credentials arranged around a fashionable market.

The product turns fragmented accelerator capacity into a single metered endpoint. OpenRelay is already processing more than 100 billion tokens weekly across 22 physical locations, four continents, and 11 accelerator SKUs. Those figures demonstrate technical execution: heterogeneous hardware is being benchmarked, routed, and served in production. The reported B300 deployment reducing infrastructure costs by more than 25% also establishes customer value. A routing layer that works only in a diagram is software; one surviving real hardware, latency, and failure conditions is infrastructure.

The unresolved question is whether that infrastructure compounds. OpenRelay’s providers contribute capacity, and greater supply density should improve availability and routing for customers. This resembles the permissionless systems I prefer, but the dossier gives no provider count, retention data, routing-performance history, or evidence that its benchmarks are exclusive. RunPod, Vast.ai, Together AI, or a hyperscaler could copy the interface and subsidize the service. OpenAI compatibility accelerates adoption while reducing switching costs. If the code were copied today, operating experience would remain; it is not yet clear that an irreproducible asset would.

The wedge has room to maneuver. Hosted inference leads naturally into dedicated accelerators, capacity aggregation, benchmarking, failover, and eventually the transaction layer for heterogeneous compute. The underlying GPU-as-a-Service market was estimated at $8.21 billion in 2025 and projected to reach $26.62 billion by 2030. This is more than an unsupported parent-market citation because OpenRelay already spans hosted inference, dedicated GPU pricing, and provider participation. The limitation is that the company has not yet shown which adjacent buyer or workflow it can own against competitors with hundreds of millions or billions of dollars in capital.

Value capture is direct: customers prepay for tokens or pay per GPU minute, while providers receive a share of served usage. The 100-billion-token weekly workload and reported customer savings make the pricing more credible than an untested price sheet, although neither ARR nor gross margin is disclosed. Security and reliability could constrain that capture: SOC 2 Type II is unfinished, third-party hardware handles customer workloads, and dedicated VMs do not yet receive the automatic migration promised for hosted inference.

The bear case is sharp. This becomes a low-margin broker between interchangeable compute suppliers and customers who can switch endpoints, while better-capitalized clouds copy the routing features and reserve the best capacity for themselves. The bull case wins only if deployments generate proprietary reliability and performance knowledge faster than competitors can reproduce it. I would invest, but as an option on that compounding infrastructure—not merely on the current software. The single flip condition is verifiable: if, within 12 months, OpenRelay cannot show that repeat customers receive measurably better cost-adjusted reliability as provider density and benchmark history grow, I would not follow on.
