# Streamline Systems -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Streamline Systems has reportedly made hardware fly without satellite positioning. That is the most interesting fact in the dossier. The missing fact is what happened when it failed. Did Gabriel Poraj discover an unexpected source of drift, change the system, and get it flying again three days later? We have no such sequence. I give the founders credit for the artifact, but cannot use it twice to manufacture evidence of resourcefulness. Poraj’s software experience and engineering studies establish relevant preparation; Charlie Jessett’s RAF reserve affiliation establishes military exposure. Neither establishes how they behave when blocked.

The product is concrete enough to take seriously. Streamline reports autonomous flight, GPS handoff, and integration with ArduPilot, INAV, and PX4 ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/streamline-systems)). Those integrations could make experimentation much easier for drone operators. But the distinction between a demonstration and useful navigation is measured in accuracy, drift, flight duration, and operating conditions. None is disclosed. The reported flights earn intermediate technical credit. They do not yet establish the numerical proof that makes me comfortable backing unfamiliar industrial technology. Undisclosed sensing also prevents me from judging whether Streamline has discovered something difficult or assembled something competitors can reproduce.

The strongest argument for investing is that this may already be further along than its public evidence suggests. Two founders have flying hardware, signed LOIs, and scheduled government trials. Customers facing jamming might tolerate a crude module if it keeps an otherwise useless aircraft working. That would be a narrow well of real need. But the LOIs have no disclosed counterparties, quantities, or values, and scheduled trials tell us nothing about repeat use ([recruitment listing](https://uk.linkedin.com/jobs/view/founding-electrical-engineer-at-streamline-systems-incorporated-4467858822)). I give those commitments some demand credit. I cannot yet tell whether operators are pulling the product into missions or agreeing to investigate it.

Hardening and manufacturing are exactly the sort of ugly work that can conceal an opportunity. Streamline’s recruitment material explicitly says that work remains. What is absent is the founders’ original collision with this problem, or a documented product change caused by field evidence. Those dimensions receive their missing-evidence defaults. Nor do I see an affirmative red flag: dropping out alone proves little, hiring engineers is reasonable, and an undisclosed cofounder history does not mean a hastily assembled relationship. Still, the unstated pattern I look for in difficult technical bets—a concrete proof spike—has not been established. Existing suppliers such as ANELLO and Advanced Navigation make mission-level performance more consequential than the category label.

I would pass on the present evidence. The quoted $154.38 million market estimate neither explains the eventual upside nor rules it out; module pricing, margins, and a path beyond the initial wedge remain unknown. More decisively, Streamline misses my founder-action gate, and the exception test does not rescue it: the reported prototype lacks independently validated performance or unmistakable user pull strong enough to substitute. My one flip condition is a documented deployment with a named operator in which the founders fix a specific navigation failure within days, demonstrate the correction against recorded mission requirements, and earn repeat paid use. That single sequence would connect founder behavior, technical capability, and need.
