# Sintropix -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence here is that the founders did the bookkeeping. Sintropix moved from manual accounting in April 2026 to its own ERP by September. That is specific knowledge acquired through work: discovering where transactions break, what accountants actually reconcile, and which exceptions resist automation. Alfonso’s Toku title matters less than this sequence. The reported ten clients give that learning contact with reality. The earlier logistics concept does not establish chronic abandonment; the subsequent accounting work establishes a concrete direction.

The investment question is whether that knowledge becomes an asset a competitor cannot reproduce. Sintropix claims more than 100 integrations, but the dossier establishes neither exclusive access nor accumulated workflow dependence. Campfire already advertises matching, coding, and intercompany reconciliation. Copying the interface would not copy everything these founders have learned, but founder learning and company defensibility are separate assets. I give the former substantial credit. The latter remains unproven.

The strongest argument against waiting is the reported improvement in customer operations: closing from day 20 to day 1, and onboarding from 30 days to 24 hours. These are company claims, with the onboarding account retrieved through a LinkedIn snippet, but they describe substantial value if repeatable. Faster migration could also weaken the very replacement friction an accounting vendor hopes to accumulate. My exception for backing an unusual working prototype before network effects appear deserves consideration here. However, the dossier does not establish exceptional capital efficiency or a distinctive product capability that would make that exception decisive.

Sintropix has a sensible expansion path from bookkeeping into the ledger and consolidation layer for companies operating across countries. Banking, invoices, payroll, and expenses converge on the same financial record. That creates adjacent workflows to own. But the cited $675 million LATAM accounting-software market is a broad category estimate, not evidence of the accessible multinational segment or its expansion economics. Ten clients across four countries demonstrate geographic reach; they do not establish that each additional jurisdiction becomes cheaper to serve. Mexico’s services remaining in beta makes that distinction concrete.

Referrals are a small affirmative distribution signal. They do not yet show that one customer improves another customer’s accounting or creates reusable supply. Sintropix therefore only partially matches my preference for converting fragmented activity into systems that compound without proportional company labor. Its posted starting price of 20 UF monthly identifies a direct way to charge for useful work. It does not establish realized revenue, software margins, or how much accountant labor remains inside that price. There is no documented integrity or fundraising-pressure veto here; the unresolved issue is the economic structure.

I am neutral at this stage. These founders have earned attention through execution, but I cannot yet distinguish a compounding software asset from an increasingly efficient accounting service. My one verifiable flip condition is a deployment study showing that accountant corrections create a proprietary learning asset that materially reduces exception-handling labor for subsequent customers at comparable accuracy. That would connect today’s specific knowledge to tomorrow’s durable advantage.
