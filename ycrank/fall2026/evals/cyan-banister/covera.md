# Covera -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine an independent broker opening a client’s account and finding the submission assembled, competing policies reconciled, and the next action ready for approval. Covera’s path there requires reliable execution across carriers, portable account context, and permission to act on sensitive records. Its present product tackles pieces of that journey. But the dossier does not establish which transition gives Covera a durable position in that future. I can supply the daydream; I cannot count my daydream as company evidence.

The small thing that gets my attention is Hello Risk’s 155 tasks in two weeks. Someone repeatedly handed real work to this product. The October 9 case study reports a 90-second median quote-preparation time and two corrected values, with review before submission. Those corrections are useful evidence: they show where automation meets responsibility. The claimed ten hours saved weekly comes from assumed manual task durations, so I would underwrite the repeated behavior more heavily than the savings calculation. The broader claim of 50-plus agencies adds less without comparable usage detail.

Why must a broker enter the same information into several portals and then update the agency management system separately? Covera identifies a concrete coordination failure: disconnected systems make the agency supply the connecting labor. Returning that time to an independent broker fits my interest in individual economic control. Yet this dossier identifies neither a quantified incumbent rent nor a restriction Covera actually removes. The broker still works through carrier portals, and some integrations cannot write back. This earns credit for repairing an irritating system, with a ceiling on how much structural liberation I can infer.

Venet and Regerat have more substance than an attractive school-and-employer list. They report meeting at Pretto in 2020, then built Simio together before its 2024 acquisition. That is a dated record of choosing a collaborator, building software, and reaching an exit. Brokerage exposure followed by CRM experience makes Covera a credible next undertaking. It does not establish that this particular submission problem was their operating wound, or document years of independent experimentation around it. I give the team meaningful credit for demonstrated execution without inventing the obsession.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that these founders could turn a mundane workflow repair into the agency’s operating center. Covera fits my pattern of finding opportunity in unglamorous work, and its account context plus portal execution offers more than a conversational interface. Missing revenue would not stop me; the prototype-and-founder-depth exception is relevant here. But FurtherAI already overlaps on submissions and policy comparison, while Fulcrum targets brokerage back-office work. Production connectors are a real technical asset, yet the dossier demonstrates no scarce dependency that makes other products require Covera. Agency-specific mappings could become one; that remains a hypothesis.

I land at **Neutral** with my own money. The working behavior is ahead of the evidence for a distinctive future position. Covera identifies a CEO and CTO, so I see no documented leadership shell game; neither does the dossier establish equal-control trouble or dependence on one closed model supplier. Sensitive email, AMS, and public-record aggregation does leave consent and custody unresolved—human approval addresses submission accuracy, not the whole privacy architecture. My one verifiable flip condition is a six-month agency cohort demonstrating repeated, authorized cross-carrier execution through reusable Covera mappings, with customer references confirming that those mappings make replacement materially difficult. That would connect today’s useful artifact to an enabling asset worth owning.
