# Meteoric -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Meteoric proposes recovering 10–30% of annual generation from existing solar farms without waiting behind 773 GW in the U.S. interconnection queue. That is useful infrastructure leverage, but it is not yet an orders-of-magnitude consequence. The hurricane ambition could be consequential; today it is a slogan attached to a late-2028 target, while the World Meteorological Organization says tropical-cyclone modification has no generally accepted evidentiary basis. I will not capitalize an unsupported second mission to make an incremental first mission look transformative.

The technical evidence is intriguing but thin. A chamber prototype dissipated an artificial cloud by 13%, which is more than a presentation and less than a validated mechanism. Meteoric has not disclosed chamber dimensions, repetitions, energy input, droplet-size change, persistence, or increased power output. Nor has it demonstrated a 5–10x result against Rainmaker, conventional seeding, or simply adding storage and generation. Chemical-free treatment may become a real discontinuity, but “undisclosed mechanism plus one chamber number” is not an auditable advantage.

The strongest bull case is the founders. Mete Karslioglu worked on cloud-reflectivity nozzles and atmospheric instrumentation; Eric Nilsson worked on microfluidics, droplet removal, controls, and atmospheric equipment. Electric drones costing a claimed $30–60 per hour rather than $2,000-plus aircraft could unlock experiments that were previously uneconomic. This is a two-person team with unusually direct preparation for the problem, not generic software founders chasing climate attention. That combination justifies testing the improbable. It does not yet justify buying the company: the dossier shows no belief revised after data, complementary expert recruited, customer committed, or natural-cloud test completed.

The money-versus-risk graph is also wrong or at least undisclosed. The largest kill risk is whether localized droplet alteration can persist in a natural cloud long enough to produce net solar output after accounting for wind, mixing, drone energy, and repeated treatment. Yet the public roadmap jumps from a 13% chamber result toward fleets of tens to hundreds and a storm operation. There is no cheapest decisive field experiment, pass/fail threshold, budget, permit path, or stop condition. Airspace restrictions at 1–5 km add a second existential dependency before the atmospheric effect has been established.

The apparent cost discontinuity is the best part of the case and could still be illusory. A $30–60 drone-hour sounds cheap only until multiplied by tens or hundreds of aircraft, treatment duration, maintenance, utilization, pilots, insurance, and BVLOS compliance. Meteoric publishes none of the required drone-hours per MW or recovered MWh. This may beat conventional aircraft while still costing more than the electricity recovered. The small direct competitor set does not create defensibility; Rainmaker already owns weather-drone, radar, forecasting, and validation infrastructure that could matter more than Meteoric’s current prototype.

I pass today. The known-exception logic—specialist founders pursuing a giant mission before conventional validation—almost applies, but the documented solar consequence is a percentage uplift and the giant hurricane consequence lacks evidence. One result would flip me: an independently instrumented natural-cloud pilot at a named utility-scale solar farm showing at least 10% net generation uplift, with an approved operating path and all-in treatment cost below 25% of the recovered electricity’s value. That would convert an atmospheric speculation into an asymmetric infrastructure option.
