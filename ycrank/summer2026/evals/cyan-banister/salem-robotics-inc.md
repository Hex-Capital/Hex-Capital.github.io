# Salem Robotics Inc -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Radiation-protection technicians are no longer walking every prescribed route with handheld meters; they supervise machines that open doors, manipulate instruments, attach measurements to facility maps, and generate the regulatory record. Salem Robotics has one artifact leaking from that future: its 2025 closed-chain planner reportedly completed ten Spot and UR5 tasks four times faster than the comparison method. What is missing is the path between laboratory capability and routine institutional acceptance—site qualification, regulator-approved safety cases, instrument integrations, and repeatable deployment economics. Those are not small details; they are the staircase.

The founders are the reason I am willing to climb it. Caleb Horan worked on autonomous radiation surveys at Los Alamos after mechanical-engineering and robotics training. Janak Panthi brings six years of quadruped deployment work, Army experience, Los Alamos exposure, and first-author robotics research. They also worked together in UT Austin’s Nuclear and Applied Robotics Laboratory and coauthored radiation-survey research before forming Salem. This looks like independently accumulated operating tuition, not two people who discovered “robotics for nuclear” while assembling a YC application.

The present system is broken in a wonderfully literal way: trained people enter hazardous areas, follow prescribed routes, annotate maps, and later transcribe readings because compliance requires a reviewable record. Salem removes exposure and clerical repetition while returning mission control to the facility operator. But the dossier does not quantify the staffing cost, outage delay, dose reduction, or regulatory rent. Without that denominator, I cannot yet tell whether the customer is buying an indispensable operating system or an impressive demonstration that loses to existing labor and procurement inertia.

The strongest bear case stings. Salem has no publicly named completed deployment, no operator or regulator accepting its theoretical safety guarantees, and only contract-scoping conversations across nuclear and oil and gas. The cited planner is BSD-licensed, while Energy Robotics, ANYbotics, and Gecko have raised $17.9 million, more than $130 million, and $347 million respectively. Worse, the supposedly hardware-agnostic public implementation has ready-made support only for Spot and a Kinova Gen3 arm. A technically excellent team could spend three years performing bespoke integrations while better-capitalized vendors absorb the reusable research.

Still, the product sits beneath the visible robotics wave in a way I like. Salem is not selling another inspection robot; it is attempting to control the manipulation, instrument, spatial-data, and compliance layer across robots. Nuclear is a difficult beachhead whose validated maps, inspection histories, and integrations could become real switching costs, then travel into chemical, oil-and-gas, biological, and space operations. Those assets are prospective today, however. A 14-star repository is technical proof, not a moat, and contract scoping is not costly user behavior.

I would invest a small personal check, with conviction constrained by deployment evidence. The single flip condition is verifiable: within twelve months, Salem must show one paid production deployment at a named nuclear operator where the robot completes recurring prescribed routes and produces records accepted for the operator’s compliance workflow. If that happens, the missing staircase becomes visible. If it does not, I expect the team to remain trapped in validation engagements rather than becoming the enabling layer its research suggests.
