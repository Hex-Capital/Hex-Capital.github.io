# EveryApp -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a small business asking for software, receiving something it can inspect and change, and keeping its data when it stops paying the person who installed it. EveryApp has a small piece of that world running today: applications deployed into the customer’s Cloudflare account, with shared sign-in and permissions. The path requires two further transitions: independently built applications must reliably share that infrastructure, and ordinary businesses must maintain them without becoming systems administrators. The January Gateway, followed by a standalone application and the October platform launch, makes that path tangible. A durable ecosystem remains a hypothesis.

Why does owning software still come with a second job? Someone must manage authentication, credentials, databases and deployment. EveryApp attacks that operating burden while returning the account and application code to the customer. That fits my preference for repairing an irritating system and giving individuals more control. But the dossier does not quantify the subscription expense displaced or the maintenance hours recovered. The announced $10/month managed service is an attractive entry price; it does not yet establish that the customer escapes the old economics.

The awkward part is where that freedom ends. EveryApp standardizes applications on Workers, D1, R2 and KV. Customers control their accounts, but moving the applications elsewhere requires engineering. Cloudron and Coolify demonstrate that centralized application management already exists with other deployment choices. EveryApp controls useful integration code; the dossier does not show a scarce enabling asset or leverage over its infrastructure supplier. Its MIT license supports the ownership promise while allowing another operator to sell the same software. I like that trade for users. I still need to understand why the enduring business belongs to this team.

Ben Senescu has paid some relevant operating tuition: senior engineering work at Klaviyo, an earlier musician-subscription venture, and experience building applications on this infrastructure. Those are substantive credentials for the problem. They do not establish years of independently pursued obsession, complementary recruiting, or unusual commercial command. I would not invent an opportunistic origin from the product changes, either. Returning to the Gateway after building a standalone application could reflect useful learning about what the platform actually needs.

The strongest argument against my hesitation is OpenSEO. Its 25.3K GitHub stars and claimed 3,000-plus entrepreneurs suggest a potential application beachhead from which EveryApp could recruit platform users. That fits my pattern of finding infrastructure beneath a visible application. But those numbers belong to OpenSEO, and neither establishes repeated EveryApp deployments. The platform’s 351 stars and 16 forks, one day after launch, show attention rather than costly dependence. My pre-revenue exceptions require something else to carry conviction—exceptional founder obsession, consequential commitments, or unmistakable user behavior. This dossier supplies working software without that stronger substitute.

I am **Neutral**. EveryApp offers a future I would enjoy inhabiting, but its current architecture transfers substantial dependence to Cloudflare, and backward compatibility is explicitly unpromised. Those are concrete operating concerns. Customer-account deployment is relevant to data custody, although the dossier does not establish the full consent and credential-access architecture; it also gives no affirmative evidence of a leadership shell game or governance conflict. My one verifiable flip condition is a documented 90-day cohort of ten independent businesses repeatedly running at least two EveryApp applications through a platform upgrade, with measured maintenance hours below their prior setup. That would show the shared layer returning time and control in practice.
