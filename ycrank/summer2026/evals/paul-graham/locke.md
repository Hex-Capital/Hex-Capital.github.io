# Locke -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The impressive fact is not that Locke calls itself AI-native. It is that clients bought more than $500K of annualized work during its first six weeks ([YC Launch](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/SSp-locke-the-first-ai-government-affairs-firm)). Early-stage companies evidently need help navigating legislation, procurement, compliance, and proposals badly enough to pay a two-person firm immediately. That is real pull. Clients are still unnamed, however, and the dossier gives no renewal, usage, or pricing evidence, so I cannot tell whether Locke has found a narrow well or merely sold several expensive consulting engagements.

Parth Badhwar has unusually direct exposure to the problem. He worked in the White House, Deloitte’s policy team, a presidential campaign, and GTM at Clipbook before starting Locke ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/locke)). Government affairs is also full of schleps: policymaker meetings, lobbying disclosures, procurement rules, proposal writing, and accuracy-sensitive regulatory briefs. The hybrid model attacks these burdens instead of pretending software alone removes them. But the dossier does not identify the incident that caused Badhwar to build Locke, or a named obstacle he personally overcame after starting it. Revenue shows that he sold something. It does not by itself show what broke, what workaround he shipped, or how rapidly it changed the situation.

The technical case is less developed. Locke says agents analyze legislation, monitor signals through Slack, track stakeholder engagement, and draft proposals and compliance briefs ([Locke Government Affairs](https://locke.inc/government-affairs)). Those workflows appear deployed inside a revenue-producing service, which is more evidence than a diagram. Yet there is no accuracy result, benchmark, proprietary dataset, or customer workflow showing the agents doing something a conventional firm could not. Quorum already serves more than 2,000 organizations with overlapping policy data, AI assistance, stakeholder management, and professional services ([Quorum](https://www.quorum.us/company-news/quorum-receives-strategic-investment-from-enlightenment-capital-to-accelerate-ai-powered-public-affairs-platform/)). Locke has shipped an operating system for its firm, but it has not yet supplied the measured technical proof that this is a software advantage.

The strongest case for investing is that the revenue spike itself resembles the concrete pre-round proof I look for: Badhwar knew the domain, sold personally, and found paying demand almost immediately. The bear case stings more than the ordinary “services do not scale” objection. Locke is already recruiting policy staff as well as technical staff, with policy compensation reaching $200K, suggesting that revenue may grow by adding expensive humans ([YC Jobs](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/locke/jobs)). There is no disclosed retention or gross margin, no proprietary substrate, and no evidence that the product route has changed in response to customer behavior. The public record also leaves the cofounder relationship ambiguous. None of the known exceptions applies: there is strong commercial evidence, but no extraordinary prototype, independent technical validation, or unmistakable user behavior capable of replacing the failed resourcefulness gate.

My 36-month expectation is a useful but services-heavy government-affairs firm at roughly $4M–$8M ARR, with AI improving employee throughput rather than becoming a standalone platform. That could still be a good company. It is not yet enough for me to invest my own money, because the central question is whether software makes each additional client materially cheaper to serve. One condition would flip me: three named clients renewing after twelve months while Locke’s delivery hours per client decline by at least half. That would connect the current demand signal to a genuine technical advantage instead of headcount-supported growth.
