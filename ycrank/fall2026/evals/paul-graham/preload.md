# Preload -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact about Preload is what happened when its sensors met a robot that couldn't learn from them. What broke? What did the founders change? How long did it take? The dossier describes a sensing stack, but no such episode. That leaves my resourcefulness score at its missing-evidence default. It doesn't establish that the founders wait around. It means I haven't seen the behavior on which I would base a personal check.

There is a serious case for these founders. Leo Li has worked at Neuralink and studied robotics. Surya Pandiaraju has experience across several neural-interface companies. Tony Wang has done EMG and motion-tracking research and fabricated neural hardware. These are relevant skills for collecting signals that video misses. The combination of egocentric video, tactile gloves, and EMG earns some technical credit, discounted because the dossier establishes a described integration rather than a verified working system or measured robot-learning result. Preload fits my unstated preference for technical teams building a difficult substrate, but the accumulated substrate itself remains unverified.

The strongest bull case is that these founders understand information the robot-training industry is throwing away. Contact and muscle signals could distinguish two movements that look identical on camera but require different forces. If that information materially improves manipulation, collecting and calibrating it could become valuable, unpleasant work that customers gladly outsource. But Touch2Robot's results belong to Touch2Robot. Preload still has to show that its human signals survive transfer to robot hands. Axonnic already offers EMG capture, and Human Archive collects multimodal wearable data. Another sensor earns its place by improving the customer's policy enough to justify collection and integration costs. Dataset licensing is only an inferred business model here; neither prices nor collection economics establish that tradeoff.

Who needs the crude version badly enough to use it twice? Preload solicits manipulation-policy, VLA, and world-model teams, which is a sensible place to look. The October 1 launch video's reported 246,186 views establish attention, not repeated use. There is no documented returning user, customer dataset acceptance, or payment, so demand receives its default score. I would not turn the launch into a red flag: nothing establishes that publicity is the founders' entire acquisition plan. Likewise, unverified cofounder history is uncertainty, not evidence of a hastily assembled team.

My exceptions permit unusual founders or uncertain relationships when a working product, unmistakable pull, or validated experiment supplies the missing conviction. Preload's dossier supplies none of those overrides yet. It also documents neither an evidence-driven product revision nor the founders' original collision with this particular customer problem. Relevant research experience makes the opportunity credible, but doesn't establish either fact. Those dimensions retain their defaults. This is a technically plausible company whose strongest evidence concerns what the team might build; my investment gate requires evidence of what they do when building becomes difficult.

I would pass today. One verifiable condition would change my mind: a documented customer trial in which Preload encounters a specific transfer failure, ships a workaround within weeks, measurably improves the customer's robot task success, and earns a paid repeat collection order. That single operating history would answer the questions that matter here.
