# Viraj Aero -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Viraj Aero’s candidate secret is that recovering combustion water makes steam-injected turbines commercially superior where electricity and cooling are constrained. That is a concrete engineering proposition. Steam injection itself is decades old; the discovery must reside in the condenser and the economics of closing the water loop. An endurance test showing that condensation consumes the additional useful output, or requires substantial external water under hot-site conditions, would undermine it. The published 45% electrical efficiency and 2 ppm NOx are development specifications, however, and the dossier identifies neither independent validation nor named peers who reject the proposition. I give the specific architecture some credit without treating its advertised performance as demonstrated.

Who owns the value if it works? Viraj reports a condenser patent, which offers a more credible starting point than undifferentiated turbine assembly. But its publication number and claim scope remain unavailable. Boom offers waterless generation; Mainspring offers another route to on-site power. Customers can purchase the desired outcome through different architectures. The retrofit offering could give Viraj a bounded entry through existing genset manufacturers, but the dossier does not establish dominance in that segment or exclusive control of an essential component. A useful condenser becomes a monopoly only if customers need something competitors cannot economically supply.

The strongest argument against passing is that this team may have assembled the right bridge between propulsion and electricity infrastructure. Joseph Risson has worked on propulsion at Ascendance and Beyond Aero; Mathilde Bouilloux developed six power projects totaling more than 120 MW. The reported aircraft-prototype integration and €13 million of aviation LOIs give the technical program substance beyond a website. That combination fits my less-publicized interest in operators building the physical production stack behind AI. It does not establish the separate founding conditions I require: prior collaboration, full-time status, meaningful retained ownership, and control of essential engineering remain undocumented. Those receive the missing-evidence default, without an invented adverse finding.

Viraj also deserves the strongest applicable exception to my coordination objection: an immediate compute-power constraint can justify financing unusual physical technology before conventional commercial proof. But that exception requires a definite deployment path. The disclosed prototype and LOIs concern aviation, where commercialization is targeted for 2029; they do not qualify a data-center generator. Retrofits could reduce the burden of building an entire turbine business, which materially improves the bull case. Nevertheless, condenser validation, manufacturing and customer qualification remain unresolved, while aviation adds certification. The unexplained 30% versus 40% additional-power claims require reconciliation; they do not yet demonstrate a collapsed technical claim.

I would pass on a personal check today. Viraj names a plausible buyer route—turbine-genset manufacturers—but reports no repeatable acquisition process or delivery economics. Equipment sales and upgrades could form one coherent business, yet pricing and margins are absent, so the $9.7 billion generator-market estimate cannot establish power-law returns. This is a specific technical opportunity with an unproved ownership position, rather than an affirmative founding-team failure. My verifiable condition for reopening diligence is a paid genset-OEM qualification, independently documented, demonstrating 45% **net** electrical efficiency with sustained closed-loop water recovery under specified hot-site conditions. That would turn the central proposition into customer-backed evidence.
