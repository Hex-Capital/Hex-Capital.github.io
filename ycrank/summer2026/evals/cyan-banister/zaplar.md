# Zaplar -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Axel Andersson Lingbert worked in hotels and restaurants before building Zaplar, giving Zaplar a real autobiographical origin rather than a market-research costume. That is Zaplar’s strongest axis. Its weakest is the four-founder structure: CEO, CTO, CPO, and CFO, with no public evidence of how long all four have worked together or who breaks a deadlock. Zaplar is an operator-born hospitality bet trapped inside a governance shape that can turn one hard product decision into four competing truths.

Niantic is the high rung because John Hanke spent a decade living inside location technology before Ingress made the thesis visible. Axel has felt hotel operations from the inside, and Zaplar’s founder-led hotel visits plus more than 100 interviews suggest he can speak to a hotelier without visiting the place as a tourist. Zaplar diverges from Niantic because the dossier shows no adjacent product Axel previously shipped to scale and no specific hotel wound that followed him for years. The origin is lived, but it is not yet tattooed: 25/32 rather than the Hanke or Luckey rung.

HQ Trivia is the low-rung warning: an observable product briefly overrode unresolved co-founder authority, then the Yusupov-Kroll fracture helped consume the company. Zaplar names Axel as CEO, which is better than shared titles, but Zaplar also assigns three other co-founders domain-level executive authority and provides no operating agreement, vesting protocol, or tiebreaker evidence. “Who decides?” is not paperwork trivia when Zaplar must choose between event software, PMS replacement, POS replacement, payments, and messaging. The named CEO earns credit; the unexplained four-way structure earns only 7/16.

Mews already serves 15,000 customers and raised $300 million partly for AI expansion, so Zaplar’s magically weirdness must compound through property-specific knowledge rather than through the word “agentic.” Over 18 months, Zaplar could turn each hotel’s spaces, resources, bookings, and workflow history into painful switching costs; founder-led visits and 100 interviews are the right physical sourcing method. The opposing mechanism is brutal: Mews, Cloudbeds, Canary, and Oracle can add agents while Zaplar is still implementing fiscal rules and migrations. Zaplar is somewhat unsexy and probably non-consensus, but AI hotel software is a rebuttal to consensus, not an escape from it.

Oracle supports hospitality operations and fiscal requirements across more than 200 countries and territories, while Zaplar is currently a software-only attempt to replace PMS, POS, booking, messaging, and payments. For Zaplar’s frontier-or-regulated score to flip into the top tier, Zaplar would need to demonstrate that payment liability, multinational fiscal compliance, or autonomous booking execution creates a proprietary regulatory moat that incumbents cannot bolt on. Today the disclosed approval interface reduces autonomous-action risk without making Zaplar a SpaceX-like technical swing. Zaplar is an unsexy operational system, which I like, but it is not yet frontier infrastructure.

Flock Safety could be seen in a Wi-Fi scan and then photographed in a neighborhood; Zaplar’s analogous strength is that hotel work happens in rooms, restaurants, spas, and front desks where automation can be watched. Zaplar therefore has more physical-world truth than a developer API, but the dossier identifies no live property, repeat usage, or deployed artifact a hotel guest could encounter within 48 hours. Five waitlisted hotels for Zaplar’s earlier event-management scope are curiosity, not the tactile pull that allowed HQ Trivia’s governance exception to fire.

Five hotels joined the waitlist in 48 hours for the earlier event-booking product, and that narrower signal shapes my forecast: by July 2029, Zaplar will have retreated from replacing the entire hotel stack and will sell an event-booking or operational-agent layer atop Mews and Oracle, reaching roughly $1 million to $3 million in ARR. Zaplar’s lived hospitality insight can find a useful wedge, but Zaplar’s four-founder governance and simultaneous PMS/POS/payments scope make full-stack replacement unlikely. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing; the hotel operating system may really be a vertical workflow wedge trying to reveal itself.

SEK 3 million raised, more than 100 hotel interviews, five waitlist additions in 48 hours, and a CEO who actually worked in hospitality form the strongest bull case: Zaplar could be early Flock Safety, physically sourced before outsiders recognize the artifact, while Jon Milles’s security and autonomous-driving experience supplies the Tiz. The Known Exception is HQ Trivia—explosive, observable usage can override bad founder geometry—but Zaplar has disclosed neither explosive usage nor a live hotel, and the waitlist belongs to the narrower product Zaplar subsequently expanded beyond. I stay Neutral. Ten named hotels running Zaplar for six continuous months, paired with documented final product and personnel authority for Axel, would flip me to Invest.
