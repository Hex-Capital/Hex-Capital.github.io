# Roma -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I want to say “take care of this” and find the finished work where I left the intention. Roma’s most interesting detail is that results remain beside the task, while different assistants can access the same workspace through MCP. That could make the task list a durable record of delegated work even as assistants change. There is a small artifact of that future here. But reliable execution, portable permissions, and continuity across assistants still have to become ordinary. The dossier establishes the beginnings of that path, not the full chain.

The founders give me more confidence than the category does. Jan Senderek built Loom and sold it to Dropbox in 2014; Tobias Eichenwald co-founded Senic, later acquired by Gira, and taught himself design and programming. Their approximately twenty-year relationship includes building a running app together. Cody Osborne adds creative and Dropbox experience. These are people who have persuaded others to use things they made. What I cannot establish is whether delegating personal work is a longstanding obsession with its own experiments and operating scars. Prior exits demonstrate execution; they do not establish that particular conviction.

Why does a person still have to carry a task from a list into an email, then retrieve the answer from a chat? Roma attacks that irritating transfer of responsibility. Returning those minutes fits my preference for ordinary workflow repair that gives people control over their day. But there is no identified institutional gatekeeper, quantified rent, or artificial restriction here. The friction is real; the evidence for a deeper system Roma can unwind is limited. Its free iPhone download also leaves the economic exchange untested in the public record.

The strongest argument against my hesitation is that this team could make delegation feel natural before competitors make it dependable. A functioning Mac and iPhone product, connected tools, and background browser execution give them something concrete to improve. Nine US App Store ratings averaging 5.0 as of October 27 are encouraging, but they do not show people repeatedly entrusting consequential work to Roma. I can make exceptions for missing revenue when a prototype comes with exceptional founder obsession or compelling user behavior. Here, neither is documented strongly enough to resolve the uncertainty. The missing signal is habitual delegation, not a subscription receipt.

Roma also touches two of my red flags without conclusively triggering either. It processes email, files, recordings, and memory; its published approval controls, integration disconnection, and prohibition on training with Google data matter, but do not establish the complete custody architecture. Its reliance on OpenAI and Composio creates dependency, although the dossier does not establish an exclusive, inescapable model relationship. Meanwhile, Todoist already exposes tasks to external agents. Roma has not demonstrated control of a scarce enabling layer beneath this market. A shared interface could become valuable, but inferred switching costs earn no credit today. Senderek is identified as CEO; there is no affirmative evidence of a leadership shell game or equal-control deadlock.

I am neutral and would keep my money for now. The team is the strongest evidence; ownership of an indispensable layer is the weakest. My flip condition is an independently inspectable eight-week cohort of at least thirty users who each delegate three or more completed tasks weekly and replace their previous task-management routine with Roma. That would show the founders have changed a habit, giving me something much more substantial than an attractive promise about autonomous work.
