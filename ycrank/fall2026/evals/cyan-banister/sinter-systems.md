# Sinter -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I want a hardware engineer to change a design over breakfast and hold production-representative parts that afternoon. Sinter is pointing toward that world. But work backward: polymer molds must survive repeated cycles, simulation must predict the resulting parts, and delivery must remain economical after inspection and failed runs. Today, Sinter labels both tooling and mold-flow simulation “coming soon.” Its CAD upload and REST/MCP interfaces describe how an order enters the system; they do not yet show the difficult manufacturing transition happening. That leaves my primary future test at its missing-evidence default.

The founders give me a stronger reason to pay attention. Oliver Johnson operated a print farm and college makerspace; Justin Kwon worked on robotic fish processing at Shinkei and distributed training at Manifold. Those are useful encounters with machines, operations, and software. Kwon also says they have built projects together since thirteen. This is substantially better than two people discovering manufacturing through an accelerator application. Still, the dossier does not identify those early projects or demonstrate polymer-tooling expertise. I see credible builders with operating tuition, with the depth of their particular obsession still partly unproven.

Why does an engineer have to wait for an outsourced mold? What portion of that wait is transport, supplier scheduling, machining, or qualification? Sinter identifies the outsourcing bottleneck and proposes bringing fabrication inside. That could return iteration control to small hardware teams. But there is no measured delay, tooling bill, or supplier margin showing which economic constraint disappears. Xometry already offers automated pricing and injection-molding procurement. Sinter needs to change the economics behind the quote, because making the quote easier is already available.

The strongest argument against passing is that ordinary FDM work could finance a much harder manufacturing capability. That fits my recurring interest in a mundane beachhead supporting hidden infrastructure. Sinter also describes in-house production, giving it a physical starting point beyond an ordering interface. The relevant exception is **CosmicBrain AI**: missing revenue did not prevent a manufacturing investment when a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and deep founder history supported the technical path. Here, the proposed polymer process has no published mold-life or repeatability results. The shared mechanism is early physical enablement; the decisive difference is demonstrated technical progress toward it.

Nor can I substitute the earlier marketplace’s supply for customer behavior. The May 2026 announcement reported 150-plus printers nationally, including more than twenty around NYC, but that establishes neither completed orders nor repeat use of today’s in-house service. A quote page establishes an ordering route, not costly customer commitment. I am not deducting points for undisclosed revenue at pre-seed; I am leaving behavioral proof at its default. Likewise, physical production earns some enabling-layer credit, but the dossier does not establish control of a scarce process. No severe privacy issue, leadership deception, or founder-control conflict is documented, so those red flags do not drive my decision.

I pass with my own money today. Sinter’s strongest evidence concerns the people who could build the future; its weakest concerns whether the essential transition has begun. My one verifiable flip condition is a documented repeat customer run using Sinter’s own polymer tooling that meets an agreed part specification and beats that customer’s conventional tooling quote on total cost and delivery time. That would connect the founders, the manufacturing artifact, and the agency returned to the customer in one observable event.
