# Click -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The first question is what broke and what Aditya Asgaonkar did next. The dossier does not answer it. Click has moved from a “cursor for assistants” to browser automation for operations teams and then to web search and connectors, but it names neither the observation that forced each change nor the speed or result of the response. This could be a running back changing direction. It could also be a founder searching for a fashionable noun. Without the blocker and the shipped workaround, I cannot distinguish them.

There is nevertheless a real artifact here. A solo technical founder with Ethereum consensus research and browser-agent context work has assembled a remote MCP service spanning Exa, Crustdata, Google Maps, Reddit, X, YouTube, financial data, and travel sources. The $49 monthly plan for 500 credits makes it more than a research diagram. But there is no measured technical result: no latency or retrieval benchmark, no proprietary corpus, no connector reliability data, and no evidence that an agent repeatedly chooses Click over calling these providers directly. It is working software, but not yet a difficult proof.

The missing fact is who wants the crude product now. Click has 157 LinkedIn followers and NVIDIA Inception membership, neither of which demonstrates use. There are no paying customers, active-user counts, retention figures, or named workflows that break without the product. The market timing is favorable—MCP grew to more than 10,000 active public servers by December 2025—but a popular protocol is not demand for this particular wrapper. The narrow well has not appeared.

The strongest case for Click is Asgaonkar himself. He has relevant systems depth, has written about agent context engineering, and has already connected heterogeneous providers behind one installation. A solo founder shipping across this much surface area may be unusually impatient and capable. If users begin combining professional-network, mapping, financial, and travel data in workflows no single search API supports, the aggregation layer could become a useful programmable primitive. But the dossier shows no such appropriation, while OpenAI already supplies native search and remote MCP support. Cross-provider usage data is only a hypothetical moat, and upstream providers control access and pricing.

The connector breadth also creates schlep, but it is not yet evidence of a founder-originated wedge. The dossier does not document Asgaonkar encountering this problem in his own work, manually rescuing a blocked user, or enduring an ugly integration because someone urgently needed the result. Instead, Click is exposed to provider terms, temporary retention of tool arguments and results, and restrictions on sensitive data. Those burdens could become defensibility after usage appears. At present they are operating risks attached to an unproven demand hypothesis.

I am passing. The operational gate fails: there is no named blocker followed by a resourceful workaround, and neither measured technical proof nor user pull clears the alternative threshold. My expectation is that within 36 months Click will have pivoted back toward operations-team browser automation or become inactive rather than reaching $1 million ARR as a general connector subscription. One fact would flip me: ten named customers repeatedly using Click in production for a cross-provider workflow, with at least three months of retention and evidence that Asgaonkar personally built around the failure that initially stopped them.
