# Speko -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Beknazar Abdikamalov spent four years selecting voice stacks and previously built voice-enabled AI as Hupo’s CTO, making Speko founder-lived infrastructure with almost no physical weight. Speko’s strongest signal is autobiographical contact with the problem; Speko’s weakest is that the product remains an API sitting on other people’s models and machines. I see a capable builder who has felt the splinters, but not yet a magically weird artifact I would notice across a café. Biz and Tiz are visible. Rizz—and the hidden game Speko is really playing—remain unobserved.

Niantic is the high rung because John Hanke spent a decade turning location infrastructure into products before Pokémon Go made the technology touchable. Speko shares the load-bearing mechanism: Abdikamalov reports four years of repeated voice-stack bake-offs and built adjacent voice products at Hupo before starting Speko. Speko diverges because four years of vendor selection is not yet Hanke-scale compounding, and the dossier shows no comparable product shipped to scale. The origin is lived rather than invented in a market memo, but Speko still needs evidence that Abdikamalov is *of* voice infrastructure rather than merely a skilled visitor.

HQ Trivia exposed what happens when a software product lacks a durable underlying structure: explosive attention can briefly hide the weakness, then the floor disappears. Speko shows the same low-rung capital profile without HQ Trivia’s override—provider-cost-plus-5% routing, externally hosted models, and feature overlap with LiveKit and Vapi. Speko’s HIPAA compliance and pending SOC 2 Type 1 report add enterprise friction, but regulation is not intrinsic to Speko’s product, and no hardware, proprietary model, or difficult physical deployment compounds with time. The known HQ Trivia exception requires tactile, undeniable virality; 85 weekly SDK downloads and two GitHub stars do not trigger it.

YC lists Beknazar Abdikamalov as Speko’s sole active founder and CEO, which gives Speko an identifiable tiebreaker while decisions are still cheap. Over the next 18 months, that clarity can compound through faster provider integrations, pricing changes, and benchmark revisions across 56 providers and 159 models. Speko nevertheless has four listed employees and no public evidence of majority operating authority, founder vesting, or an exit-conflict protocol. A single title is a promising governance signal, not a completed governance system; I have learned from GameCrush that the argument nobody formalizes becomes the argument that owns the company.

Vapi has raised $72 million and LiveKit $181.3 million around adjacent voice infrastructure, placing Speko inside a hot category rather than outside the city walls. Speko would flip my non-consensus score if credible investors rejected Speko because benchmark-driven routing looked too narrow, and Abdikamalov could show that those rejections left the round materially underpriced. The present evidence points the other way: voice AI is crowded, well-funded, and legible, while Speko’s distinction is a seven-board benchmarking asset competitors can plausibly reproduce. “OpenRouter for voice AI” is concise positioning, but concise is not the same as magically weird.

Niantic could be installed, played, and felt; Speko can be exercised only by a developer wiring its TypeScript or Python SDK into a voice application. Speko’s route preview, LiveKit and Pipecat adapters, and hosted MCP server make Speko more observable than a slide-deck abstraction, but a normal human cannot ride it, scan it, or photograph it within 48 hours. My unstated sourcing pattern comes from environmental evidence—the Flock Safety Wi-Fi name, the Uber ride—not founder testimony about an API. Speko may become tactile through the calls it powers, but the dossier does not identify a shipped end-user experience where Speko’s routing advantage can actually be heard.

Seven public benchmark boards covering 56 providers and 159 models lead me to predict that by July 2029 Speko will have pivoted toward voice-model benchmarking and observability, remain below $5 million ARR, and be acquired by LiveKit primarily for integrations and benchmark data. Speko’s founder-lived insight should produce a useful technical asset, while Speko’s weightless, overlapping routing layer makes independent platform control difficult. I’m never playing the game I seem to be playing: the router may really be an acquisition-shaped measurement company.

Speko’s $0.09-per-minute hosted offering is the strongest bull case because every routed call could generate proprietary failure observations, turning today’s maintenance burden into a data flywheel that LiveKit and Vapi cannot reconstruct from public benchmarks alone. Speko could become the neutral control plane precisely because those larger competitors sell broader platforms with conflicted incentives. That case stings—but the dossier explicitly says no proprietary dataset or sustained routing advantage has been demonstrated, while public usage remains 90 weekly MCP downloads, 85 SDK downloads, and two stars per principal repository. Neither the DeepMind polymath exception nor the HQ Trivia product-love exception is evidenced. I would flip to Invest if Speko reports at least $25,000 MRR from five unrelated paying customers and demonstrates that workload-specific routing reduces production voice failures by at least 20% against LiveKit or Vapi.
