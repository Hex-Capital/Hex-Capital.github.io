# 6thSense -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Matt Wulff has built vision-guided robotics at Tesla and captured tactile data at Mecka AI, while four “best friends” now share 6thSense. That pairing is the signature: 6thSense is strongest where I can touch the artifact—a $4,000 sensor-skinned robotic-hand pair—and weakest where I need to know who wins the argument when four founders disagree. It is frontier hardware with a governance ghost. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing: 6thSense looks like a data vendor, but the interesting asset is the nervous system being installed beneath the data.

**Flock Safety** is the high rung because a physical object in the wild exposed the product before a spreadsheet could explain it. 6thSense shares that mechanism: pressure, RGB, depth, hand pose, IMU, and task outcomes are captured by internally manufactured wearables, then mapped onto robot-hand tactile arrays. A normal person cannot validate the models, but a roboticist can wear the rig, manipulate an object, and inspect the episode. 6thSense diverges from Flock because its “pressure proxies” are not ground-truth force measurements, and no tendon-driven-hand transfer benchmark answers the public challenge. The artifact is real; the claimed bridge from human touch to robot learning is not yet fully observable.

**HQ Trivia** is the low governance rung because tactile product love temporarily concealed a co-founder structure that later became part of the failure. 6thSense names James Baek as CEO, which is better than shared titles, but 6thSense also names Ronak Agarwal, Alex Hyungwoo Noh, and Matt Wulff as founders and describes the group as best friends without disclosing equity, vesting, or tiebreaker authority. Friendship is not a governance system. HQ earned its exception through explosive, addictive usage; “a handful of teams” and a 20-place pilot do not give 6thSense the same override.

James Baek brings US Army work, biomedical engineering studies, and a prior six-figure content-business exit, but Matt Wulff—not the named CEO—owns 6thSense’s clearest lived contact with tactile robotics. The founder score flips upward if Baek can identify the pre-company moment when he personally felt the missing-touch-data problem, or if 6thSense can show that Wulff’s Mecka work generated the original architecture rather than merely adding domain credibility afterward. Without that evidence, 6thSense has strong founder-market fit distributed across four biographies, not the singular autobiographical inevitability I saw in Anduril or Niantic.

Tacta Systems has raised $75 million while GelSight, Touchlab, XELA, OSMO, and printable eFlesh crowd the tactile stack, so 6thSense is not non-consensus merely because the hardware looks strange. Over the next 18 months, “magically weird” compounds only if the $4,000 hands create a proprietary loop: deployed hands generate episodes, episodes improve customer models, and improved models make the matching 6thSense hardware harder to remove. If customers treat 6thSense as another sensor supplier while collecting data internally, the weirdness decays into a procurement line item. The red flag is that physical AI is already fashionable; the underpriced part must come from an overlooked integration advantage, not from the word “robotics.”

The $4,000 at-cost pilot puts 6thSense on the **SpaceX** axis without pretending the technical magnitude is comparable. SpaceX made capital intensity a filter against tourists; 6thSense likewise must manufacture hardware, calibrate multimodal sensors, synchronize recordings, and survive physical deployment before revenue appears. That is my hidden hardware weighting showing itself. 6thSense earns the parallel because software-only competitors cannot conjure clean touch data from a camera, but open-source gloves and printable sensors mean manufacturing difficulty alone will not become the moat.

Twenty pilot slots lead me to a falsifiable forecast: by July 2029, 6thSense will have shifted from primarily selling multimodal episodes to selling a tactile-hand, calibration, and data-infrastructure platform generating $2 million–$6 million in ARR, with at least one of the four original founders no longer operating full-time. 6thSense’s observable hardware will pull the business toward recurring integration revenue; 6thSense’s unresolved four-founder authority will produce either an explicit hierarchy or a departure before scale.

OSMO’s open-source glove, eFlesh’s printable fabrication files, and Figure’s internal collection at millions-of-hours scale make the bear mechanism sting: sophisticated customers may vertically integrate while smaller customers assemble cheaper open stacks, leaving 6thSense squeezed between both. 6thSense has one rebuttal, not yet a hard answer—the integrated clock, calibration, labeling, robot-hand analogue, and “handful of teams” remove engineering work that open components do not. I will write the check, but the verifiable flip condition is simple: if fewer than five of the 20 pilot users purchase recurring data, calibration, or integration within twelve months, I move 6thSense from Invest to Pass.
