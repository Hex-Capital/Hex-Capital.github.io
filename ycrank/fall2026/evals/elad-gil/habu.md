# HABU -- Elad Gil Evaluation

HABU’s market thesis is that AI-assisted coordination lets a fee developer deliver more projects with less overhead, allowing institutional housing investors to buy development execution at better economics. The controlling variable is contribution profit per development employee across concurrent projects, including outsourced labor and execution liabilities. HABU’s claimed fivefold increase in projects per employee makes that proposition falsifiable. But automating desk work only creates a large business if the savings survive contact with permitting, contractors, and construction. I would pass on the current evidence.

The economic bridge is the weak point. HABU estimates over 1,000 hours of manual desk work per apartment project and claims 95% faster processing, 20% faster schedules, and 10%–15% lower project costs. Those are different denominators. Faster document processing does not establish faster construction, and lower project costs do not establish higher developer margins without knowing fees and risk allocation. The dossier supplies quantified targets, which deserve limited credit, but no project-level reconciliation. Similarly, $160 billion of multifamily investment in 2025 measures capital deployed; HABU captures development fees from some portion of that activity. Phoenix’s 10.7% vacancy and declining asking rents in Q3 2026 make disciplined underwriting particularly consequential.

The strongest bull case is that HABU has chosen the right organizational boundary. Its described internal system connects sourcing, underwriting, diligence, design review, construction exceptions, and disposition. Performing development gives the company responsibility for a completed outcome and a place to embed improving models throughout a recurring workflow. That earns more architectural credit than an isolated feasibility tool. Yet the disclosed system lacks demonstrated integrations, accumulated project outcomes, or measured coordination advantages. TestFit already supplies overlapping automation, while Mosaic combines technology with construction operations. Better models could improve HABU while also making conventional developers more efficient; ownership of the service alone does not establish differentiated economics.

The founders make the bull case credible enough to revisit. Bryan Tharalson reports managing more than $80 million in development projects and was Mutiny’s first sales hire; Junhao Chen brings AI operations and Navy logistics experience. This combination could identify operational bottlenecks that a software-only team would miss. However, employer credentials do not demonstrate costly commitment, and the mention of Chen’s prior startup Tenur supplies no specific lesson or outcome, leaving the founder criterion at its default. Demand also remains at its default: the September 2026 launch, 271 LinkedIn followers, and listed backers establish visibility and financing relationships, while the dossier identifies no paid mandate, named investor commitment, or repeat customer usage.

HABU fits my revealed willingness to back physical-market businesses when expertise and software can reconstruct their economics. The exception still requires evidence that the economics change; the frontier-research exception supplies no alternative justification here. The relevant red flag is assigning software economics to a service business before establishing margins, rather than anything documented about founder motivation. Anticipated hiring across engineering, construction, development, and civil engineering makes the labor denominator especially important. My one flip condition is an independently verifiable cohort of three paid projects showing at least twice the contribution profit per development employee of comparable conventional projects, counting outsourced labor and execution liabilities. That would turn the automation claims into evidence about the business I would actually own.
