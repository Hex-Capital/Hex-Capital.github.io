# Bernard -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the founders’ résumés. It is Bernard operating inside the problem. Dan Katzman brings service operations and partnerships; David Goodfellow built enterprise automation products. Neither arrived with documented appliance-repair expertise, but by August 2026 their product was handling company-claimed thousands of calls daily, supporting large authorized servicers, and running SMS operations for Mr. Appliance of West Loop. Specific knowledge acquired through deployment is worth more than inherited credentials. Bernard has shipped into the exact workflow, although the dossier does not yet isolate outcomes such as fewer diagnostic visits or higher first-visit completion.

The product’s decisive question is what remains after ServiceTitan copies the interface. Pre-ID combines appliance identity, symptoms, repair histories, manufacturer guidance, and parts data before dispatch. Bernard also has contractual rights to improve its models using anonymized, aggregated customer data. That is a plausible durable asset: completed repairs can turn scattered technician knowledge into reusable predictions. But plausibility is not compounding. Bernard publishes no repair-corpus size, diagnostic accuracy, first-visit improvement, or evidence that cross-customer data outperforms customer-local knowledge. Meanwhile, ServiceTitan already owns customer and job records, and Bernard depends on Compass, Encompass, Marcone, Early Repair, and Samsung GSPN integrations. The code can be copied today; whether the learning can be copied remains unanswered.

The strongest bull case is that the claimed thousands of daily calls are quietly producing the missing proprietary dataset. If every completed repair improves the next diagnosis across servicers, Bernard fits an important unstated pattern: fragmented offline knowledge becomes scalable output without requiring the original technician to be present. But the observed distribution is still conventional enterprise labor—demo-led sales to servicers, manufacturers, retailers, warranty companies, and service networks. There is no measured contribution, referral, liquidity, or cross-customer utility loop. The system may learn from users; the dossier does not yet show users making the system more valuable for one another.

The appliance-repair wedge is substantial at a reported $1.90 billion, and the broader field-service-management category is $6.1 billion. Bernard names credible adjacent buyers, but naming a parent market is not a path into it. Appliance-specific model identification and parts preparation create depth in the wedge while potentially limiting transfer into HVAC, plumbing, and electrical workflows already served by ServiceTitan, Jobber, Housecall Pro, and Probook. The adjacency becomes credible only if Bernard’s underlying capability is diagnosis from messy service evidence, rather than appliance-specific workflow configuration.

Direct value capture exists structurally but is not yet demonstrated economically. Bernard requires monthly or annual paid subscriptions and supports enterprise volume commitments, so the payer and mechanism are legible. No price, ARR, renewal, expansion, or gross-margin evidence is public. Large deployments and 100-plus Android downloads establish usage, not willingness to pay. At pre-seed that is tolerable; it is not evidence I can count twice.

I see none of the explicit founder red flags: no unfair dealing, artificial deadline, reassurance dependence, or integrity failure appears in the dossier. Their absence is not affirmative credit. The known exception for an unusually crafted, capital-efficient prototype does not override the missing loop here because Bernard is already deployed; reality is available to measure. My 36-month base case is a useful, sales-led appliance-repair vertical reaching roughly $4 million to $8 million in ARR, but not yet a self-reinforcing network. I would not write the check today. One verifiable condition flips the decision: a corpus of at least 100,000 completed repairs with a held-out study showing that cross-customer learning improves first-visit completion by at least 15 percentage points over customer-local data alone.
