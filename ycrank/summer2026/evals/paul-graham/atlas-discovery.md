# Atlas Discovery -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The central obstacle is not model architecture. It is access to linked molecular, treatment, and outcome data. Atlas Discovery acknowledges that these records remain in disconnected silos, yet its response so far is to request hospital partnerships and introductions to clinical-development teams. That is waiting for the gatekeeper, not bending the gate. The dossier names no compressed sequence in which a data, technical, or customer blocker produced a founder-built workaround.

The founders have nevertheless built something real. Two public preprints, open-source code and weights, and retrospective AUROCs of 0.7–0.9 are more persuasive than a diagram of a future platform. The UNIFI result—0.760 AUROC on 358 treated patients and a modeled reduction of 458 participants at matched power—is a measured artifact. But Atlas trained and evaluated within the same trial, admits the analysis could not have enriched that trial, and says direct cross-trial generalization is currently infeasible. The model proves technical ability. It does not yet prove the thing the company proposes to sell.

Who wants the crude product now? No named biopharma customer is using it, no hospital has supplied proprietary data, and there is no public revenue, pricing, repeat analysis, or signed trial. The proposed distribution path depends on partnerships with exactly the institutions controlling the scarce input. Meanwhile Owkin already reports relationships with more than 800 hospitals, and Atlas has disclosed neither exclusive data nor a structural barrier. A four-star public repository is evidence of shipping, but not evidence that users are pulling the product back.

The team’s backgrounds make the work credible: Sanjukta Bhattacharya researches AI and gene expression, while Shaamil Karim and Christian Gensbigler coauthored the underlying models and have computational-biology or cancer-research experience. But the dossier does not show that these founders encountered an urgent clinical-development problem in their own work, nor that they have changed routes after a falsified hypothesis. Domain proximity is not the same as a founder-originated need, and a difficult regulated market is not a schlep-heavy wedge until the founders actually perform the schlep.

The strongest bull case is the known exception represented by Bindwell: very young technical founders can earn an investment through numerical proof and direct scientific exposure. The shared mechanism is that Atlas also has young researchers producing quantitative biological-model results before institutional permission. The material difference is that Bindwell paired its computation with laboratory validation, whereas Atlas’s headline result is same-trial retrospective analysis and its necessary external data remain behind prospective partners. My less explicit pattern is to fund proof spikes that settle the central uncertainty. This proof spike settles whether the team can train a model, not whether the model generalizes or whether anyone will buy it.

My 36-month base case is that Atlas pivots from prospective patient-response prediction to retrospective biomarker-analysis services for small biotech trials and remains below $1 million ARR. I am passing because the resourcefulness gate fails, despite credible technical work. One condition would flip the decision: a named biopharma company prospectively locks Atlas’s model on an unseen trial, reports at least 0.75 AUROC after outcomes arrive, and pays to deploy it on a second trial.
