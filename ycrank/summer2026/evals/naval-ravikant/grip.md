# Grip -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Jonas Gruetter has learned robotics by making machines collide with the physical world. His reinforcement-learning work reached field tests on a 12-ton excavator with 70% task success, and his experience at Gravis Robotics, ETH Zürich’s Robotic Systems Lab, and Ursa Mining overlaps materially with perception and manipulation in hostile industrial environments ([YC Company Directory](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/grip); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.17683)). This is specific knowledge acquired through action, although the dossier does not show that he has sorted waste, operated a processing facility, or sold machinery to one. The founder evidence is strong and adjacent, not yet exact.

The product passes the copyable-code test more credibly than a typical AI company. Grip is building custom gripper hardware, and successful and failed grasps could compound into proprietary manipulation policies ([YC Company Directory](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/grip)). But a mechanism is not yet an asset. There is no disclosed dataset size, deployment count, pick rate, uptime, or contaminant-removal rate. If a competitor copied the software today, the dossier does not establish what unavailable data or proven hardware advantage would remain—while EverestLabs already claims training on more than five billion recyclable objects ([EverestLabs](https://www.everestlabs.ai/recycleos)).

The wedge has room to maneuver. Removing plastic from organic waste could expand across composting, biogas, recycling, and other variable material streams using the same dexterous manipulation capability. The broader waste-sorting-robot market was estimated at $3.29 billion in 2025 and projected to grow 19.6% annually through 2034, with European regulation adding pressure to improve recovery rates ([Market Data Forecast](https://www.marketdataforecast.com/market-reports/waste-sorting-robots-market); [European Commission](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/topics/waste-and-recycling/packaging-waste_en)). Yet AMP, Glacier, Recycleye, and EverestLabs have already raised between $24.3 million and $195.85 million and disclose deployments or operating systems. A large market containing funded incumbents rewards measurable superiority, not merely a different gripper.

Grip fits my recent preference for embodied infrastructure, but not the older permissionless pattern in which participants create value for one another. Facility operating data might improve a central model, yet the dossier provides no deployed systems and no evidence that one customer measurably improves another customer’s utility. Each installation may instead demand equivalent integration, maintenance, and founder labor. That is leverage only after the learning curve outruns the service burden.

The strongest bull case is that pre-seed physical systems look weakest immediately before the first deployment makes their hidden advantage legible. Gruetter has relevant field-robotics experience, the team is only four people, the substrate is custom hardware rather than commodity software, and wet organic waste may be poorly served by suction-based competitors. The known exceptions for missing traction, however, require an unusually convincing prototype, capital efficiency, recruiting signal, or observed user behavior. Grip discloses none of those yet. I see no founder-integrity, artificial-deadline, or reassurance-seeking red flag; the issue is missing proof at the decisive technical and commercial layer.

I would not write the check today. My base case is that within 36 months Grip becomes a small pilot-stage robotics vendor with less than $2 million in annual recurring or contracted revenue, unless it proves a repeatable retrofit product rather than bespoke automation. The single flip condition is verifiable: three independent organic-waste facilities operating paid Grip cells for six consecutive months, with disclosed uptime and contaminant-removal performance that improves across deployments. That would convert hardware plus learning from an attractive hypothesis into a compounding asset.
