# Synara -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a shop owner buying a resolved security incident: someone notices the intrusion, intervenes, and takes responsibility through the handoff to responders. Synara’s detection–verification–intervention workflow points toward that world. But getting there requires reliable incident understanding and dependable response operations, with economics that survive both. The dossier describes the service offering without establishing those transitions or a present-day deployment that demonstrates them. That future is my hypothesis; I cannot count it as Synara’s evidence.

Boris Ploix is the reason I keep reading. He co-founded Calipsa as CTO in 2016, worked on machine learning for surveillance, and reached an acquisition by Motorola in April 2022. That is a decade-long thread through this exact problem, with an actual company built along the way. It strongly supports technical commitment and first-believer ability. The missing pieces are evidence of recruiting the current team and command of the monitoring station’s commercial operations. An acquisition establishes meaningful operating experience, but does not answer every question about this new business.

Why does an alert leave the customer holding the problem? Synara identifies a useful institutional failure: detection, verification, and responsibility for acting are fragmented. Its promise to take the response chain into one service addresses that failure directly. White-label distribution also lets integrators retain their customer relationships and monthly billing. Those are concrete choices about accountability and control. Still, there is no disclosed wholesale price, operator cost, or quantified coverage gap showing whose margin pays for the improvement. I see a plausible repair, with only partial evidence of the underlying economics.

The strongest argument against passing is that Ploix already built one component of this system and may understand precisely why owning the complete service matters. Integrators bring installed cameras and customer relationships; Synara could turn that existing distribution into a business before developing any exotic technology. This fits my pattern of finding consequential companies inside mundane operating problems. **CosmicBrain AI** supplies a relevant pre-revenue exception: sustained technical commitment can justify investing before revenue when a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs make the path tangible. Synara shares the founder-depth argument, but its illustrative operational displays do not supply that corresponding proof.

The October 2026 launch and coverage establish availability, not customer behavior. There is no documented completed intervention, repeated operator use, or integrator renewal, so the product-behavior dimension receives its missing-evidence default. Nor can I award infrastructure credit merely because monitoring sounds foundational. Deep Sentinel already combines existing cameras with live intervention, while CHeKT supplies monitoring infrastructure through an established channel. Synara has not demonstrated control of a scarce technical asset or exclusive incident data. The inferred learning advantage remains an inference.

I would pass with my own money today. Surveillance makes consent and footage custody a real investment gate, but the dossier establishes neither a severe violation nor an adequate limiting architecture. Likewise, an undisclosed executive title does not establish a leadership shell game, and a two-person team does not establish equal-control deadlock. My decision turns on the gap between a compelling operator biography and an unproven operating service. One verifiable flip condition: an independently audited, 90-day integrator deployment demonstrating paid renewal, fewer unresolved incidents at lower fully loaded cost, and consent-limited footage access. That would show whether Synara actually makes the problem go away.
