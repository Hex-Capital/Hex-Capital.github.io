# Decent -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I want a small employer’s health plan to behave like something maintained, rather than something purchased and forgotten until renewal. Decent offers a plausible path: read claims during the year, identify avoidable costs, and get that evidence to the insurer before next year’s price hardens. Claims-data availability and AI processing are the first unlocks. The missing transition is whether those interventions reliably change renewal economics. Decent’s own exclusions matter: fully insured and PEO arrangements fall outside its claims-report process. The future reaches only as far as the data does.

Soman is the strongest reason to keep looking. He operated an earlier health-plan business, sold it, and returned to the problem with a new entity announced September 29, 2026. That is substantially more interesting than discovering insurance when AI became fashionable. Budker brings production AI experience, and their relationship dates back roughly sixteen years to Kindle. This fits my pattern of backing founders who turn operating wounds into products. I still want the specific scar: what did Soman repeatedly fail to fix in the previous business that this architecture finally makes possible? The dossier establishes relevant persistence and complementary skills; it does not yet establish that answer.

Why does the customer have to wait a year for help? Decent identifies a useful institutional failure: billing mistakes and claims costs can accumulate while the employer’s attention is concentrated at renewal. Continuous intervention could return negotiating time and control to a small business. But follow the money. Insurers, PEOs, and administrators pay Decent variable commissions and sometimes bonuses. Written compensation disclosure is helpful; it does not establish that recommending the cheapest suitable arrangement is economically attractive to the broker. Decent could repair the renewal ritual while retaining its underlying incentive problem.

The strongest objection to my hesitation is that customers already describe meaningful outcomes. Serif Health reports $60,000 in annual savings; Locate reports more than $100,000 against its renewal proposal. Those are concrete reasons for an employer to engage, especially this early. The reported 67 companies “working with us” also suggests a functioning service. Yet those testimonials concern comparing arrangements, while the distinctive bet is continuous claims intervention. Neither the customer count nor the claimed 89% automation establishes repeated use of that mechanism. I give the working business credit without treating shopping savings as proof of a year-round feedback loop.

Underneath the interface, I find licensed distribution and quoting relationships, but no demonstrated scarce asset that Decent controls. Nava already offers year-round support and bill audits. That makes the likely competitive test execution and service economics, with carriers still controlling data access, eligibility, and final prices. My preference for concealed infrastructure is not satisfied merely because insurance administration is complicated. The team might build a valuable brokerage here; the dossier has not shown why other brokers must eventually depend on it.

I would hold my personal check. My exceptions for companies without revenue depend on unusually strong technical artifacts or founder obsession; the missing revenue itself is not the obstacle here. The named CEO gives me no affirmative leadership-shell-game signal, and the dossier establishes no severe privacy violation, although claims-data consent and custody remain important diligence. My one flip condition is a documented renewal cohort showing that Decent’s in-year claims interventions produced incremental savings beyond plan shopping, with unchanged coverage and broker compensation disclosed. That would turn an appealing promise into evidence that this team has changed the system.
