# Altair Health -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Altair Health’s proposed secret is that insurer policies can be formalized well enough to make autonomous billing reliably executable. What would prove that wrong? Claims that pass its theorem prover but fail because the encoded policy omitted a requirement—or human escalation so frequent that automation never changes the economics. The architecture gives us a testable technical proposition. The dossier does not establish which prevailing belief it overturns, name peers who reject it, or report a result testing it. I leave the secret at the absent-evidence default rather than award contrarian credit for a theorem prover.

The strongest argument for Altair is that it has crossed from proposing software to operating a customer’s billing. A founder reports several hundred thousand dollars in monthly insurer collections for a substance-use-treatment practice. That is meaningful operational exposure at pre-seed, although it is client money, not Altair revenue. Disputed insurer decisions also fit my less visible interest in systems that make adversarial institutional behavior legible. The important counterargument to my skepticism is that running the operation could reveal rules and exceptions that software vendors never see. But the dossier does not separate what the agents accomplish from what human billers rescue. My exception for independently demonstrated technical breakthroughs therefore does not apply yet; live customer activity validates the workflow’s importance, without validating the claimed breakthrough.

Can Altair keep the value it creates? Substance-use-treatment practices offer a plausible starting point, but one deployment establishes neither dominance nor a compounding monopoly mechanism. Adonis, Candid Health, and Anomaly address overlapping parts of the payment workflow; their existence does not prove Altair is interchangeable, particularly given its verification architecture. Equally, a different architecture does not establish proprietary advantage. Policy formalization could become reusable infrastructure, or it could remain expensive work performed separately for each practice. There is no measured result, exclusive data asset, or demonstrated scale economy to resolve that distinction, so monopoly evidence remains at its default.

Aouss and Jad Sbai being brothers reduces concern about strangers assembling a company for an application. It does not establish prior shipping history, current ownership, or full-time commitment. Cambridge’s listing of Jad as a PhD student requires clarification rather than an inference that he is part-time. I find no documented founding-structure red flag, and retain the default score. Distribution has firmer evidence: Aouss acquired a design partner through Texas clinic visits, supported by relevant enterprise-sales experience. Yet the reported year from initial conversation to full deployment makes delivery economics consequential. The free EHR and conflicting $395/year authorization listing provide no usable managed-billing price against which to judge that effort. One customer route exists; a repeatable economic engine remains unproved.

I would pass on writing a check today. This is an unresolved investment hypothesis, with no documented fatal gate, but insufficient evidence of a secret that produces durable value capture. The $7.73 billion outsourcing market cannot supply the missing revenue arithmetic. My verifiable flip condition for moving to investigate is an independently audited, six-month cohort across multiple unaffiliated substance-use-treatment practices showing that Altair’s verification system reduces human billing labor tenfold while maintaining comparable payment accuracy. That would distinguish a proprietary automation engine from a billing operation whose software happens to include formal proofs.
