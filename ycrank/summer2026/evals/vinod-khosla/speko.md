# Speko -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Beknazar Abdikamalov spent four years selecting voice stacks by trial and error, yet Speko’s highest relative signal is founder learning and its lowest is capital depth. Speko is an experienced founder’s rational response to a recurring infrastructure headache, not a capital-hostile attempt to remake an economic system. That pairing usually produces a useful product and a modest outcome. Speko may save developers from repeated vendor bake-offs, but no economic mechanism breaks when Speko succeeds; LiveKit, Vapi, and the model providers continue to exist.

**Slash** (2024) is the high rung for demonstrated learning rate because its documented Kanye West pivot exposed how quickly the founders could discard an obsolete thesis. Abdikamalov’s prior Hupo co-founder/CTO role and four years of voice-stack frustration give Speko more learning evidence than a first-time founder discovering voice AI during YC. The analogy stops where it matters: Speko’s dossier names no specific pivot, mistaken thesis, or decision Abdikamalov reversed. Prior experience is not automatically learning rate. Experts often repeat an error with greater efficiency. Speko earns credit for lived iteration, but not the Slash override.

**DoorDash** (2013) lacked science-scale capital requirements, but ambition and consumer-commerce consequences overrode that weakness; capital-light economics alone did not make DoorDash a bad investment. Speko has the same low-rung capital profile without DoorDash’s override signal: provider cost plus 5%, hosted infrastructure at $0.09 per minute, external models, and self-service SDK distribution. Speko needs integrations and working capital, not a $115M Commonwealth Fusion Series A or a $105M Genesis AI seed. Speko is precisely the kind of deal where my capital supplies no unusual advantage. Being over-resourced is not a virtue.

The **$35.24 billion voice-agent market forecast for 2033** gives Speko a real tailwind, but Speko’s consequence compounds only if routing becomes the control plane for voice inference. Over 18 months, more providers could create more benchmark maintenance, more failure observations, and better selection; that is a legitimate data flywheel. The mechanism currently ends at optimization: lower latency, lower cost, better failover. Speko does not eliminate contact-center labor by itself, and provider failover does not repair voice agents completing only 26–38% of tasks under realistic noise and accents. “OpenRouter for voice AI” reorganizes suppliers. It does not yet collapse an incumbent regime.

**Amazon software engineer and former Hupo CTO** is a profile of expert continuity, exactly the pattern my TED argument warns about: experts extrapolate the past. Speko’s founder saw painful bake-offs and built a better bake-off machine. For Speko’s founder-defiance score to flip a tier, Abdikamalov would need to articulate and prove a controversial thesis—for example, that static voice stacks become technically unviable and workload-specific routing improves end-task completion, not merely benchmark scores. Seven benchmark boards across 56 providers and 159 models show execution. Speko has not shown that experts believe the underlying thesis is impossible or that Abdikamalov has contradicted his own field.

**Physical Intelligence** (2024) took the opposite technical path: proprietary, stack-deep physical AI where model capability itself was the miracle. Speko aggregates externally hosted models and inherits provider pricing, uptime, and API changes. The difficult work is broad—159 models, language-specific testing, latency measurement—but breadth is not a technical miracle. LiveKit already routes inference, and Vapi already supports provider swapping. Speko’s benchmark portability problem makes matters worse because public rankings may not transfer to customer audio. Speko can build valuable reliability infrastructure, but consensus VCs are not repelled by the science; they are invited by ordinary software economics.

The **90 weekly MCP downloads and 85 weekly SDK downloads** suggest that Speko has found curiosity, not dependence. By July 2029, I predict Speko will have pivoted its primary product from provider routing to voice-agent evaluation and observability and will remain between $1 million and $3 million ARR. Speko’s learning-rate advantage makes the pivot plausible; Speko’s low capital requirements and weak structural barrier make an independent routing control plane unlikely. The money may take care of itself only after Speko finds a more defensible problem.

**Seven public benchmark boards covering 56 providers and 159 models** form the strongest bull case: frequent model releases could make Speko the neutral default, while accumulated provider-failure telemetry creates a flywheel that LiveKit and Vapi cannot reproduce without compromising their platform incentives. DoorDash proves that capital-light infrastructure can outrun my science bias, and Slash proves that a YC founder’s learning rate can override limited consequence. The bull case still loses today because Speko has disclosed no proprietary dataset, no sustained routing advantage, no revenue, and only two GitHub stars per principal SDK while funded competitors already offer overlapping functionality. I would flip to Invest if Speko verifies ten enterprise customers each routing more than one million production minutes per month with workload-specific selection that materially outperforms static provider choices.
