# Routant -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a small company hiring software agents from several vendors without handing each one the digital equivalent of its office keys. The customer grants a narrow permission, watches what happens, and takes that permission back. Routant is pointing at that world. Getting there requires reliable enforcement across private systems and a security buyer willing to delegate authority. The dossier supplies neither a demonstrated transition nor a working artifact showing that Routant has crossed it. I can imagine the destination; that does not earn the company credit for reaching it.

Why does an agent need broad credentials to perform a narrow task? That is the useful systems question here. Broad API keys and anonymous service accounts force customers to choose between access and control. In the January 2026 Okta-commissioned survey, 69% of 150 decision-makers said security concerns slowed agent adoption. Routant proposes letting customers specify resources, require human review, and inspect action histories. That addresses an identifiable institutional blockage and could return control to the customer. But the dossier establishes neither the cost of that blockage nor how much Routant removes. The survey measures category pain, not this product’s effectiveness.

The strongest case against my pass is that deployment permission could become the scarce ingredient beneath an enormous agent economy. A single integration might spare vendors repeated security work while letting customers accept useful software safely. That fits my recurring interest in mundane workflow repair and concealed infrastructure. But Keycard already documents scoped credentials and delegation chains; Arcade documents action-level authorization, approvals, and audits. Routant has not demonstrated a controlled technical asset or a dependency those alternatives cannot serve. A necessary function can still be a difficult independent business.

I also have no founder story to connect this problem to someone who has been unable to leave it alone. Founder identities, prior experiments, recruiting history, and domain experience are unavailable. Those are missing facts, not evidence of opportunism; the reported team size of zero tells me nothing reliable about execution. Likewise, absent revenue is ordinary at this stage. My exceptions for pre-revenue companies depend on affirmative substitutes—working prototypes, substantive customer commitments, or demonstrated founder obsession. None is documented here. There is no verified product interaction or repeated user behavior to make that exception persuasive.

Routant’s proposed customer controls point in a direction I like on privacy, but permission settings and audit trails do not establish consent boundaries, data custody, or effective revocation. I cannot infer either a severe privacy violation or a cleared privacy gate. Unknown leadership also does not establish a governance problem. I pass because the evidence supports a useful problem more strongly than a particular company capable of solving it. One verifiable event would reopen the decision: a named customer renews a paid production deployment after an independently observed test confirms that revoked permissions stop agent actions inside its private systems. That would put something concrete on the table where the dossier currently offers an intention.
