# Bayworks -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Eighty dealerships in 110 days makes me want to meet these founders. It does not tell me what happened when they were blocked. Bayworks reports a remarkably fast deployment, but the dossier contains no specific instance of Bansal or Goyal encountering resistance, finding a workaround, and shipping it. I would keep those facts separate. Customer acquisition is evidence of demand; it cannot also supply the missing story about resourcefulness. Goyal’s backend work at Navi and internships at Scapia and Cisco establish relevant experience, but they do not answer that question.

The product does something concrete. A technician dictates against a repair-order line, gets asked for missing measurements, and reviews a cause-and-correction narrative. The Android release dated October 3, 2026 gives this more substance than an architecture diagram. Asking for missing information could be the valuable part: faster typing alone does little if the resulting warranty documentation remains incomplete. I give the shipped workflow meaningful technical credit, while reserving the highest scores for demonstrated accuracy, successful DMS writeback, or measured improvement in claim acceptance. None is established here.

The strongest argument for investing is that Bayworks may already have found a narrow well. Technicians lose billable time writing narratives, and dealerships have a financial reason to make those narratives complete. The reported 80+ dealerships, including an unnamed top-50 group, are substantial evidence for this stage. But the 4,000 daily repairs describe those dealerships’ activity; they do not establish 4,000 daily uses of Bayworks. The group’s $3B+ revenue likewise belongs to the customer. With no technician retention, paid conversion, or product usage figures, I can credit distribution without assuming habitual use. The Android listing’s “0+” downloads is too uninformative to overturn the deployment claim.

Bayworks also faces a useful test of what it actually owns. Tekion already offers native technician voice documentation. An independent application therefore has to earn its place through better results or by serving dealerships whose existing systems leave this job unfinished. DMS integrations and repair histories could make that position durable, but the histories belong to the dealerships and broader learning rights are unverified. This resembles my unstated preference for concrete proof before a round only partially: there is a released product and reported deployment, but no independently measured proof spike. The dossier also supplies neither a founder’s prior collision with this problem nor an evidence-driven product revision, so both dimensions receive their missing-evidence defaults.

I would pass on the evidence available. The bull case stings: two founders may have installed a useful product across 80 dealerships faster than larger competitors could organize a meeting. Verified recurring use could make that deployment strong enough to invoke the working-product and unmistakable-pull exceptions in my framework. The current report does not establish that override, and the resourcefulness gate remains unmet. Nor would I invent red flags: undocumented cofounder history is not evidence of an improvised relationship, and DMS dependence is not proof of a partner-led growth plan. My one flip condition is a documented deployment case showing a named integration blocker, the founders’ dated shipped workaround, and repair-order logs demonstrating sustained technician use afterward.
