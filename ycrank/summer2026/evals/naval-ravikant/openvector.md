# OpenVector -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the founders’ résumés; it is what they have already built. Andrey Gizdov co-authored a CVPR 2025 Spotlight system that processes roughly 3% of pixels with claimed 5–10× lower bandwidth, while Vishal Urlam previously worked on camera systems, network protocols, and embedded infrastructure. OpenVector is a direct continuation of that specific knowledge: real-time vision on constrained hardware, connected to legacy operational systems. This is unusually credible founder-problem fit for pre-seed.

The harder question is what remains after a competitor copies the workflow builder. Natural-language event definitions, webhooks, and existing-camera support are reproducible, and Spot AI, Ambient.ai, and Voxel already have overlapping products and substantially more capital. The foveated-vision work is a real technical asset—not merely an AI wrapper—but the dossier does not establish exclusive rights, a proprietary model, or deployment data that compounds. Sampling fewer pixels reduces cost; it does not yet create increasing returns.

The market is broad enough to support a large company but too broad to constitute a strategy. Manufacturing stoppages, warehouse processes, retail queues, and security incidents share a camera-to-action primitive, within a video-analytics market projected to reach $37.84 billion by 2030. They do not share identical buyers, reliability thresholds, integrations, or liability. Demo-led selling across gyms, car washes, offices, warehouses, and restaurants risks turning a horizontal platform into a collection of custom workflows. Leverage disappears when every customer requires equivalent sales and configuration labor.

There is direct pricing—$449 monthly or $6,499 upfront for the station—but a refundable $100 reservation is not a transaction proving value capture. “Live use cases” without named customers, deployment counts, retention, or revenue cannot distinguish persistent utility from demonstrations. More importantly, no user currently creates value or distribution for another user. Deployment-specific corrections might eventually produce a proprietary learning asset, but inferred future data rights are not a moat today.

The bull case is sharp: a technically credible team has combined edge hardware, model efficiency, computer use, and a large installed base of cameras just as vision-language systems become deployable. A pre-seed exception for absent monetization can apply when product behavior or technical progress is already extraordinary. Here the CVPR result supports technical progress, but there is no corresponding evidence of obsessive repeat usage, capital-efficient adoption, or a self-reinforcing system. Nor does the dossier reveal integrity, morale, or artificial-deadline red flags; the problem is missing compounding evidence, not adverse founder conduct.

My base case is that within 36 months OpenVector specializes in one operational vertical—most plausibly manufacturing or logistics—and reaches $2–5 million ARR through enterprise deployments, but remains a sales-led vision vendor rather than a general autonomous-worker network. I would not write the check yet. The verifiable flip condition is ten paying sites in one vertical, with six-month retention and documented evidence that each deployment’s proprietary corrections improve accuracy or deployment time for subsequent customers without proportional engineering labor.
