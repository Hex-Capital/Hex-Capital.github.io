# Letter -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Pregasen has done something more informative than describe a market: he ran the writing agency whose problems informed Letter. That is specific knowledge acquired through serving customers. Letterpress-1’s November 2 beta adds a shipped artifact: software that reads growth signals and produces website changes as pull requests with live previews. I give the founder meaningful credit for that progression. Columbia and the prior CEO title contribute much less; the dossier does not establish what those experiences produced.

The investment turns on what Letter retains when someone reproduces that workflow. Sandboxes, previews, Slack interfaces, and MCP access make the product usable. Company-reported task-specific fine-tuning and brand evaluations offer a possible technical distinction, but there is no demonstrated performance advantage or exclusive training asset. Customer experiment outcomes could become valuable feedback. The dossier does not establish that Letter captures those outcomes reliably, owns reusable rights, or improves its recommendations with each deployment. I cannot buy a compounding asset on the strength of a proposed feedback loop.

The strongest argument against passing is the combination of agency experience and apparent customer pull. Pregasen names Cloudflare, Speakeasy, Runlayer, and others, plus “100+ more.” If those teams repeatedly delegate consequential website changes, Letter could acquire unusually useful knowledge about which interventions produce growth. My exceptions for an early prototype without a distribution loop leave room for this bet. But named customers do not establish repeated delegation, and the dossier supplies neither exceptional capital efficiency nor founder recruiting evidence sufficient to override the technical uncertainty. Lettertrace’s 80 GitHub stars belong to a separate product; they do not establish Letterpress-1 adoption.

Letter does fit my pattern of turning individual expertise into software others can use without hiring the expert. Its open-source acquisition channel is also directionally attractive. Neither establishes that users create value for one another: no contribution, referral, or shared-learning mechanism is documented. The market offers room to maneuver—from repository-based website changes into content maintenance and broader growth execution—but the $1.23 billion AI search optimization estimate is an adjacent category, not a demonstrated expansion path. Profound already moving into execution makes access to growth signals less distinctive. Reported subscriptions from $25 to $10,000 per month identify an intended way to charge; unverified pricing and undisclosed payment status establish little about realized value capture.

I would pass at 44/100. There is no documented integrity violation, artificial financing deadline, or dependence on investor reassurance; I see no founder veto. The weakness is structural: Letter has more evidence of knowing the work than of owning something that compounds as the work gets automated. My flip condition is one measured result: across successive customer cohorts, a Letter-owned model trained on permissioned experiment outcomes must produce a growing, reproducible conversion lift over the same workflow using a general coding agent. That would turn its operating experience into an asset I could underwrite.
