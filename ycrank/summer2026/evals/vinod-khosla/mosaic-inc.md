# Mosaic -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Mosaic addresses a real annoyance, not yet a consequential bottleneck. Engineering teams lose context across coding-agent sessions, but the dossier never quantifies the hours, dollars, or software output being lost, much less a mechanism that changes that cost by 10x or 100x. A $29.47 billion AI-code-tools TAM and a $7.71 billion multi-agent segment describe fashionable markets; they do not establish Mosaic’s consequence of success.

The product distinction is legible: multiple humans can share terminal control, observe agents, preserve state, and connect sessions across devices. But Conductor, Superset, and Pentagon already cover adjacent orchestration, persistence, or multiplayer workflows, and the dossier identifies no benchmark in completion time, handoff accuracy, security, or agent throughput. Without measured superiority or proprietary architecture, shared control is a feature competitors can reproduce, not a technical discontinuity.

The bull case is that timing and talent compensate for weak technical proof. Claude Code reached $1 billion in run-rate revenue by November 2025; Rohani has distributed-inference experience at Tenstorrent and research exposure at NVIDIA, Vector Institute, and Deep Genomics; Patil has built developer-collaboration interfaces. The July 2026 demonstrations earned coverage, and $200 per seat per month gives Mosaic a direct route to meaningful team revenue. If routine usage accumulates permissions, histories, and organizational context, a multiplayer interface could become the control plane for agentic engineering.

That case still fails the evidence test today. There is no disclosed revenue, repeat usage, retention, defensibility, or difficult recruit. More importantly, Mosaic connects agents to shared terminals while publishing no security architecture, despite credential exfiltration succeeding in 24 of 25 controlled Claude Code tests cited in the dossier. Security is the kill risk. The company supplies no experiment, threshold, cost, or milestone that demonstrates isolation under hostile agent behavior. Capital spent polishing collaboration before proving containment would remove little risk.

The economics are similarly unproven. A $200-per-seat price is not a scale model, and no evidence shows that Mosaic saves more engineering cost than it charges or can maintain margins as agent activity, storage, and synchronization grow. Platform dependence on Claude Code, Codex, and OpenCode also lets upstream vendors change authentication, permissions, or pricing while competitors copy the visible collaboration layer.

I would not buy this option yet. The downside is one check, but the upside currently resembles a crowded developer-workflow product rather than a company capable of rewriting the cost of software production. The verifiable flip condition is a controlled customer study showing at least a 5x reduction in multi-agent handoff time across ten paid teams, with zero credential escapes under a published adversarial security test. Until then, I expect Mosaic within 36 months either to pivot toward secure agent-session infrastructure or remain below $3 million ARR as orchestration platforms absorb multiplayer control.
