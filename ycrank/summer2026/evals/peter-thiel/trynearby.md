# TryNearby -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The contested truth is that local restaurant discovery is becoming a recurring logistics problem, not a sequence of bespoke influencer campaigns. The claim is falsifiable: if restaurants do not renew after novelty and complimentary-visit effects fade, or if creator exposure cannot produce attributable incremental sales, there is no secret—only an agency with software. TryNearby has meaningful evidence on the first test: 120+ paying restaurants after ten months, over 90% monthly retention, and approximately half of new customers arriving through owner referrals. But the reported revenue gains and 35 million views lack independent attribution, and the dossier never identifies the prevailing belief that these results contradict. This is a promising hypothesis under test, not yet a demonstrated secret.

Southern California restaurants form a legitimate narrow beachhead. Thousands of nearby creators, 2,500 videos, and more than 10,000 agent-managed texts each month create local liquidity and useful preference, availability, travel, and rejection data. The problem is value capture. Hyper and Tablevert already automate local-creator campaigns for restaurants, while Hummingbirds coordinates hyperlocal creators more broadly. Operational data becomes a monopoly mechanism only when it measurably improves fill rates, outcomes, or retention in a way a competitor cannot reproduce. Today, the evidence establishes density; it does not establish dominance.

The founding structure is better than the ordinary creator-economy startup. Yousef Abdelfattah helped build FaZe Clan from age 14, while Obaida Albaroudi and Ahmad Ibrahim previously built Clipping Exe together; Ibrahim’s infrastructure tracked more than 10 billion views, and Albaroudi reportedly helped grow a consumer application from $360,000 to $2 million ARR. This is not a team assembled around a fashionable market slide. It also matches the less-publicized pattern of backing operators who understand consumer and commerce systems. But only two founders have documented prior working history, and the dossier supplies no direct evidence about full-time status, ownership, salaries, or secondary liquidity. There is no affirmative founding red flag, but economic alignment remains unproved.

Distribution is TryNearby’s strongest fact. At $399 per month and 120+ customers, disclosed pricing implies at least $47,880 MRR, or roughly $575,000 ARR. Owner referrals generating about half of new restaurant acquisition suggest the product’s outcome can help sell the next account, while California Restaurant Association membership and the Southern California creator funnel provide identifiable channels rather than the usual incantation of “partnerships and PLG.” Yet over 90% monthly retention may still mean nearly 10% monthly churn; without cohort data and creator-visit gross margins, the same evidence can describe either an efficient network or a labor-intensive service replacing customers almost as quickly as it acquires them.

The strongest bull case is that 37% month-over-month growth, referral-led acquisition, and dense local supply already constitute more validation than most pre-seed companies ever obtain. If those economics repeat city by city, the interaction data could compound into a genuine local network. But one $399 subscription must expand across an enormous number of geographically fragmented SMBs to produce a power-law outcome, and each city restarts the liquidity problem. Platform dependence, endorsement compliance, and competing campaign operators are manageable individually; together they make durability depend on execution rather than proprietary technology. The known exceptions in my framework require either a verified zero-to-one event or customer behavior so powerful that it directly overrides the failed gate. TryNearby has real demand, but no such monopoly override yet.

I would not write a personal check today. This is distribution before monopoly: unusually good evidence that restaurants will buy, but insufficient evidence that TryNearby will own what it creates. One condition would flip the decision: launch a second non-California city and reach 100 paying restaurants within nine months while preserving at least 90% six-month cohort retention, 70% gross margin, and 40% referral-sourced acquisition. That would turn geographic replication from an inferred possibility into a measured mechanism.
