# Verdun -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Multimodal AI may let commercial-property operators buy verified security responses with substantially less human review; Verdun becomes a large company if that reduction exceeds its inference costs without increasing missed threats. That is the controlling variable. The dossier identifies its components: nuisance queues reportedly delay review by more than 20 minutes, while video ingestion and model selection can produce negative unit economics. Verdun’s filtering cascade supplies a causal approach, but no measured operating threshold establishes that the economics work.

The October 2026 claim that agents became capable of interpreting footage and taking follow-on actions roughly twelve months earlier gives Verdun a plausible technology window. Contextual reasoning could remove work that object detection merely forwards to an operator. However, the dossier quantifies neither labor displaced nor inference cost per monitored site. The estimated $2.382 billion monitoring category establishes an existing spending pool; it does not establish that Verdun can expand its margins or addressable demand. This is precisely where my physical-business caution matters: selling a monitoring service only becomes a different economic proposition if technology changes its delivery costs.

The strongest argument for investing before those measurements is the founders’ prior operating experience. Sonny Tai founded Actuate, and Tung Hoang built its first gun-detection model. Actuate’s reported 110,000 cameras across more than 15,000 sites belong to their previous company, but they establish relevant experience with deployment conditions and security buyers. Their prior working relationship also makes this more credible than two AI engineers discovering security through a market report. That earns substantial founder credit. It supplies neither Verdun customer traction nor evidence that the new architecture is economical.

Verdun’s product has more substance than a video-summary feature. Site-specific protocols, temporal analysis, and escalation to operators place it inside a recurring operational process. Better models could improve that process while Verdun retains the site context and response responsibility. Nevertheless, the advertised operator-feedback loop does not yet establish proprietary workflow state or an expensive-to-reproduce dataset. Hakimo already offers contextual verification and autonomous responses. This market is crystallizing, and shared access to better models could improve competitors just as quickly. Verdun needs a measurable operating advantage beyond the architecture’s description.

Demand remains unestablished in this dossier. YC admission, two engineering vacancies, newsletter coverage, and Charlie Warren’s referral solicitation are useful access signals, but none demonstrates repeated use or willingness to pay. I assign the prescribed missing-evidence score rather than infer rejection. My exception for precommercial technical teams also does not settle the decision: the dossier establishes experienced applied engineers, but not dated frontier research and a credible capital path sufficient to override absent product pull. Likewise, there is no documented change in service economics that would justify overriding the physical-business concern.

I would monitor Verdun and keep my personal capital uncommitted. The founders make the bull case credible; the missing economic proof keeps it below my investment gates. One result would change my decision: a paid, independently auditable deployment across multiple commercial sites showing positive contribution margins after inference and operator labor, with materially fewer human-review minutes and no deterioration in independently assessed threat detection or response time versus the incumbent service. That would connect the technical mechanism, customer demand, and economic outcome.
