# Assemble -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Enterprise systems are becoming agent-accessible, CIO organizations now have a plausible buyer for autonomous configuration work, and the controlling variable is whether Assemble can earn permission to execute repeated production changes—not merely solve Salesforce exercises—without creating more review labor than it removes.

The product is unusually well constructed around that variable. Assemble records Salesforce queries and mutations, scopes changes into verified branches, generates tests, validates against a shadow environment, and supports deterministic rollback. Completing 30 Superbadges in roughly 6.4 hours with a 93% first-attempt pass rate is credible technical proof that the agent can reason through configuration tasks. It does not establish the threshold that matters, however: trusted operation inside a live system containing undocumented dependencies, privileged data, and organizationally specific logic.

The technology shift is real but the economic step change remains unmeasured. Governed connectors, APIs, and emerging MCP interfaces make execution inside systems of record newly feasible, while the adjacent system-integration services market was estimated at $491.5 billion in 2024. Yet there is no evidence that Assemble reduces implementation cost by 50%, compresses a six-month project into weeks, or produces software-like margins after enterprise onboarding and exception handling. Salesforce, Workato, SAP, Oracle, and Workday also control the interfaces and distribution Assemble needs. A large services pool is not automatically a large software market.

The strongest bull case is that technical capability precedes customer evidence at this stage. The founders have relevant agent and evaluation experience: Aliyan worked at LangChain, contributed to a repository with 10,400 stars, and previously built ParaAI to more than 100,000 users and $50,000 ARR; the broader team combines Stanford AI research, AWS, Apple, and cybersecurity exposure. This is more than a generic wrapper team. The architecture also owns workflow state—dependencies, permissions, tests, mutations, and rollback—so stronger models should improve task completion rather than absorb the entire product.

The objection still stings because there is no demonstrated product-market pull. The 629 Trailhead badges and 371,300 points measure agent competence in a standardized environment, not whether an IT leader will delegate production authority, pay materially, and return for the next change. Hiring two go-to-market roles before disclosing a deployment, paid pilot, or repeat user risks scaling sales around an unresolved trust boundary. None of the known exceptions applies cleanly: this is neither frontier research with scarce model-building talent nor a physical market whose unit economics have already been reconstructed.

My 36-month base case is that Assemble narrows into a Salesforce-specific change-control and validation product at $2–5 million ARR rather than becoming a general agent across ERP, CRM, and HRIS. I would monitor rather than invest today. The single flip condition is three named enterprises each renewing after allowing Assemble to execute at least 100 production changes with fewer than 1% requiring human reversal; that would simultaneously demonstrate trust, recurring pull, and an economic workflow rather than a benchmark.
