# Exosat -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The obstacle is real: countries outside the U.S. and China want satellite connectivity that a foreign government cannot switch off. Edward Ge’s response has been unusually concrete for a pre-seed company. Exosat designed around ITAR/EAR-controlled content, published ITU filings for 11,000 satellites, booked an April 2027 launch, and obtained company-reported LOIs exceeding $400 million across six nations. This is more than waiting for permission. But the dossier supplies neither the elapsed time from blocker to workaround nor a shipped Exosat system that changed the situation. It is intermediate evidence of resourcefulness, not the completed proof.

Ge is not merely a finance graduate with a space presentation. He previously helped launch two Aethero satellites, grew that company to millions of dollars in revenue, and worked on a $109,740 Air Force satellite-edge-computing award. Those are functioning artifacts attributable to the founder. They do not, however, validate Exosat’s claimed $300,000 satellite cost, days-long production cycle, three-to-five-year life, or multi-band service. The first spacecraft remains eight months from launch, and commercial service is targeted for the end of 2028. The concrete pre-round proof spike I look for in hard technical companies belongs mostly to Ge’s previous company, not this one.

The $400 million of LOIs is the strongest case for demand. Government and network-operator procurement often must begin before hardware exists, so six countries expressing interest is more informative here than an ordinary waitlist. Yet none of the customers is named, no deposits or executed capacity contracts are disclosed, and the early pilots have no reported usage. Exosat therefore has prospective institutional demand rather than a narrow group pulling a crude product back. Its distribution also depends on network operators, national agencies, and device manufacturers—the exact kind of large-partner chain that becomes dangerous when the internal owner, timetable, and incentives are unspecified.

The arithmetic makes that distinction decisive. Even at the claimed $300,000 manufacturing cost, 11,000 satellites imply $3.3 billion of spacecraft before launches, gateways, replacements, and network operations. Meanwhile Starlink produced $11.4 billion of 2025 revenue, AST had 13 BlueBird satellites in orbit by August 2026, and Lynk had operating spacecraft plus roughly 51 signed mobile-network operators. Exosat’s spectrum filing is a potentially valuable position, but it is also a deployment clock. A filing does not behave like a network until capital and hardware arrive.

The deepest positive is founder-originated schlep. Ge has already built space companies, launched satellites, sold to Blue Origin, Booz Allen Hamilton, and AFRL, and encountered the export controls, spectrum coordination, manufacturing, and government sales that make this market unpleasant. This does not look like a “sovereign Starlink” idea invented from a market report. The weakest evidence is flexibility: the dossier contains no rejected hypothesis, customer observation, or changed route. Exosat may eventually become a satellite manufacturer, capacity wholesaler, or constellation operator, but listing several routes is not evidence of changing intelligently among them.

The strongest objection to passing is that orbital infrastructure cannot offer the sort of immediate product proof expected from software. A founder with two prior launches, a booked new launch, an 11,000-satellite filing, early pilots, and $400 million of multinational LOIs may have assembled exactly the right sequence of pre-launch evidence. The known exceptions in my framework permit missing conventional signals when working technology, unmistakable pull, or independently validated proof replaces them. Here the prior launches make that argument serious, but they do not validate Exosat’s new cost structure, and anonymous nonbinding demand does not yet supply the override.

I would not write the check today. My 36-month forecast is that by August 2029 Exosat will have no more than one operational demonstration satellite, less than $3 million in annualized revenue, and most of the reported $400 million LOI pipeline still unconverted. The one verifiable flip condition is a named customer making a non-refundable payment under an executed capacity contract after Exosat’s first satellite independently demonstrates the promised service and approximately $300,000 unit cost. That would turn both the technical claim and the institutional interest into evidence.
