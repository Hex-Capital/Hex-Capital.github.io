# hiloop -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Fifty Nvidia B200 GPUs running 4,188 experiments in two days make **hiloop** a technically ambitious infrastructure bet built by consensus-shaped experts. Technical risk is **hiloop’s** strongest dimension; founder defiance is its weakest. Karan Brar led ML infrastructure at DynamoAI, while Thomas Boser worked in production ML at Discord, SoFi, Crosswise, and Reducto. That is excellent preparation. It is not evidence of defying the field. **hiloop** is trying to automate the research loop, but the founders currently look like experts extrapolating that loop rather than instigators breaking it.

**Genesis AI** is the high rung on technical risk because full-stack robot intelligence requires foundation-model training, embodiment, and real-world deployment to work together. **hiloop** shares the load-bearing mechanism: autonomous systems propose, execute, and statistically verify thousands of experiments, making compute itself part of the product rather than a rented input. But **hiloop** diverges where the miracles begin. Its disclosed accomplishments—0.9016 validation bits-per-byte and a 12.54% NanoGPT runtime reduction—show engineering optimization, not a new learning architecture. Fifty B200s establish real compute risk, but **hiloop** has not yet shown a technical barrier that Prime Intellect, Together AI, or a well-funded internal platform group cannot reproduce.

The generic GPT-4 sales-call wrapper fails because its founders merely package an incumbent model around a workflow and call 20% productivity a revolution. That failure mechanism only partly appears in **hiloop**: brokered secrets, forkable snapshots, experiment lineage, and SQL-queryable traces are deeper than an API wrapper, yet all remain orchestration features around models and accelerators owned by others. Brar and Boser have no documented controversial thesis, public break with expert consensus, or prior pivot—no evidence, applying the absent-evidence default. **hiloop** could still become important, as Glean did despite an expert-continuity founder, but credentials cannot substitute for a contrarian mechanism.

The projected $223.5 billion AI-infrastructure market matters less than the economic mechanism **hiloop** might break. If autonomous experimentation reduces model research from human-designed campaigns to continuously running machine loops, **hiloop** could collapse the current economics of ML engineering and accelerate every downstream AI capability. Over 18 months, that mechanism compounds only if results transfer across campaigns: every experiment must make the next customer’s search more efficient. Without that cross-customer learning, **hiloop** remains expensive experiment management, and 4,188 benchmark runs become usage rather than intelligence. The consequence is potentially enormous, but **hiloop** has not yet demonstrated that the loop improves itself rather than merely operating itself.

Karan Brar’s DynamoAI and Reducto experience gives **hiloop** direct knowledge of ML-platform pain, while Boser’s work at Discord and SoFi adds production context. The learning-rate score flips upward only if **hiloop** documents a thesis revision driven by customer evidence—for example, abandoning generic campaign orchestration after discovering that evaluation design, not experiment execution, is the binding constraint. No prior founder exit, product pivot, or “we were wrong” moment appears in the dossier. I do not penalize **hiloop** for being two people at pre-seed; I do decline to invent learning-rate evidence because YC selected them.

**OpenAI** justified my $50 million check because its roadmap required capital at a scale that frightened normal venture portfolios, while the success-state justified that capital. **hiloop’s** use of 50 B200s over two days suggests a roadmap that can absorb serious money, and hosted plus customer-cloud deployments create legitimate infrastructure demands. But **hiloop** has not shown that it must own the compute, train a foundation model, or finance more than $50 million before reaching a commercial milestone. If customers supply their own clouds, capital intensity moves off **hiloop’s** balance sheet. That makes the opportunity financeable, but it weakens the specific reason my capital is advantaged.

Prime Intellect’s reported 6,000 customers make my 36-month forecast unforgiving: by July 2029, **hiloop** will have pivoted from general recursive-self-improvement infrastructure into managed optimization and evaluation for coding agents, reaching $3 million–$8 million ARR rather than becoming the universal AI-research control plane. The mechanism is bundling. Together AI and Prime Intellect already own adjacent compute and training surfaces, while **hiloop’s** strongest public results come from stock coding agents and NanoGPT. Specialization gives **hiloop** a defensible evaluation corpus; remaining horizontal leaves it competing against suppliers that can bundle orchestration with compute.

The 0.9016 validation result is the strongest objection to my forecast because **hiloop** has already shown that two stock agents can coordinate 4,188 experiments and beat the cited prior result. The bull case is the Glean exception: domain experts build an initially ordinary-looking control plane, proprietary usage data compounds, and the layer becomes indispensable before incumbents recognize the consequence. **hiloop** could become the operating system for automated research. But seven public repositories with at most one star each, no disclosed customers or revenue, and Prime Intellect’s direct overlap leave no evidence that such compounding exists yet. I would flip to Invest if **hiloop** showed three unrelated production customers each running more than 10,000 autonomous experiments monthly, with measured evidence that learning from one campaign improves another customer’s search efficiency.
