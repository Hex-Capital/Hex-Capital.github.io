# Praxis -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Praxis identifies a real cost pool: an unnamed firm estimates losing $350,000 annually through slow responses, and a corporate-law partner reports spending 1.5 hours daily on client setup and engagement letters. Recovering that time could matter substantially to a small practice. But neither figure measures what Praxis actually recovers. The consequence case earns some credit for identifying a concrete bottleneck; the dossier does not establish how administrative automation makes legal services radically cheaper or available to a much larger population. The $3.34 billion practice-management software market cannot supply that missing causal link.

The technical question is whether Praxis can eliminate work that existing systems still require humans to perform. Clio already offers workflow-executing agents. Lawmatics already qualifies inquiries against firm rules and triggers follow-ups. Praxis spans intake through invoicing, but broader coverage does not establish a discontinuity. There is no comparative result on completion rate, human intervention or execution accuracy. I cannot call this a generic model wrapper from the evidence supplied. Equally, I cannot purchase a technical advantage merely because the product is described as agents.

The strongest argument for investing is the team’s ability to turn working AI into adoption. Diana reports taking Gorgias’s AI Agent to 1,000-plus customers and $8 million ARR in under a year. Those are prior-employer results, but they are relevant. Maxence has built multimodal systems at SightCall and worked in data engineering at Uber and BlaBlaCar. Praxis is already live with U.S. law firms. That combination could produce a substantial business before a more technically ambitious competitor finds distribution. My execution-heavy investment pattern keeps this case open. However, the record does not document belief revision or difficult recruiting against a named weakness, so those credentials cannot earn the founder criterion’s higher scores.

My known exceptions would require an exceptional instigator addressing a giant existing value pool, or a consequence so large that weak initial proof becomes tolerable. Praxis has credible operators and an identifiable workflow problem, but the dossier establishes neither override. Its clearest competitive risk is that incumbents deliver enough automation inside systems firms already use. That resembles my concern about AI businesses differentiated through implementation and distribution, although architectural sameness is unproven. Nor is there evidence that ordinary usage creates the proprietary learning loop I look for in scarce expert workflows.

The next dollar should test whether supervised execution removes substantial work after review and correction are counted. Conflict checks and engagement actions make that measurement consequential. Live deployment establishes that the team can ship; it does not establish a threshold for acceptable errors, an experiment budget or a stop condition. Likewise, undisclosed pricing, inference costs and support requirements leave economic parity unproven. I see no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence. I simply lack a cost model to underwrite.

I pass with my own money. My flip condition is one independently auditable, 90-day study across at least five paying firms showing Praxis completes a matched intake-to-invoice workload with at least 10x fewer total human minutes than the firms’ existing Clio-based process, no increase in material errors, and lower fully loaded cost including review and support. That result would establish a mechanism capable of changing the economics of running a practice. Today, the evidence establishes useful automation and credible execution.
