# Orca Aerospace -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Onboard AI could let satellite operators buy useful decisions between ground contacts; the controlling variable is how much operator intervention Orca Aerospace can eliminate safely per spacecraft. That determines both customer savings and whether this becomes a repeatable software business. The dossier establishes the communications bottleneck and a plausible response, but supplies no measured intervention reduction or customer threshold for adoption. I can identify the market mechanism. I cannot yet underwrite it.

The strongest evidence is the product architecture: AI selects objectives, a deterministic planner constructs actions, and guardrails approve execution. Orca is targeting an operational loop with consequences, which gives it more substance than a reasoning interface alone. Better models could improve objective selection while the planner and safety layer remain necessary. However, the engineering vacancy describes avionics integration and hardware-in-the-loop testing responsibilities; it does not establish completed integrations or accumulated validation assets. The architecture earns credit. Presumed switching costs do not.

The August 2026 Shield AI–Sedaro demonstration of 189 approved commands aboard a NOVI satellite makes the technical opening more credible. It also places Orca in a category where competing implementations already exist. That demonstration supports feasibility, not Orca’s differentiation or customer economics. Lyra’s finding that model memory affects board selection and board power affects spacecraft sizing matters here: moving intelligence onboard can introduce physical costs alongside labor savings. The quoted $5.88 billion autonomy market includes hardware and services, so it cannot establish the revenue pool available to Orca’s software.

The strongest case against passing is the founders’ unusually direct experience. Jacob Ososke spent more than a decade in Lockheed Martin GNC and founded its first AI-GNC team; Hakan Chunton brings MIT trajectory research and onboard collision-avoidance experience. Their joint concept study with Lyra suggests they understand deployment constraints rather than merely attaching AI to a spacecraft pitch. This fits my pattern of entering physical industries when specialized expertise can change the economics. But expertise has not yet demonstrated that economic change. The research-stage exception would require distinctive technical results and a credible capital path; neither is established here. Their biographies also provide no documented prior startup learning or costly personal commitment, so I leave that dimension at its default.

The Lyra study is useful technical engagement, but it does not establish repeat usage, a purchase commitment, or a customer accepting responsibility for deployment. The 302 LinkedIn followers, hiring activity, and CAGE registration add no product-market-pull evidence. I would not treat absent revenue as a failure at this stage. I would distinguish the opportunity from demonstrated demand. Likewise, the relevant physical-business red flag remains unresolved: platform-specific integration could consume the value that recurring software licenses are supposed to capture. There is no evidence of commodity margins today, but there is also no basis for assuming software margins.

I would pass on a personal check today. Orca has a credible workflow architecture, while the decisive market variable remains unmeasured; it falls below my market gates. My single flip condition is a customer-verified paid orbital deployment showing at least a 50% reduction in ground-operator intervention at unchanged mission requirements, with the full integration and onboard-compute costs included in the customer’s savings calculation. That is an underwriting threshold, not a reported result. Meeting it would connect Orca’s technical design to the economic reason this company should exist.
