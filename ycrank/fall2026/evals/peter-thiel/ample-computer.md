# Ample Computer -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Ample Computer’s potentially important truth is that agents will choose infrastructure before humans choose a cloud provider. Its agent-created, human-claimed deployments give that proposition a concrete product form. The test is whether those deployments become enduring customer relationships once humans assume responsibility and payment. The dossier establishes the workflow, but neither a prevailing belief that named peers reject nor customer behavior validating the opposing proposition. The secret remains a hypothesis.

The competitive evidence is more definite. Railway already offers agent deployment skills; Render exposes deployment and debugging integrations. Ample’s microVMs, MCP connector, CLI, and HTTPS deployment workflow establish a functioning product. They do not establish proprietary technology or a market Ample can own. Agent-built web applications describe an initial use case, but the dossier identifies no bounded customer segment with an observable monopoly mechanism. This activates my crowded-market red flag: competitors already deliver the central function, while the documented differentiation concerns the sequence of account ownership.

The founding structure remains unknown rather than adverse. Ravi Mishra has prior entrepreneurial experience, and Ishan has published infrastructure integration work. Their separate Shopify histories do not establish that they shipped together. Full-time status, ownership, economic exposure, and even Ishan’s formal founder role remain unverified. I would not infer convenience matching, outsourced engineering, or poor alignment from those gaps. Equally, biographies cannot establish the durable founding commitment this criterion measures.

The strongest objection to passing is Ample’s distribution. Its Anthropic-verified connector arrived in October 2026, and its Hermes plugin puts deployment inside another agent environment. These are shipped acquisition routes, more substantial than a promise to pursue partnerships. If an agent creates the application and selects its infrastructure, human account claiming could turn deployment into customer acquisition. The six showcased applications make that possibility concrete. But their ownership and payment status are undisclosed; marketplace availability establishes access, without establishing conversion, repeat usage, or economical acquisition.

Ample also has one coherent revenue engine: metered infrastructure usage, with per-second compute billing, a one-minute minimum, and monthly invoices. Successful applications could expand their consumption. Yet public unit prices, gross margins, and customer spending are unavailable, so there is no arithmetic supporting a power-law outcome. That is an evidence limit, not a penalty for lacking pre-seed revenue. My interest in the complete AI production stack applies where a company controls a consequential constraint; the dossier has not shown that Ample controls one.

I pass at the current evidence level. The strongest distribution possibility does not remove the competitive gate. My known exceptions require evidence such as an independently demonstrated technical discontinuity or persistent customer transactions that directly answers the failed gate; neither is documented here. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: a controlled migration study showing that paying, human-claimed applications remain on Ample despite funded moves to functionally equivalent Railway or Render deployments, with the retained usage attributable to an Ample-controlled switching barrier. That would turn a plausible workflow into evidence of value capture.
