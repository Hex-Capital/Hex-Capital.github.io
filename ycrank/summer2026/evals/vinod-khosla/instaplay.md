# Instaplay -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence case is underpowered. Instaplay can collapse game creation from coding, asset production, and engine setup into an English prompt and a browser link. That could turn more players into creators inside a $201.6 billion games market. But the dossier does not quantify the hours, cost, or expert labor displaced, nor show that this creates an order-of-magnitude expansion in participation. A large entertainment pool is not itself a 100x societal consequence.

The product has real usage: three founders have reached 20,000 creators and 100,000 monthly users through organic discovery, group chats, Discord, and ChatGPT. That is unusually informative at pre-seed. The technical evidence points the other way. FunBench reports only 3.0–7.9 seconds of median engaged play, alongside inverted controls, incomplete progression, and broken interfaces. Astrocade already reports 20 million users and Verse8 reports 3.5 million monthly users with similar prompt-generated experiences. Instaplay has demonstrated distribution, not a measured 5–10x technical discontinuity.

FunBench is the best evidence in the dossier because 3,000 blinded votes attack the actual kill risk: whether generated games are fun enough to retain players. This is better than spending the next dollar on influencer acquisition. But there is no declared pass threshold, experiment budget, retention target, or if/then response. A benchmark becomes a risk-removal milestone only when failure changes what the company builds.

The team looks like doers. Gary Wu brings AI product work and an earlier LLM-evaluation startup; Frank Liu brings Instagram engineering and serious gaming familiarity; Akhil Aggarwal adds globally deployed AWS security work and venture exposure. Those skills are complementary, and the usage achieved by three people matters more than their résumés. Still, the dossier supplies no example of a material belief revised after evidence or a difficult recruit brought in to own game quality, model architecture, or consumer economics.

The strongest bull case is the known exception for a tiny team using an initially incremental product to enter an enormous existing value pool. Instaplay’s organic reach and prompt-to-link distribution could become a broader social-creation network. The material difference is that funded competitors already offer comparable creation loops at much greater scale, while Instaplay has not shown that its telemetry improves model performance. The portfolio’s broader consumer pattern keeps the company from being dismissed merely for selling entertainment, but the red flag remains: today the differentiation appears to be harness, interface, and distribution around prevailing models.

Economics closes the decision. Each delivered game costs $0.86–$7.11 in model spend, the product is free to start, and no paid tier or contribution-margin model is disclosed. Viral creation could therefore scale cost faster than value. My 36-month base case is a pivot toward game-prototyping or evaluation tools for professional studios rather than a durable consumer network. I pass today. I would flip to Invest if one controlled benchmark shows Instaplay’s proprietary data and harness raising median engaged play from at most 7.9 seconds to at least 60 seconds while holding generation cost below $1.
