# Roma -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Roma has shipped enough to make the interesting question concrete: what happens when its Operator gets stuck? Its browser documentation names verification barriers, blocked automation, and payments that still require the user. These are opportunities to observe resourcefulness. But the dossier contains no dated episode showing a founder encountering one of these obstacles, building a workaround, and getting a user’s task finished. I cannot infer that behavior from previous acquisitions. The missing evidence is how these founders respond to resistance.

The product itself deserves substantial credit. Roma has Mac distribution, an iPhone app, background execution, connected tools, and a public Claude plugin. Keeping the result beside the task is a sensible answer to work disappearing into chat histories. This fits my pattern of backing useful interfaces through which technical users can compose workflows. But the other half of that pattern is users actually appropriating the interface. A published integration establishes an artifact; it does not establish dependence. Nor does the dossier measure successful task completion or how often users must rescue the Operator.

The strongest argument for investing is the team. Senderek built Loom, Eichenwald co-founded Senic, and both companies were acquired. Eichenwald reports knowing Senderek for approximately twenty years and building a running app together. That substantially weakens the hastily assembled cofounder concern. Experienced consumer-product founders with something already shipped could discover the right use case quickly. The working-product exception to incomplete founder evidence is therefore relevant. It does not carry the decision here: Roma’s central execution claim lacks measured validation, and the dossier supplies neither unmistakable user pull nor independently validated technical proof strong enough to override the resourcefulness gate.

Who needs Roma badly enough to tolerate its interruptions? Nine US App Store ratings averaging 5.0 as of October 27 are encouraging, but they do not identify that person or establish return usage. The free download supplies no payment evidence. Todoist already exposes tasks to external AI assistants, so connectivity alone gives users little reason to move. Roma could earn that move by reliably finishing one neglected class of work. The dossier does not yet identify such a narrow well. Its public launch is distribution activity, but there is no evidence that the founders mistake launch day for growth or depend on a large partner.

I would pass today. There is no documented evidence-driven route change, and no account connecting the founders’ own recurring need to the integration and service burden they have undertaken. Those omissions receive the stated defaults; they do not establish rigidity or an invented problem. My one verifiable flip condition is a documented recovery cycle: a specific execution failure affecting a defined user niche, a founder-built fix shipped within two weeks, and those affected users subsequently returning weekly for four weeks. That would connect the software Roma has built to the founder behavior and user need on which I would risk my own money.
