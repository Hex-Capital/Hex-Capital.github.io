# SE3 Labs -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

SE3 Labs has put policies on real robots and published the failures. That is useful work. Its October 30 report supplies 100 Dream-model evaluation episodes across 10 tasks, with recordings and telemetry. Copying the software would not reproduce an operating workcell or the discipline required to run repeatable experiments. But I cannot attribute that specific knowledge to verified founders: the YC Founders section is empty, and Paige Xu’s founder status is unconfirmed. The artifacts establish company execution; they do not establish who I would be backing for the next decade.

The investment turns on what accumulates after each experiment. SE3 captures interventions and packages training trajectories, but its published work has not yet demonstrated that those trajectories improve a policy on retesting. Customer raw data also requires written authorization before reuse in general-purpose training. Those restrictions are commercially understandable. They nevertheless prevent me from assuming that serving one customer automatically improves the product for another. Aggregated benchmarking is permitted in some circumstances; an exclusive, compounding asset has not been established.

The strongest argument for investing is that physical evaluation could become the scarce complement to abundant robot intelligence. SE3 evaluated Dream through a closed-source API, showing that it can test models without possessing their weights. Its September study also measured a concrete difference: 18 completed placements with agentic control versus six with code-as-policy, over 20 attempts each. This fits my shift toward physical infrastructure bottlenecks. A credible independent testing layer could become valuable across model developers and industrial deployments. But Poke & Wiggle already offers physical evaluation, and RoboArena demonstrates distributed academic infrastructure. Owning working stations creates an operational advantage; the dossier does not show why that advantage widens with usage.

The commercial wedge is legible: robot-policy developers need physical testing and failure data. The expansion case remains unproven. Industrial partners appear in the offering, but there is no evidence here of adjacent purchasing demand or a shared capability already unlocking those budgets. Contract-specific fees provide a direct charging mechanism, yet no price, completed paid engagement, or realized revenue is disclosed. I assign no imagined economics. Public documentation and task specifications can help developers discover SE3; they do not establish users contributing distribution or utility to one another.

I pass at this evidence level. My exceptions for unresolved monetization require an emerging network; my exceptions for missing distribution require unusually persuasive founder and prototype evidence. SE3 has the prototype evidence, but founder attribution and capital efficiency remain unknown. No integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-behavior red flag is documented. The issue is that the demonstrated output is physical testing, while the proposed compounding business remains a hypothesis. One result would reopen the decision: a documented, customer-authorized experiment in which failure data from one customer improves a second customer’s policy on held-out physical tasks without proportional additional collection labor. That would demonstrate the shared asset this investment currently asks me to assume.
