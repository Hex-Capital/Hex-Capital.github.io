# Adalat AI -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Adalat AI has persuaded courts to change how they work. Has it discovered a business that can keep the value created? The candidate secret is that Indian courts can accelerate testimony recording through multilingual AI despite entrenched manual procedures. That proposition is testable: measure completed, corrected depositions per judge-day against the previous workflow. The magistrate reporting five to six witnesses daily versus two to three provides encouraging evidence. But the dossier identifies neither a prevailing belief that named peers reject nor a controlled result establishing the opposing proposition. I cannot manufacture a contrarian secret from a useful product.

The founding structure deserves a separate judgment. Saxena’s court practice and Bhattacharya’s multilingual NLP background explain why this team could build the product. They connected in February 2024 and subsequently developed an MVP; this is not evidence of founders assembled merely to enter the current YC batch. It also does not establish their full-time status, economic exposure, or control of essential engineering. Those remain unknown. I see no documented part-time founder, investor-dominated capitalization, or outsourced core product that would trigger a founding-team rejection.

The strongest argument for investing is institutional distribution. Oxford documents more than 4,000 courts across nine states; the company subsequently reports 6,300 courtrooms across 11 states. Kerala’s requirement to use Adalat primarily for witness depositions, effective November 1, 2025, is unusually concrete government adoption. Agreements, training, and administrative authorization constitute an operating channel. This also fits my less advertised interest in systems that make contested information legible. Deployment on this scale is substantial evidence against dismissing Adalat as another untested legal AI application. However, free delivery establishes institutional acceptance without establishing an acquisition and support model that commercial receipts can sustain.

Can that distribution become a monopoly? The dossier does not establish it. Court integrations are real, but exclusive access, proprietary data rights, measured switching costs, and superior technical performance remain unproved. Kerala permits approved alternatives during technical difficulties. TERES and jhana overlap meaningful product layers, although their commercial models differ. The September 2026 credential compromise adds a concrete reliability concern for a system embedded in evidentiary workflows; the reported remediation matters, but does not establish durable technical superiority. Neither institutional familiarity nor a claimed 90%+ recognition accuracy earns monopoly credit by itself.

The decisive problem is economic structure. Adalat operates as a nonprofit, and the examined court agreements expressly provide pro bono delivery without commercial fees. The $500,000 Agency Fund grant finances the mission; it does not demonstrate a revenue engine or a shareholder claim on future cash flows. This is affirmative evidence about value capture, not an ordinary pre-seed absence of revenue. My exceptions for independently demonstrated breakthroughs, persistent transaction demand, or a credible physical deployment path do not remove that obstacle. Even nationwide adoption could fulfill Adalat’s objective while producing no return for an angel investor.

I pass on an equity investment. The bull case could make Adalat an important institution, and its distribution deserves serious respect. My verifiable condition for reopening diligence would be a completed commercial spinout transaction giving investors an enforceable economic claim on a renewed, positive-gross-margin court contract. That would establish a different investable proposition; it would still require scrutiny of the secret and monopoly mechanism.
