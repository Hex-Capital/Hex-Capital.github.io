# Kebra -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Voice and computer-use agents can now turn messy technician evidence into operational actions; field-service operators buy because back-office reconstruction is expensive, and Kebra becomes large only if it can complete most post-job work across existing systems without office intervention. That completion rate is the controlling variable. The dossier explains the mechanism but provides no completion threshold, correction rate, or labor savings from Kebra deployments, leaving the primary market gate unresolved.

The product is structurally more interesting than a field-service chatbot. Kebra captures multimodal job evidence, requests missing information, preserves customer-specific context in its Company Brain, and takes actions such as filing claims, ordering parts, preparing invoices, and initiating follow-ups. Better models should improve those tasks rather than erase the workflow. This is Kebra’s strongest dimension: it owns a recurring operational loop around the model. However, it controls neither the underlying system of record nor an exclusive distribution channel.

There is meaningful pre-seed pull. Kebra was live in Florida, Pennsylvania, and California by July 2026, and Quality Air is a named customer using it across technicians and the back office. That is more informative than launch votes or follower counts. It is not yet high-conviction product-market fit because the dossier supplies no revenue, repeat-usage period, jobs processed, customer expansion, or evidence that users tolerate errors rather than reverting to existing processes.

The technology shift is real but the economic step change remains generic. A 2025 Housecall Pro survey found 40% AI adoption among its customers and average savings of four hours per week, while the FSM market is projected to grow from $5.10 billion in 2025 to $9.17 billion in 2030. Neither figure establishes Kebra’s customer ROI. More importantly, this market is already crystallizing: ServiceTitan generated $614 million in fiscal-2024 revenue, and it, Jobber, and Housecall Pro distribute overlapping AI capabilities through installed products. Model progress helps Kebra, but it also lowers the incumbents’ replication cost.

The founders have relevant construction skills—Luca Hadife worked on multimodal AI at Microsoft, while Mohamed Hosny brings systems experience from Berkeley EECS and Arista Networks—but the dossier documents neither prior startup learning nor costly commitment to field services. I treat that as missing evidence, not a negative. I also give no credit to a prospective data moat: customer-specific records can create switching costs, but existing FSM vendors already hold much of the underlying data, and Kebra has disclosed no scale, exclusivity, or reproduction-cost evidence. None of the known exception patterns applies; this is not frontier research, a demonstrated reconstruction of physical-market margins, or a scarce underlying-model team.

The strongest bull case is that fragmented contractor stacks make a neutral overlay more valuable than any incumbent’s bundled agent, while every completed job compounds Kebra’s workflow context. The three-state deployment and Quality Air usage make that plausible. They do not yet show that cross-stack breadth overcomes incumbent distribution. I would monitor rather than invest my own money today. The verifiable flip condition is 20 paying contractors each allowing Kebra to complete at least 70% of eligible post-job workflows for 90 consecutive days without back-office correction.
