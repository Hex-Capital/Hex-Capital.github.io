# Standard Machines -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Jacob Peake has specific knowledge of the terrain, but the dossier does not yet prove specific knowledge of the product. He worked on GPU architecture at Apple and published material on computer architecture, hardware/software co-design, and AI-chip architectures ([Y Combinator, Aug 2026](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/standard-machines); [Jacob Peake](https://www.jepeake.com/)). That is substantially better than credentials alone. It is not evidence that he has built an RL environment, trained an agent inside one, or sold such infrastructure to a chip-design team. The founder signal is credible but incomplete.

The central problem is durability. Standard Machines occupies the RL-environment layer, yet Cadence Cerebrus and Synopsys DSO.ai already apply reinforcement learning to chip-design optimization, while heavily funded startups attack adjacent layers. No benchmark, deployment, proprietary dataset, taped-out chip, or reward-function asset has been disclosed. If a competitor copies the code tomorrow, the dossier identifies nothing Standard Machines retains. Future customer trajectories could become scarce data, but a possible moat is not a present asset.

The strongest bull case is that the environment becomes the bottleneck: chip-design IP is private, valid reward functions are difficult to construct, and repeated deployments could accumulate failure cases that general-purpose models cannot access. Peake’s Apple experience may help him obtain and encode that knowledge. The worldwide AI-in-chip-design market was estimated at $3.46 billion in 2025 and growing 35% annually ([Research and Markets, 2025](https://www.researchandmarkets.com/reports/6035344/artificial-intelligence-ai-in-chip-design)). But Standard Machines has not established the causal path from its initial environment product into that spend, and the same data-access constraint that could create a moat could prevent the moat from ever forming.

This is adjacent to the infrastructure shift I favor, but adjacency is not substrate. There is no hardware, proprietary model, permissionless contributor system, or customer-created network in evidence. Each prospective customer currently appears to require direct company work, while no pricing, paid pilot, or transaction proves value capture. The unstated pattern therefore cuts against the company today: participants are not yet making the system more useful for one another. None of the exception patterns overrides this—there is no demonstrated prototype, self-reinforcing adoption, or extraordinary action-revealed founder evidence beyond relevant employment and writing.

I find no disclosed integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-temperament red flag. The red flag is structural: a fashionable AI layer surrounded by incumbents and competitors with hundreds of millions of dollars, without public technical proof. Within 36 months, I expect Standard Machines either to pivot into proprietary chip-design evaluation and data infrastructure or remain below $1 million ARR as a standalone RL-environment vendor. The single condition that flips my decision is three named semiconductor customers paying for repeated agent-training runs whose exclusive trajectories demonstrably improve benchmark performance across deployments.

The downside is bounded, but nonlinear upside is not enough by itself; the mechanism that preserves the upside must already be visible. I would pass now and reopen when deployment creates an asset that survives copied code.
