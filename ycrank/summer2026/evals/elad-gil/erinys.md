# Erinys -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: AI can now perform meaningful litigation work, plaintiff lawyers may buy an operating platform rather than assemble a firm themselves, and the controlling variable is whether Erinys can produce materially more collected fees per lawyer while preserving compliant firm economics. The dossier does not yet quantify that variable. A $396.8 billion legal-services market is not the relevant denominator; the relevant market is plaintiff practices willing to surrender operational control or economics to an affiliated network.

There is a real technology shift. Eve reports more than 1,000 plaintiff-firm customers and 200,000 cases, while Lawhive generated $35 million in 2025 revenue. Lawyers are placing AI into live workflows. But these facts also sharpen the counterfactual: firms can adopt AI from Eve or Supio without joining Erinys. The company must therefore prove that integrated operations improve case throughput, acquisition cost, settlement velocity, or margin enough to overcome lawyers’ preference for independence. No current evidence establishes that step change.

The strongest bull case is Erinys’s product architecture. It combines intake, staffing, matter flow, billing, collections, compliance, working capital, and software, then claims to feed operating bottlenecks back into its technology. That is substantially more durable than formatting model output: stronger models should improve an owned workflow rather than erase it. If firms are genuinely operating on this stack, shared workflow state and distribution could compound across adjacent services. However, no member-firm count, case volume, integration detail, or operating data verifies the loop today.

Demand is the weakest part of the case. The August 2026 launch solicits lawyer referrals, but there are no named firms, repeat users, signed commitments, or revenue. The April OSINT prototype proves that Zyuzin and Guo can build together; it does not validate the current law-firm network. The rapid move from False Claims Act investigation software to turnkey plaintiff firms may be intelligent discovery, but it also means the company is asking investors to underwrite a new customer, product, regulatory structure, and business model simultaneously.

The founders bring cybersecurity, OSINT, finance, and technical experience, but the dossier shows neither legal-practice depth nor prior startup learning. More importantly, none of the legitimate pre-traction exceptions applies: this is not frontier research led by a uniquely scarce technical team, and regulatory complexity is not itself a moat. Rule 5.4 restrictions and state-specific ownership regimes could force Erinys into service-heavy structures with ordinary margins—the precise failure mode in which a business is software-aware without acquiring software economics.

I would pass today. My 36-month base case is that Erinys narrows into investigation, intake, and operating software sold to existing plaintiff firms rather than becoming a national network of operated firms. The one condition that flips the decision is verifiable evidence that at least three compliant affiliated firms each generate collected-fee revenue at least 50% above comparable lawyer capacity while producing positive contribution margin after staff, marketing, and financing costs. That would validate both the controlling variable and the reason the full-stack model wins over standalone software.
