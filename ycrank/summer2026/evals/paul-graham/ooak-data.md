# Ooak Data -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that Ooak Data calls its product a library of business workflows. It is that two frontier AI labs have reportedly signed seven-figure contracts for it. These are unusually informed buyers, paying for something the five-person team has already made usable. The founders saw that static benchmarks fail to reproduce work across documents, communications, and software, then built a much uglier system: license operational data, anonymize it without destroying its structure, reconstruct digital environments, and turn them into reinforcement-learning tasks. That is a credible obstacle-workaround-result chain, though the dossier does not reveal what specifically broke during construction or how quickly the founders repaired it.

The narrow well is exceptionally clear. The users are frontier labs trying to train tool-using agents, the job is improving multi-step work, and the evidence is contracts rather than a waitlist. Partnerships with smaller labs add breadth. I cannot yet tell whether models improve enough to renew, so I would not treat contract value as retention. But for a Summer 2026 company, two seven-figure contracts are far stronger evidence than the adjacent LSAT dataset’s 300 monthly downloads or the company’s 566 LinkedIn followers. Ooak has found users who want the crude product now.

The technical proof is real but incompletely exposed. APEX Explorer shipped in April 2026, and the company says its pipeline already converts contracted company data into RL-ready datasets. The paying labs imply there is more than a diagram behind the claim. But APEX Explorer displays another company’s dataset, while the decisive anonymization, digital-twin fidelity, and model-improvement measurements remain undisclosed. I give substantial credit for a functioning production pipeline and discount it because no reproducible benchmark shows that Ooak’s licensed workflows train agents better than Mercor’s reconstructed applications or AfterQuery’s real-API environments.

The strongest objection is that Ooak may be a bespoke data contractor disguised as a compounding library. Its two dominant customers could internalize production, Mercor and Scale can bundle adjacent capabilities, and every new source company introduces consent, licensing, GDPR, deletion, and re-identification work. The company is also recruiting for six roles, including US GTM, before the dossier shows repeat purchases or expansion. That risks delegating the learning while the founders should still be personally discovering why each lab buys. The same ugliness is also the opportunity: negotiated data rights and structure-preserving anonymization are precisely the work intelligent competitors prefer to skip. If completed workflows can be reused across customers, the service burden becomes a proprietary substrate. If each contract instead requires a fresh consulting project, it does not.

The weak part of the founder proof is the story before the contracts. Thomas Aubry has relevant ML and financial-data experience, Grégoire Lamy spent years in product and operations, and Pierre-Louis Vouteau understands distribution. But the dossier does not document a founder personally encountering this training-data problem, nor does it explain the company’s first-year pivot—the rejected hypothesis, the evidence that killed it, and the route shipped next. “One pivot later” is not enough to distinguish a running back from someone who merely changed ideas. None of the known exceptions is needed to excuse this gap; the paying users already supply the stronger evidence.

I would invest, but the check rests on customer pull rather than a fully proven founder pattern. Ooak’s recent proof spike—large contracts preceding institutional scale—matches the kind of evidence I take seriously, while the partner-dependent supply chain and early hiring contradict that lean enough to keep this well below Strong Invest. My flip condition is simple: if neither of the two seven-figure lab contracts produces a paid renewal or expansion by August 2027, I would pass, because that would show Ooak sold experiments rather than built something users pull back.
