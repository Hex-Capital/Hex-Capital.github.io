# tash -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: card demand has accelerated, retail buyers want diversified exposure without sourcing and custody work, and tash becomes large only if portfolio shares develop durable secondary liquidity after fees. The product bundles research, acquisition, authentication, storage, and resale into one position. But the governing variable is liquidity, not enthusiasm for cards. Collectable halted secondary trading in 2024 and issued no new offerings during 2024–2025. That precedent directly weakens the proposition tash must prove.

The strongest bull case is the team’s unusually direct relationship with the market. The founders report more than $1 million of personal collectibles transactions, while their prior exchange produced $22,000-plus of volume across 710 trades and 250 listed cards. Walmart Marketplace card sales rose 200% between February 2024 and June 2025, with Pokémon sales increasing more than tenfold year over year. A multi-card index is also meaningfully different from Rally’s and Collectable’s single-asset shares: diversification could reduce asset-selection friction and create a more repeatable consumer product.

The problem is that none of this demonstrates demand for the regulated index. The current product has only an early-access waitlist, no disclosed revenue, and no completed offering. Prior marketplace trades validate that the founders can attract some card activity; they do not establish that customers will hold portfolio securities or trade them deeply enough to cover custody, compliance, brokerage, and acquisition costs. The $13.51 billion underlying card market is not the relevant market boundary. The relevant SAM is capital willing to buy regulated card-index shares, and the dossier provides no estimate or observed conversion.

I also do not see a technology or institutional discontinuity that changes the category’s economics. tash relies on SEC qualification, Rialto Markets, and North Capital rather than owning a new regulatory or technical rail. Rally, Collectable, Dibbs, and Alt already established fractionalization, custody, valuation, and trading. The interpolated CL50 performance chart is particularly problematic because historical outperformance is central to customer acquisition, yet the final daily-series chart has not been produced. A retail collectibles boom can increase demand while leaving spreads, compliance costs, and episodic liquidity unchanged.

There is a potentially useful owned workflow here. If tash controls portfolio construction, sourcing, custody, issuance, performance history, and secondary trading, each new index could reuse the same operational and distribution infrastructure. Today, however, this is planned integration rather than demonstrated control: qualification is pending, offerings have not launched, and the terms still describe the prior exchange. The future data moat is similarly hypothetical. A funded competitor already operating custody and a marketplace could reproduce portfolio construction more easily than tash could manufacture liquidity.

The team’s lived experience makes a pivot plausible, but it does not create an exception to the failed market gate. This is neither a frontier technology with scarce technical talent nor a physical product whose technology reconstructs incumbent margins. My base case is that within 36 months tash either closes or pivots toward card-marketplace, valuation, or portfolio-management software after regulated index liquidity proves insufficient. I would revise that view if an SEC-qualified portfolio reaches at least $5 million of net subscriptions and sustains monthly secondary turnover above 10% for six consecutive months. Until then, I pass.
