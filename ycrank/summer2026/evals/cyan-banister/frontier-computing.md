# Frontier Computing -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: machine-learning teams rent living substrates whose memory and computation occupy the same tissue, then program them through electrical interfaces and an API. Getting there requires reliable differentiation, sustained large cultures, precise read/write hardware, reproducible learning across structured circuits, and finally workloads whose economics beat silicon. Frontier Computing can name several of those transitions, and its 92% peak Frogger crossing rate after one hour across 25 games is a delightful little leak from that future. But the leak is not yet the bridge: reproducibility, biological credit assignment, survival at scale, and transfer beyond simple circuits remain unnamed miracles rather than completed steps.

Michael Domarkas is the strongest evidence here. At nineteen, he already has four years in neuromorphic computing, two years growing neuronal tissue, a $20,000 Emergent Ventures grant earned at seventeen, and a functioning experiment. That is pre-company obsession, not a fashionable pivot assembled for Summer 2026. Raising a $10 million pre-seed and recruiting a scientific leader with £100,000–£180,000 plus 1%–4% equity also show unusual belief-recruiting ability. Commercial command remains unproven, but the founder’s clock started well before this round.

The product sits exactly where I look for hidden leverage. Frontier is not decorating a third-party model; it is attempting to control the tissue culture, electrical interface, substrate software, and cloud-access layer beneath future biological models. Its proposed 100-million- and 500-million-neuron systems would be materially different from Cortical Labs’ roughly 200,000-neuron CL1. Yet “under construction” is doing important work. Cortical already sells $35,000 systems and cloud access, while another competitor has a paying biological-compute customer. Frontier controls an intriguing process claim, not yet a demonstrated scarce resource.

The Frogger experiment proves that something works, but it does not prove costly or strange customer behavior. The 25 games were an internal demonstration; there is no repeat outside usage, pricing, revenue, named collaborator, or paid experiment. Nor has Frontier identified a rent it removes. GPU memory bandwidth is a technical constraint, but the dossier does not quantify whose cost falls, which gatekeeper loses power, or what agency a developer gains. The $10 million round and press coverage are financing behavior around a hot category, not product behavior.

The closest known exception is CosmicBrain AI: a functioning hard-technical prototype and a founder’s unusually early obsession justified overlooking missing revenue. Frontier shares that mechanism. The material difference is that CosmicBrain had signed industrial proofs of concept, while Frontier has neither external workload validation nor a published donor-consent architecture. That second omission is a gate, not tasteful ethics language. Living tissue used for computation creates explicit custody and consent questions, and the dossier says donors may not understand that use. A magically weird substrate becomes unacceptable very quickly if the people supplying its biology lack informed control.

My 36-month prediction is that Frontier becomes a paid biological-research-access platform producing $1 million–$3 million in annualized revenue, but has not yet trained a third party’s production ML model. That would still be meaningful infrastructure, just narrower than the current promise. I would not write the personal check today. One event flips me: an independent paying laboratory publicly reproduces structured-circuit learning on Frontier’s system at 100 million or more neurons under a documented donor-consent protocol. That would turn the Frogger artifact into both external behavior and ethically usable infrastructure.
