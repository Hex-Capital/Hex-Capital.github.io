# Raven -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Why must the agent be a phone? Raven’s investment case depends on a specific proposition: controlling the device enables workplace automation that software on existing devices cannot reliably deliver. That proposition is testable against the same workflows running on ordinary phones and computers. The dossier does not establish that Raven claims or has demonstrated this advantage. Its organizer-confirmed prototype performs flight search, generates interfaces, and changes calendar events. This establishes a working demonstration; it leaves the important secret unidentified.

Isa Usmanov makes the strongest case for taking Raven seriously. His previous businesses include droov, reporting $3.4 million in GMV and 32,000 users, an ed-tech SaaS reporting $1.1 million ARR, and the Reinio sale. Those figures belong to earlier companies, but they make the objection substantial: an experienced software entrepreneur has already built a demonstrable product and reports initial shipments. My preference for operator-created companies, and my actual willingness to finance consumer businesses, both favor further attention here. However, prior commercial success cannot establish Raven’s founding alignment. Full-time status, ownership, and economic exposure remain undisclosed. A solo founder has no co-founder relationship to prove; that is neither a defect nor evidence of durable commitment.

The decisive adverse evidence concerns monopoly. “Employees” is a broad audience, and Gmail-to-Drive-to-Slack coordination does not identify a bounded market Raven can own. Brain already has an intention-based phone with SoftBank distribution. Deutsche Telekom offers overlapping assistant functions. Rabbit executes across existing devices, challenging the necessity of buying another phone. These are concrete substitutes, rather than hypothetical future competition. Raven has no documented proprietary execution advantage, exclusive distribution, or accumulated data that explains why successful adoption would leave it with durable pricing power. The crowded-product red flag applies even though the dossier does not establish that Usmanov copied anyone.

Could the prototype justify overriding that failed monopoly gate? This is the strongest bull case: hardware control could eventually make reliable execution possible, and the reported 160,000 demo views, hundreds of signups, and initial shipments suggest an audience willing to consider the product. But my known exceptions require evidence that directly removes the relevant risk: an independently demonstrated technical discontinuity, persistent transaction demand, or a concrete deployment path addressing a binding constraint. The hackathon result demonstrates functionality without comparative reliability. Shipments have undisclosed quantities and payment status. Neither establishes that Raven possesses something its substitutes cannot reproduce.

The commercial evidence leaves the remaining dimensions unresolved. Raven’s individual and team request form supplies a contact route, but no demonstrated acquisition economics or repeatable sales process. Pricing, gross margins, and recurring charges are undisclosed, so device sales remain an inferred revenue engine. The $393.3 billion smartphone spending forecast cannot supply that missing arithmetic. These are ordinary pre-seed unknowns and receive the stated defaults. My pass rests on demonstrated competitive overlap without a monopoly mechanism. One result would reopen the decision: an independently observed deployment in a named workplace segment demonstrating a sustained 10x execution advantage over the best existing-device alternative, attributable to technology Raven owns. Until then, I pass.
