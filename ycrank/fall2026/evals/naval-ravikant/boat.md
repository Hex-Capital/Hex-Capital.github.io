# Boat -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Boat’s founders learned the problem by building cloud coding agents, then extracted the computers those agents needed into a product other developers could buy. That is specific knowledge acquired through action. The July 22 Box launch and September 15 migration to Boat provide shipped artifacts beyond the founder’s account of his research-computing experience. I give the working infrastructure more weight than the university affiliations or GitHub stars.

The difficulty is what Boat gets to keep when competitors reproduce the product. Separate kernels, nested virtualization, and dedicated Ryzen hosts are concrete engineering choices. They do not establish exclusive hardware, proprietary models, or scarce supply. Google already offers sandbox provisioning and snapshots; Modal offers VM sandboxes. Boat’s advertised $0.036 hourly price could win customers, but its vendor-authored comparison does not establish a durable cost advantage. Without that advantage, competitors can compress the spread between what Boat charges and what its machines cost.

The strongest argument against waiting is that customers are already choosing it. The founders report more than 1,000 active paying users on October 24 and six-figure ARR. Those are meaningful signals at pre-seed. Published plans start at $20 monthly, with metered overages, so value capture has an identifiable payer and mechanism. OpenInspect’s October 23 integration also shows another developer building around Boat. A small team supplying infrastructure that others voluntarily adopt deserves attention. My objection is that the dossier does not show whether additional usage improves gross profit or merely purchases more compute.

The market offers room to maneuver. Persistent terminals, browsers, Docker, and snapshots serve both coding agents and broader knowledge-work agents through substantially the same execution environment. Google’s investment in this category supports the demand thesis. Modal’s reported billion-dollar annualized run rate demonstrates spending on adjacent compute, although its inference and training business cannot be counted as Boat’s addressable revenue. The opportunity is credible without inventing a TAM.

Boat fits my pattern of tools that let individuals produce more without an employer’s permission, and it addresses a real infrastructure bottleneck. The missing mechanism is customer-created compounding: one customer’s private VM does not demonstrably improve another customer’s machine. OpenInspect supplies an integration, not evidence of a self-reinforcing user network. I sometimes accept an absent network when an exceptional prototype and demonstrated capital efficiency compensate; Boat has the shipped product, but the dossier does not establish the capital efficiency. Nor does it show unusual founder recruiting or a forming communication network sufficient to override the durability concern. There is no documented integrity problem, artificial financing deadline, or dependence on investor reassurance; I would not manufacture those red flags from silence.

I would hold off on investing my own money. Boat has stronger evidence of founder execution than of an advantage that survives successful imitation. My flip condition is a verifiable six-month production cohort showing that Boat’s measured cost per comparable workload falls as usage scales while customer retention remains strong. That would establish a compounding operating advantage behind the price. Today, I can see why developers buy Boat; I cannot yet see why growing demand makes Boat progressively harder to replace.
