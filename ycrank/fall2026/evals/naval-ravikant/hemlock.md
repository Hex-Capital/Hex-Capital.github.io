# Hemlock -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Julian Olschwang has earned specific knowledge by building things that touch hands. His Talk To The Hand glove received a $25,000 Davidson Fellowship in 2024; his robotics-data work connects sensing to model training. Those are relevant artifacts, not borrowed prestige from a university. Hemlock extends that work into wrist-based EMG capture. I give the founder substantial credit for arriving at this problem through action. I do not count those same accomplishments again as company-owned intellectual property.

The Bracelet addresses a concrete information gap: video records movement but omits the forces that make manipulation succeed. Hemlock reports 10× better egg-handling success and 2.7× better lightbulb installation with its data. These are company-reported, task-specific results, but they give the hardware a purpose beyond collecting another sensor stream. Copying the software would not automatically reproduce the physical capture system. Still, a working instrument and a durable advantage are different accomplishments. Four claimed patents without identifiers establish neither ownership nor protection, and the dossier supplies no exclusive corpus or demonstrated collection advantage across workers.

The strongest objection is that Hemlock could prove technically useful and still become a custom data-collection business. Each new lab might require new workers, calibration, task design, and validation. MANUS and StretchSense already sell capture systems; FACT introduces an alternative force-supervised dataset. Hemlock fits my recent interest in physical infrastructure bottlenecks, but the permissionless contribution and customer-created value patterns are unproven. There is no evidence that adding one customer makes another customer's product better or acquisition cheaper. Hardware earns some defensibility credit; an imagined data flywheel earns none.

The initial buyer is identifiable: robotics teams training dexterous manipulation. Expansion across tools and force-sensitive tasks is plausible, but Hemlock has not established the adjacent buyers, purchasing budgets, or reusable collection economics. The cited $1.05 billion robot-training-data estimate includes simulation and internal collection, so it cannot stand in for accessible demand. Dataset licensing could capture value directly, but that business model is inferred. There is no disclosed payer, price, or completed transaction. Both monetization and user-to-user compounding receive their absent-evidence defaults.

I sometimes accept missing distribution and revenue when an unusual prototype reveals exceptional founder knowledge. Hemlock has enough substance to invoke that exception, which keeps this well above a categorical pass. Nothing supplied triggers my integrity or artificial-deadline veto; uncertain patent scope is not evidence of misconduct. But my money would currently buy exposure to an accomplished builder without sufficient evidence that the resulting asset compounds. **I remain neutral.** One verifiable result would change my decision: a paid robotics-lab deployment showing that the same Hemlock-owned dataset improves manipulation on held-out workers and tasks without bespoke recollection. That would connect the promising instrument to a reusable asset and an actual buyer.
