# SpaceFlow Technologies, Inc. -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and procurement departments should no longer shuttle supplier facts between inboxes, spreadsheets, and customized ERP fields. Agents will execute routine work inside corporate permission boundaries while humans intervene at explicit thresholds. SpaceFlow Technologies, Inc. has found a convincing leak from that world: hundreds of daily production users already route $400 million of annual supplier spend through a governed runtime. But the dossier does not map the journey beyond procurement—what other functions cross next, which technical constraints fall, and why this becomes a horizontal control plane rather than an excellent sourcing product. That missing chain matters more to me than the $10.1 billion procurement-software TAM.

The artifact is unusually real for pre-seed. SpaceFlow has six-figure contracted annual revenue, a signed national-restaurant contract, and reported customer outcomes including four-times-faster RFQ cycles, 42% less maverick spending, and $120,000 in annual savings. The supply-chain executive who bought the product then invested personally. That is costly behavior twice over: an enterprise entrusted operational spend to the software, and an informed user placed personal capital behind what they experienced. I give this much more weight than the company’s 388 LinkedIn followers or the impressive-sounding—but easily misread—$400 million spend figure.

The founders also paid some operating tuition before adopting the current AI vocabulary. Hakan Aksu generated $40,000 from an ad-automation service and sold a tender-matching engine for five figures; the team then built an autonomous procurement engine with a medical manufacturer before YC. Emre Isik’s low-data machine-learning research and Ali Aytekin’s classifier, paper, and energy-storage patent add technical range. This is stronger than résumé tourism, although I cannot yet see a long, dated procurement obsession or evidence that one founder recruited the others into an independently derived mission. Emre is identified as CEO, but with three co-founders and no disclosed governance structure, I would verify decision authority before signing—not presume a problem from silence.

The broken system is visible but incompletely decoded. Buyers manually read and re-enter supplier information because customized ERPs, email, documents, and approval chains do not share usable context. SpaceFlow can quantify some of that waste, yet it does not identify who captures the rent or show that procurement workers gain meaningful economic or occupational agency. Approval thresholds, identity passthrough, and on-premises deployment return operational control to the customer, but this is primarily institutional efficiency, not liberation from an artificial gatekeeper.

The bull case says SpaceFlow is actually the concealed enabling layer: model-agnostic execution inside customer networks, customer-specific evals generated from corrections, short-lived credentials, allowlists, and HSM-signed audit logs. The bear case stings harder than “SAP is big.” Every customized table and approval chain can turn the supposed runtime into a bespoke integration business, while hostile supplier emails make prompt injection part of the production environment. SAP already offers routing, bid analysis, supply-chain agents, and managed runtime capabilities inside its installed base. If SpaceFlow’s accumulated context remains customer-isolated and procurement-specific, the moat is implementation labor while the platform advantage belongs to SAP.

I am not writing the check yet. This is a strong working company hiding inside a less-proven horizontal-platform story: excellent present-day behavior, credible builders, and thoughtful security, but no six-year causal map showing why SpaceFlow owns what comes after procurement automation. My flip condition is one existing customer expanding the same runtime into a second non-procurement workflow—with audited production usage and no bespoke rebuild. That would show me a real enabling layer rather than a well-executed application riding today’s crowded agent wave.
