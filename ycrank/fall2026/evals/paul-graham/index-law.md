# Index Law -- Paul Graham Evaluation

What happened when Quddus Pativada needed a legal answer and it took weeks? That is where I would start. His June founding post describes a problem encountered while running a company, and Index Law has since released products addressing it. But the dossier never connects a particular obstacle to a founder-built workaround, a short elapsed time, and an observable result. His previous company’s reported $20M+ in booked revenue makes me interested in meeting him. It cannot supply the missing evidence about how he handles resistance at Index Law.

There is something promising in the work itself. Legal services contain exactly the unpleasant coordination that founders often overlook: retaining context, finding the right specialist, handling jurisdictional differences, and deciding when software must hand responsibility to a licensed person. Pativada has bought these services, and Index acknowledges licensing, insurance, supervision, and accountability. That gives the idea an organic origin and a concrete operational burden. The origin account remains retrospective, however, and recognizing the burden earns less credit than demonstrating that the team has overcome it.

Index Law has also shipped beyond a proposal. Assess launched on July 14, followed by Mike in November. A public research preview with document workflows and persistent company context deserves technical credit. The limits are equally specific: proprietary harnesses and evaluations are described without published reliability results, and the reported contract turnaround below 60 minutes has no disclosed sample size. Moving from matter assessment to contracts could represent useful learning. Without the observation that caused the change, I cannot score it as evidence-driven adaptation.

The strongest case for investing is that Pativada already knows how to sell to difficult institutions and is now making an expensive service accessible directly to business users. Mike’s proposed $149 monthly subscription offers a small company a much easier starting point than hiring counsel. Founder-led setup calls and recruitment of independent lawyers could teach Index where automation actually helps. Harvey and Legora principally serve legal professionals, leaving a plausible opening around the customer’s whole problem. This is a serious bull case. It becomes compelling if businesses repeatedly entrust Mike with their next contract.

That last step remains unproved. The claims of quotes up to 80% lower suggest potential value, but there are no named customers, repeat-use figures, or payments for Mike. Its 191,088 launch-post views cannot establish that businesses return. My pattern of backing concrete proof spikes therefore offers limited support here; neither a developer substrate being appropriated by users nor a measured technical breakthrough is established. The dossier also does not establish the red flags of waiting for a large partner or delegating early selling. The concern is missing affirmative evidence. The exceptions that could overcome that concern require unusually convincing product use or independently validated proof, and this preview supplies neither at that strength.

I would pass at present. My people-first gate remains unmet even though the product and problem are credible. One verifiable flip condition would be a documented customer cohort showing a specific legal-delivery obstacle, the workaround Pativada personally shipped within weeks, and repeated use of the resulting workflow across subsequent matters. That would reveal both resourcefulness and a narrow well of demand. Index Law’s next contracts can tell us much more than its launch did.
