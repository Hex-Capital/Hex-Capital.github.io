# HillStar -- Paul Graham Evaluation

HillStar’s account of the lowest-priced bidder lacking a mandatory certification gets my attention. Two of 14 bidders apparently failed the requirement, and the committee confirmed it. This is an ugly, consequential job that someone actually has to do. But finding an obstacle in a customer’s workflow is different from showing how founders behave when blocked. The dossier contains no dated sequence of resistance, founder action, and a shipped workaround. I cannot turn a useful product feature into evidence of resourcefulness.

The strongest technical evidence is the reported evaluation of 22 submissions, roughly 19,800 pages, in nine minutes. Source-linked findings and committee review make more sense here than asking buyers to trust an unexplained score. Yet the case study is company-published, the utility is anonymous, and the comparison baseline comes from experience with other procurements. Nine minutes measures speed; it does not establish completeness or correctness. I give HillStar partial credit for a reported functioning system, with a substantial discount on its measured performance.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders. Nooshin Behroyan built a utility-services company and reports a 2025 exit. Kenny Stone co-founded PlanGrid, acquired for $875 million in 2018, and subsequently worked in geothermal software. These are unusually relevant backgrounds for selling software into operationally difficult institutions. Their experience makes the choice of problem credible. It does not document the particular procurement failure that caused them to start HillStar, however. Nor does the dossier show a rejected product hypothesis and an evidence-driven change of route. Those two dimensions remain at their missing-evidence defaults; neither deserves an adverse score.

There is also more demand evidence than a demo-booking page. An electric utility reportedly completed a sourcing cycle in seven days across approximately 250 equipment SKUs per vendor. That is a specific job performed for a specific kind of user. It earns provisional demand credit, separately from the document-processing benchmark. The important uncertainty is whether buyers pull the software into subsequent procurements. Pairing procurement professionals with software could be an excellent way to learn. It could also produce a business where every new engagement requires another expert team. Against Fairmarkit and Keelvar, the distinction matters: accumulated award history becomes useful protection only if customers repeatedly use it.

HillStar fits my preference for founders entering work that others find tedious. It has not yet demonstrated the concrete technical proof spike or habitual product use that strengthens my other recent bets. I see no affirmative evidence of dependence on a large partner, premature sales delegation, or an artificially assembled cofounder relationship; missing relationship history proves none of those things. I also considered whether a working product or unmistakable customer pull warrants an exception to my founder-action gate. The anonymous, company-published outcomes are encouraging but insufficient for that override. I would pass today. One verifiable development would change my decision: a referenceable utility documents a procurement failure, a founder-built correction shipped within two weeks, and a paid second sourcing cycle using that correction. That would connect founder behavior to a product customers want back.
