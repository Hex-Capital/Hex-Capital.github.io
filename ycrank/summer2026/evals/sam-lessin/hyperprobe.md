# HyperProbe -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and use of proceeds. HyperProbe may be a good product, but I cannot tell whether this is a scarce first check or another YC auction, and I cannot identify the finite experiment my money would fund. “Sell more services” is not a venture moment. The company needs to name the technical or commercial proposition that one check will prove, the deadline, and the result that changes its value.

The strongest evidence is not the inherited claim that HyperTest served more than 100 product teams. Those metrics belong to the prior product. It is the current workflow evidence: CheQ Digital reportedly found a production-data mismatch on the first attempt, while Housing.com exposed live memory during a traffic spike and fixed a race condition within an hour. Those are concrete customer actions, not follower theater. Booked proofs of concept add a modest distribution signal, but the dossier does not establish paid conversion, repeat investigations, or retention.

The mechanism is legible. HyperProbe gives coding agents production state they otherwise lack, without a restart or redeployment. The founders have earned technical proximity: Karan Raina was CTO of an SDK-based, trace-driven integration-testing company, and Shailendra Singh built that company with him after prior product and startup work. This is more than résumé fit. It is still only one side of the bridge. The dossier shows that they have shipped developer infrastructure, but not that they own coding-agent distribution or an embedded enterprise workflow unavailable to competitors.

The bear case is commoditization. Lightrun has raised $110 million, Multiplayer offers an open-source self-hosted debugging agent, and Dynatrace already owns Rookout. HyperProbe reports less than 1% overhead at 3,000 requests per second and documents PII redaction, audit logs, expiry, approval gates, and private-VPC deployment, but independent validation is absent. More importantly, those controls are necessary product features, not a proprietary dark pool. The inferred corpus connecting runtime states to fixes does not count until repeated investigations actually produce exclusive data or workflow lock-in.

The bull case stings because the two testimonials may be the beginning of exactly that loop: a narrow probe resolves incidents faster, developers return, and accumulated runtime evidence improves future diagnosis. At $99 per service monthly, with a three-service minimum, HyperProbe could also become a capital-efficient developer business without requiring a huge sales organization. But pricing is not revenue, and an eight-person team has disclosed no gross margin, burn, runway, paid-service count, or route to break-even. Delete the next financing and the operating model disappears from view.

I am passing today. The verifiable flip condition is five named customers paying for at least three services each, with six-month retention and evidence that repeat investigations reduce median incident-resolution time by at least 50%, accompanied by a sub-$10 million post-money round funding that specific test. That would convert the current anecdotes into owned workflow, expose the economics, and justify taking competitive risk with my own money.
