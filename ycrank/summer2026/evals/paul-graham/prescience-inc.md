# Prescience, Inc. -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The first question is not whether American health insurance is expensive. It is what broke, what these founders did, and how quickly the result changed. The dossier gives no such episode. Rishab and Aditya Jain have strong credentials and a decade of shared building, but credentials are not resourcefulness. There is no named insurer rejection, failed clinical hypothesis, or employer objection followed by a founder-built workaround. For a people-first investor, that missing sequence is decisive.

There is nevertheless more here than an insurance pitch wearing an AI costume. Prescience has obtained California Administrator License #6019366, assembled integrations across provider networks, compliance, HSAs, banking, risk, and plan administration, and reportedly serves U.S. employers. Time Machine and Crystal therefore appear to be parts of an operating system, not merely diagrams. But the ambitious part—the reinforcement-learning system that searches medical trajectories—has no published benchmark, peer-reviewed validation, sensitivity data, or measured clinical result. The founders have built through substantial schlep; they have not yet turned their largest technical claim into inspectable proof.

The demand evidence is weaker. The company reports serving an undisclosed number of employers, but names none and supplies no usage, renewal, savings, or revenue figure. Its Time Machine waitlist and 51 Launch YC points are attention, not pull. Charging only when an employer saves money is a sensible mechanism because it makes Prescience absorb performance risk, but without a disclosed fee or documented savings it is still a proposed alignment rather than evidence that users cannot let go of the product.

The strongest case for investing is the team. Two brothers who have built together for ten years combine ML and health research, an MD, prior company-building, and enough operational persistence to obtain a regulated license. This resembles an unstated pattern I like: technical founders assembling a difficult substrate before asking permission to call it a platform. The problem is the missing proof spike. No known exception applies because there is neither independently validated technical performance nor unmistakable user behavior strong enough to replace the failed resourcefulness gate.

There is also a distribution red flag. Prescience’s launch asks for introductions to CFOs, benefits managers, HRIS platforms, and brokers. Introductions are reasonable at pre-seed, but a route whose decisive verbs become “partner” and “integrate” can hide the absence of founder-led selling. Angle Health and Collective Health already offer overlapping administration, navigation, and AI capabilities. If employers do not measurably adopt Prescience’s care routing, the company risks becoming another benefits-administration layer in a crowded market rather than a new healthcare primitive.

I would pass today. The verifiable condition that would flip me is a named employer case study showing that a specific deployment blocker was overcome by a founder-shipped workaround within weeks, followed by at least 10% independently verified savings and a renewal. That single sequence would demonstrate resourcefulness, technical effect, and user pull. Until then, Prescience has built an impressive machine around a result it has not yet shown.
