# Archangel Health -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Archangel Health’s most useful founder signal is Patel’s proximity to clinical agents through SimCare AI and medical research at Cedars-Sinai. Those are places where someone could learn why a medically correct answer still produces an incorrectly executed workflow. Bhatia brings research experience and a reported $70K/year prior business, evidence of initiative rather than mastery of this particular problem. The current infrastructure reportedly has frontier-lab and medical AI users. That earns attention. The dossier does not yet identify a clinical system either founder personally built, its measured performance, or a customer who corroborates the outcome. Specific knowledge is plausible here; its depth remains unproven.

The decisive question is what Archangel owns after a competitor reproduces its EHR sandbox. Physician-graded trajectories could become valuable training assets: which action failed, what correction worked, and whether the agent subsequently completed the task. But the dossier establishes neither an exclusive corpus nor rights that let those corrections compound across customers. Consent, provenance, and content hashes describe responsible data handling; they do not establish scarcity. Stanford’s publicly available MedAgentBench also gives buyers an alternative for part of the workflow. I give the durable advantage the absent-evidence default rather than assuming the proposed data asset already exists.

The strongest argument against passing is that Archangel may be standing directly in front of a new bottleneck. Stanford reports only 46% single-attempt completion on harder PhysicianBench tasks. A capable agent that fails to execute reliably needs environments, feedback, and adversarial cases—the combination Archangel advertises. Its reported use by frontier labs makes that possibility more concrete than an unused research concept. The shared environment could expand from training into regression testing and deployment validation. However, the independently estimated $645.4 million healthcare training-dataset market establishes a real spending category, while leaving the scale and purchasing behavior of those adjacent markets unresolved.

Archangel also resembles my pattern of turning fragmented expertise into useful supply. Physician registration and contractor compensation of $25–60+ per case provide a mechanism for recruiting that supply. They do not demonstrate that one contributor improves another customer’s experience without equivalent additional work. A reusable correction library could create that effect; bespoke physician grading could instead require labor with every contract. There is no observed contribution or reuse loop to distinguish the two. Similarly, contributor compensation is a cost, not customer pricing. With no disclosed payer, license price, or realized revenue, direct value capture receives its default score.

My exceptions matter here. I can accept missing monetization when a network is already forming, or missing distribution when a demonstrated, unusually efficient product supplies the conviction. Archangel has reported usage, but neither mechanism is established strongly enough to override the gaps. Its April-to-October change from surgery-center support to AI infrastructure does not establish the red flag of abandoning direction whenever criticized; the reason for the change is unknown. Nor does the dossier supply evidence of unfair dealings, artificial financing pressure, or misconduct. The pass rests on the economic mechanism, not a judgment about founder integrity.

I would pass with my own money today. Archangel could build a useful clinical evaluation business, but the evidence currently supports access to expertise more clearly than ownership of something that compounds. One verifiable flip condition would reopen the decision: a paid renewal in which a named customer demonstrates that a rights-controlled evaluation corpus accumulated from earlier deployments materially outperforms the public benchmark alternative. That would show the company retaining value from its work rather than repeatedly selling the work itself.
