# Ploid -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Hernandez has built something closer to his knowledge than his credentials. Recommendation systems and embedding pipelines at Wander are relevant preparation; Ploid’s shipped search product, developer interfaces, and September 4 Monid integration are stronger evidence of action. This earns meaningful founder credit. The remaining uncertainty is whether the team has learned something unusual about resolving human identities across sources. Karic’s research background supplies little direct evidence on that question.

The decisive issue is what Ploid owns after a competitor reproduces its interfaces. A claimed two-billion-person index could represent an expensive accumulated asset, but record count does not establish exclusive information or a refresh advantage. Ploid acknowledges dependence on outside vendors for some contact data and does not control their sourcing or refresh practices. That is a concrete weakness in part of the supply chain. Monid’s October announcement places Ploid alongside Apollo and People Data Labs behind one connection: distribution arrives through a channel that also makes substitution convenient.

The strongest argument against my hesitation is the reported benchmark: 81% accuracy across 1,000 queries, versus Exa’s 63% and Apollo’s 23%. If representative, that is a substantial product advantage, especially alongside the claim that thousands of companies already use Ploid. A small team can discover a better retrieval architecture before incumbents recognize it. But the benchmark is company-published and Gemini-graded; it establishes neither independent performance nor an advantage that grows with deployment. There is also no evidence that one customer’s corrections improve another customer’s results. I give the absent user loop its default score.

Ploid has room to maneuver. Sales, recruiting, and professional research share a need for identity resolution, current contact information, and supporting evidence. The same retrieval capability can serve those adjacent buyers, and the cited $4 billion sales-intelligence market establishes meaningful existing spend. Its API, CLI, and MCP access also fit my preference for tools that let individuals turn knowledge into scalable output. Those interfaces provide access; the dossier does not establish proprietary distribution. Published $200 and $600 monthly plans identify a payer and a direct charging mechanism, earning some monetization credit. Realized revenue and contribution economics remain unknown.

I sometimes accept missing distribution or monetization when demonstrated founder insight or an exceptional prototype overwhelms the gap. Ploid’s shipped product and benchmark make that exception worth considering. The evidence does not yet establish unusual capital efficiency, independently demonstrated product superiority, or an emerging self-reinforcing network strong enough to carry the decision. I see no reported founder-conduct red flag. My hesitation concerns the asset being accumulated, rather than the founders’ age, team size, or lack of financing history.

At present, I would keep watching Ploid and retain my money. The team has demonstrated more ability to build than the business has demonstrated ability to compound. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an independent six-month audit showing that identity corrections owned by Ploid produce a widening accuracy advantage over Apollo and Exa on fresh, held-out queries. That would show deployment creating an asset competitors cannot obtain simply by rebuilding the software.
