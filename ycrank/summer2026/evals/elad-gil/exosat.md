# Exosat -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is clear: NTN-capable devices and geopolitical anxiety are making governments and network operators outside the U.S. and China credible buyers of sovereign connectivity; the controlling variable is whether Exosat can deploy reliable capacity cheaply enough to convert political interest into binding contracts before established constellations close the opening.

Exosat has unusually strong evidence around that variable for a pre-seed company, but not yet proof of it. The $400 million-plus of company-reported LOIs across six nations, early pilots, and published ITU filings for 11,000 satellites indicate that sovereign demand is not purely theoretical. The missing threshold is conversion economics. No customer is named, no capacity contract or revenue is disclosed, and the approximately $300,000 satellite cost has not been validated on orbit. A spectrum filing creates an option; it is not equivalent to a defensible spectrum position until coordination and deployment milestones are met.

The technology shift is directionally compelling. Automotive-grade components, an ITAR/EAR-free supply chain, lower orbits, and standard NTN-compatible devices could make dedicated constellations affordable to countries that cannot build them domestically. But the dossier quantifies a satellite price rather than the complete economics: launch, gateway infrastructure, replenishment, spectrum coordination, network operations, customer acquisition, and financing an 11,000-satellite system remain outside the calculation. Exosat is currently software-aware hardware with an ambitious cost claim, not yet a demonstrated change in incumbent margin structure.

The strongest bull case is that this resembles a genuine sovereign-market vacuum rather than another satellite startup. Edward Ge has already launched two satellites, built Aethero to millions of dollars in revenue, served Blue Origin, Booz Allen Hamilton, and AFRL, and participated in a U.S. Air Force SBIR. Combined with six-country LOIs, this is the rare-team and strategic-demand pattern that can justify investing before revenue. The material difference from the relevant known exception, Anduril, is that Exosat enters against deployed, capitalized competitors—including Starlink at $11.4 billion of 2025 revenue and AST SpaceMobile with 13 BlueBird satellites—without yet demonstrating either product economics or a mission-ready system.

The product could eventually own a substantial operational loop: spectrum, satellites, gateways, operator integrations, capacity management, and replenishment. Better components and launch economics would then strengthen the system. Today, however, most of that loop is prospective. External manufacturing lowers differentiation, the first satellite is scheduled for April 2027, and commercial service is targeted only for the end of 2028. Within 36 months, I expect Exosat to have narrowed toward regional satellite IoT or capacity wholesale rather than completed a turnkey sovereign constellation, with revenue below $10 million unless one LOI becomes a financed deployment.

I would monitor rather than invest today. The one verifiable flip condition is a named government or network operator converting at least $20 million of the reported pipeline into a deposit-backed deployment contract after an Exosat satellite validates the claimed production cost and service performance on orbit. That event would simultaneously establish demand quality, physical economics, and a credible capital path.
