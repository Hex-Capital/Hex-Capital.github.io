# Amulet -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Nithik Bala’s Rockset and Meta infrastructure work makes Amulet an infrastructure-native founder bet whose strongest signal is proximity to the problem and whose weakest is physical observability. Amulet has credible Tiz—content-addressed commits, copy-on-write forks, and conflict-safe publishing—but almost none of the environmental evidence I use to detect Biz or Rizz. I can mount Amulet in Linux; I cannot yet watch anyone love it.

Niantic is the high rung because John Hanke lived inside mapping and location products for a decade before Pokémon Go—the biography and product were one continuous line. Amulet sits below that rung: Bala worked at Rockset and Meta Web Infrastructure, while Varun Puru worked on backend systems at Meta and interned at Amazon, so Amulet did not come from a market-analysis memo. The divergence is the recent pivot from customer-success agents to an AI filesystem; Amulet’s founders have relevant technical exposure, but the dossier contains no specific moment when either founder personally felt disposable compute destroy shared data or previously shipped a comparable filesystem at scale.

The consensus YC AI-agent darling fails my physical-world test because value exists only in a founder’s abstraction and can be rebuilt before anyone forms an emotional relationship with it. That pattern appears in Amulet: a normal human cannot ride, photograph, or play Amulet, and even a developer must trust company-published claims of 1.9-ms p99 reads and 8.4 GB/s throughput. Amulet is more tangible than a slide-deck agent wrapper because a Linux mount, fork, and immutable commit can actually be exercised, but it remains infrastructure testimony rather than the Flock Safety camera appearing in my physical environment.

Archil’s $18 million financing makes Amulet non-consensus only in a narrow sense: the specific zero-copy writable-fork architecture is unusual, while the agent-infrastructure category is already attracting serious capital. Over the next 18 months, Amulet’s weirdness compounds only if workspace histories and per-run provenance become operational dependencies; each fork would then make Amulet harder to remove. Without repeat private-beta usage, Amulet instead becomes a feature checklist that Archil, E2B, Modal, or a cloud platform can absorb. Magically weird needs social rejection plus hidden inevitability; Amulet currently shows competition, not rejection.

Nithik Bala and Varun Puru are both publicly titled “Founder,” and Amulet names neither a CEO nor a tiebreaker. Amulet would flip from governance liability to invest-grade evidence if one founder held explicit CEO authority, founder vesting covered the recent pivot, and an operating agreement resolved product deadlocks. Until then I apply the absent-evidence default: two former Meta engineers changing products shortly before YC is exactly when concealed 50/50 authority becomes dangerous.

SpaceX is the single-anchor parallel for capital intensity: the hardware, manufacturing, and regulatory burden created a moat precisely because conventional investors would not fund it. Amulet has real systems complexity—deduplication, partial POSIX behavior, workspace-scoped credentials—but Amulet charges $0.20 per GiB-month and runs as software infrastructure without a comparable physical or regulatory barrier. Technical difficulty earns some credit; capital intensity does not. A cloud provider can subsidize storage primitives in a way it cannot casually subsidize rockets.

Amulet’s $0.20-per-GiB-month model and private beta lead me to a falsifiable forecast: by July 2029, Amulet will have abandoned the independent general-purpose filesystem position and become an embedded storage layer for agent-sandbox platforms, with less than $3 million ARR or an acquisition by E2B below $50 million. Amulet’s relevant infrastructure talent makes a useful product likely, while its socially invisible artifact and the distribution power of Modal, E2B, and cloud vendors make an independent category winner unlikely.

Amulet’s 3-ms forks, sub-100-ms mounts, and conflict-checked publishing form the strongest bull case: Amulet could become Git for agent data, and the customer-success pivot could reveal founders willing to follow reality rather than defend a dead idea. That case stings because HQ Trivia taught me that extraordinary product behavior can override other weaknesses. The evidence that wins today is that every performance number is company-published, no repeat usage or revenue is disclosed, Archil already has $18 million, and Amulet’s artifact lacks HQ Trivia’s directly observable pull. Three named beta partners repeatedly managing at least 100 TB through Amulet, with one expanding into paid production after six months, would flip my verdict.
