# Fleety -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most promising thing about Fleety is that both founders worked as dispatchers before building it. Ben Zhou also founded a roofing business and a contractor-permitting company. Ariyaman Molanorouzi previously automated property operations covering 3,000+ units. These founders have encountered the people and machinery behind the product noun. Spending weeks in customer shops is a sensible way to discover which parts of dispatch can actually be automated.

But what happened when something broke? The dossier does not give me a specific dispatch problem, the workaround a founder shipped, and the time it took to change the outcome. Onsite work creates opportunities to demonstrate resourcefulness; it does not by itself demonstrate it. Timrep’s six-figure revenue and Molanorouzi’s six-figure SEO business are encouraging evidence of prior execution. Neither supplies the missing episode at Fleety. There is also no documented rejected hypothesis followed by an evidence-driven product change. I leave those questions open rather than treating silence as passivity.

Fleety describes a concrete system: compare call transcripts with equipment records, assign technicians, and rearrange schedules. That earns some technical credit, discounted because the evidence is the company’s own description. Compatibility with ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, and Jobber could involve considerable integration work, but advertised compatibility does not establish reliable production operation. My pattern of backing technical teams after a numerical proof spike has little support here: there is no measured assignment result, reproducible benchmark, or independently established technical threshold.

The three customer references—Albert Nahman, Ace Plumbing, and Apex Plumbing—are more useful than a large market estimate. They identify people who could tell us whether the product matters. They do not tell us whether dispatchers return to it, accept its assignments, or insist on keeping it. Likewise, subscription terms specifying advance billing establish a commercial mechanism, not collected revenue. The claimed $500,000 gap between the best and average technician makes the problem economically interesting. It does not establish that changing assignments closes that gap.

The strongest argument against passing is that Fleety may already be doing exactly the right early work. Former dispatchers are sitting inside shops, learning exceptions that a broad software vendor might overlook. ServiceTitan’s existing Dispatch Pro makes this test sharper: why would a shop tolerate another vendor when its CRM already optimizes assignments? A sufficiently useful answer could emerge from those weeks onsite. But the dossier does not yet identify it. Cross-CRM support offers a possible opening; proprietary learning effects remain an inference. I see no evidence of a distribution plan dependent on one promised partnership, premature delegation, or dismissal of customer feedback. Those red flags are absent, but their absence cannot substitute for demonstrated founder behavior.

I would pass on the evidence available. Fleety’s strongest signal is an organically encountered, operationally ugly problem; its weakest is the missing account of overcoming a specific obstacle. Its founder-action score therefore fails my investment gate. A working product or unmistakable user pull could justify an exception, but the public descriptions and customer references do not establish either strongly enough. My flip condition is one independently verified customer case showing a named dispatch failure, a founder-built correction shipped within weeks, and the shop repeatedly using that correction afterward. That would connect the founders’ experience to the behavior I would be buying with my own money.
