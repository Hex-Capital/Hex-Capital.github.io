# OpenVector -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Andrey Gizdov’s CVPR 2025 vision research makes OpenVector a hacker-led technical invention whose weakest link is whether anyone built it from personal pain. OpenVector has the signature of a research artifact searching for its inevitable use: unusually strong technical genealogy, but no restaurant manager, warehouse operator, or car-wash owner among the founders and no story that Gizdov or Vishal Urlam personally needed a camera to operate a POS. That distinction matters. Hackers can build surprising things, but organic ideas tell them which surprising thing to keep building.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for hacker genealogy: the Collisons had Lisp, Auctomatic, and years of commerce hacking before they reduced payments to seven lines of code. OpenVector shares the load-bearing mechanism because Gizdov published compute-efficient adaptive vision at CVPR 2025, Urlam worked on IoT and Tata Power substations, and the pair won the Google Prize at MIT Grand Hack 2025. OpenVector falls short of the Stripe rung because neither founder has a visible GitHub trail, and predictive coding, continuous-time dynamics, and attention-driven sampling remain research signals rather than a publicly inspectable artifact with developer adoption. Still, hackers leave tracks, and a CVPR paper plus working camera-to-POS demonstrations are real tracks.

**Loopt** (2005) exposed the low-rung failure mechanism on organic founder pain: formidable founder force can carry a product whose use is not yet native or urgent, but force cannot manufacture the right use indefinitely. OpenVector shows the same gap. Gizdov has lived inside computer vision and Urlam inside physical computing, yet OpenVector provides no evidence that either lived inside restaurant loss prevention, car-wash operations, or warehouse incident response. OpenVector may be organic at the technology layer and synthetic at the customer layer. That is better than two MBAs shopping for an AI market, but it is not Drew Houston repeatedly forgetting his USB stick.

OpenVector’s $100 refundable reservations, live-stream demonstrations, and undisclosed design partners are early animal tracks, but they are not the cereal boxes. Resourcefulness compounds when each manual deployment teaches founders a new integration, failure mode, and sales argument; over 18 months, OpenVector could turn DRB SiteWatch and POS hookups into a grimy library of operational edge cases. The opposite outcome is equally legible: OpenVector hires a $135,000–$250,000 engineer before proving that anyone will pay nonrefundable money, and research ambition substitutes for selling. With no named customer, collected station revenue, or pre-funding stunt, I cannot call Gizdov and Urlam animals yet.

DRB SiteWatch integration and a 92% confidence threshold put OpenVector in a genuine schlep zone: false actions touch transactions, staff, and physical operations rather than merely producing an amusingly wrong chatbot answer. OpenVector would move into the top tier on this dimension if founders personally installed cameras, reconciled mistaken discounts, handled privacy objections, and produced a named multi-site operator using actions repeatedly for 90 days. OpenVector would fall sharply if the “operator” becomes another alert dashboard, because Spot AI, Coram AI, and Ambient.ai already have far more money to sell detection. The unpleasant integration work is the opportunity; avoiding it would erase the opportunity.

**Stripe** (2010) also supplies the clean parallel for cofounder configuration: two founders with complementary technical instincts who had built together before the decisive product. OpenVector has a plausible version in Gizdov and Urlam, whose relationship includes MIT Grand Hack 2025, the Google Prize, and continued work afterward. OpenVector does not yet have the Collisons’ years of shared company-building or a documented long relationship under stress. A hackathon can reveal speed, but it cannot reveal whether two people still like each other after the fifth broken deployment at 3 a.m.

Spot AI’s $93 million, Coram AI’s $66 million, and OpenVector’s separate Foveal Vision positioning point to a specific 36-month outcome: by July 2029, OpenVector will have pivoted from a horizontal camera-to-action operator into an edge-vision SDK or appliance for robotics and industrial OEMs, reaching roughly $1–5 million in ARR. OpenVector’s strongest asset is Gizdov’s compute-efficient vision work, while OpenVector’s weakest evidence concerns urgent personal ownership of restaurant, gym, and car-wash workflows. When the technology is organic but the use is not, the technology usually survives and the first use does not.

OpenVector’s $449-per-month Station is the strongest case against that forecast. OpenVector could become the action layer above existing cameras because competitors emphasize security while OpenVector demonstrates POS changes, calls, messages, and webhooks; **Airbnb** (2009) is the warning that a wrong-seeming use can conceal the right founders. But Airbnb had 1,000 cereal boxes sold for $40, while OpenVector has refundable $100 reservations, undisclosed design partners, and no named operating customer. The bull case wins—and I would invest—if OpenVector documents five named multi-site operators paying nonrefundable revenue and repeatedly allowing autonomous actions for at least 90 days. Until then, the research is more convincing than the customer truth.
