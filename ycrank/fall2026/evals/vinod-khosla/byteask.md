# ByteAsk -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

ByteAsk names a consequential cost pool: an estimated $400 billion in annual C++ salaries. Making reliable systems engineering radically cheaper could change what gets built in automotive, embedded systems, and computing infrastructure. But salary spending is not spending this product can eliminate. The dossier does not quantify how much engineering work becomes autonomous or how verification changes the cost of delivering working software. I can see the consequence worth investigating. I cannot yet price the option on it.

The technical wedge is credible. Build-specific context, compilers, sanitizers, and access to an existing debugger session address constraints that a language model cannot resolve by reading source files alone. ByteAsk reports 89% firmware-ticket resolution against 61% for a frontier-model baseline: a 28-percentage-point improvement, approximately 1.46x the resolution rate. That deserves attention. It does not establish a 5–10x discontinuity, particularly without a reproducible evaluation across customer toolchains. Sixfold usage intensity versus an unnamed agent measures behavior, not engineering productivity. The question is whether verification creates a capability advantage that survives Claude Code, Cursor, and Codex adopting similar tools.

The September 27 changelog exposes the most important failure mode: tools could report clean results when execution was absent, interrupted, or incompletely instrumented. An agent that incorrectly certifies its own work can increase the cost of engineering. The disclosed fixes earn modest credit for attacking that risk. They do not establish that it is retired; the dossier supplies no measured false-clean rate, decisive acceptance threshold, or costed next experiment. ByteAsk’s $20–$200 plans likewise establish an asking price, not the cost of reliably resolving a ticket after inference, execution, and human review. I would spend the next dollar measuring that complete loop.

The strongest argument against passing is the team’s combination of actual C++ trading-system experience and AI infrastructure experience. Kulkarni and Saini have already built and sold a company together. Reported use by LLVM, Godot, and llama.cpp contributors gives their product a more demanding audience than a generic coding demonstration. This fits my preference for scarce technical workflows that can become repeatable platforms, and my broader investing record leaves room for execution-led software businesses. But contributor usage has not yet demonstrated a committed technical community or a proprietary learning loop. The prior sale demonstrates execution; it does not document rapid belief revision or difficult complementary recruiting. Nor does it establish an exit-first red flag. The exceptions for extraordinary instigators addressing giant value pools remain relevant, but this dossier supplies neither that founder evidence nor a sufficiently demonstrated consequence to override the technical gap.

I pass with my own money today. ByteAsk has substantive application engineering, so dismissing it as merely an interface would miss the bull case. The unresolved issue is whether that engineering compounds into a dramatic reduction in verified delivery cost while general agents absorb the accessible integrations. My single flip condition is an independently reproducible trial across three unrelated production C/C++ toolchains showing at least 10x lower fully loaded cost per accepted ticket than the strongest general-agent baseline, with complete execution traces and no false-clean claims in the audited sample. That would turn useful tooling into evidence of a cost-curve change.
