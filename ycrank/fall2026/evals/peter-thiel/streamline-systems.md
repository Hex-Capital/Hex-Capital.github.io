# Streamline Systems -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Streamline Systems needs a secret more precise than “drones must navigate without GPS.” Advanced Navigation, ANELLO and Sine already agree with that proposition. The potentially important claim is that a drop-in module can deliver mission-grade positioning without emitting an RF signature, at economics suitable for expendable aircraft. That claim is testable: measure positioning error and mission completion during sustained GPS denial under representative operating conditions. The dossier supplies neither those results nor a prevailing technical belief the founders have overturned. I cannot infer a secret from an undisclosed sensing mechanism.

Who would become dependent on this particular supplier? ArduPilot, INAV and PX4 integrations establish compatibility, but the same interfaces could accommodate competing modules. Streamline identifies an application, yet does not establish a bounded customer segment it can dominate. ANELLO’s photonic gyroscopes and Advanced Navigation’s sensor fusion demonstrate alternative approaches; they do not establish that Streamline is interchangeable with them. The monopoly mechanism remains unproved. Qualification costs might eventually preserve its position, but future switching costs cannot be counted as an existing advantage.

The strongest argument for investing is that the founders have already moved beyond a presentation. Streamline reports satellite-independent autonomous flight and GPS handoff, signed LOIs and scheduled government trials. For a two-person pre-seed company, that is meaningful progress. It also fits my interest in physical technology that removes an operational constraint. My known exceptions permit deployment evidence or a verified technical discontinuity to outweigh substantial execution risk. Here, however, the trials remain scheduled and the performance claims remain company-reported. They establish a reason to investigate, without resolving the technical or monopoly question.

The founding structure receives a cautious prior. Gabriel Poraj’s software experience and Cambridge engineering studies support his ability to attempt the product; Charlie Jessett’s RAF reserve affiliation supplies military exposure. Neither establishes that both founders are fully committed, have shipped together over time, or retain meaningful economic ownership. Poraj’s reported departure from Cambridge is a concrete commitment signal for him, but cannot establish the alignment of the whole team. There is also no documented convenience pairing, outsourced core engineering or investor-dominated capitalization that would justify an adverse founding-team judgment.

Distribution is the most encouraging commercial evidence. Reported LOIs and government trials indicate engagement beyond an early-access webpage, although counterparties, procurement owners and sales cycles remain undisclosed. The recruitment listing separately acknowledges that flying hardware still needs hardening, productization and manufacturing work. Streamline therefore has a prototype-to-procurement sequence to complete. That is a real coordination burden, though the dossier does not establish the sprawling stack of simultaneous dependencies that would trigger my strongest hardware red flag.

I pass on writing a check today. Per-module sales are inferred; price, margins and expansion economics are unknown. The cited $154.38 million market estimate for 2025 cannot substitute for arithmetic showing how this supplier becomes a power-law outcome, and it need not define the eventual market’s ceiling. My single flip condition is a paid production design win with a named UAV manufacturer, supported by independently witnessed GPS-denied performance that competing modules cannot meet at the contracted unit economics. That event would connect the possible secret to ownership of a market. Today those connections remain hypotheses.
