# Amorphic Labs -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Frank Li has built computer-use systems and ML infrastructure at OpenAI, NVIDIA, Two Sigma, and Amazon. Dylan Kelly has shipped AI research, including a NeurIPS 2025 benchmark. This is evidence of technical action, not evidence that either founder has sold sales software or repeatedly endured the demo-production problem. Amorphic Labs itself is the strongest domain artifact, but without identified users or completed deployments, founder-specific knowledge remains promising rather than proven.

The product does real work: it researches a prospect, populates the vendor’s application, executes a workflow, records the result, and generates narration. That is more substantial than rearranging pre-recorded clips. But if a competitor copies the workflow, what remains? The dossier identifies no proprietary model, exclusive data, hardware, workflow lock-in, or customer-created network. Consensus, Walnut, and Reprise already possess adjacent products and have raised approximately $143 million, $56 million, and $82 million. Code creates the demo; it does not yet defend the company.

The best opposing case is that application-specific workflow maps, corrections, and execution traces could compound into a proprietary reliability system. Li’s computer-use experience makes that path credible, and accurate operation of arbitrary software is harder than the finished video makes it look. But credibility is not an asset. Amorphic has disclosed neither cross-customer learning nor improving task-completion rates, and its July 2026 terms still require vendor review because agents may take inaccurate or incomplete actions. The same traces could become durable data or remain debugging exhaust.

The initial buyer is legible—software founders, sales leaders, and RevOps teams—and the broader sales-enablement market is estimated at $6.0 billion in 2025, growing to $21.2 billion by 2033. The causal expansion path is not yet legible. A top-down category forecast does not show how personalized videos extend into adjacent transactions, and the incumbent overlap suggests a feature market unless Amorphic turns browser execution into a broader capability.

There is also no permissionless loop. Each customer currently appears to require direct sales, application access, workflow configuration, and company-supplied computation. No user makes the product more useful or cheaper to acquire for another user. Subscriptions, metered usage, and negotiated order forms are contemplated, but no payer, price, completed transaction, or revenue is disclosed. Those absences are normal at pre-seed; they simply cannot establish leverage or value capture.

I find no disclosed integrity, artificial-scarcity, or founder-morale red flag. The known exception for an unusually capable team with an early prototype deserves consideration, but it cannot override both an explicitly copyable architecture and the absence of observed user behavior. My 36-month base case is that Amorphic pivots from personalized demo videos into browser-agent reliability or QA infrastructure, with the original product remaining below $1 million ARR. I pass today. I would reopen if ten paying software vendors produce evidence that Amorphic’s accumulated correction traces improve autonomous workflow completion across customers without proportional human review; that would convert the strongest inferred advantage into a verifiable compounding asset.
