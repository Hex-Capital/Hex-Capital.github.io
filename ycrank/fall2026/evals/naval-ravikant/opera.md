# OpEra -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

OpEra’s strongest asset is knowledge the founders acquired by solving an actual routing problem together. Rossignol and Chalita met at MIT’s Operations Research Center; their retailer work produced a public implementation with carrier tariffs, heuristic assignment, and mathematical optimization. That tells me more than their McKinsey, BMW, or Sanofi affiliations. The October 2026 launch reports 38% lower delivery costs and 10% more on-time deliveries. But the repository describes a historical backtest against nearest-store, single-order dispatch. I credit demonstrated problem-solving, without treating those percentages as evidence of a deployed customer business.

The investment question is what survives someone copying that implementation. OpEra’s operational modeling may require considerable skill, but scarce founder knowledge and a durable company asset are different things. JuMP/Gurobi supplies optimization machinery; OR-Tools supplies competing building blocks. Neither fact makes the founders’ work trivial. Neither establishes exclusive data, a proprietary model, or an accumulated deployment advantage. Customer-specific integrations could eventually create switching costs, but the dossier supplies no evidence that they already do. I give the founders credit for what they built once, rather than awarding it again as defensibility.

The strongest bull case is that OpEra makes operational expertise repeatable. Its proposed interface lets planners change constraints in natural language while the underlying model recomputes routes, schedules, or allocations. Retail delivery offers a concrete starting point; warehouse capacity and factory scheduling involve adjacent buyers using related constraint-modeling capabilities. The independently estimated $11.03 billion supply-chain planning category supports room to maneuver, although it is not OpEra’s addressable revenue. The sugar producer’s reported 10.4% premium improvement and marketplace’s 54% reduction in unfulfilled orders suggest breadth of application. They do not yet establish a common product that travels between customers.

That distinction determines whether this becomes software with compounding returns or an excellent optimization practice. Each customer bringing different rules can increase implementation work without making the product better for anyone else. OpEra has no demonstrated contribution, referral, or shared-learning loop. Against my unstated patterns, this fits the move toward real operational bottlenecks, but there is no evidence of permissionless participation or customers supplying value to one another. FICO’s May 2026 acquisition of Nextmv also makes configurable optimization infrastructure an occupied competitive position. The team needs an asset that accumulates through use.

I checked the exceptions before withholding a check. A forming network can justify unresolved monetization; exceptional founder behavior or an unusually capital-efficient prototype can justify missing distribution. OpEra has substantive technical work, but the dossier does not establish those additional override signals. Nor does it establish an integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-instability red flag. Missing pricing and revenue receive the pre-seed default, without turning the retailer’s cost savings into company revenue. The unresolved issue is whether customer value becomes repeatable value capture.

I would wait. These founders have earned technical credibility; the company has not yet demonstrated compounding economics or an advantage beyond their personal effort. My flip condition is a paid deployment at a second unrelated customer showing that a proprietary operational dataset, with verified rights to reuse it, improves planning outcomes while reducing implementation work relative to the first deployment. That would show the founders’ learning becoming an asset the company owns.
