# Verdant -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: local governments are beginning to buy AI-assisted permitting, but Verdant becomes large only if it can configure each jurisdiction faster and more cheaply than the resulting contract value. The controlling variable is repeatable deployment economics—implementation time and cost per jurisdiction—not whether planners dislike their current process. The dossier documents 200-plus interviews and a painful loop of incomplete applications, but it supplies neither a deployment threshold nor evidence that ordinance, GIS, and workflow configuration can be standardized.

The product is directionally correct. Verdant spans applicant intake, completeness checks, staff review, hearings, and report drafting while preserving government approval authority. Better models should improve document interpretation, and jurisdiction-specific rules could deepen the workflow. Yet there is no documented production integration, proprietary feedback loop, or operational state that GovWell or Clariti could not reproduce. PermitFlow already reports 12 million permitting datapoints. Calling future local configurations a moat without measuring their reproduction cost would invert the evidence.

The technology shift is real but its economic effect remains unquantified. San Jose began an AI pre-check pilot in 2025, and SwiftGov reportedly secured more than $3 million in Florida contracts by May 2026. Those facts show institutional willingness to experiment. They do not show that Verdant reduces review labor, shortens permit cycles, expands departmental capacity, or earns software margins after implementation. This matters because the published category is only $307 million in 2025, reaching a projected $567 million by 2032. Verdant needs either unusually efficient capture of that market or a credible distribution path into adjacent government workflows; neither is demonstrated.

The strongest bull case is that the published market understates institutional work and the founders found the right initial wedge before incumbents fully crystallized it. Aidan Ng brings government and underwriting exposure, Jason Yi has built government HR and legislative software, and 200 interviews are unusually serious discovery for a two-person pre-seed team. Modular deployments could let Verdant enter through completeness checking and expand across the permitting lifecycle. But interviews are not product-market pull: there are no named pilots, paying jurisdictions, repeat users, or signed orders. The July 2026 marketing launch, 470 LinkedIn followers, and conference sponsorship do not substitute for usage.

This is also not a frontier-research or scarce-technical-team exception to absent demand. The core opportunity is applied workflow execution in a market where GovWell has raised $34.5 million and Clariti already owns an AI plan-review product. Verdant’s end-to-end design is its strongest attribute; its lowest signal is whether anyone has adopted it. The founders’ backgrounds establish relevance, but the dossier documents neither prior startup learning nor the costly commitment that would establish desperation rather than interest.

I pass today. My base case is that by August 2029 Verdant remains below $2 million ARR with a small number of implementation-heavy municipal deployments rather than becoming a permitting system of record. One condition flips the decision: three named jurisdictions paying for production deployments completed within 60 days, each showing at least a 30% reduction in incomplete-application handling time and at least one expanding into a second module. That would validate the deployment economics, product pull, and reusable distribution mechanism at once.
