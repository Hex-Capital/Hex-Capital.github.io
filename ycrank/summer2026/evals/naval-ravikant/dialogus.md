# Dialogus -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Dialogus has done the thing that matters more than its founders’ résumés: a three-person team has put agents into production for thousands of enterprise calls. Papa Johns, KFC, and Totalplay are presented as served organizations, and the system does more than speak—it reads enterprise data, executes constrained actions, records evidence, and transfers exceptions with context. Hans Ibarra’s Gemini experience and Rodrigo Terán Hernández’s platform internships help explain the technical velocity, but they do not establish prior contact-center expertise. The deployments do. This is strong specific knowledge acquired through action, though the missing customer outcomes and limited history keep it below the highest founder score.

The harder question is what remains after a competitor copies the code. Policy-limited tools, SOC 2 controls, bilingual operation, audit logs, and replayable regression scenarios constitute real engineering. They do not yet constitute a durable asset. OpenAI supplies SIP-connected realtime models, Salesforce bundles voice with CRM and human handoffs, and Bland, PolyAI, Sierra, and Decagon have far more capital or deployment volume. Dialogus may accumulate valuable failure traces, but “could create proprietary data” is still a hypothesis. Thousands of calls are sufficient to prove deployment and insufficient to prove that the resulting data compounds into an advantage unavailable to the underlying model vendors.

The market provides room to maneuver. Customer support, restaurant ordering, and collections share the same capability: reliable voice execution against enterprise systems. The broader voice-agent market was already $2.54 billion in 2025 and is projected to grow 39% annually through 2033. But this is a large, legible market rather than a permissionless system. Each new enterprise presently appears to require another sale, integration, policy configuration, and support relationship; there is no evidence that Papa Johns makes the product more useful to KFC. Dialogus therefore misses the user-created value loop that would let the system compound without proportional company labor.

The strongest bull case is unusually strong for pre-seed: household-name production deployments, SOC 2, and permissioned actions involving orders, vouchers, and payment promises suggest that Dialogus has crossed an enterprise trust barrier that many voice demos never reach. If those organizations are paying and renewing, production failures could become the proprietary evaluation corpus that converts an integration company into infrastructure. The early-product exception resembles Notion only superficially: both had working products before an observable network loop, but Notion developed a distinctive product language and compounding taste in a strange category. Dialogus enters a consensus category where heavily funded competitors and platform vendors already reproduce the core capabilities.

No disclosed founder conduct triggers the integrity, manufactured-scarcity, or reassurance red flags, but silence is not affirmative evidence. Economics remain equally unresolved: thousands of calls prove usage, not pricing, completed transactions, gross margin, or recurring revenue. My 36-month base case is a real but services-heavy bilingual voice business reaching roughly $3 million to $10 million ARR, with its margin and bargaining power compressed by model and CRM vendors. I would not write the check today. One result would flip the decision: demonstrate that pooled production traces reduce task-error rates for newly deployed customers by at least 30% without customer-specific engineering, proving that every deployment creates a transferable asset rather than another integration obligation.
