# Roma -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Roma sits at the transition from organizing work to executing it: consumers can now delegate tasks through connected assistants, and the controlling variable is the fraction of recurring tasks completed without users having to redo the work. That determines whether Roma replaces effort or adds another inbox to supervise. The dossier establishes the product’s components, but it does not establish a completion threshold or measured performance against it. I can formulate the market hypothesis; I cannot yet underwrite it.

The architecture gives that hypothesis substance. Roma connects email, calendars, files, and a cloud browser, retaining results beside persistent tasks. Its documented approval controls are part of owning execution. A stronger foundation model should improve what this system can accomplish while Roma retains task context and permissions. That substantially reduces my model-absorption concern. However, the browser stops at unsupported verification steps, and users must complete payments. These are documented boundaries on delegation; their frequency and effect on usefulness remain unknown.

MCP’s November 2024 introduction supplies a concrete change in integration infrastructure, and Roma already exposes its workspace to other assistants. This earns some credit for making the product accessible across interfaces. It does not establish a change in delivery economics. There is no measured reduction in supervision time or cost per completed task, and the free iPhone download does not reveal willingness to pay. Todoist already offers integrations with ChatGPT, Claude, and Muse. Standardization expands access for Roma while also making similar access available to an incumbent with existing distribution.

The strongest bull case is the combination of experienced consumer builders and a working execution surface. Senderek founded Loom and subsequently worked on Dropbox mobile products; Eichenwald co-founded Senic, which Gira acquired. Those are concrete repetitions of building and selling companies, deserving substantially more weight than employer prestige. The team could turn task history into a recurring distribution channel for additional delegated work. But that last mechanism remains a hypothesis. The nine US App Store ratings, averaging 5.0 as of October 27, are a small positive signal; the 231 LinkedIn followers and launch coverage do not establish repeat product use.

Roma fits my pattern of exploring multiple application layers during a technology discontinuity. That pattern supports taking the meeting, not automatically writing the check. The exceptions that would justify overlooking weak demand evidence are also unproven here: the dossier does not document scarce frontier-model research capability or an incumbent abandoning the market. Nor does it establish a proprietary-data advantage. Task state and integrations are useful assets, but their reproduction cost and switching value have not been demonstrated. The founders’ prior exits strengthen execution confidence without resolving those market questions.

I would pass today. Roma has more workflow substance than a generic assistant demo, but the evidence is strongest on repeat-founder experience and weakest on the proposition that makes delegation a durable consumer business. My single flip condition would be a verified cohort of at least 100 paying users in which 60% still complete three or more delegated tasks weekly after eight weeks, without manually redoing those tasks. That would directly test whether Roma’s execution creates a habit worth purchasing.
