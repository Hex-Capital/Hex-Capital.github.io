# The Subvocal Company -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Silent physiological input could make computer interaction practical wherever audible dictation is unacceptable; The Subvocal Company becomes a large business only if its wearable delivers enough usable text, after calibration and corrections, to change daily behavior. That is the controlling variable. The reported **200-plus words per minute** measures prototype decoding speed. It does not establish effective throughput across ordinary users, sessions, and device placements—the generalization problems the company itself identifies. I would pass on a personal check today.

The technical opening deserves attention. A March 2026 academic demonstration of low-power EMG inference suggests a pathway toward portable silent input, although Subvocal has not disclosed whether it uses that architecture. Its continuous-speech prototype is affirmative evidence of development. But the earlier reported **45% accuracy**, obtained with under two hours of training data, cannot be combined with the newer speed claim to imply accurate, wearable dictation. The measurements describe different things. This earns partial credit for a potentially important technology shift, without establishing the adoption threshold or improved hardware economics.

The strongest argument against passing is that demanding commercial proof now could miss the enabling technology. Yadav previously co-founded GreenSat, which Georgia Tech reports reached more than **$2 million in annual revenue**; Kumar separately shipped an app reaching **80,000-plus users**. Those are meaningful operating experiences. Approximately **$1.3 million in reported backing**, with $1.5 million stated elsewhere, provides resources for further experiments. My research-stage exception is relevant, but the dossier does not establish a rare sensing-research team, independently validated performance, or a capital path through wearable deployment. Recruiting heads of sensing, ML, and hardware could close those gaps; the openings do not close them today.

Market structure also matters. AlterEgo directly targets silent physiological speech, while Subtle offers whisper-based input that could satisfy part of the same need. Subvocal must make wearing and calibrating another device worthwhile relative to these alternatives. Planned pre-orders, a showcase win, and website traffic establish neither repeat use nor willingness to pay. I therefore use the demand default rather than treat absent revenue as evidence of rejection. My pattern of investing across a technology transition makes this category interesting, but it does not establish which participant captures the value.

Better models could improve decoding, yet the dossier establishes no retained workflow state, integrations, or exclusive dataset that would let Subvocal compound that improvement. Withholding the sensing architecture protects disclosure; it does not establish reproduction cost. The physical-business red flag remains an underwriting question: no pricing, manufacturing costs, or margins show that technology changes the economics of selling hardware. There is also no evidence of the founder-liquidity or status-fundraising red flags. The founders’ previous products support execution credit, without proving unusual sacrifice or motivation.

One result would flip me from pass to active diligence: an independently observed, paid 30-day trial in which at least **15 of 20 unrelated users** achieve **100 correction-adjusted words per minute after no more than five minutes of daily calibration**, and still use the wearable at least four days in the final week. Those are my proposed thresholds. That experiment would connect the technical achievement to repeated customer usefulness—the missing bridge in The Subvocal Company’s investment case.
