# Pennant -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Pennant’s implied secret is that institutional voting is not primarily a research problem; it is a client-specific policy-memory problem that software can encode, audit, and improve. What would prove this wrong? If asset owners still require the same manual judgment after Pennant has accumulated their policies, overrides, and precedents. The dossier never states that test, names who rejects the proposition, or reports a head-to-head result. The secret remains my reconstruction of the product—not yet Pennant’s demonstrated discovery.

The founders are the strongest evidence. Ryan Stewart spent more than a decade across State Street proxy voting, BlackRock Aladdin, and PJT’s board-advisory work; Tomas Taylor combines institutional-product experience with AI-agent engineering. They met at Cornell 15 years ago and later worked on the same Aladdin desk. This is a company formed from long-shared context, not two people assembled for YC. Their Founder/CEO and Founder/CTO roles indicate commitment, although the dossier does not establish ownership, compensation, or whether every essential technical component is in-house.

The monopoly question is much weaker. Pennant names institutional investors, corporate advisors, and public companies simultaneously rather than a bounded beachhead it already dominates. ISS and Glass Lewis control a reported 97% of proxy advice, while Tumelo, Minerva, Proxymity, and internal systems overlap with parts of the workflow. Public filings, general-purpose models, and policy rules can reproduce the visible product. Retained approvals, overrides, and rationales might eventually create proprietary institutional memory, but “might accumulate” is not a moat. It is a description of how a moat could begin.

The strongest bull case is unusually substantive for pre-seed: a large U.S. public pension plan, a Top AmLaw 100 firm, and governance advisors are already paying; a global asset manager is a design partner; and S&P 500 companies are engaged. Founder-led enterprise sales are appropriate for custom-priced, high-trust governance software. This also fits the less-visible pattern of backing systems that make contested institutional judgments legible. Yet the dossier supplies no ARR, contract values, sales-cycle duration, retention, margins, or customer-expansion evidence. Revenue proves pain and execution; it does not yet prove compounding distribution or fund-scale economics.

A genuine zero-to-one technical result can override an uncertain monopoly, but Pennant has not shown one. The Startup Fellowship selection, audit trails, and human-review controls are useful credibility signals, not independent evidence that its recommendations are more accurate, ten times faster, or difficult for incumbents to reproduce. Nor does the December 2025 regulatory change solve this: regulation may fragment demand toward custom policies while simultaneously encouraging every established vendor to add the same feature.

By August 2029, I expect Pennant to reach roughly $3–8 million in ARR and be acquired by Diligent as governance-workflow software rather than become the independent last mover. I pass today—not because the team is generic, but because the company currently creates more value than it can demonstrably capture. One event would flip me: a top-20 asset manager converting a measured head-to-head pilot into a multi-year system-of-record contract after documenting a tenfold reduction in review labor without degrading policy accuracy. That would turn the inferred secret, beachhead, and proprietary workflow memory into one observable fact.
