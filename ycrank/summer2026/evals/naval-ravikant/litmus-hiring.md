# Litmus -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the founders’ résumés. It is that two recent engineering candidates turned their recruiting experience into a product that has already evaluated 3,000 assessments and reached $60K ARR. Shaivi Rau brings employer-side hiring exposure; Elena Zhao has worked as an engineer at Meta and Two Sigma. They have converted specific knowledge into something employers buy. For a three-person pre-seed team, that is real execution—not claimed insight.

Litmus also fixes a genuine mismatch. Engineers now work with AI inside large codebases, while conventional screens isolate them inside artificial problems and hosted editors. Generating trials from repositories and tickets, allowing normal AI tools, recording the workflow, and applying deterministic tests observes the work rather than a proxy for the work. Autumn AI, Sleuth, and Neo Scholars provide early evidence that employers recognize the difference.

The problem is that product accuracy and company durability are not the same thing. HackerRank, CodeSignal, and Karat already possess customer relationships, assessment data, ATS integrations, and adjacent features. They can copy repository-derived tasks and workflow recording. Litmus has permission to learn from 3,000-plus submissions, but it has not shown that this corpus produces a grading advantage competitors cannot reproduce. Code is leverage for the builder; code alone is not leverage against the copier.

The customer-created loop is weaker still. Candidates generate activity data that may improve future grading, but there is no cohort, referral, benchmarking, or validity evidence showing that one participant measurably improves the product for another. This is currently a vendor processing assessments, not a permissionless network compounding while the team sleeps. The unstated patterns I favor—outsiders contributing useful supply, fragmented markets becoming networks, or individual knowledge becoming scalable distribution—are possible futures here, not present facts.

The bull case is that $60K ARR and 3,000 assessments this early signal a category transition: AI makes legacy coding tests obsolete, and employer-specific work trials become the new system of record for technical hiring. The direct contracts matter more than the $2.30B top-down market estimate. But Litmus has not yet identified the adjacent market unlocked by the same capability, and repository security, extensive candidate recording, ownership terms, and unvalidated LLM grading can slow adoption precisely where trust matters most. I see no founder-integrity, artificial-deadline, or morale red flag. I also see no known-exception mechanism—an already-compounding network, an unusually irreplaceable technical artifact, or demonstrated capital-efficient product language—that overrides the copyability problem.

I would not pass permanently, but I would not write the personal check today. My verifiable flip condition is a longitudinal study across multiple named employers showing Litmus assessments predict six-month engineering performance materially better than incumbent screens, alongside retained customers and a grading advantage that improves with proprietary submission data. That would turn 3,000 processed assessments from usage into an asset. Until then, Litmus is a capable early business with direct value capture, but not yet an asymmetric option.
