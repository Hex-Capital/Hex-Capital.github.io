# Induction Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the compelling version is not merely an agent clicking buttons. It is software that learns unfamiliar interfaces by watching them, imagines the next state, and acts without an application-specific labeling project. Induction Labs has built a credible leak from that future: Photon-1 is a 106B-A5B mixture-of-experts model trained on 575 million frames, using discrete latent tokens, future-state prediction, instruction tuning, and online reinforcement learning. The causal chain is unusually concrete. What remains missing is the lived destination—who delegates what work, which institutions change, and why this architecture wins after six more years of model progress.

The artifact itself is substantial. An index of two billion public videos, roughly two million filtered screen recordings, a differential encoder compressing frames by more than 100x, and custom fused kernels constitute real lower-stack work. The reported 30x reduction in pretraining compute and 3x lower serving cost than Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite could make computer-use intelligence available to many downstream agent builders. This is the strongest part of the bet: Induction Labs controls a corpus and training system beneath the fashionable application layer rather than wrapping somebody else’s API. But the benchmark results are internal, and H Company already offers an inference API and open-weight Holo3.1 models. A bottleneck is not controlled merely because it is technically difficult.

Jonathan Li’s biography looks independently derived rather than assembled for this round. He conducted NLP research in high school, published first-author ACL work in 2023, and then worked on synthetic data, reasoning, and reinforcement-learning infrastructure at Cohere. David Li brings software and machine-learning experience from Waterloo, Shopify, and Sunnybrook, and the brothers say they are building together “again.” That is strong Tizz and credible technical Biz. I have less evidence of Rizz beyond recruiting one another, and almost none that either founder has learned the commercial levers of selling foundational infrastructure.

The broken-system economics are narrower than the technology. Application-specific action labels impose cost and delay; Induction reports replacing that machinery with unlabeled screen video at dramatically lower compute and serving expense. Yet the dossier does not identify who currently captures that rent, quantify a buyer’s labeling bill, or show whose occupational control is returned. The company may be eliminating an engineering expense without yet creating user agency. Those are different things.

Here is the objection that stings: pre-revenue is not the problem. A functioning prototype plus unusual founder obsession can override missing revenue, as it did with CosmicBrain AI; Photon-1 is arguably the more ambitious technical artifact. If its cost claims survive independent testing, this team could become the model layer through which thousands of agents learn software. The material difference is that CosmicBrain had signed industrial proofs of concept, while Induction Labs discloses no customer, design partner, repeat usage, or completed external action. Eighty thousand announcement views are attention, not costly behavior. My 36-month base case is therefore an acquisition by Cohere for the team, corpus, and training IP before Induction reaches $5 million ARR—not an independent platform business.

The harder gate is data custody. Training on approximately two million public screen recordings without disclosed licenses, consent rules, or removal procedures could expose passwords, personal messages, customer records, and other information whose publication did not confer permission for model training. That directly activates my surveillance and consent red flag. I am not calling the dataset unlawful from an inferred risk, but I will not invest my own money while the architecture’s scarce asset may also be its liability. I would flip to invest if an independent data-rights audit verifies a documented chain of permission and enforceable deletion process for the training corpus. Until then, this is a technically magnetic company I watch rather than fund.
