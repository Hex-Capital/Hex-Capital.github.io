# Mireye -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I begin with three blanks: no proposed post-money valuation, no competing-term-sheet count, and no evidence that this is genuinely the first scarce check. For me, those are not administrative omissions. They determine whether Mireye offers cheap exposure to a non-consensus belief or asks me to buy an increasingly fashionable “infrastructure for physical-world agents” story. With round terms absent, I underwrite the deal gate at its low prior.

The product is more concrete than the category language. Mireye has normalized 85 sources into more than 300 fields, attaches provenance, timestamps, and confidence levels, and lets accepted field requests expand the catalog. A customer reportedly used agents built on it to source off-market data-center land 100 times faster. That proves useful workflow compression, but not yet the dark pool I need: no paid conversion, repeat usage, request volume, or retention shows that Mireye owns the workflow rather than temporarily assembling public data more conveniently.

The strongest bull case is that this is unusually substantive pre-seed infrastructure. Ansh Chokshi previously built Poker Pit to $250,000 ARR in four months and an acquisition, then manually tested outbound until he reached a 10% response rate. The catalog is growing 20% week over week, and data-center development is a narrow initial wedge where fragmented county, utility, and government records impose real costs. Those facts make the team more credible than another generic MCP wrapper.

But the translation layer is incomplete. Chokshi has shipped AI software and sold a company, yet the dossier shows no GIS, land-development, utility, insurance, or industrial operating receipt. Meanwhile, Mapbox already exposes hosted and self-hosted MCP access, CARTO operates over enterprise warehouse data, and Fused supports programmable geospatial workflows. If customers can obtain the necessary location context from those platforms—or build internally once their workflow becomes valuable—Mireye’s 85-source catalog remains integration labor, not proprietary access.

The capital plan is the decisive failure. There is no named hypothesis, dated milestone, pass/fail metric, or explanation of what this round funds that could not be financed through customer revenue. The $19 and $99 monthly plans establish packaging, not economics: there is no revenue, gross margin, burn, runway, or evidence that on-demand field construction becomes cheaper with scale. Delete the next financing and I cannot tell whether Mireye reaches break-even or merely accumulates more connectors while subsidizing difficult queries.

I pass at the current evidence level. My one verifiable flip condition is ten paying data-center customers who each retain for six months, with positive gross margin and at least half requesting proprietary fields unavailable through Mapbox, CARTO, or Fused. That would show both that the catalog causes purchases and that customer demand compounds into an owned data asset. Without it—and without a disclosed, cheap round tied to that experiment—I am financing a plausible product inside a consensus category, not a scarce truth.
