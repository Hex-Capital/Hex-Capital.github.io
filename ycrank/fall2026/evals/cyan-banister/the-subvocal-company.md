# The Subvocal Company -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine sending a computer instructions on a crowded train without making a sound or taking my hands out of my pockets. The Subvocal Company is aiming at a small, strange change that could make computers available in many more moments of ordinary life. But that is my daydream. The dossier establishes deliberate internal articulation as the intended input; it does not establish a six-year path from laboratory decoding to an interface people can forget they are wearing. Cross-session reliability and portable hardware remain unresolved engineering work.

The number I want to get underneath is **200 words per minute**. At what accuracy, on whose vocabulary, after how much calibration? The company reports that prototype speed, while an earlier prototype reportedly achieved 45% accuracy using under two hours of training data. Those measurements describe different things; they neither establish a contradiction nor demonstrate usable continuous dictation. The recruiting posts are unusually useful here: the team explicitly identifies changes in user, session, and sensor placement as decoding challenges. A device that works until I put it back on tomorrow has a very different future from one that becomes an everyday input method. There is no documented repeated user behavior to settle that distinction.

The founders give me a reason to keep watching. Aarush Yadav previously co-founded GreenSat, which Georgia Tech reports reached more than **$2 million in annual revenue**; Arnav Kumar reports shipping a separate consumer app to **80,000-plus users**. Those are meaningful indications of commercial execution and getting software into people’s hands. Their February 2026 TreeHacks prototype also gives this project a concrete beginning. What I cannot establish is an independently developed obsession with physiological interfaces predating that event. These are builders with relevant accomplishments; the evidence for this particular lifelong pursuit is missing.

Why must a computer hear us to understand an instruction? That is a worthwhile systems question. Silent input could return discretion and freedom of movement to users, but the dossier identifies an acoustic constraint rather than a priced gatekeeper or institutional rent. There is no disclosed price or customer evidence establishing the economics of removing it. The decoder also fits my preference for infrastructure underneath visible applications: text entry and computer commands could both depend on it. However, withholding the sensing architecture establishes secrecy, not control of a scarce bottleneck. AlterEgo’s overlapping approach makes that distinction consequential.

The strongest objection to passing is that waiting for consumer adoption could mean waiting until the interesting technical bet has already been won. **CosmicBrain AI** illustrates the relevant exception: a functioning prototype can justify investing before revenue when accompanied by a founder’s longstanding technical obsession and signed industrial proofs of concept. Subvocal has the prototype claim and accomplished builders, but neither comparable domain history nor documented external commitments. Its physical enabling layer fits my otherwise unglamorous infrastructure pattern; the evidence does not yet establish the concealed asset that makes that pattern valuable. The CEO and CTO are identified, and no governance misconduct or dependence on one closed model supplier is documented. Physiological recordings do raise my consent-and-custody gate, although undisclosed safeguards are not evidence of an actual privacy violation.

I would **pass with my own money today**. My concern is the distance between an impressive decoding experiment and a habit someone chooses repeatedly. One verifiable result would change my decision: an independently observed 30-day trial in which ten previously untrained users voluntarily use the wearable daily, sustain at least 95% word accuracy across daily refitting with no more than two minutes of calibration, and retain control over their physiological recordings. That would make the future considerably less imaginary.
