# Mireye -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Ansh Chokshi took Poker Pit to $250,000 ARR in four months and an acquisition, while Mireye currently needs only two founders, four HTTP endpoints, and public federal datasets. That pairing is Mireye’s signature: unusually credible founder learning velocity attached to a capital-light product too small for my comparative advantage. Mireye may become a useful business. “Useful” is not the same as “too important not to do.”

**Slash** (2024) is the high rung for demonstrated learning rate because its documented pivot revealed founders who updated faster than the market and earned a $100M Series C. Mireye shares part of that mechanism: Chokshi has already built, monetized, and exited Poker Pit, reaching $250,000 ARR in four months. Mireye diverges where it matters—the dossier identifies no pivot, rejected thesis, or “we were wrong” moment behind Mireye itself. Prior velocity earns credit; it does not prove the intellectual flexibility that made Slash exceptional.

**DoorDash** (2013) is the low rung on required-capital fit: a capital-light software-and-logistics bet that did not require my balance sheet, rescued by ambition large enough to reshape commerce and produce a $72B IPO. Mireye exhibits the same initial mismatch without the override signal. Mireye’s free early-access API, public datasets, and deferred pricing suggest ordinary software capitalization, not the >$50M technical milestone of Commonwealth Fusion. Nothing in Mireye’s roadmap explains why a concentrated Khosla-sized check changes what becomes possible.

The **$102.7B geospatial-analytics market** could compound for Mireye if every physical-world agent begins querying one provenance-bearing answer layer before making insurance, energy, construction, or logistics decisions. Over 18 months, query feedback could sharpen Mireye’s schemas, coverage, and confidence estimates into a data flywheel. The dossier does not yet establish that mechanism: Mireye aggregates 175 fields from USGS, NOAA, FEMA, USDA, EPA, EIA, and NREL, while developers can access those sources directly. No incumbent regime disappears. Today Mireye improves geospatial retrieval; it does not break the economics of geospatial decision-making.

**Mapbox’s 28 billion daily map detections** define the technical counterfactual. Mireye would move up a tier if Mireye developed a proprietary observation network, uniquely accurate temporal model, or reliability architecture that Mapbox and CARTO could not reproduce without years of data. Four REST endpoints, a hosted MCP server, and normalized federal fields are competent engineering, not a falsifiable scientific miracle. MCP is an interface standard. Interface standards reduce adoption friction and imitation friction simultaneously.

**Glean** is the single useful parallel on founder defiance: Arvind Jain represented expert continuity, but consequence and enterprise information asymmetry overrode my skepticism of experts who extrapolate the past. Mireye has the continuity without that override. Chokshi previously led AI/ML engineering at Seismic, and Shashwat Kapoor specializes in scalable data platforms and distributed systems—the precise backgrounds expected for Mireye’s architecture. Mireye provides no documented controversial thesis, rejected industry assumption, or public contradiction of GIS orthodoxy. Credentials establish capability, not contrarian truth.

The **175-field catalog across seven layers and 14 presets** leads to a specific forecast: by July 2029, Mireye will have pivoted from horizontal physical-world infrastructure into a U.S. property-risk API for insurance and real-estate underwriting, reaching $1M-$3M ARR rather than remaining a general-purpose agent layer. Mireye’s learning-rate advantage makes adaptation plausible; Mireye’s low capital requirements and public-source dependency make vertical specialization more likely than infrastructure dominance. A focused buyer pays for workflow accuracy. A horizontal agent does not pay much for fields Mapbox, CARTO, or federal agencies can expose.

**Poker Pit’s $250,000 ARR in four months** makes the strongest bull case sting: Chokshi has already demonstrated speed, Mireye already has design partners, and Mireye entered early enough that MCP distribution could make its schema the default before Mapbox recognizes the category. Slash shows that founder learning can override modest consequence, while DoorDash shows that capital-light businesses can grow into consequential systems. I still pass because Mireye has disclosed neither paying usage nor proprietary data, and adjacent platforms already possess distribution and overlapping capabilities. I would flip to Invest if Mireye reaches $1M ARR from at least ten paying agent-platform customers while demonstrating a proprietary dataset or accuracy advantage unavailable from Mapbox and the underlying federal sources.
