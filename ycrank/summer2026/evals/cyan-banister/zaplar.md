# Zaplar -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move six years forward: a hotel is no longer a pile of reservation, payment, housekeeping, and messaging systems. It is a spatial model of the property; software assigns routine work, and people handle exceptions. Zaplar has one artifact leaking from that future: its property graph and constrained-optimization system reportedly reduced estimated housekeeping walking distance by 20% across tens of schedules. That is specific enough to register. It is still an estimate from company-run tests, however, not evidence that operating hotels repeatedly trust agents with consequential decisions.

The hidden system economics are real. Hotels reconcile context across 5–15 products because each vendor owns a functional boundary, while staff pay the resulting tax in duplicated work and physical movement. Zaplar could return attention and operational control to hotel teams. But the dossier does not quantify integration spending, labor hours recovered, or the rent captured by any particular gatekeeper. Worse, replacing the core stack ties adoption to infrequent procurement windows, migrations, retraining, and production reliability. A broken architecture is not automatically a reachable market.

The founders have useful pieces of operating tuition. Douglas encountered event booking through his family’s hotel, Axel worked in hospitality, Jon has worked in cybersecurity since seventeen and built autonomous-driving systems, and Oscar has led substantial B2B projects. More than 100 hotel interviews and founder-led property visits show curiosity extending beyond a Demo Day story. What I cannot yet see is a long, dated trail of hotel software artifacts or one founder whose pre-company obsession made this outcome feel inevitable. Douglas is identified as CEO, but ownership and the contractual tiebreaker are undisclosed rather than proven safe.

Zaplar most strongly matches my concealed-infrastructure pattern: the property graph sits beneath visible hotel workflows and could improve housekeeping, bookings, revenue management, payments, messaging, and vendor coordination. That is more interesting than another hotel chatbot. It also concentrates risk. Agents spanning guest data, payments, and physical access create consent, custody, security, and operational-safety questions; staff approval helps, but no uptime history or security certification is public. Mews has 15,000 customers and $300 million of fresh capital, while Apaleo already connects more than 250 applications. Zaplar must prove that spatial context creates an architectural advantage they cannot reproduce, not merely a cleaner interface.

The strongest bull case is that pilots already operate in several countries and the 20% scheduling result is the first narrow proof of a much larger operating layer. The closest known exception is CosmicBrain AI: missing revenue did not matter because a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a founder’s lifelong robotics record jointly reduced the uncertainty. Zaplar has the prototype-like optimization evidence, but its pilots are unnamed, revenue and repeat usage are undisclosed, and the founder record is materially thinner. One piece of that override exists; the complete exception does not.

My 36-month expectation is that Zaplar reaches roughly $1–3 million ARR through housekeeping and operational-coordination deployments but has not displaced the core PMS across meaningful hotel groups. I am Neutral and would not write the personal check today: the enabling layer is compelling, while the costly user behavior needed to validate a system-of-record migration is absent. My flip condition is three named multi-property hotel groups operating Zaplar across bookings, payments, and housekeeping for twelve months and then renewing as paying customers.
