# Canos -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Canos becomes a large company if molecular monitoring gives veterinary oncologists information worth repeatedly buying: a treatment change they can make earlier, at a price owners will pay and a cost that leaves attractive margins. Its proposed combination of cfDNA analysis and longitudinal comparison creates a plausible route to that market. But the dossier does not establish the decisive link between detecting a molecular change and improving a clinical decision. I would pass on a personal check today. The missing piece is the market’s controlling mechanism, rather than a larger screening TAM.

The strongest technical evidence is the company-reported analysis of 303 dogs: 90.6% sensitivity at 95.1% specificity under nested cross-validation. That warrants scientific attention. It does not establish prospective performance for the marketed assay, treatment-monitoring utility, or an economic discontinuity. At an illustrative 1% cancer prevalence, those sensitivity and specificity figures would imply roughly 16% positive predictive value, assuming they transferred unchanged. This is why the initial customer matters: oncology monitoring and broad screening have different economics. Origin’s $699 price includes a veterinary visit, but the dossier provides no assay cost, margin, or demonstrated reduction in diagnostic expense. I cannot yet award Canos a technology-driven market-expansion premium.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ relevant prior learning. Tsai previously co-founded ZYTCA, whose pathology product her YC biography reports reached more than 10,000 clinics; Li brings computational pathology research and experience building veterinary AI with her. That is substantive second-time operating experience, although the prior clinic reach is not Canos distribution. The combination fits my pattern of backing physical-world businesses where scientific expertise can change what is technically possible. My research-stage exception deserves consideration here: the benchmark and domain experience are real. However, the dossier does not establish the exceptional technical advance and credible development-capital path needed to substitute for commercial pull.

Canos also owns more workflow than an isolated prediction model. Origin connects veterinary collection, laboratory processing, inherited genetics and a banked baseline. That persistent baseline earns some durability credit because subsequent measurements can accumulate around the same animal. The proposed treatment-outcome dataset remains prospective, however; neither exclusivity nor the cost of reproducing it is established. CanCan already sells sequencing-based canine blood testing. Better analysis could improve both companies, so Canos needs a defensible clinical loop beyond access to sequencing and research datasets. Its physical delivery also makes contribution margin a necessary test of the business.

Samples processing is an operational milestone, but it does not establish repeat demand. Completed-test counts and realized revenue are undisclosed, while accepted OncoTrack trial participants receive free testing. I treat those omissions as missing evidence, without assuming failed adoption. My verifiable flip condition is a prospective, paid monitoring cohort across independent oncology practices demonstrating repeat orders, veterinarian-documented treatment changes and positive contribution margin. That single result would connect Canos’s strongest assets—scientific capability and prior operating experience—to the buying behavior that determines whether this market works.
