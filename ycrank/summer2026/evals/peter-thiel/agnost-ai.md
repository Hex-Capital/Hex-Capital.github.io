# Agnost AI -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Agnost AI’s proposed secret is that the decisive failures of AI agents are encoded in conversations, not conventional traces or prewritten evaluations, and that those failures can be converted into tested code changes. What would prove this wrong? If teams using Agnost discover no more actionable failures—or ship no better fixes—than teams using LangSmith, Braintrust, Arize, or Langfuse. The product is real and processes more than 1 million events daily, but the dossier provides no comparative detection rate, accepted-PR rate, or independently measured improvement. This remains a plausible secret, not a demonstrated one.

The founders are the strongest part of the company. Shubham Palriwala and Parth Ajmera have known each other for eight years rather than meeting opportunistically to apply to YC. Their experience spans analytics at Cisco, early product work at Formbricks, terabyte-scale Spark pipelines at Microsoft, and graphics systems at Infurnia. Both appear operationally committed, and the core engineering is internal. Founder ownership, salaries, and secondary liquidity remain undisclosed, so alignment is promising rather than fully proved.

The central problem is capitalism versus competition. Agnost has selected a recognizable wedge—conversation-derived frustration, demand, and automated fixes—but not yet a monopoly mechanism. LangSmith already categorizes agent behavior, while Braintrust, Arize, and Langfuse control adjacent tracing and evaluation workflows. One million daily events could become proprietary data, yet there is no evidence that customer data is pooled, that its taxonomies improve across accounts, or that customers cannot leave after the published 7-to-90-day retention window. Product-specific history may create switching costs eventually; resemblance to a moat is not a moat today.

The bull case is substantial enough to sting. Teams associated with Google, Exa Labs, and Corgi Insurance are reportedly using the product; an engineer specifically described integrating Agnost with Google’s MCP Toolbox for Databases. The earlier MCP product handled roughly 2.5 million calls monthly, the current product exceeds 1 million events daily, and the founders have converted technical content and personal reach into 77 Hacker News points, 40 comments, and 2,885 company followers. This could be an early developer-distribution loop inside the expanding AI production stack—one of the areas where infrastructure companies can become last movers.

But usage is not yet value capture. Pricing is coherent—$49 for 10,000 monthly events, $499 for 1 million, and custom Enterprise contracts—but no paid-customer count, ARR, margin, conversion rate, or sales cycle is disclosed. At published Pro pricing, enormous event volume can still produce modest revenue, while sensitive conversation data adds enterprise-security work. My 36-month base case is that Agnost reaches roughly $1–3 million ARR and is acquired by LangChain for under $100 million because its conversation-product analytics becomes a valuable suite feature rather than an independent monopoly.

I would pass today. The known exception for a crowded AI market requires an independently verified zero-to-one technical event; processing volume and reviewed PR generation do not yet meet that standard. One result would flip the decision: a cohort study showing that at least 30% of Agnost-generated fixes are merged and produce a sustained, independently measured reduction in agent-task failures across multiple paying customers. That would turn the secret from slogan into proof and could make the accumulated failure-to-fix dataset genuinely proprietary.
