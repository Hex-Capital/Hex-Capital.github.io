# GUILD -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The February 2026 arms-transfer strategy is pushing government buyers toward additional domestic capacity, and GUILD becomes large only if external factories can deliver compliant aerospace parts faster while leaving the company with attractive gross profit after supplier, testing, and failure costs.

That is the controlling belief. The 40% decline in small-business participation establishes a supply-base problem, but the $431.2 billion U.S. aerospace-parts market does not establish GUILD’s economics. The company says it wins and executes government contracts, yet discloses neither contract count nor value, delivery performance, repeat awards, or gross margin. Without those numbers, the dossier identifies the mechanism but does not demonstrate the threshold at which orchestration becomes a scalable business rather than a service-heavy prime contractor.

The product architecture is the strongest part of the case. GUILD retains responsibility for pricing, compliance, production, testing, documentation, logistics, and delivery, while its ontology preserves requirements, quotations, supplier interactions, awards, and outcomes. This is more durable than an AI interface: better models should improve specification extraction and factory matching without absorbing the underlying permissions, execution history, or contractual accountability. The unresolved issue is whether that execution memory materially improves bids and deliveries before a competitor can reproduce it. No integration count, proprietary-data volume, or measured learning effect is disclosed.

Demand remains suggestive rather than proven. An active supplier-integration process demonstrates an attempt to assemble capacity, not customer pull. The statement that GUILD executes contracts deserves more weight than its 197 LinkedIn followers or YC’s $500,000 investment, but undisclosed awards cannot show repeat usage or willingness to pay materially. Hadrian’s roughly $1.89 billion of funding and owned factories also create a difficult counterfactual: if controlled capacity determines delivery reliability, GUILD’s capital-light network may be structurally weaker rather than more scalable.

The strongest bull case is that policy has created a defense-production discontinuity precisely when fragmented factories need a new prime contractor. This resembles the mechanism behind Anduril—an institutional opening combined with a different delivery model—but the material difference is that GUILD has not shown product-style margins or controlled production capacity. The known exception therefore does not yet apply. My physical-business red flag remains active: AI-aware contracting does not create software economics if commodity fabrication, compliance labor, supplier failures, and working capital retain the economics.

Celebi brings credible ML experience and Gurlemis brings finance and client-operations exposure, but neither biography documents defense manufacturing, prior startup learning, or costly personal commitment. My base case is that by August 2029 GUILD has shifted its center of gravity toward selling Forge as procurement and compliance software, with less than $3 million in ARR, because the owned workflow is more defensible than assuming prime-contract execution risk. I pass today. The verifiable flip condition is $2 million in annualized gross profit from repeat defense customers at a disclosed gross margin above 25%.
