# Async -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Ben Smith is the named CEO and Connor Park is the named CTO, making Async strongest on command structure and weakest on autobiographical problem ownership: a clear-command, unlived-problem company. **Anduril** is the high rung because Palmer Luckey’s authority and product conviction were inseparable; Async shares the explicit CEO/CTO delineation, but Async provides no equity split, tiebreaker protocol, or evidence that Smith has final authority when the founders disagree. Biz and Tiz have assigned seats. Rizz under pressure—and the actual steering wheel—remain unobserved.

**DeepMind** is the low rung on lived experience because elite credentials substituted for an autobiographical encounter with the problem; the exception worked because Demis Hassabis brought extraordinary chess, neuroscience, and game-design compounding. Async offers no comparable exception. Smith sold shoes, trading cards, and collectibles, while Park studied computer science; neither Async founder is shown running a law firm, clinic, or real-estate operation. Async’s progression from nonprofit agents to billing software to legal agents and then horizontal service businesses looks like searching for a market, not being found by one. The dossier contains no lived-domain evidence, so I apply the absent-evidence default.

Relevance AI has raised $37 million, Gumloop has raised $70 million, and Anthropic now ships 15 small-business workflows, placing Async inside consensus rather than outside the map. Over 18 months, Async’s hands-on audits could compound into proprietary workflow memory and action history, but only if deployments become reusable software rather than bespoke labor. Otherwise every Async customer creates another snowflake while the model providers absorb the common layer. “Long-horizon agents” could become magically weird; today Async is playing exactly the game it seems to be playing.

Async’s homepage claims SOC 2 Type I while Async’s security page says certification remains in progress, which is the wrong kind of mystery in legal and healthcare operations. The capital-intensive score would flip upward if Async had built a regulated execution layer—auditable approvals, malpractice-sensitive controls, or infrastructure that customers could not safely replace with Claude. Instead Async depends on external models and has disclosed no technical moat. The regulated customers are a rebuttal to “generic AI wrapper,” but the conflicting compliance claim prevents them from becoming an answer.

**Niantic** let me play Ingress and feel the product before Pokémon Go became the closest we ever came to world peace. Async has a weaker but real physical echo: Clifton Law and the Law Office of Joshua Lowenthal reportedly use Async for intake, conflict checks, engagement letters, drafting, research, and email. Those completed artifacts can be opened, approved, and observed, which matters more than another agent demo. Async still cannot be installed or experienced by a normal human within 48 hours, so the tactile signal belongs inside two customer offices rather than out in the world.

Two named law-firm customer stories point toward Async’s real shape more clearly than the $7.6 billion AI-agent TAM. By July 2029, Async will have abandoned its horizontal SMB positioning, narrowed to legal operations, and been acquired by Clio for under $50 million before reaching $5 million ARR. Async’s clear roles and consultative selling can build a useful vertical business; its unlived founder origin, custom delivery economics, and platform dependence make a category-defining horizontal company much less likely.

The $2.23 billion U.S. AI-agent market supplies the strongest bull case: Async already has named deployments, prices outcomes rather than seats, and has repeatedly pivoted until customers used the work. HQ Trivia is the exception worth respecting—observable product pull can override a structural concern—and Async’s customer-specific memory could become a switching-cost flywheel before Anthropic serves the messy last mile. The case still loses today because Async discloses no revenue, retention, deployment volume, proprietary data, or defensibility, while the Skope-to-Async sequence and conflicting SOC 2 language amplify two red flags at once. I would flip to Invest if Async verifies at least 20 paying law firms, 70% or better gross margins, and expansion revenue from more than half of them without proportional implementation headcount.
