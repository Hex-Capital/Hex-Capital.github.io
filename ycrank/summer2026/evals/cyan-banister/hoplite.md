# Hoplite -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: engineers no longer babysit coding agents on laptops; they supervise software factories that wake on an error, repair the code, prove the repair, and ask for human judgment only at the boundary. Hoplite has one lovely leak from that future: its first business installation turned ignored Sentry errors into proactive pull requests and continuously merged lower-priority tickets. But I am supplying most of the daydream. The dossier does not map the intervening transitions—credential isolation, trustworthy verification, environment portability, or organizational accountability—and Cursor and Factory already provide pieces of this behavior today.

The irritating system is real. Laptops fail, parallel agents collide over worktrees and ports, and cloud agents wake up without the sessions, dependencies, CLIs, or MCP configuration they need. Why does that complexity exist, who collects rent from it, and whose agency returns when Hoplite removes it? I cannot yet answer. The founders’ hands-on “Collison” installations suggest they are presently absorbing the integration labor rather than eliminating a quantified institutional restriction. That can be excellent discovery work, but it is not yet a demonstrated economic transfer.

Ryan Morrissey and Bence Redmond have more first-believer proof than their short résumés initially suggest. They met in university, built applications, freelanced, founded prior products together, interned at Stripe and AWS, and produced a previous product that reported 250-plus beta users and $855,000 in trading volume. Traveling to install Hoplite also shows useful willingness to touch the ugly operational layer. What is missing is a dated obsession with agent infrastructure before the category became crowded. The evidence supports persistent builders, not yet a team that independently derived this particular future years ahead of consensus.

The sharp bull case is that manual migration becomes the apprenticeship through which Hoplite learns every dependency, credential, and workflow that larger agent vendors overlook. The first installation produced completed engineering work, not a waitlist or rendering, and retained customer environments could eventually make Hoplite the agent-agnostic control plane. That case stings. It is also still supported by one founder-reported installation, no disclosed revenue or renewal, and no evidence that the customer chose Hoplite after testing Cursor or Factory. I credit the working artifact, but not repeated costly user behavior beyond the reporting cap.

This looks like unglamorous workflow repair beneath an overheated application market, which is attractive, but Hoplite does not yet control the concealed infrastructure. Cursor can already hand work between local and cloud agents; Factory provides persistent environments, alert triggers, and customer-hosted machines. More importantly, Hoplite moves sessions, code, credentials, CLIs, and MCP servers into internet-enabled environments without a disclosed limiting architecture for prompt-injection or exfiltration. That activates my privacy and custody gate. Ryan is identified as CEO and Bence as CTO, so there is no evidence of a leadership shell game; equal control or a contractual tiebreaker remains unknown rather than adverse.

I pass today, with curiosity. A functioning prototype alone does not earn the pre-revenue exception when signed proofs of concept, unusual founder obsession, and control of a hard bottleneck are also absent. My 36-month prediction is that Hoplite will have pivoted into security and observability infrastructure for enterprise coding agents, because the durable problem is governing autonomous access rather than teleporting sessions. One condition flips me: three named businesses renew paid six-month deployments after security review while routing at least two competing agent vendors through Hoplite’s control plane.
