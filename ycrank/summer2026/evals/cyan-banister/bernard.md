# Bernard -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the interesting version of Bernard is not an AI receptionist. It is the operating memory for appliance repair: identifying the machine, diagnosing the failure, ordering the correct part, and dispatching the right technician before a human reconstructs the case across five systems. The dossier gives me one leak from that future—large authorized servicers reportedly route thousands of daily calls through a working product—but it never maps the intervening steps from assisted Pre-ID to an autonomous repair company. That missing chain matters more than the $6.1 billion field-service-management TAM.

The present wound is real. Disconnected phones, inboxes, catalogs, and technician-held knowledge turn a first visit into a diagnostic trip. Bernard gathers model, symptom, repair-history, manufacturer, and parts evidence before dispatch. But who captures the rent today? How many truck rolls disappear? Whose income or control changes? The company sells monthly or annual subscriptions, yet publishes neither revenue nor improvement in first-visit completion. This is convincing workflow irritation without quantified system economics.

There is a functioning artifact, not a rendering. Mr. Appliance of West Loop uses its SMS operations; Bernard claims deployments with major authorized servicers and thousands of calls handled daily. That outweighs 100-plus Android downloads or 139 LinkedIn followers. The bull case stings: every completed repair could turn an appliance-specific beachhead into a proprietary symptoms-parts-outcomes graph that broad field-service platforms cannot easily reproduce. But Bernard publishes no cohort, accuracy, repeat-use, or repair-outcome data. Its own terms describe recommendations as probabilistic and leave qualified technicians holding the diagnostic risk.

The founders look capable but not inevitable. Dan Katzman brings operations, partnerships, and investing experience; David Goodfellow managed fraud and geo-compliance products and previously worked on enterprise automation. A nine-person team suggests some recruiting ability. What I cannot find is the pre-company obsession: a dated repair experiment, years spent inside a service network, or an operating wound that forced them to build this. Degrees and adjacent employment do not establish first-believer proof.

Bernard also sits awkwardly in the stack. Aggregated repair data could become an enabling asset, but today the company depends on Compass, Encompass, Marcone, Early Repair, and Samsung GSPN while ServiceTitan—already at $961 million in FY2026 revenue—can bundle voice, dispatch, billing, and customer records. Data-improvement rights are not yet control of a scarce bottleneck. The consent architecture is another live gate: customers bear TCPA and recording-consent obligations, while Bernard can aggregate customer data for model improvement. The dossier identifies a CEO and CTO, so I do not infer a leadership shell game, but it provides no ownership or contractual-tiebreaker evidence.

My base case is that Bernard becomes a useful appliance-software vendor reaching $3–8 million ARR by August 2029, not yet the hidden infrastructure beneath home services. None of my known exception patterns changes that: working-product evidence can overcome missing revenue, but not when the six-year mechanism and founder obsession are both absent. I pass today. One verifiable condition flips me: a customer-validated 90-day cohort showing at least a 20% increase in first-visit completion, with no material diagnostic or consent incidents.
