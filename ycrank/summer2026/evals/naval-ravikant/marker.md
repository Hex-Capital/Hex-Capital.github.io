# Marker -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders are the asset. Berwick, Drevno, and Pastewka have built together for 15 years, sold Drapr to Gap in 2021, and then operated inside the buyer; Berwick went further and founded Gap’s Office of AI. That is specific knowledge acquired from both sides of enterprise transformation: selling software into a large organization and discovering why deployment fails from within one. Few pre-seed teams have evidence this direct.

The same history creates the central risk. Marker embeds people, interviews frontline teams, maps undocumented workflows, and builds custom agents. The Fortune 500 retailer’s 80% reduction in manual work and the prop-tech customer’s reduction of onboarding from 120 to 60 days are meaningful product evidence. They do not yet prove leverage. If every new customer requires another expedition through the organization, Marker has built an excellent AI consultancy with a software interface.

Copy the Command Center and the question becomes uncomfortable. Distyl AI already combines a platform with embedded engineers and has raised approximately $202 million. Marker says deployments accumulate process knowledge and make subsequent agents faster, but it has disclosed neither deployment volume nor evidence that learning transfers across customers. Company-specific knowledge may create customer switching costs while remaining unavailable as a reusable company asset. A moat that stays trapped inside each account does not compound across the portfolio.

The strongest bull case is that these founders know how to convert consulting labor into product because they have already built and sold enterprise software together. Their reported deployments reached production in days and produced large operational improvements, while better-capitalized platforms often stall in pilots. Repeated workflow exposure could become a proprietary library of integrations, evaluations, and operating patterns. But today that is a plausible trajectory, not an existing substrate: the customers are unnamed, pricing is undisclosed, and there is no evidence that implementation effort declines with each deployment.

Marker does not match the permissionless pattern. Customers are not contributing supply, distribution, or utility for one another; value currently flows from a small embedded team to each customer. Nor does a known-exception override apply: a compelling prototype can excuse an absent network at pre-seed, but it cannot establish that bespoke delivery will become scalable. I see no evidence of an integrity, FOMO, or founder-stability red flag. The failure mode is structural, not personal.

My 36-month base case is that Marker becomes a $5–15 million annual-revenue AI transformation firm with software-assisted delivery, but not yet a self-reinforcing platform. That can be a good business without being the asymmetric angel option I want. I would not write the check today. One verifiable condition flips me: a cohort of at least ten paying customers showing that median implementation hours per deployed agent fell by 50% while dollar retention remained above 90%.
