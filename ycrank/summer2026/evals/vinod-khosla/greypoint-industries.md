# Greypoint Industries -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

$55.25 billion is the projected 2034 counter-UAS market, but the more important number is the million-euro missile fired at a drone swarm costing roughly €100,000. Greypoint Industries is a high-consequence, low-contrarian-defiance company: Greypoint Industries could break the economics of air defense, yet its founders currently look like capable practitioners entering an established technical race rather than instigators rejecting expert consensus. The world-state matters—cheap autonomous mass defeats expensive defensive scarcity—but the dossier does not yet show why Greypoint Industries, rather than Anduril, Fortem, or Airobotics, causes that transition.

**OpenAI** (2019) is the high rung on consequence because the $50 million bet addressed a civilizational variable before revenue or a settled moat existed. Greypoint Industries shares the load-bearing mechanism: if LEGION and ARROW make autonomous interception cheaper than autonomous attack, military procurement, force structure, and the economics of aerial warfare change. Greypoint Industries diverges because its maximum disclosed consequence remains bounded by a $55.25 billion counter-UAS category already occupied by Anduril’s Anvil and Fortem’s 4,500 reported captures. OpenAI could redirect most knowledge labor; Greypoint Industries currently redirects one weapons market. Consequence is substantial, not yet civilizationally general.

**Glean** exposes the low-rung founder pattern: domain experts extending a known playbook rather than publicly contradicting their field. Greypoint Industries shows the same pattern without Glean’s capital scale or information-market leverage. Nic Chu and Jordan Khoo came through General Dynamics, Darcy Zhang served as a Canadian Armed Forces infanteer, and Steven Xu built embedded controls at Bosch and UBC Sailbot. Those are useful credentials, but credentials are not heresy. Greypoint Industries provides no documented controversial thesis, rejected doctrine, or founder claim that incumbent counter-UAS experts consider impossible. Experts extrapolate the past; Greypoint Industries has not yet shown it refuses their extrapolation.

Fortem’s 4,500 captures define the technical compounding problem over the next 18 months. Greypoint Industries can create a real flywheel if its first Arrow contract produces encounter data that improves LEGION geolocation, interceptor guidance, manufacturing yield, and qualification evidence with every deployment. Greypoint Industries can also fail as four loosely coupled miracles: passive emitter localization, shared battlefield fusion, reliable autonomous interception, and low-cost manufacturing. No published range, speed, accuracy, test volume, patent, or field result establishes even the first miracle. This is harder than commodity software and capable of repelling generalist capital, but difficulty alone is not a moat—KiOR taught that expensive miracles still have to become measurable miracles.

Greypoint Industries’ signed first Arrow contract is evidence of customer access, not evidence of founder learning rate. For Greypoint Industries to flip this dimension into an invest-level signal, Darcy Zhang or Jordan Khoo would need to document a specific thesis update—for example, abandoning radar-led interception after field tests showed passive geolocation produced a superior cost exchange—and show that the revised system won repeat deployment. No public pivot, prior exit, or “we were wrong about X” episode appears in the dossier, so I apply the absent-evidence default rather than invent adaptability from a short pre-seed history. Learning rate is above everything else, but it must leave evidence.

**Genesis AI** (2025) is the relevant capital parallel: its $105 million seed financed a full-stack robotics ambition that ordinary SaaS capital could not attempt. Greypoint Industries has hardware, autonomy, electronic-warfare integration, qualification, and manufacturing needs that could eventually absorb large checks, but Greypoint Industries discloses no roadmap requiring more than $50 million to a first commercial milestone or more than $200 million to scale. A four-person company with one contract may become capital-hostile; it has not yet demonstrated a capital plan that makes my check uniquely useful. Understating defense burn would be especially dangerous—the Range Fuels lesson is that insufficient capital does not make hard technology capital-efficient.

Anduril’s approximately $2.2 billion of 2025 revenue makes my 36-month forecast specific: by July 2029, Greypoint Industries will reach roughly $3 million to $8 million in annualized defense-contract value and be acquired by General Dynamics Mission Systems–Canada for under $150 million rather than become an independent counter-UAS prime. Greypoint Industries has credible General Dynamics founder ties, a Canadian procurement opening, and a product strategically useful to an incumbent, but no disclosed performance or structural moat against platforms with operational histories. High consequence attracts capital and customers; low doctrinal defiance makes absorption by the existing regime more likely than collapse of that regime.

The signed first Arrow contract, Canada’s July 2026 Defence Drone Initiative, and the Defence Tech Hackathon win form the strongest bull case: Greypoint Industries could resemble the **Glean** exception, where domain-expert continuity becomes acceptable because consequence and execution scale overwhelm the lack of contrarian founder posture. Greypoint Industries could use Canadian procurement as a protected proving ground, accumulate threat data, then become the low-cost NATO standard before Anduril localizes. The objection stings because defense markets sometimes reward qualification timing more than original science. It does not yet win: Greypoint Industries reports no interception statistics, while Fortem reports 4,500 captures and Airobotics has an operational airport deployment. I remain Neutral. One condition flips me to Invest: Greypoint Industries publicly demonstrates at least 100 operationally representative autonomous intercepts and secures more than $10 million of contracted deployment from a named NATO defense agency while maintaining a materially better cost per kill than missile-based alternatives.
