# Adalat AI -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most revealing event in Adalat AI’s dossier is the September 8 security investigation. Someone demonstrated access to production-cluster administration. The team disabled the affected host, rotated credentials, and restricted access. That is a concrete response to a concrete obstacle. But the record does not establish which founder drove the repair, how quickly it happened, or whether the underlying weakness was independently retested. I give the response credit without turning it into proof of exceptional founder resourcefulness. In a courtroom, reliability is part of the product.

There is clearly a product here. Oxford’s institutional overview documented deployment in more than 4,000 courts across nine states by March 2026. That is substantially better evidence than an architecture diagram or an impressive résumé. Bhattacharya’s multilingual NLP background also fits the work. Adalat matches my pattern of backing technical teams that produce something concrete before asking investors to believe a large claim. Deployment establishes that the software functions in real institutions; it does not independently establish the company’s claimed 90%+ recognition accuracy.

The strongest demand evidence is smaller than the deployment headline. A judicial magistrate reports handling five to six witnesses daily instead of two to three. If that improvement survives careful measurement, judges have an urgent reason to keep this product. But the testimonial appears on Adalat’s website, and Kerala’s November 2025 adoption requirement complicates the interpretation of subsequent uptake. Required installation cannot tell us how many users would insist on keeping it. TERES’s human-reviewed transcription is a useful competitive reminder: the relevant output is a correct, finalized deposition, not merely recognized speech.

Saxena’s court practice and Bhattacharya’s language-technology experience make this a credible problem for these founders to attack. Court integrations, agreements, and staff training are precisely the tedious work that can conceal an opportunity. I give that combination meaningful credit, though the dossier lacks a dated account of Saxena encountering the problem and initiating the solution. The founders connected in February 2024 and subsequently built an MVP, so this is not merely a newly assembled application team. What remains absent is evidence that a rejected hypothesis caused them to change the product route. I cannot infer flexibility from rollout scale.

The strongest argument against withholding my money is **Watsi**: a nonprofit can deserve backing when actual behavior demonstrates extraordinary usefulness. Adalat has a serious version of that argument—external evidence of thousands of deployments, an operational court mandate, and a reported improvement in judicial throughput. The material difference is that Watsi’s launch produced immediate voluntary funding of every listed treatment; Adalat’s free, partly mandated adoption leaves both voluntary dependence and continuing financial support less clear. The $500,000 Agency Fund grant awarded in October 2025 establishes one funding event, not a durable financing loop. Pro bono court agreements also provide no conventional equity-return mechanism.

I would not write an angel check today. The partnership red flag is partly answered: these are executed court relationships, not promised introductions. Nor would I mistake nonprofit status for evidence of a bad product. My hesitation is that institutional reach currently outruns the evidence of founder-led adaptation and independently measured user dependence. One verifiable result would make me reconsider under the nonprofit exception: an independent 90-day audit showing that courts offered an opt-out continue using Adalat and sustain the magistrate’s reported improvement in correctly finalized depositions. That would show courts actively choosing a materially better way to work.
