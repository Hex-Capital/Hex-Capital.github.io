# 83 Sciences -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Eric Riesel developed materials-data ML systems during his MIT chemistry PhD, while 83 Sciences says all three founders personally suffered the problem of searching old experimental data. That makes 83 Sciences an organic scientific idea with an animal-evidence deficit. The founders understand the laboratory; the dossier does not yet show them doing anything unreasonable to win it. I see knowledge before resourcefulness. That is a promising but dangerous order.

**Dropbox** (2007) is the high rung for organic ideas: Drew Houston built the thing he needed after repeatedly losing his USB stick. 83 Sciences shares that mechanism because Riesel, Ian Naccarella, and Yankang Yang reportedly encountered the experimental-data problem themselves. But 83 Sciences diverges from Dropbox at the artifact. Houston's demo existed before the argument; 83 Sciences offers a claim about producing a co-authored manuscript in under two months without naming the manuscript, partner laboratory, or result. The pain is organic. The proof is still promotional.

**Kiko** (2005) exposed the low-rung failure mechanism: a clever product can end in a tidy sale when determination is not yet aimed at something enormous. 83 Sciences does not use acquihire language, but 83 Sciences gives me credentials where I want tracks in the snow. Naccarella has Stanford, HBS, Sila, and BCG; Yang was a BCG principal; Riesel has an MIT PhD. The grants of up to $50,000 could be a resourceful wedge, but paying laboratories to participate is not the same as persuading them to pay. No named partner, hacked-together deployment, rejected-customer reversal, or pre-funding stunt clears the animal test.

The $50,000 early-partner grants reveal the schlep inside 83 Sciences. Over the next 18 months, 83 Sciences must extract incompatible instrument files, recover undocumented experimental context, negotiate ownership of failed trials, earn scientists' trust, and settle patent rights. Each completed integration could compound into a proprietary history of conditions and outcomes. Each bespoke manuscript project could also compound into consulting work that never becomes software. **Coinbase** (2012) turned compliance into an asset because Armstrong ran toward the grind; 83 Sciences earns credit for choosing a similarly unpleasant data-access problem, but not yet for surviving it.

Eric Riesel's generative method for chemical experimental data and Yankang Yang's AI programs deployed to more than 30,000 users make 83 Sciences technically credible. They do not yet establish hacker genealogy. No public repository contains the working agent, no benchmark demonstrates discovery quality, and Naccarella's visible GitHub trail consists of Beer-Type-Prediction and MBTI projects. The score would flip upward if 83 Sciences released even a narrow technical artifact—an instrument-data parser, reproducible benchmark, or working scientific-agent demo—that researchers adopted without being paid. Hackers leave artifacts, not merely biographies.

**Stripe** (2010) had two brothers who had already built and sold Auctomatic together; the prior working relationship was part of the evidence. 83 Sciences has the right numerical configuration at three founders, and 83 Sciences has complementary coverage across chemical engineering, experimental chemistry, materials ML, and large-scale AI deployment. Naccarella and Yang also overlapped at BCG. But the dossier does not say how long they built together, how Riesel joined them, or whether the three have endured a failed project together. Three is structurally better than one. It is not automatically a band.

Citrine's 550 deployed models, 380 projects, and 70,000 monthly experiment suggestions show what 83 Sciences must displace. By July 2029, I expect 83 Sciences to have pivoted from a general cross-laboratory discovery network into bespoke materials-data integration for battery and chemical laboratories, producing $1–3 million in annual recurring revenue without becoming the shared experimental-data system it describes today. The organic founder insight should win 83 Sciences several serious engagements. The missing animal artifact and laboratory-by-laboratory IP schlep should keep those engagements service-heavy.

**Airbnb** (2009) is the strongest objection to that forecast. 83 Sciences could look merely unproven because the public dossier predates the cereal-box moment: three domain-matched founders, a problem all three lived, an under-two-month manuscript claim, and $50,000 grants could conceal an unusually effective way to acquire proprietary data. Airbnb teaches me not to reject a wrong-looking model when the founders are animals. But Airbnb brought the cereal boxes into the room; 83 Sciences brings no named laboratory, paid engagement, manuscript, patent, or verified discovery. I remain Neutral. Three named laboratories each renewing a paid six-figure engagement after a validated experimental result would flip my verdict to Invest.
