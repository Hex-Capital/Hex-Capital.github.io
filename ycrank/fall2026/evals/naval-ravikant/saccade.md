# Saccade -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Shreyas Bhardwaj has built something that exposes him to reality. UCSB’s 2026 project record identifies his participation in developing Saccade’s attachment, including custom cameras, illumination, chassis, and thermal management. That is specific knowledge acquired through engineering, supported by an artifact. I give it substantial founder credit. But building an instrument that measures eye movements and establishing that those movements reveal Alzheimer’s pathology are separate achievements. The investment depends on the second.

Saccade clears my simplest software objection: copying its code would not reproduce the entire instrument. Yet physical construction alone does not establish a durable advantage. The patent is pending, its claims are unavailable, and ViewMind already measures eye movements during cognitive tasks. The potentially compounding asset would be recordings linked to confirmed pathology, with rights to use them and demonstrated predictive value. The dossier establishes neither an exclusive dataset nor a model advantage. Each patient currently supplies another measurement; there is no demonstrated mechanism through which that patient improves the product or distribution for the next patient.

The strongest bull case deserves attention. A 15-minute assessment, a working attachment, and company-reported deployment in San Francisco clinics could give this team a practical way to collect valuable observations before better-funded competitors move. This fits my recent preference for physical infrastructure and proprietary models. My prototype-first exception also applies: a strange product can deserve investment before revenue or distribution becomes legible. But Saccade’s unresolved question concerns whether its central measurement supports its promised use. The homepage explicitly discloses that diagnosis and individual risk prediction remain unvalidated, while the October launch claims detection up to 15 years before symptoms. That gap limits conviction. It does not, by itself, establish dishonesty.

The initial buyer is identifiable: US longevity clinics. Expansion is much less established. The cited $10.2 billion Alzheimer’s diagnostics market includes several technologies and clinical purposes; Saccade has not shown how its present assessment earns access to that spending. The May 2025 FDA-cleared blood test creates a concrete comparator. An asymptomatic risk signal also does not establish treatment eligibility, which requires confirmed amyloid pathology and the relevant clinical stage. Saccade needs an answer that changes a buyer’s decision. Public pricing, completed paid assessments, and revenue are absent, so I assign the pre-seed default for value capture rather than assuming either demand or bad economics.

I am neutral on Saccade and would keep my money uncommitted today. The founder has demonstrated the ability to build the sensor; the evidence has not yet demonstrated the business’s scarce asset. Neither a permissionless contribution system nor customer-created distribution is visible, and the prototype exception does not resolve the biological uncertainty. My one flip condition is an independently conducted, blinded study in the intended asymptomatic population showing that Saccade’s system predicts confirmed amyloid status materially better than a standard eye-tracking baseline at the same sensitivity. That would connect the engineering achievement to a defensible clinical advantage.
