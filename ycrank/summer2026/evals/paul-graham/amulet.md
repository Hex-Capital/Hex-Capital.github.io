# Amulet -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Nithik Bala and Varun Puru are two former Meta engineers building a technically serious filesystem, but Amulet’s strongest signal is the founder configuration and its weakest is animal resourcefulness. Amulet looks like a well-matched pair capable of constructing a difficult artifact, without the cereal-box story showing they will crawl through broken glass to make anyone use it. That distinction matters. Good engineers can build impressive systems. Animals manufacture demand when the system alone does not.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for co-founder configuration: two brothers with years of shared hacking history, complementary ability, and an organic commerce problem. Amulet shares the important mechanism of two technical founders with overlapping infrastructure experience; Bala and Puru both worked at Meta, and Amulet’s copy-on-write forks and content-addressed commits require genuine systems competence. But Amulet’s dossier merely says they worked at the same large employer. It does not say Bala and Puru worked together, built together, or even knew each other for long before Amulet. Amulet sits fairly high on configuration, but well below the Collisons’ unusually strong prior bond.

**Kiko** (2005)’s eBay exit is the low-rung failure mechanism for resourcefulness: improvisation directed toward escaping a product rather than making users want it. Amulet has no acquihire framing, and Amulet’s rapid pivot from customer-success agents to an AI filesystem may represent useful stubbornness rather than aimlessness. But the dossier names no unscalable customer rescue, revenue stunt, rejected-user conversion, or pre-funding act comparable to a Dropbox demo. The 1.9-ms p99 latency and 8.4-GB/s throughput are company-published claims, not tracks left by an animal in the market. Amulet is above Kiko’s low rung, but below the point where an artifact becomes evidence of relentless resourcefulness.

Nithik Bala’s Rockset and Meta web-infrastructure experience gives Amulet plausible founder-problem proximity, while Varun Puru’s Amazon and backend-systems work reinforces it. The missing fact is that either founder personally suffered from copying large datasets across disposable agent jobs. Over 18 months, an organic idea compounds because the founders know which irritating details matter before customers can articulate them; a trend-derived idea accumulates features copied from Archil, E2B, and Modal. Amulet’s recent pivot makes that distinction especially important. Amulet has relevant biographies, but relevance is not the same as wanting the product for oneself.

Amulet’s partial POSIX coverage, conflict-checked publishing, immutable commits, and exclusion of live same-file multi-writer access reveal a real systems schlep. Amulet is doing unpleasant correctness work that most agent startups would rather hide behind object-storage APIs. But Amulet has also scoped around one of the nastiest parts: concurrent shared writes and locks. I would score the schlep much higher if a named beta partner had moved a large production dataset into Amulet and Bala or Puru had personally handled migration failures, consistency bugs, and recovery at three in the morning. Without that evidence, Amulet is attacking a hard software problem, not yet demonstrating appetite for the full operational grind.

**Reddit** (2005) had hacker genealogy before it had a business: Aaron Swartz’s RSS work and the founders’ visible hacker-community presence were evidence independent of the startup. Amulet’s filesystem is itself a non-trivial artifact, so Amulet is not the credential-only AI wrapper I normally avoid. Yet Bala and Puru have no public repositories, Hacker News trail, open-source contributions, or competitive-programming record in the dossier. The unstated pattern therefore cuts both ways: Amulet’s architecture raises the score, while the absence of a pre-Amulet trail keeps it far from the Reddit rung. Hackers usually leave files behind.

Archil’s $18 million financing makes my 36-month forecast fairly sharp: by July 2029, Amulet will have pivoted from a standalone filesystem into a persistent-workspace API distributed through agent-sandbox platforms and will reach roughly $1 million to $3 million in ARR, rather than becoming the default independent filesystem for agents. Amulet’s strong two-founder configuration should keep the product alive and technically credible. Amulet’s weak evidence of animal resourcefulness makes grassroots developer adoption and direct distribution less likely, pushing Bala and Puru toward an embedded infrastructure layer where a partner supplies the users.

Amulet’s 1.9-ms p99 reads, 3-ms forks, private-beta partners, and fast product pivot form the strongest bull case: Amulet may already be the Dropbox demo, merely hidden by a thin public record. The bull mechanism is hidden animality—Bala and Puru may be shipping so quickly that GitHub activity, revenue, and publicity lag the artifact. **Loopt** (2005) is the known exception showing that weak timing can be forgiven when founder force is extraordinary. But Amulet’s dossier supplies no equivalent evidence of force: no named customer, independently verified benchmark, repeat usage, revenue, or founder stunt. The credential-first biographies remain a red flag. I would flip to Invest if Amulet reached $500,000 ARR from at least three named AI labs showing repeat weekly fork-and-publish usage; until then, Amulet is technically interesting but not yet a personal check.
