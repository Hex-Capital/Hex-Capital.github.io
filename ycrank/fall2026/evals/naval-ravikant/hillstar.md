# HillStar -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Behroyan built Paxon in utility services and reports a 2025 exit. Stone co-founded PlanGrid and subsequently led software at Bedrock Energy. These founders have acquired knowledge through operating inside difficult industries. HillStar deserves substantial founder credit because procurement sits near problems they have lived. The remaining gap is narrower: the dossier does not establish which utility purchasing processes they personally owned. Prior exits demonstrate execution; they do not automatically transfer customers or proprietary assets into this company.

The product has encountered real procurement complexity. HillStar reports evaluating 22 submissions spanning approximately 19,800 pages in nine minutes, and another case identified two bidders missing mandatory certification. Those are useful demonstrations, although company-published, unnamed references leave accuracy and repeatability unresolved. My decisive question is what remains after a competitor reproduces the document analysis. Evaluator judgments, exceptions, and supplier outcomes could become institutional memory that customers depend on. HillStar advertises that mechanism, but the dossier establishes neither accumulated usage nor measured switching costs. I give the mechanism limited credit without treating its eventual moat as an existing asset.

There is room beyond a single document workflow. Engineering-services evaluations and equipment sourcing across roughly 250 SKUs per vendor involve different purchases supported by the same requirements-and-evidence machinery. Fairmarkit and Keelvar independently demonstrate broader enterprise sourcing demand. That supports an expansion path across procurement categories, although its economic scale remains unquantified. The competitive implication cuts both ways: established sourcing vendors already occupy adjacent workflows and could add utility-specific evaluation.

HillStar only partially fits my pattern of turning fragmented offline supply into a network. It helps a buyer compare suppliers; the dossier does not show suppliers joining a shared system that improves another buyer’s experience. Confidential customer histories may create retention without creating network effects. Likewise, the platform-plus-professionals offering could encode expert knowledge into software, or preserve a dependence on expert labor. The reported 11-person evaluation team does not establish HillStar’s staffing requirements or delivery margins. Public pricing, realized revenue, and contribution economics are absent, so direct value capture receives the missing-evidence default.

The strongest argument for investing now is that these founders may know exactly which procurement judgments deserve to become permanent software. Waiting for mature metrics could miss that learning advantage. My exception for an unusually capable team with a working early product therefore deserves consideration. But the dossier supplies neither exceptional capital efficiency nor a demonstrated user loop to override the unresolved durability question. There is no reported integrity violation, artificial financing deadline, or reassurance-seeking behavior; the reservation is structural. HillStar has demonstrated useful work more clearly than compounding ownership of that work.

I would keep the relationship and defer a check. One verifiable flip condition would be a named utility renewing a paid contract after repeated sourcing cycles because HillStar’s accumulated decision history measurably improves evaluation accuracy over a fresh deployment, without increasing HillStar’s expert hours per cycle. That would connect specific knowledge to a durable asset and scalable value capture. Today, the founders are the strongest evidence; the compounding system remains unproven.
