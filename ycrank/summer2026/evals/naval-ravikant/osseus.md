# Osseus -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have assembled the right disciplines, but they have not yet demonstrated specific knowledge of the exact problem. Anish Mariathasan has built embedded medical-device projects, conducted cancer-computer-vision research, and previously co-founded a compliance-AI company. Ayush Patel built a self-driving robot and an agentic QA system during two Apple internships. These are credible acts across hardware, software, and regulated workflows. They do not yet prove that either founder has shipped a cross-domain engineering system into a robotics organization or personally endured the change-control failures Osseus proposes to solve.

The product is more ambitious than an AI wrapper. A permissioned graph joining CAD, ECAD, GitHub, Jira, requirements, tests, supplier constraints, and responsible owners could become an operational memory that improves with every approved change. But the dossier establishes the schema, not the accumulated asset. Outputs remain reviewable drafts, no proprietary engineering corpus is independently demonstrated, and Altium already combined Valispace systems engineering with Altium 365. If a competitor copied the interface and dependency logic today, the evidence does not identify what Osseus alone would retain.

The wedge has room to expand from robotics into broader engineering coordination, and the underlying engineering-software market is projected to grow from $58.36 billion in 2026 to $120.74 billion by 2034. But this is still a parent-market statistic rather than a causal expansion plan. Osseus has not shown which adjacent R&D buyer adopts the same graph after robotics, which shared capability unlocks that buyer, or why the earlier positioning as a general “research brain” became narrower. A market is not large merely because the category above it is large.

The strongest bull case is that the graph and marketplace reinforce one another: engineering histories teach the system how physical products change, while component listings expose supplier availability and transaction data. That would match the permissionless pattern of fragmented outsiders contributing useful supply. Today it remains a diagram rather than a loop. The marketplace reports no listings, buyers, rentals, or completed transactions, and the platform reports no cohort, referral, or contribution evidence showing that one customer improves the product for another. Each R&D team may instead require sensitive integrations, VPC deployment, permission mapping, and ongoing service—labor scaling with customers rather than leverage compounding across them.

The commercial evidence is similarly preparatory. Builder, Team, and Enterprise plans identify plausible packaging, and marketplace commissions identify direct value capture, but no price, payer, revenue, or completed transaction is disclosed. “Onboarding R&D teams now” and 418 LinkedIn followers show attention, not payment or repeated use. This is acceptable at pre-seed, but it cannot substitute for proof that teams will place their confidential CAD, source code, supplier feedback, and test histories inside a new system.

I see no disclosed integrity, artificial-deadline, founder-fragility, or adverse-dealing red flag. The problem is evidentiary: Osseus currently asks me to underwrite several future conversions at once—integrations into trusted history, trusted history into proprietary models, and a marketplace into a network. The known exception for an unusually capital-efficient product does not apply without demonstrated prototype usage or product language that users are already adopting. I pass. The verifiable flip condition is three named robotics teams using Osseus weekly across mechanical, electrical, and software changes, with at least one showing that approved dependency history measurably improves automated impact analysis over a copied baseline.
