# Veltha -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important fact is the one Veltha does not supply. I cannot identify a named obstacle the founders encountered while building or selling this product, what they did when it appeared, or how quickly the workaround changed the outcome. Encoding 500-plus insurance checks is substantial work, but work completed is not automatically evidence of resourcefulness under resistance. Their Palantir backgrounds suggest they have encountered ugly implementations before. They do not prove how they behave when this company gets stuck.

The technical evidence is much stronger. Veltha is not merely proposing an insurance agent. Its system reads scanned and handwritten records, applies FCIC, LAM, and PPSH rules, and returns paragraph- and file-level citations for human approval. More importantly, a $2 billion-plus specialty carrier is reportedly running live claims, with review time falling from one hour to five minutes. That is a working artifact crossing a useful threshold before Veltha has accumulated institutional weight. I discount the magnitude because the carrier is unnamed and Veltha publishes no accuracy, override, or error rate, but I do not discount the fact that software is operating on real claims.

The user evidence is promising but incomplete. A specialty carrier processing live claims is much better than a waitlist or conference applause. Yet “live” could mean five carefully supervised files or a recurring production workflow. There is no claim volume, payment, retention, expansion, or evidence that adjusters pull the product back into their work without founder supervision. The conference booth and requests for introductions are not distribution. The danger is that Veltha becomes a series of demonstrations requiring a new enterprise integration and rule-encoding project each time.

The founders did encounter this class of problem organically. Roland converted mortgage guidebooks into executable rules at a $642 billion servicer, while Edin deployed AI inside Germany’s federal criminal police. That is unusually direct preparation for turning regulated text into reviewable decisions. The schlep is real: inconsistent documents, jurisdiction-specific rules, legacy searches, audit trails, and human accountability. It also explains why the initial wedge may survive generic model improvements. But it does not yet establish a structural moat. CLARA, Five Sigma, and Sprout.ai already occupy adjacent claims workflows, and provision-level citations are an implementation burden they could choose to absorb.

Veltha matches my less obvious pattern of technical teams assembling a concrete substrate before claiming to be a platform: 500-plus encoded checks and a live carrier deployment are real objects, not diagrams. The strongest exception case is that a sufficiently impressive working product can sometimes compensate for missing conventional founder evidence. It does not quite do so here. The product’s decisive measurement—whether its recommendations are correct enough to change carrier behavior—remains unpublished. Meanwhile, the red flag is enterprise dependence: one unnamed carrier, negotiated deployments, conference selling, and no evidence yet that the founders have found a repeatable route through procurement.

My 36-month base case is that Veltha reaches $2 million to $6 million in ARR from a narrow set of crop and workers’ compensation workflows, but has not yet become a general claims-adjuster platform. The bull case stings: two founders with unusually exact domain preparation have already reduced a live carrier’s hour-long task to five minutes, and regulation may make traceability more valuable precisely as generic AI becomes cheaper. Still, I would not write the check today because the people-first gate remains unproven. I would flip to Invest if Veltha documented one specific carrier implementation blocker, the workaround the founders shipped within two weeks, and a paid expansion following at least 1,000 live claims with a disclosed material-override rate.
