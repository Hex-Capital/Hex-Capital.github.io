# Dock -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Mike Molinet built Branch past $100 million ARR, and that makes Dock a repeat-operator company with real B2B scar tissue whose weakest dimension is the bet itself: a software workspace in the most crowded consensus category of Summer 2026. Dock’s analytical signature is high founder adjacency and nearly absent frontier risk. Molinet and Govind Kavaturi spent six years together at Branch and later co-founded Thena, so Dock did not emerge from a TAM memo—but the dossier never gives me the physical moment when either founder felt agent fragmentation in his bones. I see Biz and Rizz; Dock has not shown me enough Tiz that only these two people can possess.

Niantic is the high rung because John Hanke spent a decade living location technology before Ingress became a company-shaped object. Dock shares that prior-product compounding: Molinet and Kavaturi scaled Branch across functions and continents, then built Thena together. Dock diverges because operating B2B software is broader than personally wrestling a fleet of agents into one persistent workspace; no cited history shows either founder shipping agent infrastructure, authorization systems, or collaborative editors. Dock sits above the merely observed-founder pattern of Uber, but below Niantic’s biography-to-product inevitability.

HandUp exposed the low-rung mechanism on frontier ambition: personal mission could justify the check, but a low-moat software product imposed a modest commercial ceiling. Dock shows the same structural weakness without HandUp’s autobiographical override. Dock’s tables, documents, permissions, REST endpoints, and signed webhooks are useful, but they are neither hardware nor regulated infrastructure, and Dust, Relevance AI, CrewAI, or a model vendor can reproduce them. There is nothing ugly enough here yet for other investors to refuse to touch.

Dust has raised more than $60 million, Relevance AI $34 million, and CrewAI $18 million; those numbers make Dock less magically weird every month. Over the next 18 months, Dock’s identity layer could compound through workspace history, permission graphs, and audit records, but it only compounds after adoption. Dock currently has six public repositories, one star on the leading repository, and no documented retention or proprietary-data loop. MCP provides distribution, but MCP also makes Dock’s surface easier for competitors to connect to and imitate. This looks like a rebuttal, not a hard no—but consensus is doing the rebutting.

Mike Molinet hired Govind Kavaturi as Branch’s first international employee, and their six-year working history is stronger than a pair of strangers meeting during YC. Dock nevertheless names both men only as co-founders; the dossier supplies no CEO, equity split, voting authority, or conflict protocol. Dock flips from governance risk to investable structure if one founder is publicly designated CEO with explicit tiebreaker authority and the other describes the boundary without hesitation. Until then, familiarity is not governance—HQ Trivia taught me that chemistry can conceal the fracture line.

Flock Safety could be encountered as an artifact in the world before its market was obvious. Dock offers a Mac application, web tier, npm CLI, ChatGPT integration, and public templates, so Dock is more observable than an invisible B2B API: I can install it and watch attributed agent activity appear. The analogy stops at the glass. Flock’s cameras occupied streets; Dock’s underlying value—shared state, role checks, and audit history—remains a developer-tools abstraction whose usefulness must largely be reported by operators rather than noticed in ordinary life.

Dock’s $19 Pro and $49 Scale plans point to a specific 36-month outcome: Dock pivots from a general multiplayer workspace into an agent-identity, permissions, and audit control plane and reaches $1 million–$3 million ARR, but does not become the primary workspace. Dock’s repeat B2B operators can sell the security layer they already built; Dock’s crowded collaboration surface cannot outrun Dust, Relevance AI, CrewAI, and the model platforms simultaneously. I’m never playing the game I seem to be playing—the plausible company is hidden inside the authorization machinery, not the shared table.

Branch’s $100 million ARR history is the bull case that stings. Dock has two founders who have already scaled internationally, a long working relationship, 26,000-plus combined LinkedIn followers, six distribution surfaces, and a commercial beta; DeepMind is the known exception reminding me that cross-domain polymaths can outrun a missing autobiographical origin. Dock could use superior Biz, Tiz, and Rizz to make agent permissions the trust fabric beneath every client. The evidence that wins today is one GitHub star, no documented adoption moat, commercial-beta security, and no named CEO. Dock earns my check if it reports $1 million ARR with net revenue retention above 120%; that single result would prove the identity layer is compounding rather than merely shipping.
