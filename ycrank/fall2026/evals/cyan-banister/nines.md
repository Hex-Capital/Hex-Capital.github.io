# Nines -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine a data center scheduling its own physical repairs: compute notices a fault, a robot changes the component, and capacity returns without waiting for a technician. Nines has chosen a sensible first step toward that world—commissioning fiber connections before attempting maintenance inside operating racks. Why begin there? Because connecting an unfinished facility could offer a more forgiving learning environment than touching cables beside running workloads. That is my hypothesis, however; the dossier does not establish that Nines has crossed even this first step. Microsoft’s 2024 manipulation prototypes make the future more credible, but they do not establish this team’s ability to deliver it.

The little thing that catches my attention is expensive compute waiting for someone’s hands. Nines identifies an actual dependency beneath the AI excitement: software cannot reseat an optic. Removing technician dispatch delays could give operators more control over when their equipment becomes productive. But the dossier neither measures that delay nor identifies an artificial restriction or intermediary collecting a rent. This is a concrete operating problem, with a weaker connection to my preference for returning economic agency to individuals. I would want to understand who gains control and how much idle capacity actually disappears.

Tiantian Li’s Cornell autonomous-underwater-vehicle experience is more interesting to me than the founders’ employer logos. It supplies some evidence of robotics work before Nines; the launch also reports responsibility for large-scale inference at Meta. Taerim Eom brings software delivery experience from Patreon. Those are useful ingredients, but the record does not yet show sustained manipulation experiments, independently developed hardware, or a history of recruiting others into this particular obsession. CEO and CTO responsibilities are identified. There is no documented leadership shell game, privacy violation, or dismissive claim that the operating complexity is trivial; I would not invent those negatives to explain a pass.

The strongest argument against passing is that Nines fits my pattern of funding concealed infrastructure beneath a visible boom. Its commissioning wedge might generate the manipulation and recovery experience needed for later maintenance. **CosmicBrain AI** illustrates why missing revenue need not stop that bet: industrial robotics can deserve a check before sales when a working prototype, signed proofs of concept, and longstanding founder obsession establish the bridge. The material difference is that Nines’ website sequence is explicitly simulated inspection, and comparable physical proof is absent here. The approximately 252,000 views on its November 13 launch show attention, without establishing that an operator has entrusted equipment to the system. That exception does not carry this investment.

I pass with my own money today. Nines occupies a useful layer, but the dossier establishes neither a controlled technical asset nor costly customer behavior around a working artifact. Exclaim’s overlapping maintenance offering and Microsoft’s internal research also mean the bottleneck could be real while the value accrues elsewhere. My one verifiable flip condition is an operator-verified pilot showing repeated autonomous fiber commissioning on real racks, with completion times, human interventions, and adjacent-connection disturbances reported across the full trial. That would let me examine whether Nines has found a repeatable path into this future.
