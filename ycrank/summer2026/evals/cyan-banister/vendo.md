# Vendo -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. In Vendo’s most interesting future, software vendors no longer predict every workflow: users compose permission-bound micro-apps inside the products they already inhabit. Getting there requires interoperable agent interfaces, reliable API discovery, safe execution, and enough user behavior to teach the system which generated features deserve permanence. Vendo has two leaks from that future—AG-UI adoption by major platforms and a package tested nightly against 16 applications—but the dossier does not yet show the full causal chain or prove that user-built software becomes normal rather than a clever demo.

The strongest bull case is the artifact. An Apache-2.0 repository with 3,539 commits, 377 stars, 51 forks, and 1,220 weekly npm downloads is unusually tangible for pre-seed. People are spending attention and some engineering labor around the product. The sting is that these figures describe developer interest, not the costly behavior Vendo promises from signed-in end users: no cohort repeatedly creates persistent views, schedules automations, or pays to keep them. My 36-month base case is therefore that Vendo becomes a useful open-source integration toolkit but remains below $2 million ARR because product teams—not their users—turn out to be the durable builders.

Vendo does sit beneath the visible chatbot wave. Scanning APIs, themes, components, and permissions could become an enabling layer for many B2B applications, and the sandbox, approvals, permission proxies, and audit records address the right operational ugliness. But Vendo does not yet control a scarce constraint. CopilotKit already offers authenticated tools, approvals, shared state, generative UI, and self-hosting after raising $27 million; Thesys covers generated dashboards and forms. The inferred data advantage from de-identified execution patterns is not a moat until Vendo demonstrates that those patterns make installations or guardrails measurably better.

The broken system is legible but shallowly evidenced. Customers request divergent features, product teams accumulate backlogs, and users wait for vendors to decide what deserves engineering time. Vendo returns some agency by letting users construct their own workflows. What is missing is the rent: no quantified backlog delay, engineering cost, lost contract, or gatekeeper economics explains who pays for this rigidity. The $49 and $499 plans establish a pricing hypothesis, not proof that the recovered agency is valuable enough to purchase.

Nour Zahzah and Yousef Helal have built together before, have known each other since kindergarten, and bring prior AI-product work plus credible systems experience from Apple and AMD. That supports execution, but it does not yet demonstrate a long pre-company obsession with user-programmable software or enterprise commercial command. The recent transition from open-source deployment tooling to embedded agents can be read as accumulated technical tuition; it can also be read as following a fashionable interface wave. The named CEO avoids a leadership shell game, while the unfinished governing-law clause and continued Aisle Technologies footer suggest operating details are lagging the product. The known exception for pre-revenue technical bets does not carry this over the line: Vendo has a functioning artifact, but not signed deployment proof, an independently documented lifelong obsession, or a controlled bottleneck.

I would not write the check today, but I would keep Vendo close. The one condition that flips me is three named production customers showing that at least 25% of eligible users create a persistent Vendo workflow and that at least half of those creators are still using it after 90 days. That would convert developer curiosity into the strange behavioral leak the thesis currently assumes.
