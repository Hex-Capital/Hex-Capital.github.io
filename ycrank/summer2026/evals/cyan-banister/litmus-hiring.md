# Litmus -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years out, an engineering interview may look less like an exam and more like a sampled workday: candidates use their own tools, collaborate with AI, and leave an observable trail of decisions. Litmus has one credible leak from that future—3,000-plus assessments in a functioning product that evaluates repository-specific work rather than toy problems. What is missing is the backward chain. The dossier does not show that recorded workflow predicts performance after hiring, that employers will standardize around this evidence, or that Litmus owns the mechanism required for either transition.

The working artifact is the strongest evidence here. Autumn AI, Sleuth, and Neo Scholars are not waitlist names; customers have helped produce $60,000 in ARR, and candidates complete assessments involving code, commits, AI prompts, and recorded explanations. That is costly behavior through a real product. The sting in the bull case is obvious: at pre-seed, 3,000 evaluations give these founders more behavioral material than many recruiting startups ever collect, and employer-specific calibration could compound quickly if hiring outcomes are fed back into the system.

But why is technical hiring shaped this way? Abstract screens are cheap to administer, while realistic work trials consume scarce engineering time. Litmus automates that tradeoff and returns some control to candidates by letting them work asynchronously with familiar tools. It has not yet quantified the rent—interviewer hours, false-negative rates, bad-hire costs—or shown that its scores improve the hiring decision. Worse, the agency transfer reverses at the data boundary: the product records prompts, commits, audio, video, and transcripts, takes ownership of the submission’s specific expression, and may transmit employer context to Anthropic and audio to OpenAI. Disclosure and deletion rights help, but this is still unusually invasive infrastructure for people who cannot bargain freely if they want the job.

Shaivi Rau and Elena Zhao encountered technical recruiting together, and their backgrounds cover useful sides of the problem: Rau has startup, VC, and hiring exposure; Zhao has worked as an engineer at Meta and Two Sigma. They shipped from a Columbia dorm and found paying demand, which is real execution proof. The dossier does not show a longer pre-company obsession, earlier assessment experiments, public technical artifacts, or recruiting evidence beyond the three-person team. Shaivi is clearly named CEO, so I do not see a leadership shell game, but the ownership and tiebreaker structure remain unknown.

The enabling-layer story is presently weak. Litmus may accumulate a proprietary assessment corpus, but no public validity study demonstrates that it grades better, and HackerRank, CodeSignal, and Karat already possess larger datasets, customer channels, assessment products, and ATS integrations. Repository-derived tasks and workflow recording look like features they can add. Litmus also depends on closed model providers while asking employers and candidates to entrust it with sensitive material. The unglamorous workflow-repair pattern fits me; the concealed-infrastructure pattern does not yet. My known privacy exception does not rescue this because the dossier supplies no technical artifact proving that the surveillance is uniquely necessary or disproportionately useful.

I expect Litmus to reach roughly $1–3 million ARR within 36 months but remain a recruiting feature company—likely pivoting toward enterprise interview orchestration or being acquired by an ATS vendor—unless it turns assessment traces into validated hiring infrastructure. I pass with my own money today. One condition flips me: an independently audited prospective study showing that Litmus scores materially predict twelve-month engineering performance while candidates can decline audio/video collection without losing assessment access or accuracy.
