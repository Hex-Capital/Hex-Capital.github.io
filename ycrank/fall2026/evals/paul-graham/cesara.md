# Cesara -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The useful question about Cesara is what happened when a hospital’s contract could not be translated cleanly into a payment calculation. What did Rahman and Lee do next? The dossier describes the intended system, but gives no named obstacle followed by a dated workaround. Rahman’s previous company, Auctor, and Lee’s systems experience are reasons to investigate. They cannot answer the question that matters most when I invest my own money. Resourcefulness gets the missing-evidence default here, without any inference that these founders are passive.

There is more substance in the product evidence. In his October 8 interview, Rahman reports identifying over **90% of valid underpayments**. That suggests Cesara has progressed beyond describing an agent that reads contracts. But without the cohort, denominator, or validation method, I give the measurement partial credit. Contract amendments and exceptions are precisely where a convincing demonstration can become an unreliable production system. Cesara’s advertised approval thresholds and clause-linked audit trails address the right failure modes; their existence does not establish their effectiveness.

The reported recovery of **over 70% of identified underpayments** is the strongest argument against passing. Recovering money requires the workflow to survive contact with a payer. Hospital revenue-cycle teams are a specific user niche with an immediate reason to care, and outcome-based pilots make trying Cesara easier. Yet payer recovery does not establish that hospitals repeatedly use the software or pay Cesara. The endorsements from Ed Marx and JD Whitlock are encouraging introductions, with no disclosed customer contracts behind them. My exceptions to the normal founder gate require a working product with unmistakable pull or sufficiently validated technical proof. These pilot claims could become that evidence. They have not reached that standard.

Rahman’s reported healthcare implementation exposure through Auctor gives the idea a credible origin. Contracts, claims integration, sensitive data, and appeals contain plenty of work that founders prefer to avoid. That is genuine thesis fit. It also creates the central uncertainty: can Cesara turn that work into a repeatable product? MD Clarity already offers overlapping contract intelligence and recovery capabilities, including its September 2026 agentic expansion. Competition makes fast learning important. The dossier contains no customer observation followed by a changed product route, so adaptability also receives its default.

Cesara partially matches my pattern of technical founders producing concrete evidence before asking investors to believe a platform story. The numerical claims need verification before that pattern carries much weight. Conversely, the dossier does not establish the red flags of dependence on a large partner, premature delegation, or treating launch publicity as distribution. I would pass today because the required founder evidence is missing and the product evidence is insufficient to override it. **One verifiable flip condition:** a referenceable hospital deployment documenting a named integration blocker, a founder-built fix shipped within weeks, and repeated paid recoveries reconciled to remittances. That would connect founder behavior, functioning software, and customer demand in one inspectable case.
