# Elithia -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine a physician opening a claim and spending her attention on the disputed medicine, with every relevant event already assembled and traceable. Elithia’s source-linked chronologies point toward that world. But getting there requires reliable reconstruction across conflicting records, then enough professional trust that experts change how they work. The dossier establishes neither transition yet. DigitalOwl already offers evidence-linked chronologies, so the feature’s existence does not establish that Elithia has found a distinctive path into that future.

Why is an expert spending days arranging paper? That is the question here that interests me. Elithia removes duplicates, orders documents, and exports a chronology into Word or PDF—the formats professionals already use. Giving physicians their attention back fits my preference for unglamorous workflow repair. But the identified burden is clerical labor; the dossier does not establish an artificial restriction, a gatekeeper collecting rent, or how much of the saved time becomes greater professional independence. I see useful relief, with the deeper system economics still unresolved.

The strongest argument against passing is that Elithia has already sold to physician expert witnesses and medical-legal firms. Someone apparently found this narrow workflow valuable enough to buy. The first 1,000 pages free also lets a prospective customer test an actual case rather than admire a presentation. That earns credit. What I cannot yet see is the behavior I would build a conviction around: experts returning with another case, checking the output, and entrusting the product with more consequential work. Reported sales without dates or repeat-use detail support an early product signal, not that stronger conclusion.

Sarthak Jain has relevant unstructured-data engineering experience from Winton, and leaving MIT Sloan to build is a concrete commitment. His family medical background offers a plausible route into the problem. Those facts matter more to me than the Oxford credential. They still do not show a medical-review obsession expressed through earlier experiments or sustained practitioner collaboration. My exception for a company without revenue requires other unusually strong proof, such as a working technical breakthrough and a long founder history with the problem. Elithia’s reported sales help, but the dossier supplies no comparable substitute for the missing depth.

The concealed-infrastructure possibility is an expert-corrected medical evidence layer serving several kinds of claims. That could become interesting. Today, correction rights, ownership, and performance improvements are hypotheses, while Datavant already combines retrieval with analysis. Handling medical records also makes consent and custody a real investment gate; the dossier leaves those arrangements unknown, rather than documenting a violation. Likewise, the Minerva Labs name and conflicting headcounts warrant reconciliation, but they do not establish a leadership shell game: Jain is identified as founder and CEO.

I would pass with my own money today. Elithia has a useful professional wedge, but I cannot yet connect it to a controlled asset or a sufficiently evidenced future. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independently audited pilot showing three unrelated expert-witness practices each paying for a third consecutive case, with material chronology errors measured against source records and documented permission to process the patient data. That would show professionals changing their habits around something they trust.
