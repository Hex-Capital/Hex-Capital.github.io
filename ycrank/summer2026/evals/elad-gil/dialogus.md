# Dialogus -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Realtime voice models made policy-bounded call automation feasible; enterprises now buy completed service outcomes, and the controlling variable is whether Dialogus can lower cost per correctly resolved call enough to overcome incumbent distribution. The dossier does not establish that variable. It reports no autonomous-resolution rate, error rate, contract value, retention, or cost comparison, so thousands of calls cannot yet tell me whether customers are validating superior economics or merely testing a newly available capability.

The strongest bull case is unusually substantive for pre-seed. Dialogus is handling thousands of production calls for Fortune 500 enterprises and displays Papa Johns, KFC, and Totalplay. These are operational calls, not waitlist registrations. A three-person team has also reached SOC 2 compliance while supporting bilingual interactions, policy-limited actions, human escalation, and audit trails. That suggests real customer pull despite the absence of disclosed revenue, although it remains unclear whether deployments are paid, expanding, or surviving renewal.

The product is deeper than a speech-model wrapper. It recognizes callers, reads enterprise systems, updates CRMs and orders, sends follow-ups, and turns production failures into replayable regression scenarios. Better models should improve task completion while Dialogus retains permissions, workflow state, integrations, and responsibility for actions. This is the company’s best evidence. The weakness is defensibility: the dossier calls failure traces potentially proprietary without estimating how many calls, dollars, or months Bland, Sierra, Salesforce, or an integrator using OpenAI’s SIP-enabled Realtime API would need to reproduce them.

Market timing is real but double-edged. SIP-connected realtime models became generally available in August 2025, lowering the cost of deploying voice automation. The same change lowered entry barriers for everyone. By 2026, Sierra reportedly exceeded $150 million ARR, PolyAI had more than 2,000 deployments, and Salesforce bundled telephony, CRM data, agents, digital channels, and human handoffs. This market is already crystallizing. My pattern of buying coverage across a discontinuity does not justify another entrant once scaled leaders exist unless Dialogus can identify a sharper wedge than “enterprise voice infrastructure.” Mexican Spanish collections or restaurant ordering could be that wedge, but the dossier does not yet show segment-specific economics or dominance.

The founders have credible technical exposure. Hans Ibarra left Georgia Tech and Google and Amazon internships after working around Gemini, Gmail, and YouTube; Rodrigo Terán Hernández completed engineering internships at Meta, Microsoft, and Google. Leaving those paths is meaningful commitment, but it is not evidence of prior startup learning, contact-center expertise, or a personally experienced customer problem. Juberth Rodriguez’s background and the founders’ working history remain unavailable. I give the team credit for sacrifice and execution, not an inferred second-time-founder advantage.

My 36-month base case is $2 million to $6 million ARR concentrated in Mexican Spanish restaurant ordering or collections rather than a broad enterprise voice-infrastructure position. The bull case stings: named enterprises, production usage, and an owned operational loop could let Dialogus turn one deployment into reusable distribution across adjacent call types. But none of the known exceptions applies—the team is not building a scarce frontier model, reconstructing physical-market margins, or supplying uniquely scarce technical talent—and incumbent bundling is a direct threat. I would pass today. One condition flips the decision: a named enterprise renews a paid deployment after at least 100,000 calls with independently verifiable autonomous resolution above 80% and cost per resolved call at least 25% below its prior human-assisted baseline.
