# Mirrors -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Mirrors describes what breaks: engineers inspect failed agent traces and keep adding exceptions to a global prompt. But the dossier doesn’t show what happened when these founders themselves were blocked. There is no specific failure, dated workaround, or elapsed time. Aryan Singhal’s ML research at UT Austin and UCSB makes technical competence plausible. It cannot establish resourcefulness. That distinction determines my decision here: I would pass on the present evidence.

The best evidence is in the product’s details. Engineers can scope guidance, preview matches, and activate it at the user-turn boundary through an SDK. Shadow mode, local disabling, and explicit delivery semantics suggest the team has thought about the unpleasant parts of inserting another service into production. The documentation even distinguishes an accepted server decision from context actually reaching the agent. These are useful engineering choices. They earn partial technical credit, but documentation does not establish a measured result. With a documented context-wait budget of 1,000 milliseconds, Mirrors needs to demonstrate that correcting behavior outweighs the delay and additional failure surface ([SDK reference](https://mirrors.dev/docs/sdk/reference)).

The strongest argument against passing is that Mirrors may already have found its narrow well: engineers whose customer-facing agents repeatedly make the same mistakes. Its website displays **711,933 agent sessions**. If those represent customers repeatedly depending on the current product, that would materially change the case. But the counter has no period, customer breakdown, or allocation between product versions. It cannot establish that anyone comes back because Mirrors works. Custom pricing and demo conversations could support close founder-led learning; neither establishes payment or dependence. I give no demand premium for an aggregate whose meaning is unresolved.

Mirrors fits my recurring preference for programmable infrastructure that technical users can incorporate into existing systems. The opposing possibility is substantial: reviewed conditional guidance could become a useful primitive before anyone agrees what to call it. But my other unstated pattern is concrete proof before the financing story, and that evidence is weaker here. Papaya’s optimization pull requests and Mem0’s persistent memory offer adjacent ways to address overlapping needs. Mirrors must discover the situations where runtime intervention is indispensable. Singhal’s evaluation research supplies relevant background, but it does not document a personal collision with this problem. Integration and rollout are real schleps; their existence alone does not prove these founders discovered a neglected wedge.

The change from session replay to runtime context delivery could reflect good learning. Without the observation that caused it or a dated decision sequence, I cannot distinguish learning from a change in positioning. Nor do I see affirmative evidence of partner dependence, premature delegation, or refusal to change course. Unknown cofounder history remains unknown. I checked the exceptions: an impressive working prototype, unmistakable user pull, or independently validated technical proof could override incomplete founder evidence, but the dossier does not establish those strongly enough. My verifiable flip condition is one named customer case documenting a recurring production failure, a founder-built correction shipped within two weeks, and four subsequent weeks of repeated use with a measured reduction in that failure.
