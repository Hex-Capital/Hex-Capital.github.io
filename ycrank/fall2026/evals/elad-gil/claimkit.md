# ClaimKit -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI evidence processing and expanded US reporting requirements create an opening to sell R&D claim preparation to technology-company finance teams; the economic variable is specialist labor cost per completed, defensible claim. ClaimKit becomes a large company if that cost falls substantially as volume grows. Its upfront software fee plus minimum-or-percentage service fee provides a mechanism to capture the savings. The dossier establishes neither the current labor requirement nor a measured reduction. That leaves my controlling market belief unproven.

The strongest evidence is demand: ClaimKit reports **80+ customers within six months**, and Edexia’s COO describes switching from accountants mid-engagement for lower cost and faster completion. That is meaningful pre-seed pull. Four people serving that customer base could indicate substantial automation, but customer count does not establish completed claim volume or delivery capacity. I discount the company-reported total without dismissing it. The advertised 100% success rate adds little without a denominator, measurement period, or distinction between initial processing and surviving subsequent review. The team’s historical $100M+ in credits is experience, not current-product traction.

The product has more substance than AI drafting. Engineering and accounting connections, retained claim records, and specialist review through lodgement give ClaimKit ownership of a consequential workflow. Better models should improve evidence classification while those integrations and records retain value. This fits my pattern of backing software that performs institutional work. The sharper objection is that **Boast, Neo.Tax, and Onshore already combine automation with tax expertise**. Neo.Tax also has ONESOURCE distribution. ClaimKit could deliver a useful integrated service while competitors capture the same productivity gains. No exclusive dataset or demonstrated reproduction barrier changes that assessment.

The December 2025 IRS instructions provide a specific institutional catalyst: Section G becomes mandatory for tax years beginning after 2025, subject to exemptions. More detailed reporting plausibly increases the value of evidence automation. It does not yet quantify an economic step change. My red flag here is the possibility of software pricing expectations attached to specialist-service margins; the dossier does not establish that adverse outcome, but it also supplies no margin evidence to overcome it. The strongest bull case is that early customer acquisition and Edexia’s switching experience are the first visible results of a fundamentally cheaper delivery model. I would take that seriously enough to investigate.

Huang’s KPMG R&D experience makes the workflow credible, and Mahon brings relevant engineering capability. Their prior ventures establish entrepreneurial exposure, but the dossier documents neither specific startup lessons nor costly personal commitment. None of my known exceptions supplies an override: there is no demonstrated frontier-research advantage, scarce model-building asset, or measured reconstruction of incumbent economics. **I would pass on a personal check today**, despite genuine product and demand signals. My single flip condition is a verified cohort of at least 30 paying customers completing a second annual claim cycle at **70% or greater gross margin after specialist delivery costs**. That would connect the observed pull to the economic mechanism this investment requires.
