# Osseus -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

$47.90 billion of PLM spending gives Osseus room to maneuver, but each invited workspace remains an isolated enterprise deployment. Osseus is a broad-market software product with no network: highest on pivot-room, lowest on compounding user-added value. The upside geometry is therefore bounded less by market size than by architecture. Osseus can sell to thousands of robotics and R&D organizations, yet the thousandth customer does not automatically improve the product for the first.

**Uber** is the high rung on market optionality because black cars opened into ride-sharing, delivery, logistics, and an operating system for physical movement. Osseus shares the underlying breadth: its graph can expand from robotics into CAD review, electronics, testing, supplier constraints, and eventually the wider $47.90 billion PLM market. Osseus diverges where it matters—the initial product requires custom connectors across CAD, code, simulation, supplier, and issue-management systems. Uber could pivot on a shared network; Osseus must finance each adjacency through another integration surface.

**Perplexity** is the low network rung because one user’s search does not directly make the next user’s search more valuable; I waived that weakness because permissionless distribution and Aravind’s rare retrieval knowledge were overwhelming. Osseus has the same single-sided topology without the same override. Customer-specific design graphs may create switching costs, but Osseus mirrors permissions into separate VPC or customer-cloud deployments, so valuable knowledge is structurally trapped inside each account. CoLab’s $72 million Series C also shows that category adoption can grow without Osseus acquiring a network advantage.

Invited-team access, scoped demos, forward-deployed pilots, and custom rollout pricing make Osseus code delivered through labor. Over 18 months, Osseus’s permission-aware graph could become reusable software if every connector and review pattern lowers the cost of the next deployment. The opposite loop is equally plausible: every proprietary CAD stack, supplier workflow, and security policy creates another services burden. Osseus has permissionless production leverage—the software can replicate—but permissioned distribution leverage. Code is not automatically leverage when humans must carry it through every gate.

Anish Mariathasan brings biomedical engineering, computer-vision research, a prior compliance-AI startup, and physical-system exposure; Ayush Patel brings Apple agentic-QA work and a self-driving robot project. Osseus therefore sits above the generic “ex-FAANG PM discovered a TAM” founder pattern, but the dossier does not show years spent owning robotics design releases, maintaining PLM systems, or shipping comparable engineering infrastructure. The score flips into the top tier if Osseus can show that Anish or Ayush personally lived the cross-domain failure mode through multiple production hardware revisions. Specific knowledge is not intelligence. It is knowledge that cannot be downloaded after choosing the market.

**Stack Overflow** worked because Atwood and Spolsky were builders serving builders, with public artifacts and a long record of domain writing before the equity story. Osseus has a credible four-year Imperial relationship between Anish and Ayush, and Anish has already attempted a company, but Osseus shows no public repository, technical community, prior exit, or evidence about how the founders behaved through adversity. Intelligence and energy read above neutral. Integrity remains unreadable, so the ten-year-partner test stays near its absent-evidence default rather than inheriting a halo from Imperial or Apple.

July 2029 finds Osseus pivoted from “robotics design intelligence” into broader R&D traceability, producing $2 million to $5 million in ARR from enterprise subscriptions while still carrying a meaningful forward-deployment function. Osseus reaches that range because the $93.12 billion projected PLM market supplies adjacent demand, but Osseus does not become a breakout platform because private customer graphs and connector maintenance prevent network compounding. A large market can support a good software business. It cannot manufacture permissionless leverage.

CoLab’s $72 million financing and Duro’s AI-native relaunch form the strongest bull case: the category is arriving, Osseus spans more engineering domains than either competitor, and mirrored permissions plus default-deny memory could turn “permission required” into the kind of moat that made **Anchorage Digital** an exception. Osseus could become the trusted intelligence layer above fragmented systems of record. The dossier still gives the winning evidence to the bear case: no public deployment scale, no retention, no structural barrier, and explicit custom rollout requirements across proprietary tools. I would flip to **Invest** if Osseus documents five production customers deployed in under 30 days through reusable connectors, with at least three expanding usage after six months. Until then, permission-as-moat looks more like integration-as-labor.
