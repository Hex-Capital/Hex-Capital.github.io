# Conifer -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Conifer’s contested truth is precise: most AI requests are simple enough to execute locally, so difficulty-aware escalation can eliminate more than 80% of token spending without materially degrading results. What would prove it wrong? A representative workload in which the router either saves far less than 80% or sends enough difficult requests to weak local models that users abandon it. The company has shipped an implementation, supports 18 locally runnable models, and reports a Rust engine decoding up to 60% faster than llama.cpp on Apple Silicon. This is a falsifiable technical thesis, not merely “AI costs will fall.” But Conifer has published neither routing accuracy nor independently measured savings. The secret is plausible; it is not yet proved.

The more difficult question is whether the secret creates a monopoly or merely a feature. Conifer has a coherent initial wedge—Apple Silicon users running high-volume coding-agent and support workloads—and local execution distinguishes it from hosted-first routing. Yet it occupies the seam between two well-financed layers: Ollama can add automatic escalation to its local-and-cloud runtime, while OpenRouter, Not Diamond, and Portkey already control routing or gateway relationships. A company-reported 60% inference-speed advantage is meaningful proprietary-technology evidence, but not yet the order-of-magnitude result that prevents imitation. The inferred routing dataset cannot count as a moat until Conifer demonstrates routing volume, outcome labels, and improving accuracy.

The founders improve the technical case but leave the foundational case incomplete. Charles Muehlberger researched edge-device multimodal inference and took a Princeton gap year to build the company; Michael Jeffords previously built an applied computer-vision pipeline and helped expand HeartCheck screenings to more than 10,000 people. Core work therefore appears internal and adjacent to their experience. But the dossier does not establish how long they have worked together, whether both are unequivocally full-time, or whether their ownership and economic exposure remain aligned. Missing evidence is not refutation, particularly at pre-seed, but founding structure cannot be inferred from technical credentials.

The bull case is that the 248-star repository and prior launch to more than 100 developers provide an inexpensive developer-distribution seed, while the local-first architecture could compound into proprietary routing data. If Conifer becomes the default control plane spanning local hardware and cloud models, one monthly bill could support a large usage-based revenue engine. The objection stings because this is exactly the sort of infrastructure wedge that can look small before becoming central. But today the stars span three product identities—Sage, Juniper, and Conifer—and there is no current-product usage, revenue, retention, cloud markup, or customer acquisition evidence. Distribution exists as software availability and community channels, not yet as repeatable economics.

My base case is that within 36 months Conifer either pivots toward an Apple-device inference runtime or is absorbed by Ollama for under $100 million, rather than becoming an independent routing monopoly. Local inference can create substantial customer value while the cloud router or model runtime captures most of it. The single condition that would flip me to invest is a verified cohort of at least 20 paying teams showing more than 70% sustained token-cost reduction, no material task-success degradation, and six-month retention—evidence that the router, rather than merely the fast runtime, owns the customer relationship.

This is secret versus feature, and Conifer remains on the boundary. I would investigate rather than invest today. The technical claim is unusually concrete for a pre-seed company, but the lowest-evidence pieces—revenue arithmetic and distribution economics—are also the mechanisms required to turn that claim into a last-mover position.
