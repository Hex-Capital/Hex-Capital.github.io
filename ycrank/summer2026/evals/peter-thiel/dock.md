# Dock -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Dock’s implied secret is that autonomous agents will need a neutral shared workspace, rather than merely operating inside the systems where humans already work. What would prove this wrong? Teams continuing to embed agents directly into Linear, Cursor, Replit, and other domain-specific systems instead of moving their documents, permissions, and history into Dock. The dossier provides no experiment resolving that question. Gartner’s prediction that agents will enter 40% of enterprise applications describes a consensus trend, not a contested truth.

The product is coherent but the market definition is not narrow. “General-purpose shared documents and tables for multiple humans and agents” places Dock against every system of record capable of adding agent identities. Separate credentials, workspace-scoped permissions, attributed edits, and human approvals are useful features, but the dossier identifies no structural barrier and explicitly notes that six adjacent platforms could extend comparable functionality. With one GitHub follower, one star on the leading visible repository, and no retention evidence, the proposed switching-cost moat remains an inference. This is currently feature differentiation inside a crowded category, not ownership of a beachhead.

The founders are the exception to the otherwise weak case. Govind Kavaturi and Mike Molinet have worked together across Branch, Thena, and now Dock; Kavaturi participated in Branch’s growth from zero to $100 million in revenue, while both accumulated substantial B2B product and go-to-market experience. This is durable alignment, not two strangers assembled for YC. But the dossier does not establish current ownership, salaries, full-time status, or whether essential engineering remains in-house, so the founding structure is promising rather than fully proved.

The economics expose a deeper contradiction. A $19 Pro plan and $49 Scale plan require self-serve distribution, and Dock has sensibly chosen MCP/CLI onboarding, referrals, and agency partners. Yet there is no named buyer, conversion rate, repeat usage, or channel performance. At $49 per organization per month, even $100 million of ARR requires roughly 170,000 paying organizations. Moving upmarket would demand precisely the security posture Dock lacks: no SOC 2 Type II, no third-party penetration test, and recoverably stored webhook secrets and browser tokens. The product is priced like a lightweight utility while promising to become infrastructure that runs a company.

The strongest bull case is that two long-aligned B2B operators recognize shared agent state as the next system of record before incumbents do. Workspace histories and permission schemas could then compound into genuine switching costs. But this is only a resemblance to a monopoly mechanism; no usage data shows it operating. The relevant exception would require a verified zero-to-one technical or behavioral event strong enough to outweigh uncertain value capture. Dock has shown neither.

I pass. This is not a penalty for pre-seed silence: the dossier contains affirmative evidence of commodity competition, weak present distribution, and revenue arithmetic inconsistent with the ambition. The decision would flip if one defined operational segment produced at least ten paying customers at $10,000-plus annual contracts with six-month retention, demonstrating that Dock—not the incumbent application—had become their persistent human-agent system of record. Until then: aligned founders, fashionable product, unproved company.
