# Olam Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Om Buddhdev and Shreshth Sharma have designed games together since middle school, which makes Olam Labs a strong-pair company whose weakest dimension is not founder chemistry but schlep: Olam Labs has built an impressive machine for measuring behavior without yet proving it will do the ugly customer work that makes the machine indispensable. The pair ran a charity together, Om built Roblox games with roughly 6 million visits, and Shreshth did reinforcement-learning and data-pipeline work at RBC. Those are unusually coherent tracks. But the animal test is only partly passed. Olam Labs has shipped 69,140 poker hands and 86,729 graded turns; Olam Labs has not yet shown the equivalent of selling cereal boxes when the obvious route failed.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for co-founder configuration because the Collison brothers had years of shared history, complementary abilities, and a prior commercial scar in Auctomatic before Stripe existed. Olam Labs shares the load-bearing part of that mechanism: Om and Shreshth were designing games together in middle school, long before reinforcement learning became a fashionable phrase. That history is better than meeting at an AI hackathon and manufacturing founder compatibility afterward. Olam Labs diverges from Stripe because the Collisons had already sold Auctomatic for about $5 million; Olam Labs has demonstrated durable chemistry, but not yet how that chemistry behaves when customers reject the product or money gets tight.

**Kiko** (2005) failed through a mechanism I worry about here: clever software without an ugly, accumulating operational advantage can remain merely clever software. Olam Labs records messages, actions, reasoning traces, and ground-truth states, which creates some real data-collection grind. But Olam Labs still runs its public models through Pi, relies on LLM graders for deception labels, has unreleased environments marked “soon,” and names no paying customer. The unpleasant work that mattered at Coinbase was compliance nobody wanted to touch. Olam Labs has found technical difficulty; Olam Labs has not yet proved it has found a schlep competitors with $70 million or $320 million cannot simply fund.

Om’s 6-million-visit Roblox history and Olam Labs’s 69,140 completed hands are evidence of resourcefulness because shipped systems compound differently from credentials. Over the next 18 months, Olam Labs can turn that habit into an advantage by releasing games quickly, recruiting humans, supporting native model harnesses, and hand-building evaluations for researchers before those workflows are productized. Each ugly integration would teach Olam Labs what customers actually distrust about existing benchmarks. The missing evidence is refusal-driven improvisation: there is no named customer won manually, no revenue stunt, and no account of Om or Shreshth forcing a blocked launch through an unexpected side door. Animals leave tracks; these founders have left code tracks, but not yet sales tracks.

Shreshth’s reinforcement-learning work at RBC and Om’s staff-engineering work on AI agents at Latitude put Olam Labs unusually close to its problem, but proximity is not quite the same as an organic idea. Olam Labs would move into the top tier on this dimension if Om had built the first arena because Latitude could not answer whether its own agents deceived or collaborated, or if Shreshth had personally encountered the same evaluation failure in an RBC pipeline. The dossier instead establishes relevant experience and a childhood history of game design, not a specific moment of founder pain. Olam Labs looks grown from the founders’ lives rather than selected from a market report, but one causal sentence is still missing.

**Reddit** (2005) is the useful hacker parallel because its founders arrived with evidence that they naturally built things people could inhabit, not merely the credential that they knew how to program. Olam Labs has the same underlying shape in miniature: Om shipped multiplayer games used roughly 6 million times, then worked on AI-agent infrastructure, while Shreshth combined mathematics, computer science, reinforcement learning, and data engineering. Olam Labs is weaker than the strongest hacker-genealogy cases because Om’s leading cited repository has 28 stars and Shreshth’s has 11; Olam Labs has credible artifacts, but no Lisp prize, major open-source contribution, or hacker-community reputation that makes the signal nearly deterministic.

Olam Labs’s 86,729 graded table-talk turns point to a specific 36-month outcome: by July 2029, Olam Labs will have shifted its center of gravity from a public Social Poker leaderboard to private agent red-teaming and behavioral-evaluation contracts, reaching between $2 million and $5 million in ARR. Olam Labs will discover that the leaderboard attracts attention while the real product is the custom environment, native harness, and evaluation protocol a lab trusts before deployment. The strong founder pairing makes that pivot plausible; the weak existing schlep means Olam Labs must acquire its schlep through customer work rather than merely letting a public dataset accumulate.

Patronus AI’s $70 million and General Intuition’s $320 million make the strongest bear case a benchmark-commoditization case: Olam Labs could spend two years producing scientifically interesting poker rankings while better-funded platforms copy the environments, question the LLM graders, and bundle evaluation into broader infrastructure. Harness sensitivity makes the objection sting, because Olam Labs currently routes every public model through Pi, and a benchmark whose rankings change with the harness is not a trusted benchmark. Olam Labs survives that objection only if its childhood game-design intuition lets it create adversarial environments faster than observability vendors can and its replayable human-agent traces become useful proprietary evidence rather than website activity. If Olam Labs has no repeat paid evaluation customer and still has only one publicly working environment by July 2027, I would flip from Invest to Pass.
