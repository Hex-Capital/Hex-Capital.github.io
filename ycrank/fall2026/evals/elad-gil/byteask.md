# ByteAsk -- Elad Gil Evaluation

ByteAsk’s market turns on the cost of trusting a generated patch. C/C++ teams in trading, automotive, and embedded systems already have coding agents; they still need changes that survive their actual build configurations and runtime conditions. The controlling variable is ByteAsk’s advantage in correctly verified task completion over general agents on customer codebases. Its reported 89% firmware-ticket resolution versus 61% for a frontier-model baseline bears directly on that question. But this is an internal benchmark, without evidence establishing how much advantage customers require to switch or pay.

The September 27 changelog exposes the most consequential issue: verification tools previously reported clean results when execution was absent, interrupted, or partially instrumented. The company describes fixes, so I would not treat those failures as proof the current product is broken. They nevertheless strike at the proposition ByteAsk sells. A verification agent that occasionally mistakes missing evidence for success can increase the engineer’s checking burden. The benchmark needs to measure trustworthy completion, including correctly reporting what was never tested.

There is a substantive product underneath that concern. Build-aware retrieval, compiler execution, sanitizers, and a bridge into an existing debugger session give ByteAsk actions and context beyond generated text. Stronger models could improve task completion while those integrations remain useful. The difficult counterfactual is that stronger models could also let general agents operate the same tools. ByteAsk’s MIT-licensed reference-retrieval server already supports competing agents. That is a distribution opportunity, but the dossier establishes neither exclusive workflow state nor a costly-to-reproduce evaluation asset.

The strongest case for investing is the combination of this implementation and the founders’ prior experience. Kulkarni worked on low-latency C++ at Optiver and Quantbox; Saini worked on AI infrastructure at ThirdAI and was a founding engineer at Simbian. They also built and sold LawSutra together. That is documented second-time learning, although the sale alone establishes neither desperation nor the quality of their lessons. Company-reported weekly active users doubling and sixfold daily usage intensity suggest the product may already be finding its audience. Undisclosed denominators and an unnamed comparison agent keep that evidence preliminary; contributors using ByteAsk are also distinct from their projects buying it.

The economic step change remains the weakest part of the case. Published $20, $100, and $200 plans establish asking prices, while the dossier supplies no measured engineering hours saved, deployment-cost reduction, or delivery-margin improvement. The inferred April 2025 benchmark catalyst makes specialized agents easier to evaluate; it does not establish a new economic threshold. My pattern of backing multiple layers of a technology shift makes a specialized verification company plausible. But coding-agent competition is already crystallizing around well-funded platforms, including Cognition’s automotive and chip-design customers. ByteAsk needs evidence that its specialization creates enduring customer value.

I would hold off on a personal check. The research-stage exception does not apply: this dossier shows capable application builders, without documented frontier research that would justify overriding weak market evidence. The product also has enough real integration work that dismissing it as a formatter would miss the bull case. My single flip condition is an independently auditable, paid customer deployment showing ByteAsk reduces engineer verification hours per accepted patch by at least 30% against the customer’s best general agent over eight consecutive weeks, with every claimed verification backed by execution records. That would connect the technical advantage to the economic variable this investment currently lacks.
