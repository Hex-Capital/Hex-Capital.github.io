# Mireye -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: software agents are choosing industrial parcels, pricing insurance risk, planning transmission routes, and negotiating around physical constraints before a person opens a county website. Mireye has one credible leak from that future—customers reportedly source off-market data-center land 100 times faster through a catalog of more than 300 fields. But the dossier does not map the intervening transitions from that narrow workflow to autonomous physical-world decision-making. It names the destination and shows a useful artifact; it does not yet show the road.

The most compelling part sits underneath the fashionable agent layer. Mireye has normalized 85 sources and returns field-level provenance, fetch timestamps, and confidence values. Accepted requests become reusable fields, so customer demand could gradually turn fragmented public records into shared infrastructure across real estate, energy, insurance, and industrial development. That is the right shape. The material difference between a catalog and a moat, however, is accumulated proprietary advantage. These sources are largely public, while Mapbox, CARTO, and Fused already occupy adjacent geospatial layers. Without request volume, retention, or declining fulfillment costs, the flywheel remains a hypothesis.

The broken system is visible but only partly decoded. Government documents, utility portals, census tables, and GIS maps were built for human navigation; replacing that scavenger hunt with one API plainly returns time to developers. The 100-times-faster land-sourcing claim suggests a substantial operational rent. Mireye has not identified who captures that rent today, quantified the underlying delay or expense, or shown that its product transfers durable control to a previously constrained operator. So far this is excellent workflow compression, not yet institutional unbundling.

Ansh Chokshi has real commercial evidence: Poker Pit reached $250,000 ARR in four months before being acquired, and his first 50 Mireye prospects produced a 10% outbound response rate after message testing. That demonstrates more than technical competence. What I cannot find is the pre-company GIS obsession—the dated maps, scraped county records, or physical-world project that made him the first believer before agent infrastructure became crowded. The team record also needs cleaning up: Mireye reports two people, but YC lists only Ansh as an active founder. There is a clearly identified CEO, so the governance red flag is not triggered, but ownership and decision authority require verification.

The product is functioning, priced from $19 to $99 per month, and has enabled at least one customer workflow. Still, the evidence describes an outcome rather than costly behavior: no named customer, paid usage, repeat-query cohort, signed commitment, or operator reorganizing work around Mireye. The catalog growing 20% week over week measures supply construction, not user pull. I can forgive missing revenue at pre-seed when a prototype is paired with unusually strong founder obsession or committed industrial users; neither is documented here. On the positive side, cited sources and timestamps show more respect for data custody than the average agent wrapper, and there is no dependence on a single closed model provider.

My base case is that by August 2029 Mireye becomes a $1–3 million ARR data-center site-selection API rather than the horizontal substrate for physical-world agents. The bull case stings: customer-requested fields could compound into the provenance layer that every agent needs, and Ansh has already shown he can turn outbound learning into revenue quickly. I am not ready to underwrite that compounding without evidence that demand, rather than the founders, is expanding the catalog. I am Neutral. The verifiable flip condition is three named data-center developers each paying at least $1,000 per month and using Mireye weekly for 90 consecutive days.
