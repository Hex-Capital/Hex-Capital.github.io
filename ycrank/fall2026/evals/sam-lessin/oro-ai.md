# ORO AI -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

ORO AI has demonstrated that it can pay developers to produce agent trajectories. Who pays ORO? That is where my underwriting starts. The reported $500,000 YC investment establishes funding, but gives me neither the proposed post-money valuation nor competing-term-sheet count. I cannot call this cheap or auctioned. And the dossier names no experiment tied to round proceeds, with a deadline and a result that changes business value. Those two gates remain undocumented.

The strongest argument for writing a check is technical substance. ORO’s June 2026 research reports Qwen3-4B improving from an 18.0% published baseline to 42.7% on a guarded held-out partition. Its competition has attracted over 345 agents and accumulated 4,891,831 scored trajectories. Bansal’s evaluation research and Bittensor engineering, combined with Schilbe’s AWS ECS and Cloud Map work, have become functioning infrastructure. This fits my pattern of backing people who translate between systems: crypto incentives recruit builders, and evaluation infrastructure turns their work into potentially useful training material. I give the founders credit for shipping that bridge.

But the bridge currently carries rewards toward builders; the commercial return journey is unproven. ORO reported distributing $85,000 across four competitions. That establishes an incentive mechanism, not customer demand for its output. The trajectory count measures distinct agent-version/task pairs, not purchases or proprietary access that caused purchases. The website invites research inquiries, while pricing, paid customers, and data exclusivity remain undocumented. My dark-pool test therefore stays unresolved: what does ORO control that a lab must keep buying?

The bull case could hurt my pass. Continually changing environments and hidden tasks might produce useful supervision that static datasets cannot, and the measured training improvement gives that proposition substance. Yet ORO also reports agents scoring 0.71 on practice tasks and 0.18 on hidden tasks. Its response to gaming is credible engineering, but sustaining that response creates ongoing work. With MIT-licensed code and Prime Intellect already supplying environments and RL infrastructure, ORO must establish why customers buy its continuing output. The red flag is treating participation and accumulated records as economic progress; the dossier does not establish that ORO itself makes that mistake, but I will not underwrite those counts as revenue.

I also checked whether the evidence warrants an exception to my financing gates. There is no documented paid conversion and retention to support the AI exception, no external acquisition offer or enterprise demand to support a price override, and no prior-exit-backed financing structure that removes repeated fundraising. These are absent overrides, not failures I attribute to a pre-seed team. Likewise, missing margin, burn, and runway data earn my stated economics default; reward spending alone does not establish negative unit economics.

I pass with my own money today. ORO has earned technical credibility, but its investable asset and cash engine remain unidentified. My one verifiable flip condition is a renewed, arm’s-length lab contract whose receipts exceed the fully loaded recurring cost of producing and validating its purchased trajectories. That would demonstrate that the competition creates an asset a buyer repeatedly values, and provide concrete grounds to reconsider the missing financing gates.
