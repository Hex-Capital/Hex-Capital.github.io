# Runtime.si -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The question I want to ask Runtime.si’s founders is: what broke when someone tried to use this, and what did you do next? The dossier contains a technical artifact, but no documented episode connecting a named obstacle to a founder-built workaround. Peng and Zhong’s GRASP backgrounds and Ying’s manipulation experience make them plausible builders. They do not establish resourcefulness under resistance. That distinction matters when I am investing in people whose product may change several times.

Runtime’s strongest evidence is its October 15 research. Nomad connects observations and robot state to models, validates actions, executes them in simulation, and records task outcomes. This is enough machinery to take seriously. The reported improvement from 152/525 successes to 283/525 is less conclusive: the cohorts were selected retrospectively, the comparison is in-sample, and artifact verification remains pending. I give credit for the implemented harness and provisional experimental evidence. The experiment does not yet show that Runtime can find failures an unfamiliar robotics team would otherwise miss.

The strongest case for investing is that these founders have built the beginnings of a useful instrument inside a field they understand. Runtime fits my pattern of technical teams making infrastructure for other technical teams. Evaluation also contains plenty of tedious integration work that could conceal an opportunity. But the important next step in that pattern is developers adopting the instrument for their own purposes. Runtime has a waitlist and a forthcoming 10,000-simulation collection; neither establishes repeated use. SignalFlag already spans hardware, replay, and simulation, while NVIDIA supplies open evaluation tooling. A small team could still win by solving one robotics developer’s problem much better. The dossier does not identify that developer or the urgent job they return to perform.

The Lambda Robotics job descriptions might conceal an encouraging story: founders tried building data-center robots, encountered an evaluation problem, and changed course. But that story is presently an inference. There is no dated observation explaining the change, and the earlier pilots cannot establish demand for Runtime’s current product. Likewise, robotics experience establishes proximity to the problem without documenting a founder’s personal collision with it. I would keep both dimensions at their missing-evidence defaults. The openings also do not establish premature delegation, and the waitlist does not prove dependence on a ceremonial launch. Those red flags remain unproven.

I would pass on the present evidence. The missing revenue and pricing are ordinary at this stage; the decisive omission is an observable learning loop between founders and users. I checked the grounds for an exception: a working prototype can outweigh some founder uncertainty, but Runtime’s provisional research does not yet supply unmistakable pull or independently validated proof strong enough to replace the resourcefulness test. One verifiable result would change my decision: a documented deployment where a named external robotics team repeatedly uses Nomad, encounters a specific blocker, and gets a founder-built fix within weeks that detects a subsequently confirmed physical failure. That would connect the promising machinery to the people and need behind a company.
