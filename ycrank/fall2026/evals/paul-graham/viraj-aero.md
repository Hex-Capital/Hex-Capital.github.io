# Viraj Aero -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Viraj Aero has built far enough to report full-scale aircraft-prototype integration. What I cannot find is the episode that would tell me most about the founders: what failed, what they changed, and how quickly they made it work. Joseph Risson’s propulsion experience at Ascendance and Beyond Aero makes him credible around turbines. It does not establish how he behaves when a turbine refuses to cooperate. I leave resourcefulness at its missing-evidence default. That distinction controls my decision.

The technical proposition deserves attention. Recover combustion water, use exhaust heat to make steam, and inject it to increase mass flow. The difficulty is keeping that cycle working under the conditions customers actually face. Reported prototype integration earns partial technical credit; the advertised 45% electrical efficiency and 2 ppm NOx remain development specifications. The unexplained difference between 30% and 40% additional power makes a measured baseline particularly valuable. My pattern of backing difficult engineering applies when the team has crossed an observable threshold. Viraj has disclosed an integration milestone, but not the operating results needed to establish that threshold.

The strongest argument against passing is that these founders have unusually relevant experience on both sides of the machine. Risson understands propulsion. Mathilde Bouilloux previously developed six power projects totaling more than 120 MW. That combination could help Viraj discover which engineering improvements customers can actually install and buy. Certification, cooling, and turbine integration also contain precisely the ugly work that can conceal an opportunity. I give the team partial credit for that grounded starting point, while reserving judgment about the idea’s origin: the dossier does not show either founder encountering this particular problem and deciding to solve it.

The €13 million of aviation letters of intent strengthens that argument. Light-aircraft buyers are a specific niche, and purchase intent for expensive hardware is meaningful before revenue. But the counterparties, operating experience, and repeat commitments are undisclosed. Those letters cannot establish demand for GENA among data-center operators. Grid constraints potentially delaying 20% of planned capacity explain urgency; they do not establish that Viraj’s machine is the preferred answer. The retrofit offering could provide a narrower entry point than selling complete generators, but there is no documented customer observation behind that route. I therefore leave evidence-driven adaptation at its default.

I would pass today. The €4.5 million financing and reported Safran work could give the team room to produce decisive evidence, but neither replaces it. The partnership red flag is unresolved rather than established: Safran’s role is unspecified, and the dossier does not show distribution depending on it. Likewise, an experienced COO and 15 collaborators do not establish premature founder delegation. I checked the exceptions for an impressive working prototype or independently validated technical proof; the disclosed integration lacks sufficient results to invoke either. One verifiable event would reopen the decision: a customer-witnessed continuous-duty test meeting a stated power and water-recovery baseline, following a documented technical failure that the founders themselves corrected within weeks. That would reveal both the machine and the people building it.
