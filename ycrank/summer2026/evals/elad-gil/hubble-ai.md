# Hubble -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: CMS’s April 2026 interoperability push makes patient-authorized data newly actionable for digital-health, legal, and life-sciences buyers, while Hubble becomes large only if it can retrieve complete records across fragmented systems within a predictable SLA and at software-like gross margins. Hubble has identified the causal mechanism—one identity verification followed by API, browser, or voice retrieval—but not the required completion rate, latency, or margin threshold. The published legal workflow still takes one week. This is a credible controlling belief, not yet a validated one.

The technical architecture is the strongest evidence. Forty-plus production tools cover Epic, Cerner, athenahealth, UnitedHealthcare, and Aetna; outputs include deterministic identifiers, source traceability, and per-call audit logs. Better models should improve the browser and voice agents rather than erase the integration layer. Hubble therefore looks more durable than an output-formatting wrapper, although it owns record retrieval and normalization—not yet the downstream legal, clinical, or claims workflow where the largest value may accrue.

The economic signals are promising but incompletely attributed. Hubble reports reducing paralegal work from hours to minutes, recovering $340,000 in denied claims over 90 days, and improving cost-estimate accuracy from 50% to 94%. Those outcomes show that better data access can change labor cost and recovery economics. They do not establish Hubble’s pricing, gross margin, or share of the value created, and the testimonials are anonymous. CMS’s launch may accelerate adoption, but the dossier labels that connection as inferred rather than showing buyers changing budgets.

There is more product pull than the absence of published revenue suggests: three customer use cases produced measurable operational outcomes, and the company has built tools against real production systems. Still, there are no named customers, retrieval volumes, renewals, repeat-usage cohorts, or paid expansion data. Individual Access Services are free, while B2B pricing remains undisclosed. At pre-seed this is enough to establish meaningful use, but not enough to distinguish repeatable demand from a few integration-intensive deployments.

The bear case is that the 40-tool footprint is a maintenance burden masquerading as a moat. Hubble depends on portals, phone systems, external release queues, and consent regimes it does not control; protected-record carve-outs further limit completeness. Particle Health, 1upHealth, Health Gorilla, and PicnicHealth have each raised roughly $49 million to more than $100 million, and no structural barrier or quantified reproduction cost has been identified. There is no frontier-research, rare-talent, or market-reconstruction exception that overrides these concerns. Conversely, I do not see evidence of fundraising-as-status, premature hiring, or dependence on one frozen model.

Dublish’s work at Grow Therapy and Leon’s experience building Amazon Care and One Medical infrastructure give Hubble unusually direct operating context, and their decade-long relationship lowers team risk. The dossier does not document a prior startup lesson, costly sacrifice, or the kind of personal compulsion required for additional founder credit. My 36-month base case is therefore a technically credible company reaching $3–8 million ARR by concentrating on legal-record retrieval or revenue-cycle recovery, rather than becoming a broad patient-information intelligence layer. I would monitor rather than invest today. The single flip condition is a six-month cohort showing at least five named B2B customers repeatedly achieving 80% complete-record delivery within 72 hours at 70% or better gross margin.
