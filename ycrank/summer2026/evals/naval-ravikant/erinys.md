# Erinys -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have shipped near the problem, but not yet into the exact problem. Zyuzin and Guo built an OSINT investigation prototype querying 22 government databases in April 2026. That is costly action and technical evidence. It is not evidence that the team knows how to acquire clients, supervise matters, collect fees, or operate a plaintiff firm. No founder is publicly shown to have practiced law. Erinys has crossed the prototype boundary, not the domain-knowledge boundary.

The interesting product is not another legal-AI interface. Erinys proposes to supply staff, intake, billing, compliance, capital, and matter flow—the operating system around an entire firm. If deployments generate proprietary matter data and reusable operating processes, copying the code would not copy the institution. But this loop is currently asserted, not observed: no member firm, case volume, exclusive dataset, or workflow retention is public. Eve already serves more than 1,000 plaintiff firms and 200,000 cases, while Supio offers an easier path for firms that want AI without surrendering operational independence.

The market is broad enough; the path through it is not. U.S. legal services generated $396.8 billion in 2024, but a parent market is not a wedge. Erinys must show how launching plaintiff firms expands into additional case types, jurisdictions, or shared transactions using the same operating capability. State fee-sharing restrictions make that expansion discontinuous: every jurisdiction can require a new legal and ownership structure. Regulation may become accumulated know-how, but only after the company has successfully navigated it.

The strongest bull case is that Erinys is building the network before the network is visible. Its three-person team owns technology, recruiting, operations, and working capital; each affiliated firm could expose bottlenecks that improve every later firm. Competitors validate demand for AI inside live litigation, and the “Start a Firm” funnel could aggregate fragmented lawyers into productive supply. If that mechanism works, Erinys captures more value than a software vendor and develops operating data competitors cannot buy.

But today no user is proved to create utility or distribution for another user. Referrals are solicited, not measured; cross-firm learning is inferred, not demonstrated. There is also no disclosed payer, price, take rate, completed matter, or revenue. The architecture combines regulatory approval, capital allocation, professional services, and software before proving any one of them. Complexity is not defensibility. Sometimes it is merely several businesses sharing a website.

My 36-month base case is that Erinys pivots toward legal-operations software and managed services for existing plaintiff firms, reaching $1–3 million in ARR rather than operating a meaningful firm network. I pass now. I would reopen on one verifiable condition: five licensed affiliated plaintiff firms operating for six consecutive months under disclosed compliant ownership structures, with completed paid matters and evidence that shared workflows improve acquisition or case economics across firms.
