# Open Actuation -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Open Actuation’s most interesting possible secret is that custom motors can be manufactured with the turnaround and economics of catalog components. That proposition is testable: customization either preserves delivery speed and production margins across different designs, or engineering labor consumes the advantage. But this is my reconstruction. The dossier supplies a four-week delivery offer and proprietary tooling claims; it does not identify a rejected industry belief or report a comparative experiment establishing that the founders are right. Domestic sourcing demand establishes an opportunity. It does not yet establish a secret.

The company has progressed beyond a manufacturing presentation. Its LA facility reportedly supports machining, winding, magnet insertion, balancing, and testing; July 2026 reporting describes samples undergoing customer feedback. Jon Facer’s experience at Gecko Robotics and five years at manufacturing-software company 1Factory give the undertaking practical credibility. Those credentials do not answer the separate founding questions: who performs the essential engineering, whether every core founder is committed full-time, and how ownership is distributed. I assign the founding structure its missing-evidence default. There is no basis for inventing either alignment or a founding defect.

Who owns this opportunity if domestic drone manufacturing grows? Atlas Motion already advertises application-specific motors prototyped within weeks. Westmag discloses a flexible manufacturing platform and targets volume customers. These are competitors addressing the very production problem Open Actuation proposes to solve. Proprietary tooling could eventually distinguish the company, but no measured advantage currently separates its output from those alternatives. The narrow drone-OEM customer segment is real; the monopoly mechanism remains unproved against affirmative competitive overlap. The imported CubeMars storefront offers still less exclusivity, although its commodity economics should not be imputed automatically to the custom-motor business.

The strongest argument against passing is that manufacturing knowledge compounds through production. Actual samples, installed equipment, and founder experience could let Open Actuation discover a process advantage before it can describe that advantage publicly. Motors also fit the less obvious side of the AI production stack: physical machines require physical components. That matches my preference for neglected industrial constraints. But the relevant exceptions to my usual caution require demonstrated technical discontinuity, persistent customer transactions, or a concrete deployment path that resolves the coordination risk. Customer-feedback samples establish progress, not those overrides. Conversely, imported magnets and silicon steel create execution exposure without proving an impossible supply chain. The conflicting thousands-per-month and thousands-per-week capacity statements need reconciliation; they are not evidence of a disproved technical claim.

Distribution is the most developed commercial element. Open Actuation names an identifiable buyer, accepts direct engineering inquiries, provides downloadable design files, and reports builds for drone OEMs. Those are concrete routes into a purchasing workflow, deserving modest credit beyond an untested channel label. They do not establish repeatable conversion or qualification costs. The economic engine is less legible: the disclosed $187 price belongs to a CubeMars resale product, not a proprietary custom motor. Without custom pricing or production margins, neither stated capacity nor the broader $18.62 billion actuator market supports arithmetic for an exceptional equity outcome.

I pass on Open Actuation today. The decisive problem is that the disclosed competitive offers already resemble its central promise, while the evidence needed to distinguish manufacturing capability from manufacturing advantage has not arrived. My single flip condition is an independently documented repeat-production program in which a drone OEM buys successive custom-motor lots because Open Actuation’s process achieves a material, profitable cost or delivery advantage over qualified competing bids. That would turn the tooling claim into evidence of value capture. Until then, I see a credible prospective supplier whose claim to a durable monopoly is substantially weaker than its claim to useful work.
