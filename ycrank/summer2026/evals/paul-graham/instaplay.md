# Instaplay -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Gary Wu built reinforcement-learning environments at Salesforce, Frank Liu was an Instagram engineer, and Instaplay already generates browser games in about two minutes. Instaplay is a technically capable hacker trio searching for an organic problem, rather than founders visibly solving a pain from their own lives. That is Instaplay’s signature: strong builders, weak inevitability. The founders could make this, but the dossier never explains why they had to.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for hacker genealogy: the Collisons had Lisp, Auctomatic, and years of commerce hacking before Stripe existed. Instaplay shares the important part—three technical founders shipped a nontrivial artifact combining code, art, sound, multiplayer logic, and publishing—but not the genealogy. Gary Wu’s and Frank Liu’s top owned GitHub repositories have one star each, while Akhil Aggarwal has no public repositories. Instaplay has evidence of current engineering ability, not yet the pre-company trail that made Stripe’s founders unmistakable hackers.

The polished AI-wrapper company in my pass example fails because its founders found a fashionable market before they found a problem they personally suffered. Instaplay’s dossier contains the milder version of that failure: no public source says Gary Wu, Frank Liu, or Akhil Aggarwal previously made games, struggled with game engines, or built Instaplay for themselves. Instaplay may still have an organic origin hidden from public view, but there is no evidence—so I apply the absent-evidence default. The AI-gaming statistics and Astrocade comparison do not repair that. Markets cannot manufacture founder-problem fit.

Scrub Club reached 5,567 plays, while Swordling, Neon Reflex, and Crowd Gun Bridge each exceeded 4,400. Those are artifacts, not slides, and they move Instaplay above a credentials-only founding story. Over the next 18 months, resourcefulness would compound if Instaplay’s founders personally seed games, recruit creators one by one, and turn play links into a self-propelling loop. But the dossier names no cereal-box stunt, manual distribution campaign, revenue improvisation, or rejection-defying build. Instaplay has shipped; Instaplay has not yet left animal tracks.

Astrocade reports more than 20 million engaged users and hundreds of millions of monthly plays, which exposes how little schlep currently distinguishes Instaplay. Prompting a browser game into existence is pleasant software work; IP complaints, moderation, multiplayer reliability, and creator onboarding are the unpleasant parts, and Instaplay has not shown that it embraces them. The score would flip upward if Instaplay were manually clearing rights, operating creator communities, or solving multiplayer moderation competitors avoided. Without that evidence, commoditization by general coding models is more likely than a Coinbase-like compliance schlep becoming the asset.

Airbnb (2009) had three founders whose complementary abilities and prior relationships survived an idea that initially looked absurd. Instaplay also has the preferred configuration of three, with Gary Wu covering agentic products, Frank Liu engineering, and Akhil Aggarwal technical and business work. Instaplay loses points because only Frank and Akhil’s shared NYU history is documented; nothing establishes how Gary joined or whether Instaplay’s founders have built together through difficulty. Three names on a page are not yet a strong trio, but they are better than a solo founder trying to build both a game engine and a social network.

Instaplay’s public catalog and remixable browser links lead me to a specific 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Instaplay will have pivoted from a consumer social platform to B2B branded-game creation for marketers and will produce $1 million to $3 million in ARR. Instaplay’s hacker strength makes the generation engine plausible, while Instaplay’s missing organic consumer-game origin makes sustained creator-community pull less plausible. The technology will probably survive; the initial identity probably will not.

Astrocade’s 20 million users is the strongest objection to that forecast because it proves prompt-generated social entertainment can spread quickly, and Instaplay already has four public games above 4,400 plays. The bull case is that these are the equivalent of Dropbox’s early demo: small numbers concealing a product that becomes obvious once sharing compounds. Instaplay could also be a known-exception case like Airbnb, where an initially unconvincing category is rescued by founder behavior—but the dossier contains no cereal-box-grade behavior, and the founders’ thin public hacker trails contradict my unstated pattern. I would flip to Invest if Instaplay shows that unaffiliated creators—not founder-seeded accounts—produce at least 30% monthly user growth for three consecutive months with 25% four-week creator retention.
