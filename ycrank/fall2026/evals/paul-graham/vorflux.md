# Vorflux -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The question I want answered about Vorflux is what the founders did when an engineering task resisted automation. Prasanna built engineering organizations at Rippling; Anant and Haritha have worked on substantial infrastructure at Databricks and Rubrik. Those are good reasons to take the team seriously. But the dossier contains no specific obstacle, founder-built workaround, and elapsed time. I cannot turn an impressive employment history into evidence of resourcefulness. That leaves my most important test unanswered.

There is more substance in the product. Vorflux has a live cloud service, published usage billing, and mobile clients. Its workflow combines parallel implementation, cross-model review, and browser QA. This earns meaningful credit for shipping functioning software. It earns less for technical proof: the dossier gives no reproducible completion-rate benchmark or independently measured reduction in engineering effort. Configurable review is a mechanism that might improve correctness. It is not yet a measurement of correctness.

The strongest argument for investing is Supermemory. A customer let Vorflux work on memory-graph optimization for two days using $600 of compute. That is a much more interesting experiment than asking an agent to change a button. TrashLab’s contract-color and logo-placement work supplies another concrete use case. These suggest users will entrust actual tasks to the product. But the $600 is reported compute consumption, not established Vorflux revenue, and neither story establishes repeat use. The claimed trillion tokens could include valuable work, retries, or internal experiments. I want to know which customer came back without being asked.

Vorflux does fit my pattern of backing tools that let technical users do more themselves. The founders also have relevant experience with the ugly work of enterprise infrastructure. Yet the dossier does not connect the idea to a particular problem they encountered, or show users bending the product into unexpected workflows. Nor does it document a rejected hypothesis followed by a shipped revision. Adding Slack AutoBots might represent learning, but a new feature alone does not establish that. I leave origin and adaptability at their missing-evidence defaults.

The uncomfortable bull case is that this team already knows how to build enterprise software, has shipped, and has persuaded named customers to try substantial work. Prior workplace overlap also makes an application-assembled founding team less concerning, though it does not establish a history of resolving disagreements. There is no documented dependence on a ceremonial launch or one large distribution partner. Still, my exceptions require unusually strong product proof, rapid founder correction, or unmistakable user pull. Vorflux’s evidence does not yet supply that override. Its $15 million financing in July 2026 cannot substitute for it. Meanwhile, Cursor already offers cloud execution and browser verification, so shipping those features does not explain why users will stay.

I would pass on the present evidence. This is a gap in the behavioral proof I need, rather than evidence that the founders are passive. My flip condition is one documented customer sequence: an engineering team hits a named failure, the founders ship a workaround within two weeks, and that team subsequently buys and uses Vorflux for four consecutive weeks. That would connect founder action to a working change and voluntary return usage. It would tell me considerably more than another trillion tokens.
