# ByteAsk -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

ByteAsk’s founders have earned the right to attack this problem. Kulkarni worked on low-latency C++ at Optiver and Quantbox; Saini built AI infrastructure at ThirdAI and Simbian. They previously built and sold LawSutra together, then shipped ByteAsk after founding it in June 2026. The strongest evidence is the product’s attention to build configurations and live debugger state. These are details you encounter when generated code collides with a real system. I give substantial credit for specific knowledge demonstrated through work.

The investment question is what that knowledge becomes. A competitor can reproduce compiler integration, sanitizer execution, and a debugger bridge. ByteAsk’s MIT-licensed Embedded MCP already makes part of its grounding functionality available to Claude Code, Codex, and Cursor. Open distribution can attract developers, but the disclosed mechanism also helps competitors. There is no demonstrated exclusive corpus, proprietary model, or accumulated deployment asset that survives copying the implementation. The founders’ knowledge is valuable; its conversion into durable company ownership remains unproven.

The strongest argument against that skepticism is the reported 89% firmware-ticket resolution versus 61% for a frontier-model baseline. Combined with weekly active users reportedly doubling and roughly sixfold usage intensity, that could mean the team understands a neglected workflow much better than general agents do. My exceptions for exceptional builders and a working prototype matter here: demanding a mature business would miss the opportunity. But the benchmark is internal, the usage comparison has no absolute counts, and neither establishes that the advantage compounds. September 27 release notes also acknowledge previously reporting clean results when verification had not completed. For a verification product, correctly distinguishing “passed” from “not tested” is foundational.

ByteAsk also fits my interest in tools that let individuals turn expertise into greater output. Its initial users can plausibly lead into trading firms, automotive engineering, and embedded development through the shared need to validate native code. Yet the founders’ $10 billion opportunity estimate and $400 billion salary denominator do not establish adjacent software spend. Nor does adoption by contributors to LLVM, Godot, and llama.cpp establish customer-created distribution. Another engineer installing the tool has not been shown to improve it for the next engineer. Permissionless installation is useful; a self-reinforcing network requires an additional mechanism.

Published prices of $20, $100, and $200 give ByteAsk a direct route to collecting money from users. They establish an offer, with no disclosed completed purchases or revenue. The discrepancy between monthly pricing and nonrenewing 30-day passes leaves the recurring economics unclear. I see no documented integrity violation, financing pressure, or founder instability that triggers a personal veto. Publishing verification fixes is constructive disclosure, although it does not establish their effectiveness. The concern is structural defensibility rather than an adverse judgment about the founders.

At 46, this is a capable team whose demonstrated execution substantially exceeds its demonstrated compounding advantage. I would keep the relationship open and hold my check today. One result would flip my decision: a customer-audited benchmark showing that an exclusively licensed corpus accumulated through deployments produces a repeatable resolution advantage on held-out customer codebases against the same agent without that corpus. That would show ByteAsk turning each deployment into something the next competitor cannot simply download.
