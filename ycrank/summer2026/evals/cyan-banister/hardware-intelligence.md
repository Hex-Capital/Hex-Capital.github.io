# hardware intelligence -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the attractive version is obvious: chip teams describe intended behavior, agents traverse enormous traces, test a repair, and leave an auditable proof while engineers work on architecture. But hardware intelligence has documented the first tool, not the path to that world. Wave’s July 2026 launch follows releases from ChipAgents and Synopsys, so terminal-native debugging is evidence that the present wave has arrived—not yet a leak from a distinctive future the founders uniquely understand.

The team is the strongest part of this company. Rishov Sarkar developed AMD AI Engine simulators, worked on LLVM flows at Siemens EDA, earned a Georgia Tech ECE PhD, and maintains the 77-star LightningSim project. Athreya Anand led agentic-workflow work at Google and encountered chip design through AWS Inferentia and Tesla. They have also known each other for nearly a decade, with Anand clearly identified as CEO. That is unusually coherent pre-company technical preparation, although the dossier does not yet show the repeated customer contact or recruiting pull that would complete the Rizz–Tizz–Biz picture.

The broken system is partially visible. Verification engineers spend more time debugging than on any other activity, while multi-gigabyte waveforms remain trapped in GUI workflows and incumbent license-server environments. Wave returns some agency by running in the terminal, staying on-prem, using the customer’s model key, tracing signals, rerunning the simulator, and displaying an auditable diff. I like the privacy architecture. I cannot yet see the rent: hardware intelligence does not quantify license expense, debugging delay, or the value of one avoided verification cycle, so “better interface” and “economic escape hatch” remain uncomfortably close together.

There is a working artifact, but not yet weird or costly behavior. Founder demonstrations show multi-gigabyte analysis and automated fixes, while pilot onboarding opened in July 2026. No named team has repeatedly used Wave, paid for it, replaced Verdi with it, or entrusted it with a tape-out-critical failure. This is where the bull case stings: a deeply qualified pair may only need one semiconductor team to discover that terminal-native, evidence-linked debugging is dramatically faster. At pre-seed, that could change the company quickly.

The harder counterweight is structural. Synopsys already offers natural-language commands, automated tracing, failure analysis, waveform comparison, and the established FSDB ecosystem; ChipAgents claims deployment at more than 120 semiconductor companies. Wave currently supports VCD and FST, while FSDB needs customer-specific integration. Hardware intelligence sits beneath the AI application wave, which fits my concealed-infrastructure pattern, but it does not yet control the scarce layer. On-prem deployment protects customer custody while also preventing a shared proprietary dataset from accumulating. The product could therefore become an excellent feature before it becomes an independent platform.

I am neutral today. The founders have earned another meeting and a product test, but not yet my own money: the dossier supplies exceptional preparation without a six-year map, controlled bottleneck, or user behavior that incumbent overlap cannot explain away. The verifiable flip condition is one named chip team completing three real root-cause investigations with Wave and documenting at least a 50% reduction in engineer debugging time, including one FSDB-based production workflow.
