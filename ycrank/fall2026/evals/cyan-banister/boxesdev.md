# Boxes.dev -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine closing my laptop while a dozen agents keep working, then checking their tested changes from my phone over breakfast. Boxes.dev has built a recognizable piece of that world: prepared environments that can be snapshotted, copied, and kept alive independently. But getting there requires reliable delegation and safe access to increasingly sensitive systems. The dossier establishes environment isolation; it does not yet show those further transitions beginning. I see a useful present-day tool, with the larger future still a hypothesis ([product documentation](https://boxes.dev/help/how-boxes-works)).

The founders are the strongest reason to keep looking. Nick Bushak co-founded Gem and served as CTO; Drew Regitsky was its first engineer, and their relationship goes back to MIT. These people have built together through actual operating complexity. Their reported year of intensive Claude Code and Codex use also puts them inside the problem. That supports execution confidence, although it does not establish a long-running, independently developed obsession with agent infrastructure. The founder evidence is substantially better than a résumé and a fashionable idea, but it stops short of showing why this particular problem will hold them for a decade ([About](https://boxes.dev/about), [YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/boxesdev)).

Why must a developer babysit an awake laptop so software can keep working? Why should two agents fight over shared environment state? Those are concrete constraints, and removing them returns time and freedom of movement. That activates my preference for products that give individuals control. Yet Boxes.dev has identified coordination waste rather than a gatekeeper extracting a demonstrated rent. Its $19 Starter and $99 Pro plans make the convenience purchasable; advertised prices do not establish how much value customers recover or whether they keep paying. The conflicting compute allowances between the homepage and billing documentation also need correcting before customers can confidently understand the exchange ([pricing](https://boxes.dev/#pricing), [billing documentation](https://boxes.dev/help/plans-and-box-hours)).

The strongest objection to passing is that these founders may be repairing their own daily operating wound, and the apparently mundane repair could become the durable home for agent work. That fits my pattern of finding consequential infrastructure inside an ordinary workflow. Boxes.dev already offers a working product and mobile companions. But Android’s 10+ downloads and one iOS rating, observed October 27, establish distribution, not repeated dependence. I can waive revenue when a prototype and exceptional founder evidence carry the bet; I cannot use that exception to manufacture evidence of user devotion. There is no documented cohort repeatedly entrusting substantial work to these environments ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=dev.boxes), [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/boxes-dev/id6758927946)).

The infrastructure question then becomes uncomfortable: what does Boxes.dev control that the coming world must pass through? E2B supplies its sandboxes, while Conductor already offers cloud execution, native agents, and mobile access. Persistent templates could become valuable, but hypothetical switching costs earn no credit today. Supporting both Claude Code and Codex helps avoid dependence on one agent family; it does not establish control over a scarce enabling asset. Credential custody matters here, too: code inside a box can read available GitHub credentials, potentially beyond selected repositories. The published recommendation to use narrow development credentials is useful disclosure, but I would need to verify enforced boundaries before treating this as a trusted home for autonomous work ([technology listing](https://www.producthunt.com/products/boxes-dev/built-with), [Conductor](https://www.conductor.build/), [credential documentation](https://boxes.dev/help/data-and-credentials)).

I pass with my own money today. Boxes.dev has credible builders and a practical release from laptop babysitting, but the evidence does not yet distinguish a future dependency from a replaceable convenience. My flip condition is one verifiable cohort: ten unrelated paying teams still running recurring production-development workflows in their persistent Boxes.dev environments after eight weeks. That would give me a reason to investigate what users have built their working lives around.
