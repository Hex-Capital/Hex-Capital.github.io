# Zaplar -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Jon Milles wrote security software at seventeen, while Zaplar shifted within months from event-booking automation to replacing PMS, POS, messaging, payments, and booking. That pairing defines Zaplar: credentialed-outsider potential attached to an indefinite product plan. Zaplar has unusual young founders—Axel Andersson Lingbert left engineering mathematics after working in hotels, and Oscar Karlströmer built an academic nonprofit serving 40,000 students—but intellectual range is not yet a definite thesis. Determination without direction is mimetic energy.

**Anduril** is the high rung on founder lineage because Palmer Luckey had already shipped Oculus to a billion-dollar exit before attacking defense. Zaplar shares the outsider mechanism: Jon Milles combined security engineering with autonomous driving, Axel Andersson Lingbert has direct hotel exposure, and multiple founders left prestigious programs. Zaplar diverges where the analogy becomes load-bearing: none of the four founders has shipped a commercial product to scale, produced a prior exit, or held a senior operating role comparable to Luckey. The credentials are interesting; the operating proof is absent.

**Quora** exposes the low rung of definite planning: an indefinite consumer roadmap was tolerable only because Adam D’Angelo’s Facebook CTO pedigree supplied an extraordinary override. Zaplar shows the same planning weakness without that override. Zaplar’s public scope moved from event-booking automation to replacing the entire hotel stack within months, while the five-hotel waitlist belongs to the earlier product. A plan to replace five to fifteen systems is not definite merely because it is ambitious. SpaceX committed to reusability; Zaplar has changed the object being built.

Oracle Hospitality already combines PMS, POS, events, integrations, and regulatory support across more than 200 countries and territories. Zaplar’s possible secret is that a unified property ontology—spaces, resources, people, and bookings—lets agents execute work that incumbent workflow suites can only display. Over eighteen months, Zaplar could compound that advantage through property-specific configurations and action histories. But Zaplar presently names what Oracle and Mews sell badly, not the architectural or institutional reason they cannot copy the agent layer. Mews has 15,000 customers and $300 million of new capital. “AI-native” is consensus; the secret requires an incumbent incapacity.

Mews’s 15,000-customer installed base makes Zaplar’s monopoly trajectory a counterfactual rather than a demonstrated mechanism. Zaplar would move into the invest tier if ten or twenty hotels replaced both PMS and POS, accepted materially higher pricing after deployment, and produced workflow data that could not be exported without losing automation quality. The present evidence—five waitlist hotels, no revenue, no retention data, and no proprietary dataset—supports configuration switching costs but not monopoly pricing. One year of integration friction is a feature; ten years of pricing power is a company.

**Palantir** compounded regulation, customer trust, and integration depth into a moat that ordinary software vendors could not casually cross. Zaplar touches payments, refunds, bookings, and multinational fiscal requirements, but Zaplar sells to commercial hotels rather than a concentrated government buyer whose procurement and security demands exclude entrants. Staff approval for consequential actions mitigates autonomous-action liability; it does not create a regulatory franchise. Zaplar remains atoms-light software in a market where Oracle, Cloudbeds, and Mews already absorb localization costs.

Five hotels joined the waitlist in 48 hours, and my 36-month forecast is that by July 2029 Zaplar will have pivoted from a full rip-and-replace hotel operating system into an agentic operations or event-booking layer and reached roughly $1 million to $3 million in ARR. Zaplar’s credentialed founders should find a saleable wedge, but Zaplar’s scope transition and incumbent replacement burden make the original monopoly plan unlikely to survive contact with deployments. The likely outcome is a useful application inside someone else’s platform—the Shire, not the Ring.

SEK 3 million of early backing and more than 100 hotel interviews form the strongest opposing case: Zaplar may have discovered, before coding, that fragmented hotel systems are the incumbent failure mode, while four unusually young founders are willing to rebuild the stack rather than integrate around it. **Asana** is the known exception—the missing atoms moat and consensus market were overridden by exceptional founder lineage—and Zaplar could become a similar pedigree-led exception if the property model proves 10x better. But Zaplar has no Moskovitz-level shipped-to-scale evidence, and the five-hotel waitlist predates the full-stack thesis. I would flip if, by July 2027, Zaplar had at least ten paying hotels running both PMS and POS replacements under multi-year contracts with above 90% retention. Until then, competition is not validation; it is the central fact.
