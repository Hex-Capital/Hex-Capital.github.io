# Neomatter -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, a lawyer should be able to leave a meeting and find routine requests already assembled with the relevant agreement, the company’s negotiating position, and a proposed answer. Neomatter’s intake-to-approval product points toward that world. But what has to happen between here and there? Reliable retrieval, accumulated judgment, and permission to act. The dossier describes connected inboxes and cited drafts; it does not establish that approved decisions make the next decision better. That missing transition matters more to me than the size of the legal-AI market.

Why does the lawyer have to chase the attachment in the first place? Neomatter identifies a concrete nuisance: requests arrive through Gmail, Outlook, Slack, and Teams while precedent lives elsewhere. Collecting that material could return attention to a small legal team and reduce outsourcing. That fits my attraction to unglamorous workflow repair. But the dossier does not identify an artificial restriction, quantify the coordination cost, or show who captures a rent that Neomatter can remove. Named-user subscriptions with twelve-month default terms describe how the company charges; they do not yet establish how much control the customer gets back.

The strongest argument for writing a check is the founders’ preparation. Wagner and Mainwaring previously worked together at DAHLIA and participated in research on agents taking actions across email, calendars, and reminders. They have also interviewed more than 200 legal teams. That is a credible combination: experience making software act, followed by investigation into where action would be useful. It earns more than a résumé-only founder score. What remains unestablished is a sustained, independently developed obsession with this problem, a founder’s own legal operating wound, or evidence that the team has recruited customers into a difficult new behavior.

I could be underestimating how useful a quietly competent product becomes inside a legal department. A lawyer approving one answer after another could create the company’s working memory almost incidentally. That would make Neomatter much more interesting. My exceptions for pre-revenue companies leave room for exactly this sort of early discovery; missing revenue is no reason to pass. Here, however, the 200 interviews are discovery rather than repeated product use. Subscription terms, launch attention, and demonstrations do not show lawyers repeatedly entrusting real matters to the system. Nor does the dossier establish the prototype validation and unusually deep founder history that would let me override that gap.

The infrastructure possibility also needs to survive contact with Wordsmith, which already advertises overlapping intake and execution. Neomatter has not demonstrated control of a scarce dependency beneath those applications. Customer playbooks and approval histories could become valuable, but their existence alone does not establish a durable asset. I also take privileged-data custody seriously: encryption, tenant isolation, and no-training commitments are affirmative safeguards, while the disclosed retention exceptions deserve examination. They do not prove a severe privacy violation. Likewise, the named CEO and CTO provide no evidence of the leadership shell game I avoid. My pass rests on the investment mechanism, not an invented governance or privacy failure.

I would keep my money today. Neomatter has a plausible product and relevant builders, but the dossier has not yet caught the future leaking into actual customer behavior. One verifiable result would reopen this decision: an independently referenceable, 90-day customer deployment showing repeated lawyer-approved work and a measured improvement in acceptance as that customer’s approved decisions accumulate. That would connect useful automation to a learning system worth owning.
