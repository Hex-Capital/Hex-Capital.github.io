# CONUS -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

CONUS could make device-specific surgical knowledge available without finding the one person who knows which implant, instruction, and hospital protocol apply. That is a worthwhile expert bottleneck. But what becomes 10x cheaper if this works? The dossier supplies neither preparation hours consumed today nor a measured reduction in expert labor or procedural errors. I can imagine a consequential company here. I cannot underwrite that consequence from document counts.

The product’s most interesting mechanism is linking manufacturer portfolios to hospital implant inventories. That could make an answer operationally useful where a general medical search engine is insufficient. Yet 500,000 papers and 2,000 manufacturer documents measure inputs, not capability. CONUS provides no benchmark against C8 Health, Incision, or the existing PDF-and-phone workflow. Source-page links help verification; they do not establish that verification takes less work than the search they replace. There is insufficient evidence to dismiss this as a generic AI wrapper, and insufficient evidence to price it as a technical discontinuity.

The strongest argument against passing is already in the hospitals. More than 20 European hospitals live, plus company-reported relationships with Johnson & Johnson, Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, and B. Braun, could give this small team access to both sides of a difficult information problem. Paid manufacturer services provide an actual financing mechanism. This fits my less-public pattern of backing execution businesses when a narrow wedge can become infrastructure. But the relationships’ commercial depth is undisclosed, and free hospital licenses leave willingness to pay unresolved. The exception for an ordinary initial product entering a giant value pool deserves consideration; CONUS has demonstrated access, while the magnitude and capture mechanism remain unproven. Neither a quantified extraordinary consequence nor an exceptional founder action supplies the other usual grounds for overriding my gates.

The team earns more credit than the technical evidence. Paffrath brought in Becker and Schulz in 2024, combining AI and automation experience with biomedical instrumentation, while five chief-surgeon medical founders or advisors supply complementary clinical context. That is useful talent architecture, beyond three interchangeable AI résumés. There is no documented belief revision or unusually difficult recruit won, so I would keep the credit bounded. Likewise, live deployments demonstrate execution, but the dossier does not show a budgeted experiment against the largest uncertainty: whether reliable answers remove expert work after verification. Advertising two engineering positions is not evidence that the founders are scaling recklessly; their risk-removal sequence is simply undisclosed.

I would pass with my own money. The indexed €9.99–€49.99 monthly subscriptions establish asking prices, not delivery economics; manufacturer financing does not establish permanent subsidy dependence either. Missing evidence gets a low prior, not an accusation. My single flip condition is an independently adjudicated hospital trial showing at least a 10x reduction in total expert time—including source verification—against current device-information retrieval, with no deterioration in answer correctness. That result would connect CONUS’s distribution advantage to an actual collapse in the cost of expertise. Until then, the evidence supports a useful knowledge business more strongly than a transformative one.
