# Grip -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first three numbers I need are missing: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely the first scarce check. That is not evidence of an auction, but it leaves the primary gate unproved. More importantly, Grip has not named what this round buys. “Make the robot better” is not a venture moment. The investable version would specify one facility, one waste stream, a dated contaminant-removal target, and the labor or disposal cost eliminated if the system works.

The strongest fact is Jonas Gruetter’s technical receipt. His reinforcement-learning excavation work reached field tests on a 12-ton excavator with 70% task success, and his experience spans Gravis Robotics, ETH Zürich’s Robotic Systems Lab, and Ursa Mining. That is materially better than a robotics résumé assembled around a fashionable market. He has shipped manipulation into ugly physical conditions. What remains missing is the other side of the bridge: no disclosed operation inside a composting or biogas facility, no waste-industry access, and no evidence that the four-person team understands installation, maintenance, or retrofit economics.

The product hypothesis is nevertheless coherent. Wet, dirty, overlapping organic waste is meaningfully different from the dry mixed recyclables addressed by AMP, Glacier, Recycleye, and EverestLabs. Custom dexterous grippers may therefore reveal a neglected operating system rather than merely replicate suction sorting. The bull case stings: tighter European rules began applying on August 12, 2026, the broader waste-sorting-robot market is estimated at $3.29 billion, and a successful organic-waste deployment could generate proprietary data from both successful and failed grasps in conditions competitors have not mastered.

But Grip does not yet own that dark pool. It reports no deployment, dataset size, pick rate, uptime, removal rate, customer, pilot, or purchase. EverestLabs already claims training data covering more than five billion recyclable objects, while funded competitors have raised between $24.3 million and $195.85 million and disclose real facilities or operating metrics. Founder outreach and 1,354 LinkedIn followers are rented attention until an operator changes behavior. None of the known exceptions applies: there is no viral adoption, enterprise conversion, retention, rejected acquisition offer, or operating cash evidence strong enough to override the missing deal and experiment gates.

Delete the next financing and the model becomes even less clear. Grip discloses no price, gross margin, service burden, installation footprint, throughput economics, burn, or route to break-even. Custom hardware deployed into corrosive waste streams can become a maintenance company wearing an AI multiple. My base case is that within 36 months Grip either pivots toward excavation or mining-material handling, where Gruetter already has operating receipts, or remains below $1 million ARR because organic-waste facilities will not adopt an unproven retrofit quickly enough.

I am passing now, but this is a testable pass rather than a rejection of the technical premise. The single flip condition is a paid six-month deployment at a named composting or biogas facility that sustains a disclosed contaminant-removal rate and uptime while demonstrating payback inside 24 months. Pair that with non-auctioned terms and a round explicitly funding replication of that result, and the same physical complexity currently creating risk becomes the scarce evidence.
