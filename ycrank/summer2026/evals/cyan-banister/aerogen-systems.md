# Aerogen Systems -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Hybrid-bonded chips are assembled across increasingly unforgiving surfaces, and a wafer cannot tolerate the molecular contamination accumulated while moving between tools. Instead of purifying an entire building and repeatedly throwing away chemical filters, manufacturers maintain tiny controlled environments around each wafer. Talos is a credible leak from that future: multiple hardware generations, 7N purity in 16 minutes, and a reported VOC concentration below 1 ppb. What is missing is the full bridge from today’s pilot to that factory architecture—especially which qualification milestones make CleanPipe and tool-to-tool isolation inevitable rather than merely attractive.

The delightful part is that Aerogen sits underneath the visible semiconductor wave. Advanced packaging gets the headlines; molecular contamination control determines whether its yield economics work. The company controls a physical reactor, monitoring hardware, patent-pending IP, and two years of experimental learning rather than wrapping somebody else’s platform. Semiconductor fabs, equipment OEMs, and several advanced processes could depend on that layer. This fits my pattern of concealed infrastructure beneath a fashionable beachhead, although Entegris and Camfil already occupy the tool-level filtration stack and could compress Aerogen’s window.

Vogel and Lavery are the strongest evidence in the dossier. Before YC, they co-authored NASA’s 2024 VOC-removal report, worked together in a NASA-backed laboratory, built multiple generations of hardware, and participated in more than $500,000 of R&D. Vogel’s SpaceX manufacturing experience and Lavery’s detector and photocatalytic-reactor work cover unusually complementary parts of the problem. This is not a pair of founders who selected “semiconductors” from a market map last month. The application moved from spacecraft to ducted industrial systems and then chip tools, but the underlying technical obsession stayed fixed. I read that as search driven by an artifact, not a trend-driven pivot. Commercial command and recruiting power remain unproven.

The present system is irritating but insufficiently decoded. Facility-wide cleanrooms, nitrogen purges, and consumable AMC media plainly impose cost, while Talos proposes local destruction without replacement filters. But Aerogen has not quantified the filter spend, nitrogen burden, yield loss, or qualification delay for one fab, nor shown exactly who owns the recovered budget. The user receives better process control, not obviously new occupational or economic agency. This is a real efficiency mechanism; it is not yet a broken-rule story.

The pre-revenue exception represented by CosmicBrain AI is relevant because both companies pair a functioning physical prototype with years of pre-company technical work. The material difference stings: Aerogen has not disclosed a named customer or signed industrial proof of concept. Its public behavioral evidence is an anonymous pilot and a company-reported 30-minute test, not repeated fab behavior. If Entegris can reproduce the reactor and bundle it into an already-qualified service relationship, Aerogen could spend years doing custom integrations without owning a scalable category. That is the strongest bear case, and it is much more dangerous than the absence of current revenue.

The red-flag sweep is otherwise clean: no surveillance architecture, no dependence on a closed model supplier, no claim that incumbents are trivial, and Vogel is clearly identified as CEO. I would still verify founder control and the contractual tiebreaker before signing. My 36-month base case is two to four qualified tool deployments producing $1 million to $3 million in annualized revenue, with qualification records becoming more valuable than the original reactor patent. I would invest my own money, but this is a measured check rather than a full-conviction one. The flip condition is verifiable: if an independent fab evaluation cannot reproduce sub-1-ppb performance under production conditions or fails to show lower total contamination-control cost than qualified consumable filtration, I pass.
