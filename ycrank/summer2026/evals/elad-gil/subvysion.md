# SubVysion -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Maxwell Stone shipped on-device inference SDKs into more than 10,000 pharmacies, while SubVysion discloses contracts but no revenue figure or growth rate. SubVysion is therefore a shipping-rich, distribution-poor field-robotics bet: the founders can build unusually difficult systems, but the product has not yet shown that it can become an index on construction activity rather than a specialized surveying service.

**Perplexity** is the high rung on founder iteration because Aravind Srinivas repeatedly built the requested artifact between conversations. Stone’s deployed Roboflow work, Tang’s $500 LiDAR search-and-rescue drone, founder-led cold calling, and live job-site deployments place SubVysion closer to that pattern than ordinary credential-driven pre-seed teams. SubVysion diverges where the analogy becomes load-bearing: the dossier shows prior technical artifacts and one customer conversion, not a documented sequence of SubVysion releases driven by repeat customer feedback.

**Lyft** exposed the low-rung failure mechanism for index position: participating in a valuable category does not help when the economics concentrate around a better-capitalized leader. SubVysion faces Exodigo, which has raised more than $200 million, serves 100-plus customers, and has mapped 86.1 million square feet using substantially the same multi-sensor-to-3D-map stack. SubVysion currently has to win individual sites against that incumbent; SubVysion does not yet sit in a workflow every contractor or mapping provider must touch.

The **$1.45 billion** underground-utility-mapping market is growing above 9.61% annually, but SubVysion identifies no specific technological or regulatory threshold crossed in the last 24 months. Over the next 18 months, cheaper robotics and sensor fusion could expand supply, yet those improvements also accrue to Exodigo, Groundhawk, and hardware vendors. Without a discontinuity unique to SubVysion—new excavation-liability rules, a step-change in GPR interpretation, or a distribution shift—the market compounds while SubVysion’s relative position does not. That is category growth, not a Stripe-style re-opening.

SubVysion reports contracts on U.S. job sites and a first customer who also invested, but SubVysion provides no contract count, value, repeat rate, or revenue curve. The score flips into invest territory if those deployments are recurring purchases rather than paid demonstrations—specifically, if national contractors expand SubVysion from one site to portfolios while revenue compounds above 20% month over month. Until then, the evidence is compatible with both “just working” and false-signal pilot tourism, and I do not pay a Harvey-style price without knowing which one I am buying.

**Color Genomics** worked because the hard scientific layer—commodity sequencing—already functioned, leaving workflow and distribution as the single miracle. SubVysion instead needs reliable GPR detection across variable soil and materials, centimeter-accurate georeferencing, dependable autonomous field capture, trusted AR alignment, and a liability model contractors will accept. SubVysion can collapse those dependencies into one execution problem only if the underlying sensing accuracy is already validated; the website’s “100% visibility” claim without published validation suggests the hardest miracle remains unresolved.

Exodigo’s 100-plus-customer footprint makes my 36-month forecast specific: by July 2029, SubVysion will have become a tech-enabled subsurface-surveying service generating roughly $1 million to $3 million in ARR, rather than an infrastructure platform with software-like category leverage. SubVysion’s founders are capable enough to sell and deliver difficult projects, but low index position plus uncertain detection completeness pushes SubVysion toward labor- and hardware-coupled contracts where each new site must be won and serviced individually.

SubVysion’s first customer becoming an investor is the strongest opposing fact because customers rarely invest in field technology that produces no operational value. The bull case is the **Color Genomics** exception: SubVysion could turn commodity sensors into the trusted workflow layer for underground data, then compound proprietary sensor-to-verified-utility records across every deployment. The dossier does not yet win that case because Exodigo already operates the same flywheel at 86.1 million mapped square feet, while SubVysion publishes neither dataset scale nor independent accuracy results. I would flip to Invest if SubVysion reports six consecutive months above 20% monthly recurring-revenue growth from at least two named national contractors.
