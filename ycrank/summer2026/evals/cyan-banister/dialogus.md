# Dialogus -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Thousands of production calls make Dialogus a physically verifiable artifact trapped inside a governance vacuum: Dialogus can already speak with Fortune 500 customers, yet three co-founders are named without a single CEO or tiebreaker. That high-touch-product, low-authority pairing is the signature. I can hear the machine working, but I cannot tell who makes the final call when Hans Ibarra, Juberth Rodriguez, and Rodrigo Terán Hernández disagree.

Niantic is the high rung because I could play Ingress myself and feel the product before the market understood it. Dialogus shares the load-bearing mechanism: real people can converse with Dialogus, watch tools execute, and observe latency, handoffs, and failures across thousands of production calls. Dialogus diverges because access remains behind a “Book a demo” gate; Papa Johns, KFC, and Totalplay are impressive logos, but I cannot personally call a disclosed deployment and feel the magic within 48 hours.

HQ Trivia’s low-rung failure mechanism was unresolved co-founder authority: tactile product pull briefly covered a structural crack, then the crack became the story. Dialogus names three co-founders, no CEO, no co-founder history, and no operating tiebreaker. Dialogus does not yet earn the HQ Trivia exception because “thousands of calls” demonstrates usage, not addictive pull, revenue, or retention. Multiple founders are not automatically fatal; multiple founders who have not shown me who decides are a hard no rather than a rebuttal.

Hans Ibarra worked on Gemini, Gmail, and YouTube, while Rodrigo Terán Hernández interned at Google, Meta, and Microsoft. That is Tiz, but Dialogus provides no evidence that anyone lived inside a contact center, owned an escalation queue, or felt a failed handoff at 2 a.m. Over 18 months, Dialogus can compound production traces into regression suites, but founders who know models better than call-center operations may optimize the elegant layer while Sierra and Parloa own the customer. Dialogus receives my absent-evidence founder default: technical adjacency is not autobiographical ownership.

Sierra reached $100 million ARR while Parloa raised more than $560 million, placing Dialogus inside a blazing consensus category rather than a category polite investors refuse to touch. Dialogus could flip my “magically weird” score if sophisticated buyers or investors rejected its workflow-constrained architecture as unfashionably rigid—and those constraints subsequently proved essential. The dossier instead shows Dialogus pursuing the same enterprise-agent budget as heavily funded incumbents, with no evidence that the round is underpriced or that anyone is laughing for the right reason.

Vigil Labs represents regulated software where regulation itself can deepen the moat. Dialogus processes CRM records, transcripts, account context, and tool outputs, but Dialogus cites SOC 2 compliance and auditable calls rather than a license, approval regime, or proprietary regulatory position. SOC 2 is useful plumbing; it is not my SpaceX-shaped frontier swing. The constrained workflow layer may become unsexy infrastructure, but today it remains software operating inside requirements competitors can also satisfy.

Papa Johns, KFC, and Totalplay make my 36-month forecast concrete: by July 2029, Genesys will acquire Dialogus for less than $150 million before Dialogus reaches $10 million ARR. Dialogus has built exactly the traces, replay tooling, workflow controls, and enterprise integrations a contact-center incumbent would rather buy than reproduce quickly. The same artifact strength and weak standalone moat that make Dialogus useful also make Dialogus look more like an acquisition-ready subsystem than a defining independent platform.

KFC plus thousands of production calls is the bull mechanism that stings: Dialogus may have reached genuine enterprise deployment before most pre-seed founders find a design partner. Dialogus could be the HQ Trivia exception, where observable product pull overwhelms imperfect governance, and the absence of public revenue may merely reflect negotiated contracts. I still pass because call volume can come from pilots, three logos can hide concentration, and no disclosed CEO compounds the platform risk. One verifiable condition would flip me into writing a check: at least $2 million ARR from renewed—not pilot—contracts across the three named customers.
