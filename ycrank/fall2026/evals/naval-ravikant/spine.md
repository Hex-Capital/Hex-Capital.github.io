# Spine -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Spine’s strongest founder evidence is that 30+ startups have entrusted it with benefits and HR operations. Onur Kardesler and Ece Cokar have moved beyond describing a problem into serving customers. Their third company together also suggests an established working relationship. I give that action substantial credit. Their engineering and legal backgrounds are relevant, but neither credentials nor earlier ventures establish deep insurance-brokerage knowledge. The current business supplies the evidence; the résumés add little.

My central question is what Spine accumulates while doing the work. Operating customers’ existing Rippling, Deel, Gusto, and ADP systems is a sensible entry point: customers can delegate without migrating. But dedicated specialists still perform the workflows, and the dossier establishes no exclusive carrier access, proprietary model, or contractual control over distribution. The prospective claims-data advantage remains an investor thesis. Copying the code would leave competitors needing service execution and relationships, but Spine has not demonstrated that either becomes progressively harder to reproduce.

The 50+ practitioners in its People Layer are the most interesting potential exception. Spine is connecting fragmented professional supply, which fits one of my recurring investment patterns. Yet a referral directory becomes a network advantage only when participants demonstrably create business or utility for one another. There are no referral conversion rates, repeat exchanges, or customer acquisition cohorts here. Nor is there evidence that outside contributors can expand the service without proportional company labor. Fifty relationships establish reach; they do not establish a compounding loop.

The startup wedge addresses real adjacent spending. Benefits brokerage is estimated at $49.5 billion globally in 2026, and the underlying enrollment, renewal, and compliance work also exists outside startups. But Spine has not established which adjacent buyer it can win with the same delivery system. Larger employers could require different service capabilities, while smaller employers may generate insufficient commissions. I credit the broad economic territory only modestly because the quoted market estimate does not prove Spine’s route through it.

The strongest argument against my pass is the commission engine. Acadian’s September 2026 account reports roughly 4.4× commission-revenue growth over six months through July. Carriers already pay for distribution; Spine can use that payment to remove an operating burden for employers. If automation compresses service costs, its free offering could be an unusually effective acquisition mechanism. That possibility deserves attention. But absolute revenue, commission rates, and realized service economics are undisclosed. The approximately 78% contribution margin is company-modeled, so I cannot treat it as demonstrated operating leverage.

I would pass on writing a check today. My exceptions for missing monetization, absent network effects, or an unclear initial wedge require unusually strong compensating founder or product evidence; Spine has not supplied enough to invoke them. I see no documented founder-integrity veto. The conflicting 15% and 25% savings claims need reconciliation, but they do not establish dishonesty. My one flip condition is a verified renewal cohort showing that customers acquired through practitioner referrals retain and generate rising contribution profit per support hour. That would demonstrate that Spine’s relationships and operations compound together.
