# Kairos Health -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Adhira Tippur worked at her mother’s dental front desk. That matters more to me than the team’s university names. She encountered the work Kairos Health now automates: answering calls, recalling patients, and fitting appointments around the practice’s constraints. Tanush Chintala’s prior patient-call routing work adds relevant experience. This is an organically discovered problem with an ugly implementation burden—practice-software integration, insurance workflows, and patient-data handling. There is a credible reason these founders chose it ([Rice Business](https://business.rice.edu/news/putting-end-front-desk-overload-adhira-tippurs-journey-automating-clinic-workflows), [Chintala portfolio](https://tanush-chintala.github.io/)).

They have also reached people who will pay. The founder reports 13 paying practices and $4K+ MRR; Pecan Dental reports more than 1,200 calls handled monthly. Those figures identify a narrow well of demand: dental offices with telephone work they cannot finish. The revenue snapshot is undated, and the testimonial is company-published, but both are more useful than expressions of interest. Pecan’s $21K+ in monthly dental production is neither Kairos revenue nor proof of incremental collections. I would give substantial credit for payment and actual use without turning a customer’s production into a growth claim.

The product has crossed the important boundary between describing automation and operating it. Kairos reported 16 live US locations at its October 2026 launch, with scheduling and practice-software write-back among its capabilities ([YC launch](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/W5m-kairos-health-ai-front-desk-for-dental-practices)). That fits my preference for concrete proof before a financing story. It does not yet demonstrate the unusually difficult technical achievement or proprietary foundation that sometimes makes a young company immediately compelling. Arini and Dentina offer overlapping workflows. The opportunity is therefore to learn the details of running a dental office faster than competitors, then embody that learning in software. Feature comparisons alone cannot establish that advantage.

The missing piece is how these founders behave when the software or the customer says no. The dossier contains no named blocker followed by a founder-built workaround and an observable result. Nor does it show a rejected product hypothesis and an evidence-driven change of route. Both receive my missing-evidence defaults. Founder cold calling, reportedly producing four demos, is encouraging evidence against premature delegation or dependence on a large partner. The founders’ functional titles do not establish managerial distance, and attending two universities does not establish a hastily assembled team. But neither sales activity nor relevant credentials substitutes for seeing them recover from resistance.

The strongest argument for investing is that customers have already answered the practical question: some offices want this enough to pay and let it handle real calls. A working product and genuine pull can justify an exception to my founder-evidence gate. Here, they justify continuing the conversation, but the disclosed usage and revenue do not establish overwhelming pull strong enough to replace that missing evidence. I would not write a check yet. My flip condition is one verifiable customer account showing a concrete deployment obstacle, the workaround the founders themselves shipped within days or weeks, and successful bookings afterward. Kairos Health already has a plausible business. I need to see the behavior that could make it an exceptional one.
