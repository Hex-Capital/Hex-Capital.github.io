# Almanac -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that Almanac connects Slack, Gmail, GitHub, Docs, and meeting notes. It is that the founders encountered the limits of integrations and shipped a managed browser so the assistant could operate tools without APIs. That is a real workaround to a named blocker. But the dossier does not say when the blocker appeared, how quickly they solved it, or whether the browser changed anything for a user. It shows resourcefulness in the product architecture, not yet relentlessly resourceful behavior under observed pressure.

The strongest evidence is that this team builds. CodeAlmanac accumulated 791 GitHub stars and 71 forks before the founders expanded from a coding-agent wiki to company-wide context. The current product appears functional enough to connect accounts, maintain a source-linked wiki, perform actions, and charge $250 per user per month. This matches my unstated preference for programmable infrastructure built before a category settles. But the measured proof belongs to the previous product. LinkedIn followers, a price, and a three-day trial do not establish that anyone repeatedly uses the new one.

The product transition could be rapid route-changing or merely a larger noun. We do not know what CodeAlmanac users did that caused the founders to widen the product, what hypothesis failed, or whether the same users followed them. Without that chain, I cannot give them credit for running toward evidence. The likely narrow well is engineering teams already using the 791-star repository, yet Almanac now asks every sort of employee to entrust it with company knowledge and autonomous actions. That increases the trust and integration burden before proving who wants the crude version most.

The bull case stings. Three technical founders from Harvard, IIT, BITS Pilani, Arcesium, and major ML research institutions have already shipped a public artifact, then built through the ugly connector problem instead of waiting for every vendor to expose an API. Their product also resembles the working-prototype exception that justified overlooking uncertainty around Dropbox. The material difference is user pull: Dropbox had a striking prototype and rapid founder correction, while Almanac has disclosed neither current-product usage nor the observation that caused its expansion. Credentials cannot substitute for that missing loop.

The red flags are therefore mostly absences, not active defects. Almanac does not depend on press or a single named partner, and the founders have not delegated product or sales. But it is entering a market where Glean already has more than $100 million ARR and 250 connectors, while Dust serves more than 3,000 organizations with 300,000 deployed agents. Computer use is also available from OpenAI and Anthropic. In this market, “the AI that knows your company” is close to a sitcom idea unless a particular group is already pulling the product toward an urgent job. No known exception overrides that missing evidence.

I expect that within 36 months Almanac will have pivoted back toward developer-specific knowledge and coding context, with less than $1 million ARR, because that is where its only measured pull currently exists. I am passing now. One verifiable fact would flip me: five named teams retaining the current company-wide product for at least three months at the published $250-per-user price, together with one dated example of their usage forcing the founders to ship a material change.
