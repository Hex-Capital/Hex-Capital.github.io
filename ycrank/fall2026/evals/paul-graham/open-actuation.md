# Open Actuation -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Open Actuation has made motors. That matters. But the founder story I most want is still missing: what failed, what Jon Facer did about it, and how quickly the fix reached a customer. His experience at Gecko Robotics and five years as 1Factory’s first employee makes him worth talking to. It does not establish how he behaves when blocked. I would leave resourcefulness at the missing-evidence default rather than turn his résumé into an anecdote the dossier does not contain.

The strongest evidence is physical. July 2026 reporting describes initial motor samples undergoing customer feedback, and the company describes an LA operation with winding, magnet insertion, balancing, and testing equipment. This is beyond a drawing. Yet the important technical claim is whether Open Actuation can repeatedly deliver purpose-built motors in four weeks. Neither the advertised turnaround nor the proprietary manufacturing software has a measured customer result behind it here. My pattern of backing unfamiliar technical companies after a numerical proof applies only partially: there is an artifact, but no demonstrated threshold that makes the manufacturing advantage convincing.

Drone OEMs are a sufficiently narrow starting point. A developer whose aircraft must accommodate an unsuitable catalog motor could care intensely about a custom alternative. Open Actuation reports building for such customers, and the sample feedback provides modest evidence of engagement. Neither establishes that customers return. Thousands of motors per month—or per week on the other site—is capacity, not demand. The $187 CubeMars listing establishes a price for someone else’s product, not realized revenue or willingness to pay for this team’s engineering.

The strongest argument against passing is that Facer has already entered the unpleasant part of the business. Manufacturing software experience, actual samples, and domestic machining and assembly could be the beginnings of a useful production system. Imported magnets and silicon steel need not invalidate that approach; localization can proceed in stages. But the dossier does not connect this opportunity to a specific problem Facer encountered in his prior work, or document an evidence-driven change of route. Those dimensions remain at their defaults. Meanwhile, Atlas also advertises custom motors within weeks, and Westmag has disclosed $11 million for domestic production. The company must discover an advantage customers experience, rather than rely on customization and geography alone.

I would pass on this evidence, at 38. The documented exceptions in my framework require a compelling working prototype, unmistakable pull, or validated technical performance strong enough to replace a weak prior. These samples do not yet supply that override. Nor does the dossier establish active red flags such as waiting for a partner or delegating early selling; missing evidence is not misconduct. My concern is whether this becomes a growing product company or remains a useful custom engineering business. One verifiable event would reopen the decision: a named drone OEM places a paid repeat order after Facer resolves a documented manufacturing failure and delivers the corrected motors within four weeks, meeting agreed test specifications. That would show what these founders do when reality pushes back.
