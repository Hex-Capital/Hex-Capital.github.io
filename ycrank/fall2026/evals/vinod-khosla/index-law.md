# Index Law -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Legal expertise becoming radically cheaper would matter. Index Law’s direct-to-business approach could eliminate the repeated explanation, coordination, and hourly billing that make routine legal work expensive. But the dossier does not quantify how much legal spending Mike can replace or how many businesses gain access they otherwise lack. The cited $2.1 billion legal-AI category measures software spending, not the societal cost pool being overturned. I can see the consequence hypothesis. I cannot yet price an extraordinary outcome from the evidence supplied.

The strongest product evidence is contracts delivered in under 60 minutes and quotes up to 80% below traditional firms. An 80% reduction would be a 5x improvement if scope, quality, and responsibility were comparable. Those comparisons are undisclosed, as are sample sizes and subsequent corrections. Persistent company memory, agent harnesses, and matter-level evaluations describe potentially useful engineering, but establish no measured advantage over competing approaches. Harvey and Legora already automate legal workflows; selling directly to businesses changes the customer relationship without itself proving a technical discontinuity. Index Law has not established the proprietary learning mechanism that would keep improving its advantage.

The best affirmative signal is Pativada recruiting Dominic Raab as a founding senior advisor. Legal judgment complements the founder’s prior AI-company operating experience and addresses a specific weakness in the team’s disclosed background. This fits my pattern of taking credible domain participation seriously before commercial validation. It earns limited recruiting credit, not credit for regulatory permission or demonstrated reliability. Likewise, the reported $20 million-plus booked revenue belongs to Pativada’s previous education venture. It supports operating experience, not Mike’s demand. Moving from Assess in July to Mike in November shows product evolution; without the evidence behind that change, I cannot call it demonstrated belief revision.

The bull case is substantial: business users want completed work, and Mike combines drafting, negotiation, persistent context, and independent counsel around that demand. My execution-heavy investments and exceptions for modest initial products leave room for such a starting point. A novel foundation model is unnecessary if the application creates a defensible capability. But those exceptions require evidence of an expansive mechanism or an exceptional instigator pursuing a quantified consequence. Neither is established here. Nor should I declare Mike a generic wrapper merely because its benchmarks are missing. The relevant red flag remains unresolved: could competitors reproduce the useful functionality while Index Law retains the expensive coordination and oversight?

I pass with my own money today. Index Law names the right kill risks—wrong governing law, commercially unsuitable answers, and missed escalation—but supplies no decisive experiment, threshold, budget, or stop condition. Its forthcoming $149 Pro and $499 Teams plans also leave the economics unanswered because lawyer fees are separate and supervision costs are undisclosed. This is missing evidence, not evidence of permanent subsidy dependence. **My flip condition is one independently assessed, preregistered trial of 200 representative matters showing comparable legal quality, no missed critical escalations, and total delivery cost—including lawyer review and corrections—at or below 20% of matched conventional delivery.** That would establish whether the apparent cost collapse survives responsibility for the work.
