# Synara -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Synara’s investment case turns on operator minutes per protected site at a fixed incident-response standard. AI could let security integrators sell continuous monitoring across their installed camera bases while Synara handles substantially more sites per operator. That would change the economics of the monitoring station. The dossier establishes the proposed service, but provides no measurement of this controlling variable. “Run by AI” does not establish that labor costs stop scaling with cameras or incidents.

There is evidence that customers will adopt monitoring without replacing their cameras. Deep Sentinel reported that its third-party-camera program represented nearly half of sales by June 2025. That is a useful, company-reported adoption signal for the category, not traction for Synara. It supports a distribution opening, but also shows that the opening is already contested. Alarm.com acquired a majority stake in CHeKT in February 2025, giving an incumbent monitoring infrastructure and established channel relationships. The estimated $6.2 billion remote-monitoring market is large enough to matter; the question is which layer captures its economics.

The strongest argument for Synara is Boris Ploix. He co-founded Calipsa as CTO in 2016, worked directly on machine learning for surveillance, and saw Motorola acquire the business in April 2022. This is documented second-time learning in the exact category. The bull case is that he understands why selling detection software leaves the harder—and potentially more valuable—work of resolving incidents to someone else. Synara’s move into monitoring could reflect that insight. I give the founder substantial credit for relevant startup experience, without inferring desperation, personal proceeds, or operational advantages the dossier does not establish.

The product also reaches beyond generating an alert. Synara describes camera/NVR ingestion, AI filtering, human verification, voice intervention, and police escalation. That earns partial credit for owning a response workflow: stronger models could reduce verification work while the service retains responsibility for intervention. But documented deployment integrations, accumulated incident records, and feedback loops are absent. Integrators retain the customer relationship under their own brands, so access to their distribution does not yet establish that Synara owns a reusable channel. Its proposed workflow is more substantial than a detection feature, but its defensibility remains unproven.

My pattern of backing software that performs institutional work makes this worth examining. My physical-business exception, however, requires evidence that technology changes delivery economics. Synara has not supplied that evidence, and nothing establishes a frontier-research exception to the demand requirement. Its October 2026 launch and press mention demonstrate availability, not repeat usage or willingness to pay; I assign the missing-demand default rather than treating pre-seed silence as failed adoption. Likewise, undisclosed wholesale pricing and margins leave the service-economics question unresolved rather than proving a bad business.

I would pass on writing a personal check today. The founder evidence is considerably stronger than the market-economic evidence, and it cannot substitute for it. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: a paid, 90-day deployment across multiple integrators demonstrating at least a 50% reduction in operator minutes per site against their existing monitoring baseline, without worse incident-detection or response performance. That is my proposed investment threshold, not a reported result. It would show that Synara can turn domain expertise into a different cost structure.
