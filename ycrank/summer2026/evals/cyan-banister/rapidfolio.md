# Rapidfolio -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Naveen Qureshi sold Sable in 2022, built credit products at Seen, and worked inside Capital One; Rapidfolio is founder-authentic but physically invisible. That is Rapidfolio’s signature: unusually strong lived ownership of financial operations paired with a product I cannot ride, scan, or encounter outside an enterprise workflow. Qureshi and Frederik Vanhevel have Biz and Tiz—the pair already built Sable together—but the dossier cannot show me the Rizz I would have noticed around a dinner table. Rapidfolio feels less like tourists discovering “AI for fintechs” in a market memo and more like operators automating scars they accumulated over 25 combined years.

Crusoe is the high rung because Chase Lochmiller belonged on the oilfield before anyone stapled Bitcoin to stranded gas. Rapidfolio shares that biographical mechanism: Qureshi led product and operations at Capital One, Vanhevel was Sable’s founding engineer, and both later built credit infrastructure at Seen. Rapidfolio diverges because Crusoe’s origin established social license in an alien physical environment, while Rapidfolio’s dossier establishes professional repetition inside a familiar software market. That still places Rapidfolio much closer to Crusoe than to the credentialed AI-agent founders who discovered a $26.52 billion TAM on slide one, even with Qureshi’s Columbia MBA sitting in the biography.

The consensus YC AI-agent darling fails because its value exists mainly in founder testimony and interchangeable model capability. That failure mechanism appears in Rapidfolio: access is demo-led, no public product can be installed, and the claimed 99.9% production accuracy has neither sample size nor benchmark methodology. Rapidfolio does leave observable footprints—versioned workflows, exportable audit records, human approvals, and a bank’s reported 83% queue reduction—but those are artifacts an institutional buyer audits, not something a normal human touches within 48 hours. Rapidfolio is closer to the low rung than to Notion’s immediately usable software artifact.

Naveen Qureshi is explicitly Rapidfolio’s CEO while Frederik Vanhevel is explicitly CTO, which gives Rapidfolio a visible tiebreaker shape rather than the HQ Trivia ambiguity. Over 18 months, that separation can compound: Qureshi owns regulated customers and implementation economics while Vanhevel owns deterministic execution and the 30-plus integrations. Rapidfolio’s prior Sable-and-Seen working history lowers interpersonal discovery risk, but no evidence establishes majority operating authority, founder vesting, or an exit-conflict protocol. Two titles are a good diagram; they are not yet proof of what happens when the CEO wants growth and the CTO refuses an unsafe deployment.

SOC 2 compliance, AES-256 encryption, tenant isolation, and human approval place Rapidfolio in regulated finance, but Rapidfolio remains software rather than a SpaceX-class frontier artifact. Vigil Labs is the cleaner parallel: regulation itself can become accumulated scar tissue that a casual entrant refuses to acquire. Rapidfolio earns that comparison because every customer-specific procedure, test, integration, and audit history can deepen switching costs. The score would flip upward only if Rapidfolio demonstrated that regulated execution—not a commodity model—is technically difficult to reproduce, such as independently verified error rates across millions of decisions or regulatory acceptance of its audit trail.

Bretton has raised $95.3 million, WorkFusion roughly $410 million, and Sphinx and Arva already sell overlapping agents, so Rapidfolio is not magically weird yet. The counterfactual is sharp: Rapidfolio becomes a high-scoring non-consensus bet if sophisticated banks reject autonomous execution on regulatory grounds while its live U.S. and Canadian customers nevertheless expand from one workflow into underwriting, fraud, reconciliation, and disputes. Today the under-two-minute onboarding review and projected $350,000 first-year savings are meaningful, but unnamed testimonials do not prove that Rapidfolio has found a misread everyone else is laughing at. Four funded competitors make “AI for bank operations” a crowded lane, even when Rapidfolio’s founders authentically belong there.

Live customers in the United States and Canada lead me to predict that Rapidfolio reaches $8 million to $15 million in ARR within 36 months, concentrated among fintechs and smaller banks, while remaining independent and relying on forward-deployed implementation rather than becoming a universal autonomous bank-operations layer. Rapidfolio’s lived founder advantage should win trust and deployments; Rapidfolio’s low physical verifiability and one advertised Forward Deployed Engineer role should keep growth coupled to services. The bear case stings: Bretton’s capital, WorkFusion’s installed base, unverified 99.9% accuracy, and implementation labor could compress Rapidfolio into a consultancy with software margins painted on it. I still invest because Sable, Seen, and Capital One constitute repeated contact with the exact wound—not pedigree cosplay—but I flip to pass if customer references cannot show two production expansions beyond the initial workflow within twelve months.
