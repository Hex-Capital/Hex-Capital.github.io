# Trepo -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I would like the kitchen to remember the milk so nobody has to. Trepo’s little device belongs in that world: it works with existing refrigerators, accepts voice and camera updates, and connects household inventory to recipes and shopping lists. But the important transition is from recording what somebody remembers to knowing what the household actually consumed. The dossier does not establish that transition. I can imagine the future; that imagination is not evidence that Trepo can deliver it.

The most interesting artifact is the work people apparently volunteer to do. Trepo reports six sold-out beta hardware batches and more than 150,000 item check-ins; its iOS app has 56 ratings averaging 4.9/5. Someone is putting groceries into a digital memory. That is much more interesting than a waitlist. Yet batch sizes are undisclosed, and aggregate check-ins cannot distinguish enthusiastic setup from a habit that survives six weeks of cooking, leftovers, and children moving things. I give the working product credit, while keeping founder-reported behavior within its evidence ceiling.

Why does this household need another object on the counter? Because somebody already carries the inventory in their head. Returning that person’s attention would matter to me. But if every eaten yogurt requires a report, Trepo has moved the chore onto a device. There is no identified institutional gatekeeper, artificial restriction, or quantified rent being removed here. The free app and unpublished hardware and subscription prices also leave the exchange unclear: how much attention and money does a household surrender to get its time back?

Taylor’s Tesla and Nuro engineering experience makes the hardware credible, and Slaughter’s USCAPE marketing background supplies a complementary skill. Their college water-polo relationship predates the company. Those are useful facts, but they do not establish a grocery obsession that produced independent experiments before this opportunity appeared. The strongest argument against my pass is that these two have already combined engineering and distribution into something people seek out. My willingness to back pre-revenue prototypes applies here; missing revenue is not the objection. What is missing is the unusually strong founder history or demonstrated technical breakthrough that would let me underwrite the remaining behavioral leap.

Trepo fits my pattern of taking mundane workflow repair seriously, and its kitchen device could reveal something a polished fundraising presentation would miss. The concealed-infrastructure pattern is less established. Household consumption histories might become valuable, but the dossier labels that moat as inferred. Samsung already combines food recognition with Instacart replenishment, and Cooklist offers overlapping inventory functions. Trepo has not demonstrated control of a scarce dependency beneath those products. Kitchen cameras and third-party audio processing also require care: the stated deletion of inadvertently captured faces is a useful limit, but does not resolve consent and custody for everyone sharing the kitchen. That is an unresolved diligence question, not evidence of a severe privacy violation.

I pass with my own money today. My flip condition is one consented, independently auditable 90-day trial across 50 households showing at least 90% inventory accuracy with no more than one manual correction per household per week, alongside repeat paid replenishment. That single experiment would show whether Trepo returns attention or consumes it. I want the kitchen that remembers. I need to see that the household can forget.
