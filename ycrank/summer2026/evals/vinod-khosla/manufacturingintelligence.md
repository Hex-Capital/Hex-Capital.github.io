# HERA -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

HERA can prevent a $40,000 part from reaching scrap and compress days of senior-engineer review into minutes. That is a real cost, not a fabricated workflow. But one expensive error is not yet a consequential cost pool. The dossier quantifies neither annual scrap and review spending nor the population of engineers constrained by this work. I see potentially valuable industrial software, not yet a 100x societal consequence.

The technical claim is also ahead of the evidence. HERA checks GD&T, tolerance stacks, assemblies, and company-specific rules while keeping drawings on premises. Those are useful product choices. They are not a demonstrated 5–10x discontinuity when CoLab, Axial, Redprint, and Glypad already advertise overlapping checks, and Redprint charges only $1.98–$4 per drawing. “Minutes instead of days” matters only after HERA publishes recall, false-positive rates, engineer time saved, and escaped-error performance against both manual review and these products.

The strongest bull case is the learning loop. More than 200 practitioner interviews and live demonstrations in three production plants are unusually concrete pre-seed signals. Customer-specific rules, adjudicated violations, and an accumulating drawing-error record could turn a narrow checker into industrial knowledge infrastructure. This resembles the exception made for **Block**: an initially incremental product can earn the option to expand across a giant workflow. The material difference is that HERA has not quantified an American Express-scale value pool or shown that its data compounds across customers rather than remaining bespoke configuration.

Accuracy is the kill risk. A category benchmark reporting 5.23% hallucination is unacceptable wherever one missed contradiction can scrap a $40,000 component—or worse, certify a defective aerospace or pressure-vessel part. Keeping engineers in the approval loop limits damage but also limits labor displacement. HERA names the problem yet supplies no test set, pass threshold, experiment cost, or stop condition. The next dollars should buy a blinded comparison across production drawings: escaped critical errors, false alarms, review minutes, and performance on previously unseen customer rules. Hiring and sales come later.

Patel and So have credible proximity to factories: Pfizer and TE Connectivity projects, industrial-engineering training, ML work, workflow automation, and a 40 L/s water-treatment build. They look like doers. But both emerge from the same UW industrial-systems gene pool, and the dossier contains no documented belief revision or recruitment of a senior GD&T, safety-assurance, or manufacturing-software expert to own the largest risks. Two founders can start this; the current evidence does not show the risk-tuned team required to dominate it.

Economics should be favorable for software replacing senior-engineer hours, but favorable intuition is not a model. HERA publishes no price, deployment burden, utilization assumptions, or cost of maintaining customer-specific rules and on-prem installations. My verifiable flip condition is simple: show on at least 1,000 blinded production drawings that HERA reduces total review time by 80% while missing no more critical violations than senior engineers, then convert three plants into paid deployments with gross margins above 70%. Until then, I pass. The likely outcome is useful workflow software competing in a crowded category, not yet a one-sided option on industrial transformation.
