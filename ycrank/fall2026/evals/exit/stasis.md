# STASIS -- Exit Evaluation

**Action:** Watch  
**Evidence confidence:** Low

### Company case

Strategic acquisition is the strongest path. An established eyewear maker could buy STASIS to add fatigue detection, automatic tint, and screen adjustments to its product line. The memo records prototype coverage and the opening of refundable reservations. The founder reports discussions with eyewear brands, which provide a lead for a commercial pilot. Ownership could make sense if integrating the full system into the buyer’s frames generates more incremental margin or saves more development time than building or licensing similar features. The memo leaves that buyer benefit unmeasured. (The Idea, Market & Traction)

The first operating milestone is to ship the scheduled March 2027 batch and measure how many reservations convert to paid orders. That would put production units in users’ hands for a real-world comparison against simpler alternatives. The prototype, reservation launch, and confirmed YC backing make that sequence plausible at seed. Production cost, runway, and the capital required to ship and validate the product remain unknown. STASIS reports training on 550 billion eye-data points, while ownership and transfer rights for those data remain unverified. (Market & Traction, Key Risks, Defensibility)

**Illustrative proceeds.** With shareholder terms undisclosed, assume a $500,000 check at a $10 million post-money valuation, 5% initial ownership diluted by 40% to 3%, and zero follow-ons. A 5× gross cash return within eight years requires $2.5 million to the holder, or $83.3 million in distributable equity at 3% ownership. The equity hurdle rises to $125 million at 2% and falls to $62.5 million at 4%.

Using simple pro rata allocation before investor taxes and assuming $5 million of net debt, the 3% case requires $88.3 million of enterprise value. At year eight, a $25 million sale yields $600,000 gross (1.2×) and a $100 million sale yields $2.85 million gross (5.7×). Those prices are scenario inputs. The memo supplies no acquisition pricing evidence or company-specific debt, fees, or senior claims to quantify. If growth plateaus, buyers stay away, public scale remains out of reach, and available cash is reinvested, eight years of private operation yields $0 realized and the shares remain held. A wind-down would pay only the residual after obligations and preferences, which the memo leaves unquantifiable. (Market & Traction, Key Facts)

The memo leaves each other route unevidenced. A sponsor buyout needs predictable cash generation, a secondary needs demand for eligible shares, an IPO needs evidence of public-market scale, and dividends or buybacks need distributable cash. (Market & Traction, Key Facts)

### Countercase

An eyewear maker could source inward-tracking components, build individual features using published designs, or partner with a supplier that already integrates facial sensing into third-party frames. Software-only blink training also addresses part of the customer problem. That evidence bears more directly on the buyer’s alternatives than the founder’s reported discussions bear on acquisition demand. STASIS claims over 90% accuracy for fatigue and cognitive-load measurement, with the reference measure and validation protocol undisclosed. The alternatives weaken the case for ownership. The memo leaves both a structural block and a valuation ceiling unsubstantiated, so the Pass gate stays open. (Defensibility, Market & Traction, Key Risks)

### Decisive diligence question

Can a production pilot with an established eyewear maker show enough incremental profit or development savings over its best build or licensing option to support an acquisition above the modeled $88.3 million enterprise-value hurdle? A buyer-backed comparison using shipped units and measured economics that credibly supports that price within eight years would move the action to Reach out.
