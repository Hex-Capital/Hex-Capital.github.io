# Verno -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Verno has chosen a problem where the boring work matters. Change one engineering drawing and someone must reconcile the lists, datasheets, and equipment specifications downstream. A missed update can become an incorrect order. Editing the native CAD file and carrying the change through those documents could remove a considerable burden. But the founder question remains unanswered: what broke during an installation, what did Oliver Wesche and Ujjawal Madan do about it, and how quickly did the customer get unstuck? Their previous jobs cannot answer that. I give resourcefulness the missing-evidence default.

There is enough evidence to take the product seriously. Verno reported live customer-site deployments by November 2026. That earns intermediate technical credit: this appears to have progressed beyond a proposed architecture. Yet the dossier supplies no inspectable demonstration of a native-file change propagating correctly, independently measured error rate, or reconciliation benchmark. Engineer acceptance is a sensible control. It leaves an important technical question: how much checking must the engineer repeat before accepting the machine’s work?

The strongest case for investing is the team’s proximity to the work. Wesche has engineering and ML experience at Blue Origin and Asurion; Madan has built document-extraction systems. They report years of friendship and shared work. Rohit Shinde adds more than a decade in petrochemicals and EPC consulting. This makes the opportunity more credible than a generic industrial-AI idea. Connectors, project conventions, and engineering approvals also contain the sort of schlep that can conceal a good business. But the dossier never establishes the founders’ own encounter with this particular problem, so the origin criterion stays at its default. Nor is there a documented rejected hypothesis followed by a revised product; adaptability also receives its default.

The July 2 recruiting post describing an EPC design partnership processing actual project drawings identifies a plausible narrow well. It earns demand credit beyond expressions of interest. It does not establish repeated voluntary use, payment, or referrals. Missing revenue is ordinary at this stage; the consequential uncertainty is whether engineers keep bringing Verno their next change. Autodesk already supplies authoring and configurable validation. Verno’s separate contract becomes easier to justify if customers repeatedly use its cross-document workflow. The pitch competition, followers, and accelerator selections do little to resolve that question.

I would pass on the present evidence. Verno fits my pattern of technical founders entering an operationally awkward market, but lacks the numerical proof spikes or demonstrated developer appropriation that strengthen my other bets. The established cofounder relationship is favorable, and a design partnership alone does not establish the large-partner dependency red flag. The working-product and overwhelming-pull exceptions deserve consideration; the reported deployments are promising but insufficient to override the missing resourcefulness gate. One verifiable condition would reopen this pass: an independently corroborated customer case showing a specific engineering blocker, a founder-built workaround shipped within two weeks, and that customer subsequently returning with another project.
