# Terse -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

What does Terse know that Cloudflare and Rivet do not? Its strongest candidate secret is that developers building multiplayer agents need a self-hostable actor runtime with ordinary class semantics badly enough to adopt a new infrastructure dependency. That proposition is testable: production teams should choose it over existing actors and remain after the initial experiment. But the dossier establishes a programming preference, without identifying a disputed belief or reporting that test. Durable state is already the competitors’ thesis. The secret remains undisclosed.

The more decisive problem is ownership of the resulting market. Terse offers SQLite-backed actors, serialized requests, generated clients, and WebSockets. Cloudflare supplies managed Durable Objects; Rivet supplies managed and self-hosted actors. Self-hosting therefore does not establish an exclusive beachhead. The MIT license need not prevent a valuable business, but it makes implementation ownership unavailable as the present monopoly mechanism. Embedded actor identities and accumulated state could eventually make migration expensive. Until customers demonstrate that dependence, switching costs remain a hypothesis.

The strongest objection to passing is that infrastructure adoption often begins with precisely this kind of developer preference. Terse has shipped a runtime, attracted 256 GitHub stars and 19 forks, and received 53 points and 25 comments on Show HN. Thomas Karatzas spent seven years on Siri and Apple Intelligence; Olivier Simard-Morissette has backend engineering experience at Pigeon, Squarespace, and The New York Times and publishes project releases. These founders can plausibly turn an awkward primitive into something developers actually use. But attention establishes an acquisition opening; it does not yet establish production dependence. The October 26 release’s socket-ordering and dropped-message limitations make that distinction especially consequential for coordination infrastructure.

The founding evidence supports caution without an accusation. Both founders attended McGill, but that does not establish a long working relationship. Their ownership and full-time status are undisclosed; there is no affirmative evidence of convenience matching, outsourced core engineering, or investor-dominated capitalization. My preference for concentrated operator networks finds no documented counterpart here. Nor does Karatzas’s previous acquisition establish that he is rebuilding an operating system whose failure he personally encountered. Terse participates in the AI infrastructure stack, but the dossier shows no scarce resource it controls within that stack.

The commercial question is similarly specific: what will developers pay Terse to retain? Hosting and support are inferred possibilities, with no verified pricing or margins from which to construct a power-law outcome. GitHub, comparison articles, and Show HN constitute a real developer channel, earning some distribution credit. They do not establish an economical route from installation to a paying buyer. The missing revenue history is ordinary at this stage; the unresolved relationship between free adoption and retained economic value is central to this investment.

I pass. The documented substitutes trigger my monopoly gate, and the known exceptions do not rescue it: Terse presents neither an independently demonstrated technical breakthrough, persistent customer transactions, nor a validated deployment path that directly removes the competitive risk. Its disclosed engineering limitations also do not amount to a refuted foundational claim; this is a competition problem before it is a technical failure. One verifiable event would reopen the decision: a named production-customer cohort renewing paid Terse contracts despite a materially cheaper competing offer, with migration tests demonstrating that accumulated actor state explains the choice. That would turn a useful runtime into evidence of a business that can retain value.
