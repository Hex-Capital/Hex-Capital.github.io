# Enact -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: robots encounter unfamiliar objects, detect their own failure modes, request the missing demonstrations, retrain, and return to work without a technician watching every rollout. Enact occupies a necessary layer in that world. But the dossier never completes the path from today’s manual observation and task resets to that autonomous loop. Gemini Robotics and NVIDIA Cosmos are market-opening events, not evidence that Enact owns the next step. The only present-day artifact is a website saying “Request data.”

The system wound is real but only partially decoded. Robot developers pay for humans to observe rollouts, judge success, reset tasks, and collect recovery data under deployment-specific conditions. Enact proposes to join failure identification, targeted demonstrations, training, and evaluation into one loop. That could return iteration speed to model developers, but the dossier does not quantify the labor rent, identify who captures it, or show that Enact removes rather than merely resells expensive human data collection.

This is beneath the visible robotics wave, which is the most attractive part of the company. A proprietary map connecting failure states, recovery demonstrations, and subsequent policy performance could become important infrastructure across many robot embodiments. Today that map is hypothetical. Enact discloses no dataset scale, exclusivity, benchmark, customer, or controlled technical asset, while Instance has tested a success detector on more than 10,000 held-out episodes across seven platforms, Axis has raised $12 million around adjacent post-training data, and NVIDIA and Runway can absorb parts of the loop into broader platforms. Enact currently looks like a workflow assembled between powerful suppliers, not yet a scarce layer they must traverse.

The bull case stings because pre-revenue physical-AI companies can deserve checks before conventional traction: **CosmicBrain AI** shared the enabling-layer position and lacked revenue. The material difference was a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a founder whose robotics obsession was documented from childhood. Here both founders’ biographies, roles, relationship, technical work, and customer history are absent. Missing publicity is normal at pre-seed; missing evidence of who built what leaves the first-believer test at its default. With two founders and no accountable role identified, I would also resolve control and the contractual tiebreaker before investing.

My pattern test produces only silhouettes. The company may accumulate concealed infrastructure beneath a fashionable robotics market, but it has not shown the accidental artifact, strange user behavior, or operating wound that usually makes such a system legible early. YC’s August 9, 2026 directory listing and a live request form prove availability, not use. No developer is shown paying, repeatedly submitting failures, changing deployment schedules, or obtaining a measurable policy improvement.

I pass today. My base case is that by August 2029 Enact has pivoted into a services-heavy robotics data operation generating less than $2 million ARR, because task-specific collection remains expensive and the integrated software loop is competed into larger platforms. One result flips me: a named robotics developer renews a paid deployment after Enact cuts its failure-to-recovery training cycle by at least 50% across three production tasks. That would reveal both the working artifact and the proprietary data flywheel currently missing.
