# GUILD -- Paul Graham Evaluation

GUILD bids directly on government manufacturing contracts while neither Kaya Celebi nor Erim Gurlemis has documented aerospace or government-contracting experience. That combination makes GUILD a schlep-rich startup without an organic founder origin. The highest signal is willingness to own pricing, compliance, inspection, packaging, logistics, and delivery. The lowest is that GUILD reads as a market entered from adjacent skills—machine learning and finance—not as a tool Celebi or Gurlemis first built to solve their own recurring problem.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung because Brian Armstrong ran toward the regulatory grind everyone else avoided. GUILD shares that mechanism: technical specifications, testing clauses, supplier qualification, working capital, and nonconforming-delivery risk become valuable precisely because they are unpleasant. GUILD diverges from Coinbase at the point where appetite becomes evidence. Armstrong had built the Android wallet; GUILD discloses no completed contract, realized delivery, or revenue. The schlep is real, but I cannot yet tell whether GUILD likes the schlep or merely likes the idea of automating it.

**Loopt** (2005) exposed the low-rung failure mechanism on organicity: a plausible technical future can arrive before founders possess a deeply lived need, with founder force compensating for weak product timing. GUILD shows the first half but not the exception. Celebi was an AI tech lead at Hartree and an ML engineer at Morgan Stanley; Gurlemis worked in wealth-management engagement. Neither biography shows GUILD growing out of an aerospace drawing, a failed government bid, or a late shipment they personally endured. Sam Altman’s animal quality overrode Loopt’s weakness. GUILD provides no comparable override.

Kaya Celebi’s 20 GitHub repositories and 183-star flight-analysis project are real hacker tracks, but GUILD has not shown the corresponding animal track. Over the next 18 months, resourcefulness would compound through ugly actions: manually parsing solicitations, persuading small factories to reserve capacity, financing materials, and personally rescuing late parts. Each completed GUILD contract would generate both execution data and a story about refusing to die. With no named award, revenue, or delivered part disclosed, GUILD currently has credentials and a hypothesis rather than its cereal-box moment.

Kaya Celebi’s flight-analysis repository puts GUILD closer to the **Dropbox** (2007) hacker pattern than a finance-led defense rollup. Drew Houston’s demo proved not merely that he could code, but that Dropbox itself existed before the pitch. Celebi’s repository proves technical taste outside GUILD; it does not prove that GUILD Forge can correctly turn a defense technical package into a production plan. The hacker-genealogy signal is therefore meaningful but incomplete. A working Forge demonstration against a real solicitation would matter more than another résumé line.

Kaya Celebi and Erim Gurlemis make GUILD a two-founder company, which clears the structural screen, but no public source establishes that they overlapped at Morgan Stanley or built together before GUILD. The score flips upward if GUILD can show a multi-year working relationship in which Celebi shipped systems while Gurlemis repeatedly closed or financed difficult transactions. It flips downward if Morgan Stanley is merely a shared logo and GUILD’s founders met to pursue the opportunity. Two names are not yet a strong pair; chemistry is an observed property, not arithmetic.

Hadrian’s $500 million financing makes a precise 36-month forecast possible: by July 2029, GUILD will have pivoted from acting as a broad accountable prime contractor to selling compliance and production-planning software to defense suppliers, with $1 million to $3 million in annual recurring revenue. GUILD’s strongest asset is the software-and-data layer; GUILD’s weakest is lived contracting experience. Supplier failures, inspection liability, and working-capital demands will push GUILD toward the layer Celebi can control rather than the balance-sheet risk Hadrian funded itself to absorb.

Xometry’s $178 million Q4 2025 marketplace revenue supplies the strongest opposing case: GUILD could become valuable precisely by owning accountability that software vendors avoid, while Celebi’s technical ability and Gurlemis’s finance background form an unusually relevant pair for coordinating factories and capital. That is the bull mechanism, and **Airbnb** (2009) warns me not to confuse a wrong-seeming model with a bad bet. But Airbnb already had the $40 cereal boxes. GUILD has no named contract, paid delivery, or manual stunt showing that its founders are animals rather than intelligent observers of a favorable February 2026 policy shift. One verifiable condition changes my verdict: GUILD completes a paid government contract on time, documents the compliance trail, and wins a second award from the resulting execution record.
