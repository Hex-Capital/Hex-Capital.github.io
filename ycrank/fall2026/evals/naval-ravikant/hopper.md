# Hopper -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Hopper’s founders have acquired knowledge in the right place: inside the machinery. Katta worked on post-training at Salient; Pedapudi worked on inference kernels at Meta and streaming infrastructure at Uber. More persuasive than those affiliations are the April 2026 engineering account and June voice-client work. Reusing HTTP/2 connections addresses an actual production failure mode. These are artifacts of doing. I give the team substantial credit for specific knowledge, without assuming that employment history transferred proprietary assets into Hopper.

The investment turns on what remains after another capable team reproduces those optimizations. Hopper serves open-weight models through an OpenAI-compatible API. Its disclosed connection optimization is reproducible, and Fireworks already offers specialization on customer data alongside inference. The proposed escape is customer-specific post-training. But the October 26 privacy policy requires separately negotiated training consent; serving calls does not automatically confer learning rights. An operational feedback loop becomes an asset only when Hopper can retain something valuable from it. That mechanism is not established here.

The strongest argument against passing is that realtime performance rewards accumulated engineering judgment. Hopper reports 80ms time to first token for Gemma 4 and 150ms time to first audio for Qwen 3 TTS. The Dograh integration merged on October 1 puts the product inside an existing developer workflow. Those are concrete reasons to take the team seriously. Small improvements across connection management, serving, and post-training could collectively matter more than a competitor’s feature checklist. Yet the latency figures remain company-reported comparisons without matched workload, concurrency, and quality evidence. They demonstrate a promising technical claim, not an established compounding advantage.

Distribution has started; customer-created distribution has not been demonstrated. A Dograh developer can select Hopper, but the dossier does not show that this makes another developer’s product better or brings the next customer automatically. Likewise, customer-authorized tuning may improve one customer’s model without improving anyone else’s. Hopper fits my pattern of tools that let developers produce more independently. It has not established the permissionless contribution mechanism that makes such systems compound.

There is real spending around this problem: Deepgram’s estimated $100 million annualized revenue supports that conclusion. It does not establish Hopper’s expansion path. The dossier identifies voice-agent developers as the initial buyer but supplies no evidenced route into a distinct adjacent buyer group. I leave that dimension at its missing-evidence default rather than treating unavailable TAM as proof of a small market. Monetization is more concrete: $0.30 per million Gemma 4 31B input tokens and prepaid consumption credits identify what customers would pay for. They establish pricing, while completed purchases and contribution margins remain unknown.

I would pass with my own money today. There is no documented integrity problem, artificial financing deadline, or founder instability driving that decision. I checked the exceptions: exceptional founder behavior can outweigh an unclear wedge, and a distinctive, capital-efficient prototype can justify investing before a user loop appears. Hopper’s technical artifacts make those exceptions worth considering, but neither extraordinary founder behavior nor demonstrated capital efficiency is established strongly enough to override the structural concern. My one condition for reopening is a documented production deployment in which a customer-authorized model that Hopper retains rights to produces a sustained quality-adjusted latency or cost advantage over the same base model served by Fireworks. That would turn the proposed proprietary asset into evidence.
