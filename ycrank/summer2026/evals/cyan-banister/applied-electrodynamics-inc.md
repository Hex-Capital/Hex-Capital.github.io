# Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and every renovation crew scans a room before making the first cut. Walls become queryable records: pipes, conductors, fasteners, moisture, and structural elements appear inside the contractor’s model at true scale. Applied Electrodynamics has produced a plausible artifact from that world—a handheld radio-imaging system claiming room-distance operation and 1 mm point-cloud accuracy—but the dossier does not map the intervening transitions. I still need to know how an impressive scan becomes trusted ground truth, enters construction workflows, survives varied materials, and acquires the liability posture required for crews to act on it.

The present-day leak is therefore technical, not behavioral. WaveSight reportedly works through sheetrock, tile, marble, quartz, brick, and insulation, while Walabot must travel against the wall and reaches roughly four inches. If independently demonstrated, stand-off imaging at millimeter accuracy would be a real discontinuity. Yet Applied Electrodynamics provides no benchmark, specification sheet, or field-validation dataset. Preorder registration, 148 LinkedIn followers, and hundreds of expo conversations do not show anyone trusting the device enough to drill, demolish, or sign their reputation to its output.

The founders are the strongest part of this bet. Bilgehan Avser led antenna work across four iPhone generations; Brian Huppi shipped early iPhone multitouch and the first Nest thermostat; Paul Leutheuser worked on haptics and IPX8 sealing; George McLean built semiconductor imagers, retinal implants, and optical sensors. They have worked together for more than eight years and possess unusually complete RF, imaging, mechanical, and production capability. That proves technical fluency and complementary recruitment. It does not yet prove a pre-company obsession with the specific construction wound: the dossier contains no dated experiments, customer work, or personal encounter that compelled them to build this rather than another advanced sensor.

The broken economics are legible but incomplete. More than $20 billion in annual avoidable rework is a substantial rent paid to uncertainty: contractors cut first because the wall withholds reliable information. WaveSight could return control to the person making the cut. But the dossier does not quantify the cost per incident, identify who purchases and operates the device, or show how savings flow through general contractors, subcontractors, insurers, and building owners. Without that chain, “construction is wasteful” remains less exact than a repeatable purchasing mechanism.

The sharp bull case is that this team has already solved the hardest physics and merely has not published the evidence. Company-built radio hardware and reconstruction algorithms could become a concealed enabling layer beneath construction, industrial inspection, cargo screening, and defense—not another fashionable software wrapper. But those markets require different validation and sales systems, existing RF vendors are well financed, and the cited 250-plus patents are not established as company property. My base case is that within 36 months Applied Electrodynamics narrows from a general construction camera to industrial or defense inspection and remains between $500,000 and $2 million ARR while completing validation.

There is also a gate I cannot daydream past: a camera that sees through walls is surveillance infrastructure. Soliciting defense organizations without an explicit consent, custody, retention, or human-detection architecture activates my privacy red flag. The pre-revenue hard-tech exception does not apply yet because there are neither signed industrial proofs of concept nor independently verified prototype results. I would not write the check today. One result would flip me: a paid pilot with a named general contractor that publishes ground-truthed results across at least 100 walls, demonstrates median localization error below 3 mm, and contractually limits the system to structural-object detection.
