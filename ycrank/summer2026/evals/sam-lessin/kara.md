# Kara -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I cannot underwrite Kara before I know the post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and what this first check buys. None is disclosed. That earns the financing gate’s 6/28 default—not because a Summer 2026 company should already have a financing history, but because price is part of the thesis. A technically serious company can still be a terrible seed investment if YC attention has already auctioned the uncertainty away.

The most credible asset is the team’s translation layer. Aarin Jhaveri has sold more than $5 million of diamonds across the value chain; Anuveer Chadha worked on diamond quantum-sensing systems at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory; the company says it already grows, processes, and characterizes single-crystal diamond in its own reactors. That joins commercial diamond access to technical use cases more convincingly than three generic materials résumés would. Arjun Lakhanpal adds software and data experience, while Aarin is identified as CEO, so I do not see evidence of the equal-founder committee problem despite the three-founder structure.

The missing piece is a named venture moment. Kara could use scarce capital to prove a specific wafer dimension, defect density, thermal result, yield, or customer qualification by a fixed date. Instead, the dossier provides no use-of-funds plan, measurable milestone, or deadline. “Scale manufacturing” would not be enough: the experiment must reveal whether Kara can produce a customer-qualified material at repeatable economics, not merely purchase another reactor and prepare for another round.

The bull case is real. AI racks now reach 120 kW, Akash introduced diamond-cooled AMD MI350X servers in March 2026, and Kara offers diamond at a claimed 2,400 W/m·K thermal conductivity. The founders may therefore possess exactly the proprietary process knowledge and buyer access that public sources cannot observe. But resemblance to a dark pool is not evidence that it causes purchases. Kara discloses no customer, sample qualification, repeat order, pilot, shipment, yield, capacity, patent, or revenue; 427 LinkedIn followers prove nothing economically.

This is also physical infrastructure competing against heavily financed incumbents: Diamond Foundry has raised $315 million, Diamfab €8.7 million, and Akash $18.28 million plus substantial public support. That does not make Kara uninvestable, but it makes the one-and-done question brutal. With no gross margin, burn, throughput, or contribution economics, I cannot show how a pre-seed check reaches a self-financing materials business rather than the next capital requirement. The known exceptions do not rescue it: there is no corresponding hard override such as retained enterprise demand, thousands of users, or a rejected acquisition offer.

My 36-month base case is that Kara remains a sub-$1 million annual-revenue custom diamond-materials supplier by August 2029, constrained by qualification cycles and manufacturing yield rather than demand for heat removal. I would pass today. One verifiable fact flips me: a paid qualification order from a named semiconductor, RF, or data-center hardware customer, paired with repeatable yield and gross-margin data from Kara’s own reactors. That would prove the team’s cross-system access produces a purchase and turn an interesting materials capability into an underwritable experiment.
