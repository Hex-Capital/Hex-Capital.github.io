# UFO.ai -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

UFO.ai’s strongest asset is the knowledge its founders have acquired by building. Graveley’s reported work on Copilot, Comet and Hackpad maps directly onto agents, browser execution and collaboration. Kiely brings operating experience from Perplexity and Notable Health. More useful than the titles are the September 2026 engineering reports: experiments with persistent context and a documented failure to propagate a correction. These founders are confronting the actual problem. That earns substantial founder credit. It does not automatically give the company a moat.

The decisive question is what remains when another team copies the runtime. UFO publishes its execution, identity, authorization and accounting layers under Apache licensing. That can encourage adoption, but competitors can inherit the implementation too. Remembered decisions and configured workflows could eventually create switching costs; the dossier establishes neither retention nor migration difficulty. Technical sophistication helps the founders reach the frontier. Without an accumulating asset, it also leaves them repeatedly rebuilding their lead.

There is room to maneuver. The initial buyer is a business team delegating reporting and follow-ups; connected accounts, persistent context and browser execution can extend into software maintenance and broader operational work. Glean’s disclosed $300 million ARR demonstrates substantial adjacent enterprise spending, although none of that revenue belongs to UFO.ai. I give the company credit for a plausible expansion mechanism, rather than the $182.9 billion category forecast. The unresolved issue is which recurring job makes a team choose this product over Glean or Dust.

The strongest case against waiting is that these founders could make the open runtime the default substrate for delegated work. Self-hosting and customer-supplied model keys reduce permission barriers. That fits my preference for systems outsiders can adopt and extend. But 99 stars and 13 forks establish early attention, not a contribution loop. The dossier does not show one customer making another customer’s agents better. My exception for an unusually capable team with a working prelaunch product is relevant; it earns a serious look, but does not resolve the missing compounding mechanism.

The commercial design is sensible: prepaid task consumption charges for work rather than seats. Unlimited members could encourage broader deployment inside an account. Published billing terms, however, do not establish completed paid transactions, task prices or contribution margins. Meanwhile, the September 23 stale-memory incident identifies a concrete obstacle to trusted delegation. The candid disclosure is constructive; the unresolved semantic-correction problem still matters. The founder-list discrepancy merits verification, but supplies no evidence of dishonesty or the unfair dealing that would trigger an integrity veto.

I would wait at the current evidence level. UFO.ai has exceptional relevant builders and a broad opportunity, with little demonstrated value accumulation beyond their continued engineering effort. My single flip condition is a documented deployment cohort showing that customer-contributed extensions measurably improve successful task completion for other customers without proportional UFO engineering work. That would turn permissionless distribution into compounding utility—and materially change the investment.
