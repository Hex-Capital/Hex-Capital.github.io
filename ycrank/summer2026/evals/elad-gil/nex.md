# Nex -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: better code-generating models now let GTM teams automate work across fragmented systems, the buyer is a revenue-operations leader who cannot hire a GTM engineer for every workflow, and the controlling variable is whether Nex can run consequential cross-system processes repeatedly without human verification. If that reliability threshold is reached, a $100-per-seat product can expand into the operational layer for GTM. The dossier does not provide the threshold, workflow success rate, or repeat-usage evidence needed to show that it has been reached.

The product is more substantial than a prompt wrapper. Nex generates rerunnable code, connects to more than 1,000 integrations, maintains a context graph, and can take actions through Slack messages and pull requests. Granular RBAC and SOC 2 Type 1 also address real prerequisites for handling CRM, email, and messaging data. A stronger foundation model should improve workflow generation rather than erase the entire product. However, the durable asset would need to be permissions, workflow state, evaluation data, and responsibility for completed work. Today, customer-specific decision histories and learned playbooks remain inferred, while customers must verify potentially inaccurate outputs.

The economic step change is still mostly asserted. Nex claims that individual automations can replace roughly $100–$500 per month of manual tooling, but there is no measured reduction in GTM-engineering hours, implementation cost, or tool spend. At the same time, Clay has reached $100 million ARR and already serves GTM development, while Gumloop and Relevance AI possess funded workflow runtimes and integration channels. This is a crystallizing market, not an empty category. Nex therefore needs a sharply better reliability or deployment-cost curve; natural-language workflow generation alone is unlikely to change market margins.

The strongest bull case is the team. Mohammad worked in HubSpot product and built a ChatGPT plugin used by 48,000 people; Dias was HubSpot CRM’s fourth engineer and later worked on its AI infrastructure. WUPHF’s 1,200 GitHub stars, 94 forks, and 260-point Show HN launch show that they can attract technical attention before polishing the commercial story. Shutting down the earlier AI-native CRM despite revenue and enterprise proofs of concept may also reflect useful learning rather than weak commitment.

But that evidence has not transferred into demonstrated pull for Nex’s current product. GitHub stars, Product Hunt points, and followers do not establish that GTM teams repeatedly entrust the system with live revenue workflows. The prior CRM, context-infrastructure phase, WUPHF launch, and current automation positioning create three product transitions within a short period. I sometimes buy broad coverage during a discontinuity, but this market already contains scaled, well-capitalized competitors. The frontier-talent exception does not apply: this team has strong application-layer experience, not scarce underlying-model research that independently justifies ignoring product-market pull.

I would pass with my own money today. The inferred data moat lacks a reproduction-cost estimate, permissioning is not fully solved, and the decisive market variable remains unmeasured. One condition would flip the decision: Nex reaching $1 million ARR with at least 120% net revenue retention from customers running production workflows across three or more integrated systems. That would demonstrate both product pull and an owned operational layer rather than another successful developer launch.
