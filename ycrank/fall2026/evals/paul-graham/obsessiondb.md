# ObsessionDB -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The useful fact about ObsessionDB is that its founders encountered an expensive infrastructure problem while running Numia and built something they could use. They had BigQuery, dbt, and Postgres doing different parts of the job. They replaced the storage layer beneath ClickHouse and put the result into production. That is evidence of resourcefulness. But the dossier doesn't show how quickly they went from blocked to unblocked. The October report that they raised cloud quotas after launch users hit provisioning limits supplies another small example of responding to resistance. Neither account establishes exceptional speed ([case study](https://obsessiondb.com/blog/how-numia-cut-costs-75-percent-bigquery), [founder report](https://de.linkedin.com/in/marchoeffl)).

The technical accomplishment deserves more confidence. ObsessionDB has a functioning storage and cache system beneath an existing query engine, with a reported production deployment serving APIs over 50 TB and ingesting hundreds of thousands of events per minute. Retaining ClickHouse's engine is sensible: the founders can concentrate on the part they believe is broken. Their proprietary SharedMergeTree and locality layer constitute an actual substrate built before the platform claim. This fits my preference for concrete technical proof, although the performance evidence remains company-reported rather than independently benchmarked ([YC launch](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/VpG-obsessiondb-a-real-time-data-warehouse-built-for-agents), [feature documentation](https://obsessiondb.com/docs/feature-status)).

Who wants this now? Teams serving frequent queries to customer-facing applications have a specific reason to care about predictable capacity pricing. Numia reportedly moved 200 TB and reduced monthly workload costs from roughly $8,000 to $2,000. That is a substantial enough improvement to make someone endure a migration. Paying production customers and millions of API requests add evidence of repeated use. The important qualification is that Numia is also the founders' previous company. It demonstrates usefulness more convincingly than independent distribution. I would not count their previous company's $1.5 million ARR as ObsessionDB revenue, or treat advertised pricing as realized revenue ([case study](https://obsessiondb.com/blog/how-numia-cut-costs-75-percent-bigquery), [customer claims](https://www.linkedin.com/company/obsession-db)).

Their shared Numia history matters for two separate reasons. It connects the founders directly to the problem, and it reduces the risk of a cofounder relationship assembled for an accelerator application. Database migrations, cache behavior, compatibility, and recovery are precisely the unpleasant work that can conceal a useful business. But productizing internal infrastructure does not by itself demonstrate the running-back quality I want. There is no documented rejected hypothesis followed by a materially revised route. That dimension receives the missing-evidence default. The “for agents” description earns nothing by itself; their production infrastructure history supplies the reason to take the idea seriously ([founder backgrounds and origin](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/obsessiondb)).

The strongest objection is that ObsessionDB has demonstrated a useful engineering improvement without demonstrating a durable company. ClickHouse controls the upstream engine and competes directly for these customers. It could narrow the price difference while offering fewer compatibility compromises. ObsessionDB could then become a migration and support business whose savings disappear into operational labor. The inconsistent encryption disclosures make this objection sharper: a database vendor must earn trust alongside speed. Missing certification is ordinary at this stage; contradictory statements about an existing control require resolution. Unsupported native backup operations also make the operational burden concrete ([competition](https://clickhouse.com/company/our-story), [security disclosures](https://obsessiondb.com/security), [compatibility limits](https://obsessiondb.com/docs/feature-status)).

I would invest. The founders have crossed the difficult boundary from knowing an infrastructure problem to operating a replacement that people pay to use. The demonstrated workload and reported savings outweigh the incomplete evidence of iteration, though they do not justify overwhelming conviction. The founder-action score narrowly clears my gate, and the working product supplies the stronger affirmative evidence; no exception is needed. My verifiable flip condition is a production security review finding that paying customers' data lacks the encryption at rest advertised on the homepage. That would undermine the trust on which the entire migration proposition depends.
