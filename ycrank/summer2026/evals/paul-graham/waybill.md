# Waybill -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Waybill’s awkward fact is that the dossier names many obstacles but never shows what happened when one actually stopped the founders. Supplier scarcity, broker authenticity, customs classification, freight, and last-mile delivery are real blockers. Yet the stated answers—fallback suppliers, quote comparisons, licensed customs brokers, and shipment tracking—are operating procedures, not dated examples of Rishi or Tushar improvising under resistance. I cannot infer relentless resourcefulness from a comprehensive website.

The strongest case for Waybill is the origin of the idea. Rishi previously procured aerospace hardware and managed cross-border operations at LAT Aerospace. Tushar built inventory and demand-forecasting systems used by more than 400 Indian brands at WareIQ. They are not tourists who appended AI to a procurement market report. They have lived on both sides of the mess, and they chose the ugly part: payments, international freight, customs, broker verification, delivery, and shelf inventory. That is genuine schlep blindness in reverse.

The product is reportedly live and can accept a part number, description, or BOM, compare quotes, route payment, and reconcile import records. This is more than a diagram, but the dossier provides no fulfilled order, measured landed-cost improvement, working API, or independently observable technical threshold. Waybill’s retained price and lead-time history could eventually become useful infrastructure. Today it is a plausible accumulation mechanism without disclosed scale, while Cofactr, Forge, Jiga, and Luminovo already cover overlapping workflows.

The first two weeks produced requests for an obsolete RF component, 20 tons of aerospace-grade titanium, and four Cessna aircraft. Those requests are striking because they expose a narrow class of users with unusually painful sourcing problems. But a request for four aircraft can be curiosity, a price inquiry, or a real transaction. The distinction matters. There is no payment, delivery, repeat order, retention, or named customer. Missing those metrics after two weeks is not evidence against Waybill; it simply means the product has not yet been pulled back hard enough to establish demand.

The bull case stings: two founders with direct procurement and inventory experience launch an end-to-end service, and almost immediately receive bizarre, difficult orders that ordinary procurement software cannot handle. That could be the beginning of a valuable operator network. But there is no documented exception strong enough to overcome the missing founder-action evidence. Waybill has not yet shown a working proof comparable to its claims, nor has it shown unmistakable user behavior. Its unspecified pricing also leaves open whether this becomes scalable software and data infrastructure or a labor-intensive brokerage exposed to customs errors and supplier failures.

I would pass now. My decision would flip if, within the next 90 days, three identifiable hardware teams each completed at least two paid cross-border orders and the founders documented one supplier, authenticity, or customs failure they personally routed around with a shipped process or product change. That single test would establish both user pull and the kind of founder motion the dossier currently lacks.
