# ORO AI -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

ORO AI has demonstrated that its trajectories can improve a small model. It has not demonstrated what consequential economic bottleneck that improvement removes. The company-authored study lifts Qwen3-4B from an 18.0% published baseline to 42.7% success on a guarded held-out partition. That is a meaningful experiment. But neither the spending displaced nor the population benefiting is quantified. I can lose my entire check on an improbable breakthrough. I still need evidence that success would matter enormously.

The technical signal deserves attention. Replaceable shopping environments, hidden needs, and changing inventory attack an actual weakness of agent training: learning the test instead of learning the task. ORO AI supplies a training pipeline and an empirical result, which clears my concern about businesses differentiated only by prompts and interfaces. The reported improvement is approximately 2.4x, however, and does not establish a 5–10x advantage over ShoppingBench or another named alternative under matched conditions. The October practice-to-hidden gap—0.71 versus 0.18—makes transfer the central engineering question. More trajectories are valuable only if they buy capability outside the environment that rewards their production.

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders have already assembled a functioning experimental ecosystem. Over 345 competing agents, four completed competitions, and 4,891,831 scored agent-version/task pairs are substantial activity for two people. This resembles a pattern I like: repeated experiments feeding a production platform. Bansal’s evaluation and Bittensor experience, combined with Schilbe’s AWS distributed-systems background, gives the mechanism credible operators. If independent builders continually discover better strategies and ORO converts those strategies into transferable training data, the company could own an important learning loop. MIT-licensed code would not automatically reproduce that operating community.

But the experiment factory must produce something worth more than its inputs. ORO distributed $85,000 to builders; those payments establish participation, not customer demand or economical data production. No disclosed model connects rewards, validation, compute, filtering, and environment maintenance to the cost of useful training improvement. Externally governed Bittensor rewards also leave the durability of participation unresolved. I would not assume permanent subsidy dependence. I would not assume independence from it either. Prime Intellect already offers environments and reinforcement-learning infrastructure, so ORO needs a measurable advantage that a research buyer cannot obtain more cheaply elsewhere.

The founders have shown a sensible response to abuse: after agents exploited public tasks, ORO introduced public qualification followed by hidden-task races. That is actual risk-directed work, and earns milestone credit. The dossier does not establish a costed experiment with a decisive threshold for transferable performance or reward-independent economics. Separately, relevant founder biographies do not establish exceptional recruiting or a documented pattern of rapid belief revision. These are evidence limits, not objections to a two-person pre-seed team. My exceptions for missing commercial plans require an extraordinary demonstrated instigator or an enormous quantified consequence; neither override is established here.

I pass with my own money. There is a technical kernel worth following, but the demonstrated benchmark gain does not yet support the consequence or economics I require. **One result would flip my decision:** a paying independent research buyer reproduces a 10x reduction in fully loaded training-data cost at matched performance on previously unseen, buyer-controlled commerce tasks, including all participant rewards and infrastructure costs. That would connect ORO AI’s competition to an economic discontinuity.
