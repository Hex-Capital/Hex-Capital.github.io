# Orca Aerospace -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Orca Aerospace’s strongest asset is knowledge its founders acquired doing the work. Jacob Ososke spent over a decade in Lockheed Martin guidance, navigation, and control and founded its first AI-GNC team. Hakan Chunton’s trajectory optimization and onboard collision-avoidance research addresses the planning problem directly. These are substantive operating and research signals; the degrees add little to that case. I give the founders substantial credit without assuming their former employer’s technology, data, or customer relationships belong to Orca.

The investment turns on whether that knowledge becomes an asset outside their heads. Orca separates AI-selected objectives from deterministic planning and command guardrails. That is a sensible architecture for expensive mistakes. But avionics integration and hardware-in-the-loop testing are engineering requirements, not evidence of an accumulated advantage. If a competitor copied the software tomorrow, the dossier does not establish what Orca would retain that the competitor could not reproduce. Operating aboard hardware does not itself confer a hardware moat. Shield AI and Sedaro’s August 2026 demonstration of 189 approved orbital commands makes that distinction concrete: overlapping capability already exists elsewhere.

The strongest argument for investing is that spacecraft operators buy confidence, and confidence can accumulate through deployment. Orca’s concept-level rendezvous and proximity-operations study with Lyra puts the team in contact with actual compute, power, and spacecraft-bus constraints. This fits my preference for companies attacking physical infrastructure bottlenecks. Exceptional builders can also justify investing before revenue or a user network exists. But that exception needs a demonstrated product or unusually strong execution evidence; the dossier supplies no Orca orbital results. Lyra’s study makes the opportunity more credible without establishing that validation compounds across customers. Platform-specific integration could instead make each sale another engineering project.

The market evidence leaves room for ambition but little precision. Satellite operators facing intermittent communications are identifiable buyers, and onboard planning could extend from fault recovery into proximity operations and broader mission execution. The cited $5.88 billion spacecraft-autonomy market includes hardware and services, however; it does not establish the software spend Orca can capture. Nor does one operator currently appear to improve another operator’s product or acquisition economics. The company has no demonstrated permissionless contribution loop, customer-created distribution, or aggregation of fragmented supply. A proprietary validation corpus could eventually matter, but neither access rights nor reusable results are documented. Pricing and revenue are also undisclosed, so I assign the missing-evidence defaults rather than inventing either commercial success or adverse economics.

I would keep Orca Aerospace under observation and hold my check today. There is no documented integrity problem, artificial financing pressure, or founder instability; my reservation is structural, not personal. Specific knowledge is present. Evidence that it produces repeatable economic ownership is missing. My flip condition is a paid deployment with a second independent spacecraft operator that demonstrably reuses the first deployment’s validation artifacts and requires materially fewer integration hours. That would show Orca beginning to sell accumulated capability rather than repeatedly selling its founders’ time.
