# Evardi -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Evardi’s possible secret is that utilities already possess enough scattered data to recover transformer-level visibility and check switching decisions without instrumenting every transformer. That is a proposition worth testing. It would fail if virtual-meter estimates broke down against actual transformer measurements under changing loads or incorrect network connectivity. But the dossier does not identify an industry belief that Evardi has disproved, or peers who reject its proposition. Combining GIS, SCADA and meter data establishes an engineering approach; it does not yet establish a secret.

The strongest evidence concerns the founding relationship. Shah and Borele knew each other from freshman year at Berkeley, rather than assembling a team for a funding application. Shah’s Kenya Power control-room experience also makes the chosen problem intelligible: she has encountered the institution the product must change. Those are separate advantages—shared history supports alignment, while operating experience supports customer access. Neither establishes full-time commitment, ownership or in-house responsibility for the core technology. Evan Davis’s unconfirmed current role makes the founding structure a concrete diligence question, without proving a founder conflict.

The best objection to passing is that Evardi may already have crossed the institutional boundary that defeats most utility startups. It reports deployments at utilities serving more than 10 million customers across 180,000 miles of line. If those relationships involve sustained engineering use, the company could accumulate valuable network models while competitors are still arranging demonstrations. But the published performance evidence does not establish that interpretation. The 61% inspection hit rate versus 6% from billing flags, and 42% reduction in unsafe storm-drill actions, come from simulations on public data. These are useful experiments; the deployment footprint cannot turn them into measured field outcomes.

Who owns the value if the engineering works? Evardi has a specific workflow, but utility-specific models and operator integration have not yet demonstrated exclusive data, superior retention or another monopoly mechanism. Neara and Bidgely/Grid4C are credible adjacent competitors, although the dossier does not establish that their products are interchangeable with Evardi’s. The founders must show why repeated implementation creates an advantage that compounds for Evardi, rather than additional work for each utility. Meanwhile, the demo request—one feeder map and a year of meter data—is a concrete acquisition route with a substantial delivery obligation. No documented sales cycle or contract economics yet shows whether that obligation is attractive.

Evardi fits my preference for applying technology to neglected physical infrastructure. It does not yet exhibit the operator-network company formation or control of an essential AI production bottleneck seen in my unstated patterns. Nor does the dossier establish the part-time-founder, investor-dominated ownership or synchronized factory-and-licensing red flags. I also checked the exceptions: independently demonstrated technical breakthroughs and persistent live demand can outweigh an uncertain monopoly. Simulated results and an unnamed deployment footprint do not yet supply either override. The inferred licensing model is coherent, but without pricing or margin evidence, the $7.8 billion category forecast supplies no arithmetic for Evardi’s own power-law outcome.

I would pass on writing a check today and keep the engineering question open. The decisive gap is whether Evardi has discovered something competitors cannot readily reproduce. One verifiable condition would reopen my decision: a named utility publishes an independently audited live-feeder comparison showing a material accuracy or safety advantage over its incumbent approach and selects Evardi for production on that result. That would connect the proposed secret to observable customer choice.
