# Agency Tool Company -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: increasingly capable field robots are carrying larger software payloads over unreliable connections, robotics operators now need deployment infrastructure built for heterogeneous fleets, and the controlling variable is whether deployment complexity grows fast enough with fleet size that teams pay for a dedicated system rather than extend Docker, SSH, or an incumbent OTA product. Agency Tool Company has identified that variable, but it has not yet established the threshold. The company reports transfers up to 20 times faster, while Burro, Tempo Works, and Gather AI provide three cross-industry tests of whether speed, resumability, and rollback become budget-owning requirements.

The technology mechanism is credible. Byte-level deltas reduce transferred payloads; interrupted downloads resume; software and device-specific artifacts ship together; fleet targeting, rollback, and deployment history close the operational loop. NVIDIA’s 2025 Jetson Thor increased AI compute 7.5-fold over Orin, while professional-service-robot sales grew 9% in 2024. More compute can produce larger and more frequent releases, making the old deployment stack progressively worse. What remains unproven is the economic conversion: the dossier quantifies transfer speed, not engineering hours eliminated, robot downtime avoided, or gross-margin improvement for customers.

The three private-beta partners are meaningful pre-seed evidence because they operate in agriculture, construction, and logistics rather than one founder-friendly niche. They are not yet product-market fit. There is no disclosed revenue, repeat deployment volume, retention, fleet expansion, or independent validation of the 20-times benchmark. My strongest bull interpretation is that experienced robotics founders have found the same infrastructure failure across three distinct operating environments before polishing pricing. My bear interpretation is sharper: adjacent vendors with $31 million to $59 million of disclosed funding can add delta delivery and resumability, while ATC’s support for standard OCI containers and arbitrary file bundles makes migration easy in both directions.

The durable version of this company therefore cannot stop at faster transport. Deployment targeting, approvals, hardware configuration, rollback state, audit history, and eventually embedded CI must become the system responsible for releasing software safely to physical fleets. Those are real workflow surfaces already visible in the product. But the moat is prospective: no proprietary dataset, difficult integration estate, patent, or demonstrated switching cost exists today. A stronger robotics platform does not erase ATC in the way a stronger model erases a thin AI wrapper, but a broader fleet-management vendor could absorb its current feature set.

Morrison and Foster are the strongest part of the underwriting. They worked together at Occipital, founded Scythe in 2018, deployed hundreds of autonomous mowers, operated through Scythe’s 2026 acquisition by ASI, and then built the infrastructure they say they had needed themselves. That is documented second-time learning and costly exposure to the problem, not inferred founder-market fit from prestigious employers. It raises my probability that the product reflects hidden operational requirements. It cannot, by itself, enlarge a $614 million 2024 category or prove value capture.

I would invest, but narrowly and against a concrete milestone rather than a generic robotics-infrastructure story. The current fleet-management forecast reaches only $1.91 billion by 2032, so Agency Tool Company must either capture a substantial share or expand from deployment into the broader embedded fleet-reliability workflow. My flip condition is verifiable: within twelve months, at least two of the three named beta partners must become paying customers and expand usage across their deployed fleets, with measured downtime or engineering labor reduction. Failure there would indicate that ATC Deploy is a useful feature whose market is being amplified less than its founders’ experience suggests.
