# Marengo -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most revealing fact is the three-month change of noun. In April 2026, Marengo demonstrated software for space-mission design. By August it was selling engineering automation for data centers. The underlying goal—turning simulation-heavy engineering into software—survived, while the market changed materially. That could be running-back behavior. But the dossier never names the observation that forced the turn. Without the rejected hypothesis, customer evidence, or elapsed build cycle, I can give credit for changing routes, not for learning unusually fast.

The team has built real things. Gad Marconi authored the HITEN orbital-dynamics library, and Marengo demonstrated an aerospace product before joining YC. Those are better evidence than diagrams or credentials. But they do not validate the current product. The claims of twice the design speed and half the cost have no disclosed benchmark, completed project, engineering approval, or reliability result. In a domain where tier-four facilities target 99.999% availability, the absence of a measured current-product proof matters. The strongest unstated pattern in my recent checks is a concrete proof spike before the round. Marengo does not yet have one publicly.

The user evidence is similarly suggestive but soft. Marengo says it is working with some of the world’s largest data-center builders, which is more meaningful than its 329 LinkedIn followers or selection as one of 16 Onstage finalists from 1,614 applicants. But the builders are unnamed, and “working with” does not tell me whether anyone used a generated design, paid, returned, or trusted it in a second project. A narrow well may exist here. The dossier does not show users pulling the crude product back.

The founders did encounter the broader problem organically. Their physics, control-systems, aerospace, and simulation work plausibly led them to engineering-design automation rather than to a manufactured “AI for construction” thesis. The data-center wedge also contains abundant schlep: BIM integration, redundant-system validation, coordination with engineers, and eventual approval liability. But neither founder is documented as having designed data centers, and Marengo has disclosed no Revit, ArchiCAD, CAD, or document-management integration. The schlep is visible; their willingness and ability to carry it is not yet proved.

The bull case stings. A three-person technical team moved from orbital-design tooling into a $21.93 billion design-services market while hyperscale capital expenditure exceeded $400 billion. If “working with large builders” means the founders are already sitting beside engineers and manually converting live projects, the vertical change may be rapid customer discovery rather than flailing. Their prior artifact could also make public benchmarks lag actual product capability. This is exactly the sort of awkward industrial software that can look unimpressive until it becomes indispensable.

But I invest in demonstrated founder motion, not the charitable interpretation of missing facts. Marengo has not named a blocker and shipped workaround, and neither current technical proof nor user pull clears the operational gate. The usual exceptions require a working product with unmistakable use or independently validated performance; finalist selection, followers, and unnamed builder work do not substitute for those. There is also a red-flag-shaped distribution risk: one or two large builders could be mistaken for a market before their owner, timetable, deployment, and repeat use are known. I am passing now. The verifiable flip condition is a named builder publishing a paid second-project deployment with independently approved designs and measured cycle-time improvement.
