# Dialogus -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the interesting future is not that contact centers employ fewer people. It is that a caller can authorize a machine to negotiate a payment, change an order, touch several systems, and leave behind evidence that an auditor and a human operator can replay. Dialogus has a small leak from that world: thousands of production calls for Fortune 500 enterprises, with Papa Johns, KFC, and Totalplay displayed as users. But the dossier never works backward from that future. It tells me what realtime models made possible in 2026, not which institutional transitions Dialogus must own over the next six years.

The working artifact is the strongest evidence. Enterprises are permitting this three-person company to recognize customers, read live records, issue vouchers, submit orders, and transfer exceptions with context. That is costly behavior: a bad call can corrupt a CRM, mishandle a collection, or lose a customer. Policy-limited tools, audit logs, SOC 2 compliance, and replayable regression scenarios show respect for the hidden operations that separate a demo from production. Thousands of calls are more persuasive than a waitlist, although the absence of resolution rates, error rates, contract values, and retention prevents me from knowing whether customers are merely experimenting or reorganizing work around the product.

Why is this system expensive? Labor, queues, and fragmented software are real wounds, but Dialogus has not isolated the broken rule beneath them. I can see who receives efficiency—the enterprise—but not whose agency is restored or which artificial rent disappears. Collections makes this especially sharp: automating payment negotiations may improve availability, yet the dossier does not explain whether consumers gain control or whether the existing creditor relationship simply becomes cheaper to enforce.

The founders can clearly build. Hans Ibarra worked on Gemini, Gmail, and YouTube; Rodrigo Terán Hernández interned at Meta, Microsoft, and Google; the team has already crossed enterprise security and integration barriers. What I cannot find is the dated pre-company obsession: the strange prototype, operating wound, or independently pursued artifact that explains why these three people had to build voice infrastructure. Deepwave-cli exists, but zero stars and no documented connection to the company’s origin cannot carry that burden. Juberth Rodriguez is identified as CEO, which avoids an obvious accountability problem, though the dossier provides no founder-history or governance evidence beyond the title.

The painful bear case is that Dialogus is an excellent integration team standing on rented ground. OpenAI supplies SIP calling, realtime speech, and tool use; Salesforce combines telephony, CRM data, AI agents, and human handoffs; Sierra already reports more than $150 million ARR. Dialogus may accumulate valuable failure traces, but today that moat is inferred, while its model dependency and customer-specific integrations are concrete. Mexican Spanish and constrained workflows could become a durable operating wedge, yet they could also strand the company as a services-heavy regional implementer.

I pass today—not because the product is imaginary, but because its strongest proof is execution inside the current wave while my two primary priors, a mapped next-wave future and first-believer founder evidence, remain thin. The verifiable flip condition is twelve months of cohort evidence showing at least three named enterprises expanding call volume after deployment while Dialogus’s proprietary regression system cuts critical action-error rates by at least 50%. That would show that production failures are becoming a controlled asset rather than integration tuition paid on someone else’s platform.
