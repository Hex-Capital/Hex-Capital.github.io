# Spectre Intelligence -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have crossed the line from describing AI traders to letting them trade real capital. Michelle Li brings biology research and personal trading experience to the biotech pod; Joshua Zyzak brings reinforcement learning, AI engineering, and Optiver exposure to the trading system. Two live sector pods and claimed profitability are meaningful pre-seed evidence of action. The missing evidence is attribution: no return series, drawdown, AUM, or audited result shows whether the founders have created repeatable alpha rather than observed a favorable short run.

The possible durable asset is not the agent code. It is the growing record of decisions, reasoning traces, trades, and ground-truth P&L. That dataset already exists in principle because the pods are live, but Spectre Intelligence discloses neither its size nor evidence that models improve as it compounds. Abundance, Nof1, Numerai, and established quantitative funds can access similar foundation models, market data, and execution infrastructure. Spectre also admits that successful strategies will be copied and their alpha will decay. A feedback loop is only a moat when it improves faster than competitors can reproduce the output.

The two initial sectors provide a plausible path into additional strategies, but the dossier substitutes the hedge-fund industry’s $5.6 trillion of capital for a demonstrated expansion mechanism. Semiconductor and biotech expertise may transfer into additional research-heavy equity pods; it does not automatically transfer into credit, commodities, macro, or other asset classes. Large capital pools are not the same as a large accessible market. Capital follows verified returns, and those are precisely what remain undisclosed.

Spectre’s compensated expert network resembles a permissionless contribution system only from a distance. Experts provide inputs to the firm; the dossier does not show that one expert improves the experience, distribution, or economics for another participant. Unlike Numerai’s external model contributors, Spectre keeps model competition inside the company and must continue recruiting, compensating, and evaluating specialists. This is proprietary production, not yet a customer-created network.

The strongest bull case is that requiring verification now misses the unusual pre-seed signal: two students already have multiple agents trading real money, claim profit, and generate proprietary labels while competitors are still publicizing laboratories and benchmarks. A strange working system can deserve time before its distribution is legible. But the known-exception logic does not remove the critical distinction between an observable self-reinforcing behavior and an unverified performance claim. Nor do Spectre’s unstated-pattern tests rescue it: the expert supply is managed rather than permissionless, and no participant loop compounds independently of company labor. I see no disclosed integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-morale red flag; the structural red flag is that the company itself says its alpha is copyable and decays.

I would pass today, while keeping the file open. Spectre Intelligence may become a profitable boutique trading firm, but the current evidence does not yet establish a venture-scale compounding asset. One verifiable condition flips the decision: twelve consecutive months of third-party-administered results disclosing AUM, net benchmark-relative returns, and maximum drawdown. If the agents produce durable net alpha through different market regimes, the trading record becomes evidence of specific knowledge and the labeled dataset becomes more than a moat narrative.
