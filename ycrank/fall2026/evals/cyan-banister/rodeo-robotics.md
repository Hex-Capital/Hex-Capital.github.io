# Rodeo Robotics -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

The interesting object here is the instrumented hand tool. By 2032, I can imagine fleet vehicles arriving overnight, receiving routine maintenance, and returning to work while technicians handle exceptions. Getting there requires demonstrations that transfer across vehicle configurations, followed by steadily less human intervention. Rodeo Robotics describes collecting those demonstrations through tool use and teleoperation ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/rodeo-robotics)). That is a concrete beginning to a future I care about. Whether each assisted oil change actually makes the next one more autonomous remains unverified.

Why does an oil change keep a vehicle waiting? Rodeo identifies scarce technician labor competing across maintenance jobs, compounded by fragmented service information. Automating routine work could return technicians’ attention to difficult repairs and give fleet operators more usable vehicle hours. But the dossier establishes a capacity problem more clearly than an institutional rent. There is no quantified delay, identified gatekeeper, or demonstrated transfer of occupational control. Human assistance also makes the economics unusually important: Rodeo could move the labor into teleoperation without removing enough of it.

Michael Hazard’s four years at Applied Intuition and Colby Duke’s neural-simulation technical leadership give the team relevant experience with vehicles, autonomy, and testing. Having met at Applied Intuition also provides a substantive working connection ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/rodeo-robotics)). I give those facts weight. I cannot turn them into evidence of a longstanding maintenance obsession, independently shipped robotics experiments, or an ability to recruit technicians into an unfamiliar operating model. The dossier establishes useful technical context; the first-believer story is still missing.

The strongest argument for investing is that assisted service could finance the learning needed for automation. Oil changes provide a bounded starting task, and Rodeo acknowledges vehicle variation rather than pretending the operating complexity disappears. That fits my pattern of finding hidden infrastructure inside an ordinary business. The pre-revenue exception represented by **CosmicBrain AI** is relevant: both bets concern physical automation before revenue becomes informative, but that exception included a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept. Rodeo’s dossier documents neither comparable technical proof nor completed customer behavior. Its reported $500,000 financing and October 13 launch coverage do not establish either.

I also cannot yet underwrite the enabling layer. Task-specific demonstrations might become a valuable maintenance asset, but dataset scale, exclusivity, and learning gains are unverified. PitPro’s tire automation and UVeye’s inspections show adjacent activity; they do not prove Rodeo controls the difficult dependency beneath those services. On my red-flag checks, the dossier identifies a CEO and CTO and supplies no affirmative evidence of governance confusion, severe privacy problems, or dependence on one closed model supplier. Those concerns do not drive my decision. The unresolved issue is whether this becomes accumulating technical capability or a service business carrying expensive robotic equipment.

I pass today. Missing revenue is ordinary at this stage; the missing connection between assisted work and improving autonomy is central to this particular bet. My one verifiable flip condition is a paid fleet pilot showing, across a documented mix of vehicles, that repeated oil changes reduce human minutes per completed job while maintaining service quality and positive contribution margin after assistance costs. That would connect the tool, the learning loop, and the economics. Right now, Rodeo Robotics has described that connection more clearly than it has demonstrated it.
