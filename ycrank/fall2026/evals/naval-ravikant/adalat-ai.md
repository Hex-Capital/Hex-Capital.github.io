# Adalat AI -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Adalat AI’s founders have acquired specific knowledge through action. Saxena practiced law inside the system he wants to improve; Bhattacharya worked on multilingual NLP. They connected in February 2024, built an MVP for court demonstrations, and reached deployments independently documented across more than 4,000 courts by March 2026. That is a substantial founder signal. My obstacle to investing is the destination of the value they create: the documented operating model provides software free through a nonprofit, with no demonstrated mechanism for an equity investor to participate in the upside.

The strongest argument against passing is that Adalat AI has already crossed the hardest institutional barrier. Kerala required its use for witness depositions effective November 1, 2025. A magistrate reports handling five to six witnesses daily against two to three previously. The company subsequently reported 6,300-plus courtrooms across 11 states. These are meaningful adoption and utility signals. They make a compelling case that the founders can improve judicial productivity. They do not establish that adoption produces distributable earnings; the $500,000 Agency Fund award is grant financing.

Copying the software would not instantly reproduce the team’s court relationships. But the dossier does not establish contractual exclusivity, proprietary training-data rights, or a model whose performance compounds uniquely with deployment. Kerala also permits approved alternatives during technical difficulties, and TERES and jhana already overlap the product’s transcription and workflow layers. I give the absent-evidence default for a durable asset. The September 2026 production-administration compromise adds a concrete reliability concern. The reported remediation and joint incident disclosure are useful evidence of response; the incident alone supplies no basis for an integrity veto.

The expansion path is understandable: speech recognition leads into document processing and case-flow management for the same institutions. The adjacent court-case-management market is estimated at $2.17 billion in 2026. That establishes relevant spend, but only modest evidence of how Adalat AI captures adjacent demand. My recurring preference for converting fragmented work into scalable systems is present. My preference for permissionless contribution is weak here: expansion requires institutional authorization and training. Nor does the dossier show one court making the product measurably more useful for another. Courtroom count is deployment scale, not evidence of a network effect.

I have accepted unresolved monetization when an emerging network or an exceptional, capital-efficient prototype supplied the overriding reason. Those exceptions do not resolve documented pro bono agreements and an explicitly philanthropic operating model. Adalat AI could become important public infrastructure while remaining unsuitable for my personal angel capital. My 36-month expectation is that it remains predominantly grant-funded, with less than $1 million in annual recurring commercial revenue. Successful court expansion would be entirely consistent with that forecast.

I pass on an equity investment. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: audited evidence that a legally investable Adalat entity earns recurring gross profit from paying deployments while honoring its existing pro bono obligations. That would establish a financial participation mechanism without requiring me to invent one from social impact.
