# Voyager -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a filmmaker describing a scene, leaving an agent to assemble footage and sound, and returning to a project she can actually edit. The important object is the editable project. Whoever controls that can change their mind. Voyager’s desktop application control and model routing are small, concrete pieces of that future already shipping. But reliable execution across applications and dependable creative revision still have to follow. Windows remains alpha, and Linux lacks computer control. I see the beginning of a path, with considerable distance left to travel.

The founders are the strongest reason to keep looking. Pai and Holliman have already worked together at Dover; Parikh co-founded Heap and Airplane, both acquired. Building Moda’s design agent gives this team relevant experience beyond impressive employer names. Parikh’s touring-musician background adds contact with creative work. These are people who have persuaded others to build and have carried software businesses through consequential transitions. What I cannot establish is whether this particular problem reflects a sustained, independently developed obsession. The March Moda launch followed by October’s Voyager launch does not establish opportunism, either. I would leave that question open.

Why does a filmmaker have to become the integration department? Voyager identifies a real coordination burden between storyboards, generation providers, sound, and editing. Yet the dossier does not establish who extracts an artificial rent, how much labor the handoffs consume, or which restriction disappears. Its own subscriptions run from $19 to $500 monthly, plus credit purchases. Whether creators recover more time and control than they spend managing another tool remains unmeasured. This gives me less conviction than a product that demonstrably changes who can earn a living or own a customer relationship.

The strongest case against my hesitation is that experienced builders could turn this coordination layer into the place creative work accumulates. Proprietary editors and reusable skills give Voyager something it controls, spanning design, video, and music. That fits my recurring interest in infrastructure concealed beneath a visible application. Multiple providers and customer-supplied credentials also weaken the concern that the business merely borrows access from one closed supplier. But the editors are not yet a demonstrated scarce dependency. Runway and other studios can expand into orchestration; Voyager needs a reason creative work stays here as those capabilities spread.

The missing evidence is someone changing their life around this product. Moda’s 100,000-plus users belong to Moda. Voyager’s October 8 launch, download repository, and published prices establish availability, without establishing repeated expenditure by creators. The founder-reported $16 million establishes financing, not product attachment. I can invest before revenue when a functioning prototype is accompanied by unusually deep founder obsession and concrete commitments from users. That exception does not resolve the evidence here. Nor does the dossier establish a governance violation or severe privacy problem; desktop access does make consent and custody consequential diligence questions.

I would stay interested and keep my money for now. Voyager has credible builders and a plausible route toward creative infrastructure, but I cannot yet see the user behavior that makes this particular implementation necessary. My verifiable flip condition is a dated cohort of ten independent filmmakers completing at least three paid client projects each through Voyager over eight weeks, with project records showing repeated cross-application execution and continued editing of the delivered files. That would turn a promising architectural idea into an observed working habit.
