# Peer -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and freight coordination may be mostly machine-operated: pricing, carrier selection, tracking, and routine exceptions disappear into software while humans handle the genuinely strange failures. But Peer has not mapped the transitions required to reach that world or shown the small artifact leaking from it today. The dossier instead begins with the present wave—voice and text agents became deployable in 2025–2026—and a product that pivoted from screen-recorded workflow automation in May 2026 to freight brokerage by August. That looks more like following the wave than discovering what the wave enables next.

The founders are the strongest part. Aaron Zhang previously built voice agents for trucking fleets at Waylens and researched agents and computer vision at MIT Media Lab. Felix Peng shipped internal AI tools used by more than 250 LADWP employees. They attended Harvey Mudd, ran the Claremont Accelerator, and won a DeepMind hackathon together. That is real technical production and a useful working relationship. It is not yet pre-company obsession with freight brokerage: neither founder is documented operating a brokerage, and the recent pivot weakens the claim that this particular system wound pulled them into the company.

The irritating system is visible but not yet decoded. Phone calls, email, quoting, tracking, and exception handling are certainly messy, yet Peer does not quantify the delay or cost, identify who captures the rent, or show whose agency materially increases. The FMCSA’s $75,000 bond requirement is a concrete rule, but Peer must comply with it; the product does not remove it for customers or carriers. A brokerage earning the conventional per-load spread may automate the incumbent mechanism without changing who controls the relationship.

The bear case has a sharp bull case inside it. If Peer operates actual loads, every quote, carrier outcome, and exception could become proprietary operational data. Zhang’s trucking-agent experience and Peng’s deployed systems experience could let two people learn unusually fast. But no current shipment, repeat shipper, carrier relationship, brokerage authority, or reliability result is disclosed. FleetWorks already reports 10,000 carriers and more than 15 top-100 broker relationships, while Cartage combines automation with human escalation. Peer presently controls neither the network nor the lower-stack bottleneck.

I looked for the kind of exception that can justify investing before revenue: a functioning artifact, signed proof of concept, unusually costly user behavior, or an obsession that clearly predates the market. The only documented usage belongs to Peer’s prior workflow-automation product. There is no comparable evidence for freight. Nor is there an odd artifact discovered outside fundraising, a concealed infrastructure asset beneath the visible application, or a differentiated behavior worth protecting. The red flag is not simply “no traction”; that is normal at pre-seed. It is a consensus AI application, entered through a recent pivot, with no disclosed controlled asset.

My 36-month expectation is that Peer either shuts down or retreats from full-stack brokerage into voice and exception-handling software for incumbent brokers, remaining below $1 million ARR. I would reopen the decision upon one verifiable result: 100 paid loads completed for at least three repeat shippers within 90 days, under documented FMCSA authority and bond coverage, with published on-time and human-escalation rates. That would prove a working operational organism rather than an attractive automation diagram. Until then, I pass.
