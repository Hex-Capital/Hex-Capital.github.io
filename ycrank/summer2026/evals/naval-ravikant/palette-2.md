# Palette -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have real technical contact with the problem, but not yet costly commercial knowledge. Serena Pei researched diffusion models and multimodal systems across CSAIL, the Media Lab, and LIDS; Josephine Lee built AI-music and software projects; together they shipped a public studio and API. That is stronger than résumé theater. It is still below proof that they have repeatedly produced media for demanding customers, sold creative workflows, or learned through retained usage. They know the machinery better than the buyer—for now.

Palette’s central weakness is structural. Its routing layer reports 35% faster generation, 50% lower cost, and 130-plus regression tests, but those benchmarks prove execution, not durability. The company depends on models from Google, ByteDance, Kling, MiniMax, and others that may be substituted or retired. Runway and Luma already combine proprietary and third-party models inside creative workspaces. If a competitor copies the interface and routing logic, Palette has disclosed no exclusive data, proprietary model, hardware, rights, or accumulated user network that remains.

The market offers room to maneuver, although the path is only partially drawn. Palette can enter through individual creators and extend the same multimodal context system into marketing, operations, and learning-and-development teams. The $788.5 million AI-video-generator market growing toward $3.44 billion supports expanding demand, but it does not establish Palette’s enterprise workflow segment. “All creative work in one conversation” is broad product surface; it is not yet a causal map from one urgent buyer to a much larger adjacent transaction layer.

The economics are legible but unproven. Individuals can buy credits at $0.01 each, while enterprises receive custom quotes. No paying customer, recurring revenue, renewal, completed enterprise engagement, or unit economics appears in the dossier. Likewise, one creator’s prompts, characters, and storyboards do not currently improve the product for another creator. Customer-specific context may create private workflow lock-in, but that is a future possibility—not a demonstrated network or proprietary-data asset.

The strongest opposing case is the known exception for capital-efficient technical founders who have already built a strange, integrated product before its category becomes obvious. Palette has the working product, two unusually capable builders, and a genuine usability insight: model proliferation makes orchestration valuable. But the material difference from Notion is that Palette’s underlying capabilities are rented from rapidly improving suppliers while heavily financed competitors already own models and orchestration surfaces. The legal documents still identifying themselves as starter templates, despite enterprise positioning and customer-media processing, reinforce the gap between a sophisticated demo and a trusted system of record. I see no integrity or artificial-scarcity red flag, but I also see none of the permissionless contribution loops that would override the copyability problem.

My 36-month base case is that Palette abandons the horizontal “AI-native media platform” position, narrows into enterprise brand-workflow automation, and remains below $3 million ARR as model vendors absorb generic routing. I pass today. I would flip if a cohort of at least ten enterprises renewed contracts above $100,000 annually while accumulated customer workflow data produced a measured advantage—such as at least 20% lower cost or higher accepted-output rates than a fresh account. Then the history would be an asset rather than a feature.
