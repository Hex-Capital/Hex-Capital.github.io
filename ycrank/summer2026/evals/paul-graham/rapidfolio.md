# Rapidfolio -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Naveen Qureshi sold Sable in 2022 and then built credit products at Seen with Frederik Vanhevel, making **Rapidfolio** a strong repeat-founder pair whose weakest signal is hacker genealogy. **Rapidfolio** looks less like two hackers discovering something strange and more like two experienced fintech operators returning to a schlep they know unusually well. That is still attractive. A pair that has already built and exited together is much harder to manufacture than a clever AI workflow.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for co-founder configuration: two brothers with years of shared context, complementary abilities, and no need to negotiate trust while building. Qureshi and Vanhevel worked together at Sable and then Seen before founding **Rapidfolio**, which puts **Rapidfolio** near that rung rather than among pairs assembled for an application. The divergence is technical intensity. The Collisons had an unmistakable hacker trail before Stripe; Rapidfolio has a durable working relationship, but not yet evidence of that same compulsive inventiveness.

**Loopt** (2005) is the low-rung warning on hacker genealogy: force of personality can justify an exception, but it is dangerous when conviction outruns a technical artifact. Qureshi’s Columbia MBA and Capital One résumé would be a red flag if that were all **Rapidfolio** had. It is not. Vanhevel was Sable’s founding engineer and built Seen’s infrastructure and credit-decisioning engine, while **Rapidfolio** already claims deterministic workflows and 30+ integrations. But neither founder has a public repository or hacker-community trail. The code may be real; the hacker genealogy remains mostly inferred.

Sable’s 2022 exit, live U.S. and Canadian customers, SOC 2 work, and 30+ integrations give **Rapidfolio** more animal tracks than most pre-seed companies. Over the next 18 months, this dimension compounds if Qureshi and Vanhevel personally turn ugly customer procedures into working production systems faster than larger competitors can sell templates. The Forward Deployed Engineer opening suggests **Rapidfolio** is already doing the unscalable work. What is missing is the cereal-box moment: no specific rejection overcome, impossible integration completed, or customer won through an ingenious stunt. Resourceful, probably; animal, not yet proven.

Qureshi led product and operations at Capital One, then Qureshi and Vanhevel built Sable’s back-office systems and Seen’s credit infrastructure before starting **Rapidfolio**. That is a genuinely organic idea: **Rapidfolio** is machinery they appear to have wanted inside their previous companies. The score would fall a full tier if customer interviews revealed that Rapidfolio began as a generic search for an agentic-AI market and the Sable connection was merely retrospective storytelling. The documented sequence points the other way.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the closest parallel on the schlep dimension. Compliance work looks like a liability until one notices that everyone else is avoiding it. **Rapidfolio** must handle identity documents, sanctions reviews, underwriting decisions, audit exports, tenant isolation, human approvals, and customer-specific integrations; **Rapidfolio** cannot merely wrap a model and hope. The claimed 99.9% accuracy is unverified, but the unpleasantness is genuine. The Forward Deployed Engineer requirement is not just implementation risk. It is where the knowledge accumulates.

Live customer outcomes of an 83% queue reduction, onboarding reviews falling from three days to under two minutes, and $350K in projected savings lead me to predict that **Rapidfolio** reaches $8–12M ARR within 36 months while retaining a substantial forward-deployed engineering operation. **Rapidfolio** will grow by turning institution-specific procedures into tested workflows, not by winning a generic AI-agent contest. The strong founder pair should sell and deploy the product; the weaker hacker signature makes a frictionless self-serve platform less likely.

Bretton’s $95.3M, WorkFusion’s $410M, Sphinx’s browser-native onboarding, and Arva’s entity intelligence form the strongest bear case: **Rapidfolio** could become a services-heavy integrator in a crowded category while its claimed 99.9% accuracy collapses under independent measurement. That objection stings because **Rapidfolio** has no disclosed revenue, retention, benchmark methodology, or structural barrier, and the Forward Deployed Engineer role could mean every customer requires bespoke labor. The facts that win for now are the repeat founder relationship, the prior Sable exit, live customers, and unusually concrete operational outcomes. I would flip to Pass if, by July 2027, Rapidfolio cannot show at least three customers running two or more recurring production workflows without continuous forward-deployed intervention.
