# Telamont -- Paul Graham Evaluation

What happened when Charles Goulet and William Gagnon got stuck? That is the fact I most want about Telamont, and the dossier doesn’t contain it. There is considerable activity: 220-plus product updates, ten-plus integrations, and 300-plus client touchpoints. But none identifies a particular obstacle, the founders’ response, and what changed afterward. Counting updates cannot tell me whether these founders are unusually resourceful. Nor does it establish that they changed direction when a customer disproved an assumption. Both questions remain unanswered.

The problem is well chosen. Goulet has construction-family exposure and prior work at EBC and RGI Electrical; Gagnon worked on data and AI at Poka. These are concrete connections to the world they are entering, though neither biography establishes the particular incident that produced the idea. Construction documents also contain precisely the sort of ugly work people overlook: reconciling revisions, interpreting contractual obligations, and getting notices approved before a deadline. Telamont could become valuable by patiently doing that work with contractors. Its [construction-specific playbooks and professional feedback](https://www.telamont.com/blog/why-we-built-telamont-document-problem-construction-disputes) make this more credible than a generic document chatbot.

There is some product evidence. The [Autodesk listing](https://marketplace.autodesk.com/apps/d25166ba-2e85-4817-8f59-8c22d91b6e9f), integrations, and described review workflows support provisional credit for a built product. They don’t establish the difficult claim: that continuous checking reliably catches consequential conflicts across drawings, contracts, and codes. Telamont acknowledges omissions and requires human review. That is sensible, but the decisive measurement is whether it saves reviewers work after they investigate its alerts. Advertised capabilities alone cannot answer that. My preference for teams that produce concrete technical proof before a round is only partly satisfied here.

The strongest argument against passing is that contractors may already be answering the question through use. Telamont names Canmec, TCI+, and other customers, and reports outcomes including $1.4 million in avoided rework. One genuine catch could justify a substantial subscription. That is a much stronger possibility than its “billions” in project coverage, which measures the underlying projects rather than demand for the software. But the savings claims concern unnamed clients, and the named logos lack evidence of repeated use or payment. [Commercial terms](https://www.telamont.com/terms-of-service) establish an offering, not realized revenue. These signals earn partial demand credit; they don’t yet supply the unmistakable pull that would justify an exception to my founder-evidence gate.

I would pass on this evidence. The exceptions in my framework require a compelling working artifact, independently validated proof, or overwhelming user behavior; none is established strongly enough here. There is also no basis to accuse these founders of rigidity, a hastily assembled relationship, or dependence on a major partner. Recruiting salespeople raises the question of whether the founders have learned to sell personally, but doesn’t answer it adversely. The competitive pressure is concrete: Trimble can put overlapping functionality inside existing workflows. Telamont’s opportunity is to learn a narrow contractor’s problem faster than that distribution advantage matters. One verifiable flip condition would be a customer-confirmed deployment history showing a named blocker, a founder-shipped correction within weeks, and subsequent repeated paid use across projects. That would reveal the learning engine behind the update count.
