# Ryhla -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Eliminating eight bad battery projects within days may be more valuable than accelerating ten applications. Ryhla reports saving a developer approximately $160,000 in fees by doing exactly that. The founders have also reportedly researched grid models at Stanford and deployed them with utilities. That is specific knowledge acquired through action. Their mathematics and machine-learning credentials matter less than having put predictions in front of people who bear the cost of being wrong.

The investment turns on who owns the learning from those decisions. Ryhla describes proprietary distribution-grid data and models, but the dossier establishes neither exclusive access nor an asset that compounds with each deployment. Its reported 91% utility approval rate among model-approved 2025 applications is encouraging operating evidence. Without sample size, a comparison against ordinary screening, or results across utilities, it does not establish a durable predictive advantage. A competitor copying the code must still lack something important. I cannot identify that something confidently here.

The strongest argument for investing is that Ryhla has already crossed from research into consequential decisions. An unnamed Midwest solar developer is using the product, and rejecting unsuitable projects can create value before a connection is completed. Solar and battery screening also offers a plausible route into charging and data-center siting through the same capacity, timing, and upgrade-cost questions. DOE’s June 2026 assessment supports the existence of the adjacent constraint. But distribution-grid knowledge will not necessarily transfer to every large-load project, and Paces already combines development diligence with interconnection studies. Enverus owns simulation capabilities through its Pearl Street acquisition. The market offers room to maneuver; it also contains competitors with existing data and customer relationships.

Ryhla fits my preference for turning individual knowledge into scalable output. It has not demonstrated my stronger pattern of customers creating value for other customers. Another developer currently adds a project; the dossier does not show that this improves anyone else’s predictions or acquisition. Utilities retain approval authority, so the product helps customers navigate a permissioned system. Managing interconnection could secure valuable outcome data, but it could also require substantial work on every account. The $160,000 belongs to the customer’s savings calculation. There is no disclosed price, payment, or revenue establishing Ryhla’s share.

I would make exceptions for an unfinished business model or an unproven distribution loop when demonstrated founder ability and an unusual product make the upside compelling. Ryhla’s deployment evidence gives that argument substance. There is no documented integrity or financing-pressure red flag to veto it. Still, the exception does not resolve whether these founders are building an accumulating asset or a capable interconnection service. My present expectation is a useful, specialist business before a self-reinforcing platform. I would hold off on a personal check. One verifiable result would change that: a held-out comparison showing that legally reusable outcomes from earlier customers materially improve approval predictions for later customers over public-data screening. That would demonstrate the compounding mechanism I am currently being asked to assume.
