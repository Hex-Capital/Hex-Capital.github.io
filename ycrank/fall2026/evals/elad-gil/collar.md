# Collar -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI can now prepare earnings materials and maintain investor histories for public-company IR teams; the economic question is whether Collar captures a recurring software budget or supplies features inside an incumbent’s existing contract. Q4 launched overlapping AI CRM capabilities in April 2026. Collar’s controlling belief therefore has to be that live, source-linked earnings assistance is valuable enough to make issuers move their relationship workflow. The dossier establishes a plausible opening, but supplies no evidence of that purchasing behavior. I would pass with my own money today.

The market boundary matters. The cited estimate puts global IR software at $712 million in 2025, growing 9.3% annually. Q4 reported $56.4 million ARR in September 2023, demonstrating that meaningful budgets exist, although its suite includes IR websites. Collar could build a substantial company by expanding what issuers buy. Its reported 40 hours saved monthly and 50% reduction in Q&A preparation time provide a concrete mechanism: automate work that previously consumed staff time. I give those claims partial credit because they are company-reported and lack a measurement methodology. They demonstrate a possible productivity gain, without establishing higher willingness to pay or a larger software market.

The strongest argument for investing is the product’s scope. Collar connects filings, guidance, conversations, and holdings data; it also retains relationship histories and advertises approved-source restrictions. Those are useful foundations for an owned workflow. Better models could improve answers while customer-specific context remains inside Collar. This fits my preference for applications that perform institutional work. However, public materials do not establish how completely the product executes that work or how difficult replacement would be. Mapping 12,000 portfolio managers does not establish a data moat without exclusivity or a credible reproduction-cost estimate.

The customer evidence could be more significant than it initially looks. IR partners representing $300 billion in combined market capitalization suggest access to consequential buyers, and the reported preparation savings suggest a specific use case worth testing. But market capitalization measures the partners’ scale, not their commitment to Collar. There are no disclosed current-product contract values, renewal cohorts, or named deployments. The prior diligence product’s advertised $1,000 monthly price cannot establish IR revenue. With September reporting still describing private-fund workflows and October materials describing issuer IR, I would treat this as a recently changed commercial hypothesis rather than transfer validation across products.

Koh and Yau bring relevant private-credit, asset-management, and prior founding experience. That supports familiarity with financial workflows, but the dossier does not document lessons from those earlier startups or costly commitment sufficient to establish my founder-specific conviction. My pattern of investing across a technology transition does favor examining Collar; my preference for reusable distribution remains unproven because the diligence-to-IR transition does not establish continuity of buyer access. The exceptions that justify investing before demand—scarce frontier research talent or an incumbent abandoning a category—also do not apply on this evidence. Q4 is actively entering the workflow. Collar’s limited disclosed traction is understandable at pre-seed; the competitive opening still needs affirmative proof.

One result would change my decision: a verified cohort of six public-company issuers replacing an incumbent IR workflow with Collar and renewing at an annual contract value of at least $50,000 after two earnings cycles. That is a proposed underwriting threshold, not a claimed industry benchmark. It would directly test whether live assistance creates enough value to transfer budget and workflow ownership. Until then, the product architecture is more convincing than the market-entry mechanism.
