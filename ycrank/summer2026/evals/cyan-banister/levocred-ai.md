# Levocred AI -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and credit committees should not be assembling a picture of risk from emailed spreadsheets, stale SharePoint files, and somebody’s private Python script. Loan tapes, covenants, servicer reports, and bank movements should reconcile continuously; humans should spend their time deciding which risks to own. I can see that future. Levocred AI has not shown me the path to it. The dossier names today’s model capabilities and current workflow, but not the intervening institutional changes or a controlled artifact that makes the future inevitable.

The strongest evidence is wonderfully unglamorous: approximately $1 billion in receivables already runs through the product across multiple credit teams. These customers are not clicking on a demo for amusement. They are supplying sensitive agreements, bank feeds, and portfolio data; Pier Asset Management reports spending less time on portfolio management and understanding its facilities more deeply. Paying revenue, even undisclosed, confirms that Levocred is a working artifact rather than a credit-AI rendering.

Mohit and Saksham also paid real operating tuition before starting the company. They worked together for roughly three years at Edge Focus, where Mohit built AI, trading, and lending infrastructure and Saksham helped scale data systems as assets grew from $150 million to more than $1 billion. That is much better than discovering private credit through a market report. It is evidence of a wound carried out of the prior workplace, although the dossier does not yet show earlier independent experiments, unusual recruiting ability, or a pre-market obsession extending beyond their employment.

What is the rent? Manual reconciliation consumes analyst time, and version confusion can corrupt decisions, but the dossier never quantifies that cost or identifies an institutional rule trapping users inside it. Levocred makes credit professionals more efficient; it does not yet demonstrably return ownership, pricing power, or occupational control to someone excluded by a gatekeeper. That matters because F2 already spans screening through monitoring, Chronograph is entering private credit with a $140 million growth investment, and Rogo has raised more than $300 million. Without a deeper system change, this can become a very useful feature bundle inside somebody else’s platform.

The painful countercase is that the founders’ facility-level context becomes the system of record before those larger competitors notice the narrow workflow seams. A two-person team already processing $1 billion and earning revenue could accumulate covenant histories and decision context that generic document products cannot reproduce. But that asset is still hypothetical: Levocred has not said whether customer data produces a proprietary model, remains customer-isolated, or can even be retained. Today the integrations create switching friction, not control of a scarce enabling layer.

My harder gate is custody. Levocred ingests precisely the material that should make an investor ask annoying questions about consent, retention, deployment boundaries, and unauthorized model training. No public security certification or data architecture answers them, while the product also drafts investment-committee memos and checks compliance without disclosed accuracy benchmarks. The existing usage is strong enough to keep me curious, but not exceptional enough to override that gate. I would not write the check yet. One condition flips me: five unaffiliated credit teams renewing six-figure annual contracts after completing documented security and model-data audits.
