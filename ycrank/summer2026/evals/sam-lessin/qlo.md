# Qlo -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first three numbers I need are missing: proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely the first scarce check. For this company, that is not administrative detail. AI underwriting is already a financed consensus category: Federato has raised more than $180 million, Sixfold $51.5 million, and Kalepa $16 million. Without round terms, I cannot tell whether I am buying an overlooked workflow wedge or paying YC-auction pricing for an obvious market.

Qlo does contain a legitimate venture experiment, but the financing is not yet attached to it. The two-week setup and 30-day paid pilot create a finite parity test: compile the customer’s Excel rater, reproduce approved pricing logic, and determine whether the system can move submissions from broker email through issuance without corrupting judgment or documents. That is unusually crisp. But the dossier never says what the round funds, what dated result the capital must produce, or why the experiment cannot be financed through customer payments. A good product-level test is not automatically a capital-level venture moment.

The strongest evidence is inside the workflow. Distinguished Programs reports roughly 3× primary-quote throughput, and the first enterprise customer uses Qlo for daily quoting. This is not follower theater; an underwriter repeatedly entrusting live pricing work to the product is observable behavior. Customer-specific raters, source references, and submission-through-renewal records could become a proprietary dark pool because they encode approved decisions rather than public insurance knowledge. The problem is that one deployment does not yet prove retention or defensibility. Federato, Sixfold, and Kalepa can attack the same workflow, while Qlo’s two-week co-building process may turn proprietary configuration into services burden rather than software leverage.

The team has real receipts. Ryan Hu spent approximately 2.5 years across MGA, carrier, and broker workflows and previously built a commercial-insurance marketplace within a product studio that reached roughly $500,000 ARR and received acquisition term sheets. Liv Huang has shipped software at CuboAi and can build the automation layer. Together they span insurance operations and engineering better than credential-only founders, although the advisors’ underwriting experience cannot substitute for founder-owned operating access. The translation-layer pattern is present: preserve the PAS and underwriter’s judgment while replacing the manual movement among email, spreadsheets, raters, and policy documents.

The bull case stings because the usual AI-wrapper critique may be wrong here. Qlo is executing customer-controlled rating logic, not asking a foundation model to invent underwriting judgment, and daily enterprise use plus 3× throughput is meaningful at pre-seed. But the known-exception bar for an AI company requires harder operating evidence—completed paid conversions, retention, accuracy, and customer economics. Qlo discloses no actual pilot revenue, pricing, gross margin, burn, or evidence that implementation pays back within the first-year flat fee. Delete the next financing and I cannot yet model the company.

I am not writing the check today. The product has found a narrow, consequential workflow, but the deal and capital plan—the two things that determine whether this is cheap convexity or another capital factory—are undocumented. My verifiable flip condition is a disclosed financing package below a $10 million post-money, without competing offers, that commits the proceeds to completing five paid parity pilots within 12 months and defines success as at least four converting to two-year contracts at positive contribution margin.
