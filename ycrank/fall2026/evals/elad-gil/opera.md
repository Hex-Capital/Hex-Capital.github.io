# OpEra -- Elad Gil Evaluation

OpEra’s market turns on the engineering effort required to deploy and maintain optimization at each customer site. Factory and logistics planners already have expensive decisions to make; natural-language interfaces could let them update operational constraints without repeatedly commissioning a new model. The causal proposition is credible: reduce the cost of adapting optimization, and more operations can afford it. But the dossier does not establish how much implementation effort OpEra removes. That determines whether this becomes repeatable software or a collection of bespoke projects.

The reported outcomes make the opportunity concrete. OpEra claims 38% lower delivery costs and 10% more on-time deliveries for a retailer, alongside a 10.4% higher sugar-sales premium and 54% fewer unfulfilled marketplace orders. Those results deserve some economic credit. However, the retailer result is a historical backtest against dispatching individual orders from the nearest store. Consolidation and conventional optimization can explain substantial improvement over that baseline. It does not establish that the new language interface changes deployment economics, or that OpEra beats an optimized incumbent system. The inferred connection to AlphaEvolve is a possible development path, not demonstrated productivity.

The strongest bull case is that the founders understand the difficult part beneath the interface. Rossignol and Chalita met at MIT’s Operations Research Center, collaborated on retailer routing, and bring experience spanning McKinsey, BMW, and Sanofi. The public tariff-aware implementation combines heuristic assignment with mathematical optimization. That gives OpEra more substance than an output formatter: better models could help translate requirements while the optimization layer enforces feasibility. This fits my pattern of backing software that changes physical-industry economics. But documented customer permissions, persistent operating state, production feedback, and recurring integrations are still missing. Their credentials establish relevant expertise, not evidence of founder sacrifice or prior startup learning.

Commercially, the three operational examples establish neither repeat usage nor customer pull. They are unnamed, and the dossier does not distinguish deployed decisions from analyses on historical data. I would use the absent-demand default rather than treat this as failed adoption. Meanwhile, FICO’s May 2026 acquisition of Nextmv matters: model deployment and governance already have established suppliers with distribution. OpEra needs to own the planner’s recurring decision cycle. A first deployment could become a channel into adjacent workflows, but the launch’s request for introductions does not yet demonstrate that distribution mechanism.

I would pass with my own money today. The decisive risk is implementation labor absorbing the value created by optimization—the service-heavy economics red flag. The technical work makes the bull case credible, but it does not qualify for a frontier-research exception to demonstrated demand, and there is no documented economic reconstruction sufficient to override that concern. One verifiable flip condition would be a paid rollout from an initial site to a second site using substantially the same model, with measured implementation hours and sustained planner usage. That would directly test whether OpEra’s bespoke work compounds into a product.
