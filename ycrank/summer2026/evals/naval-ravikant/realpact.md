# RealPact -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have acted. Ranvir Deshmukh and Erik Peterson built CourseMe together, reached 4,000 monthly users and 90% of Dartmouth students, then carried SignPact into a real-estate product now used by 10-plus brokerages. Four Seasons Sotheby’s is running a firm-wide pilot, and the company reports current-product sales. They lack years inside a brokerage, but they have shipped, sold, and learned inside the exact workflow. Specific knowledge can be acquired quickly when reality supplies the curriculum.

The weakness is structural, not entrepreneurial. Starting from an address or voice command and returning a New Hampshire contract in five minutes is useful automation. It is not yet a durable asset. RealPact names no exclusive data, proprietary model, hardware, user-created network, or demonstrated workflow lock-in. SkySlope already distributes overlapping AI automation to 900,000 professionals across three million annual transactions. If a competitor copies the code, RealPact is left maintaining integrations and state-specific templates while the incumbent retains distribution.

The bull case stings because adoption is ahead of what most pre-seed enterprise companies can show. Named brokerages are running the product, the team is selling subscriptions, and the buyer reportedly saves 30–40 minutes per contract. A $515.8 million transaction-management market growing at 25.1% could support a meaningful company. But the dossier gives no current price, recurring-revenue amount, retention cohort, or transaction economics. Ten customers prove a problem and some ability to sell; they do not yet prove leverage.

Nor does each brokerage make the product more valuable to another brokerage. RealPact presently resembles conventional enterprise software: every new state adds forms, regulations, MLS agreements, security work, and accuracy liability. Its inferred future corpus of templates and workflows could compound, but an inferred feedback loop is not a network effect. The unstated pattern I look for—outsiders contributing supply, knowledge, or distribution without proportional company labor—is absent. RealPact digitizes fragmented work; it does not yet turn the participants into the system.

The closest known exception is Notion: a capital-efficient team can deserve patience before observable network effects when it has created a strange product language that competitors do not understand. RealPact shares the early product-building persistence, but not the material distinction. Address-to-contract automation is immediately legible, general models support it, and an incumbent already owns the surrounding transaction platform. I see no documented integrity, morale, or financing-pressure red flag in the founders; the red flag is treating reproducible AI orchestration as the asset.

I pass with my own money today. My 36-month base case is a capable niche workflow vendor below $3 million ARR, rather than an independent brokerage operating system. The one condition that flips me is a verified cross-brokerage compounding result: deployment data showing that workflows contributed by existing customers cut implementation time or contract-error rates for later customers by at least 50%. That would turn customer count from sales traction into a proprietary asset.
