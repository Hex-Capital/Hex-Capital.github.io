# Parasma -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Parasma’s contested truth is that living neurons can become a commercially superior computing substrate, not merely an unusual laboratory instrument. The falsifying event is straightforward: on controlled tasks, the system fails to produce a repeatable advantage in accuracy, adaptation speed, energy consumption, or cost after accounting for cell degradation. Sean Cole has supplied more than a slogan—the roughly 200,000-neuron Doom system reportedly outperformed its learning algorithm alone—but Parasma has published no task accuracy, latency, energy, lifetime, or cost benchmark. This is an intriguing natural secret with a real experiment behind it, but not yet a demonstrated computing advantage.

Cole is unusually well matched to the technical problem. He researched spiking neural networks, personally wrote the software connecting Cortical Labs’ neurons to Doom, and built the prototype before institutional funding. That is much stronger than assembling a team around a fashionable AI category. Yet Parasma remains a one-person company, with no disclosed founder ownership or salary and no evidence of a team that has endured difficult work together. Advertising a founding-scientist role at a $1 million salary with only 1%–3% equity is also an odd foundation: it risks hiring an employee where the company needs an economically exposed co-creator.

The monopoly question is worse. Parasma occupies a plausible narrow wedge—the training layer for biological computers—but a wedge is not a monopoly mechanism. Cortical Labs controls hardware and API access, The Biological Computing Company is developing neuronal adapters, and Intactis Bio offers cloud-accessible processing units. Parasma discloses no exclusive biological-response dataset, patent, substrate control, or measured learning advantage, while its best-known implementation is GPL-licensed. If biological computing works, the hardware platforms can potentially internalize the training layer; if it does not, specialized training expertise has no market. This is dependence, not defensibility.

The coordination stack compounds the problem. Useful production compute requires reliable cells, durable stimulation, observability, repeatable manufacturing, hardware access, ethical governance, and customer integration to work together. Parasma itself acknowledges noise, non-stationarity, cell degradation, and possible consciousness concerns. The recent shift toward physical AI infrastructure favors ambitious substrate companies, but the exception requires a validated unit tied to an immediate constraint. Here, AI energy scarcity is asserted while the unit’s energy advantage remains unmeasured.

The repository’s 255 stars and 29 forks, plus coverage in *The Guardian* and *Scientific American*, prove research curiosity. They do not identify a buyer. A collaboration with Cortical Labs could become distribution, but today there is no paid deployment, price, sales cycle, implementation burden, or evidence that a licensing or hosted-access model supports attractive margins. My 36-month base case is that Parasma becomes a biological-computing research-tools layer with less than $1 million in ARR, rather than a production AI-compute provider.

The strongest case against passing is that the Doom result may be an early zero-to-one event: a solo founder with direct expertise trained a living system before raising capital, and an independently reported hybrid result exceeded the software-only baseline. A verified technical discontinuity can override uncertain market structure. But this result does not yet establish practical superiority, and spectacle cannot substitute for monopoly or distribution. I would reverse the pass on one verifiable event: a named customer completes and renews a paid 90-day deployment that demonstrates at least a tenfold energy advantage over the best relevant silicon baseline at comparable task accuracy while maintaining cell viability. Until then, Parasma has a possible secret but no company-shaped proof.
