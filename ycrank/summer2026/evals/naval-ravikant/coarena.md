# CoArena -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have already paid tuition in the exact problem. Before building the benchmark, they built Coasty, produced an 82.81% OSWorld computer-use result, and reached thousands of active users and paying customers across four continents. CoArena then generated $60,000 within two weeks of release. Prateek Jannu and Nitish Kovuru are not reasoning from résumés; they have built the agent, encountered the benchmark’s limitations, and sold the resulting evaluation product. That is specific knowledge revealed through action.

The product has the right compounding shape. A free user supplies a real task and a blind preference judgment; CoArena converts that contribution into two trajectories, workflow outcomes, and data it can license to labs. The public product already covers 14 agents and 57 measures. This resembles a permissionless system in which outsiders create the valuable supply while the company sleeps. But the mechanism is ahead of its evidence: no task-category data or annotation fields are yet displayed, and there is no contribution, retention, or density measurement showing that one user makes the arena more useful to another. A flywheel described is not yet a flywheel observed.

The strongest bear case is trust, not technology. Raw prompts and unmasked screenshots go to model providers without SOC 2, ISO 27001, or an independent security audit. Coasty also operates the supposedly neutral arena while developing its own computer-use agent. Excluding that agent from matches reduces the immediate conflict but does not eliminate the incentive conflict. If valuable enterprise users withhold real workflows and labs distrust the referee, CoArena collects toy trajectories while LMArena’s three million votes and 400 evaluated models compound elsewhere. Competitors can copy the arena interface; only a scarce, consented, high-quality corpus survives the copy.

The $60,000 launch revenue materially changes the option. CoArena has contract-signed, consumption-priced data and evaluation agreements rather than free-user enthusiasm alone. Product Hunt’s 109 points are nearly irrelevant beside actual payment. Still, the dossier names neither the payers nor prices, renewals, or unit economics, so I cannot tell whether this is repeatable data licensing, a launch contract, or founder-intensive evaluation work. Direct value capture exists; scalable value capture remains unproven.

The market has room, but not yet unlimited room. The cited evaluation-and-simulation market is $980 million in 2026, with benchmarking representing roughly $304 million. Robotics is named as an adjacent buyer, and the same workflow trajectories could support training, simulation, and reliability testing, but no robotics deployment demonstrates that path. The known-exception case for an early product without a visible network applies partially: the team has a working, capital-efficient product and revenue before the corpus matures. It does not excuse the missing durable substrate. In 2026, code is demonstration; proprietary data is the company.

I would invest a small personal check as a bounded-downside option on the founders converting exact-domain knowledge into a trusted trajectory network. My 36-month base case is that CoArena becomes an enterprise computer-use evaluation and data vendor at $4 million–$9 million ARR by August 2029, rather than the dominant public arena. I flip to Pass if, by August 2027, it cannot show at least three lab customers renewing specifically for proprietary trajectory data rather than benchmark software or services. The dossier shows no founder-conduct, artificial-deadline, or reassurance-seeking red flag; the unresolved veto is whether neutral governance and security mature quickly enough for the data asset to compound.
