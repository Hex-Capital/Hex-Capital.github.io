# Glen -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Glen addresses a real tax on organizational intelligence, but the dossier never quantifies that tax or shows an orders-of-magnitude consequence from removing it. The only baseline is a $5.86 billion enterprise-search market growing 9.03% annually. That describes a respectable software category, not a 100x societal consequence. Glen may make agents less forgetful; there is no evidence yet that it radically reduces the cost of expertise or changes how many people can access it.

The technical case is weaker. Continuous capture, context retrieval, prompt injection, and skill distillation sound useful, but Glen supplies no measured comparison against Glean, Dust, Zep, or Mem0. Dust already reports 3,000 organizations, 51,000 monthly active users, and 300,000 deployed agents. The dossier explicitly says the same integrations and MCP interfaces are available to enterprise-search vendors, agent platforms, and model providers. Without an auditable accuracy, latency, cost, or task-success discontinuity, this is currently product assembly in a crowded architecture—not a technical option on a new one.

The bull case is Nikos Dritsakos. He worked inside agent infrastructure at Composio, built and sold SalesBop for $500,000 after roughly 18 months, and has already put Glen into production with testimonials from Amorphic Labs, Zaplar, and Composio. Those facts make him more credible than a generic AI founder. They do not demonstrate the fast belief revision or risk-tuned recruiting I look for: Glen remains a one-person company, and the dossier identifies neither a thesis-changing experiment nor a complementary recruit assigned to context accuracy, security, or retrieval science.

The largest kill risk is whether captured organizational memory improves agent work reliably enough to outweigh privacy exposure and stale-context errors. Glen’s own terms acknowledge that recalled memory and summaries may be inaccurate, incomplete, or outdated; public-channel and shared-transcript coverage also omits part of the decision graph. Source-linked histories and access controls are sensible mitigations, but there is no pass/fail benchmark, experiment cost, or stop condition. Capital could therefore drift into integrations and onboarding before establishing that synthesized memory is causally better than retrieval from the underlying sources.

A known exception could be argued from an already large value pool and a product capable of expanding from a narrow wedge, as happened with Block. Glen has the value pool and a founder who has shipped before. The material difference is that Block’s small team had room to evolve into new financial infrastructure, while Glen begins between heavily funded incumbents whose existing data, distribution, and context graphs can absorb its features. The inferred switching costs from accumulated histories and reusable skills are not evidence of a moat until customers repeatedly depend on them.

I pass today. My one flip condition is a paid, source-audited benchmark across three named design partners showing that Glen reduces agent task time or error rates by at least 5x versus direct retrieval from the same Slack, GitHub, ticket, call, and document sources. That result would convert “unified context” from a convenient interface into a measurable technical discontinuity. Without it, the likely outcome is a narrow agent-workflow product competing on integrations, not a defining context platform.
