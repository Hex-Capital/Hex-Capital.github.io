# Allia Health -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The résumé is secondary because the product has already encountered reality. A three-person company has put one operating system into daily use across 8,000 clinicians, 1,000 practices, 300,000 patients, and nearly one million monthly visits. Amie Leighton brings healthcare research and operating exposure; Saroosh Khan built the population-health agent; clinical co-founders add direct patient and practice knowledge. The evidence is in the shipped system and its adoption, not their credentials.

The free EHR is distribution, not the company. Allia uses it to aggregate independent practices, activate 600 clinicians across 70 locations and 32 states, and negotiate national payer contracts covering more than $50 million in annualized care capacity. That creates room to move from software into care coordination, claims, outcomes, and value-based contracting. The listed $1.56 billion EHR market understates the opportunity; the adjacent transaction is mental-health care itself.

But scale is not yet leverage. Headway already offers a free AI-assisted behavioral-health EHR and has $327.5 million behind its payer network. If a competitor copies Allia’s software, what remains today is workflow adoption, payer contracts, and the beginnings of a clinical operating graph—not yet exclusive data or a demonstrated outcomes advantage. Longitudinal claims data could become proprietary, but future data is not a present moat. Allia must turn usage into an asset that compounds faster than funded competitors can purchase distribution.

The strongest bear case is that Allia is building a regulated services consolidator while describing it as a network. Each added practice may require equivalent onboarding, credentialing, compliance, and payer operations. SOC 2 and HITRUST were still in process, multi-state Part 2 obligations are real, and the 600 activated clinicians have not yet begun contracted care. There is no cohort, referral, liquidity, or matching evidence showing that one clinician presently improves the product or distribution for another. A large provider count is not automatically a network effect.

The opposing fact is unusually strong for pre-seed: these are signed national contracts, not merely a market diagram, and the system is already used daily at material scale. Even modest conversion of $50 million in care capacity would create direct revenue and proprietary operating data. No disclosed founder-conduct red flag, artificial financing deadline, or integrity problem supplies a veto. The known-exception logic is unnecessary here because Allia has already shipped and distributed; the unresolved question is value capture.

I would invest, but size the check as an option rather than pretend the outcome is proven. The single flip condition for greater conviction is verifiable evidence by March 2027 that the September 2026 launch converted at least $10 million of contracted capacity into paid care while producing retained-practice cohorts and payer-accepted outcome data. If that occurs, Allia stops being copyable software attached to services and becomes a data-and-contract network. If it does not, the apparent leverage was coordinated labor.
