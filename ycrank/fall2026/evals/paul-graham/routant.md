# Routant -- Paul Graham Evaluation

What happened when a customer refused to let an agent into its systems? That is the story I want from Routant. Which permission blocked deployment, what did the founders build, and how soon did the agent start working? The dossier describes the obstacle but contains no founder action against it. It does not identify the founders. I cannot infer resourcefulness from an attractive problem, though I also cannot infer passivity from a failed research lookup.

The proposed product has a sensible shape. Agent vendors integrate once; customers specify accessible resources, actions needing approval, and audit requirements. The hard part is making those promises survive contact with actual customer systems. Can an agent bypass the check? Does revoking access stop an action already underway? Routant has no verified implementation or security test in this dossier. That leaves technical proof at the absent-evidence default. An inaccessible website establishes a research limitation, not that the founders have built nothing.

The strongest case for investing is that permissions may be the last obstacle between an agent someone wants and a deployment they will pay for. In the January 2026 Okta-commissioned survey, 69% of 150 decision-makers said security concerns slowed adoption. A founder who personally gets one vendor through one customer's identity system could discover a useful business inside that unpleasant work. But the survey does not identify Routant's narrow well. There is no named vendor returning to the product, repeated deployment, or payment. Pricing is unverified too. The adjacent market estimate of $210 million in 2026 cannot tell me whether anyone urgently needs this particular implementation.

Competition makes the missing customer story more consequential. Keycard documents scoped credentials and delegation chains; Arcade documents action-level authorization, approvals, and audits. Routant could still win by making a specific deployment much easier. “Deployment and connectivity” needs to resolve into that concrete advantage. This resembles my recurring interest in programmable infrastructure, but the corresponding pattern includes developers actually using the primitive or a difficult substrate already assembled. Neither is established here. There is also no founder work history connecting the team to this problem and no documented product revision after contrary evidence. Those remain unknowns. I see no affirmative evidence of the partnership dependence, premature delegation, or stubbornness that would justify scoring below the defaults.

I would pass on writing a personal check from this dossier. My exceptions depend on a working product, unmistakable pull, or validated technical proof; none is documented strongly enough to replace the missing founder evidence. One verifiable flip condition would be a dated deployment record showing a founder-built workaround to a named customer's access blocker, followed by that customer repeatedly running the agent through Routant. That would connect founder behavior to something a user needs. Today the dossier establishes a plausible place to look for a company, but gives me too little evidence about the people doing the looking.
