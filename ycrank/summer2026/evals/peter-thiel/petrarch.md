# Petrarch -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Petrarch’s contested truth is that the scarce input for applied AI is no longer public internet content but legally usable operational data stranded inside ordinary and failed businesses. The falsifier is equally clear: buyers do not return after testing these datasets because the data produces no measurable model improvement. Petrarch has partially tested the supply side—several thousand construction plans were redacted and transferred within one week, alongside transactions involving autonomous-driving recordings and codebases—but has published neither repeat purchasing nor downstream model-performance results. This is a plausible secret, not yet a proved one.

The team is the strongest part of the company. Samuel Hahn worked in data-compliance GTM, Ian Lee built training-data infrastructure and advised Scale AI, and Sudhish Swain led deployed computer-vision engineering and researched biological datasets. All three attended Harvard and dropped out together to build Petrarch. That is genuine domain convergence and full-time commitment, not a generic team assembled around a fashionable market. But attending school together is weaker than having shipped together, and the dossier provides no ownership, salary, or secondary-liquidity evidence. They look like founders rather than professional managers; their economic exposure remains unverified.

What is the initial monopoly? “Dormant enterprise data” sounds narrow until it includes construction plans, driving recordings, codebases, manufacturing, and logistics. That is a category assembled by intersection, not yet a beachhead that can be dominated. The PII-redaction and legal-clearance workflow enables transactions, but it is not shown to be proprietary, and Petrarch disclaims important chain-of-title responsibility to suppliers. Troveo, Defined.ai, Datarade, and Human Native already overlap across sourcing, processing, licensing, and discovery. Without exclusive supply or demonstrated buyer lock-in, every successful introduction creates an opportunity for disintermediation.

The bull case is sharper than the current economics. Petrarch has completed transactions before disclosing institutional financing, claims customers ranging from first-time founders to Fortune 500 enterprises, and reaches supply through restructuring contacts, an appraisal funnel, direct outreach, and Constellation fellows. This fits the favorable unstated pattern of infrastructure forming around the complete AI production stack. If failed companies contain difficult-to-reproduce operational histories, Petrarch could accumulate both exclusive data and proprietary pricing knowledge. But today the distribution system still resembles labor-intensive brokerage: no named buyer, conversion rate, sales cycle, transaction value, repeat rate, or acquisition cost is public.

The revenue engine is therefore the decisive absence. A commission or buy-resell spread is inferred rather than documented, revenue is undisclosed, and a projected $4.44 billion global training-dataset market does not show how one intermediary captures a fund-scale share. The founders may have discovered valuable inventory without yet discovering a compounding business. My 36-month base case is that Petrarch pivots from horizontal dataset brokerage into managed data-rights and liquidation infrastructure for distressed companies, remaining below $3 million ARR by August 2029.

None of the known-exception mechanisms presently overrides this weakness: there is no independently verified technical discontinuity, quantified persistent demand, or production deployment tied to an immediate bottleneck. Petrarch avoids the most serious founding-team red flags, but it triggers the market-definition and coordination concerns—heterogeneous data must be sourced, cleared, transformed, validated, and sold before either side bypasses the broker. I would not invest today. One condition flips the decision: evidence that at least three AI buyers each completed a second purchase from exclusive Petrarch-controlled supply, with disclosed transaction economics and measured model improvement. That would convert brokerage into a monopoly mechanism and a slogan into a secret.
