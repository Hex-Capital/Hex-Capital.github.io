# Hesper AI -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Hesper AI’s strongest evidence is that the founders tried selling fraud scores, failed, and changed what they delivered. Since April 2026, they have moved toward completed investigations and reported an MGA production deployment. That is specific knowledge acquired through action: the customer needs a case resolved, not another signal to investigate. Dhariwal’s document-automation engineering and Badu’s enterprise AI sales experience make that learning credible. The Nanonets revenue belongs to their former employer; the product correction belongs to them.

The investment question is what Hesper keeps after delivering the investigation. Its agents inspect documents, query records and assemble evidence, but customers supply the database licences and access rights. Its contract prohibits pooling customer data for shared model training. That protection may help win customers, but it also closes an obvious route from investigation volume to a proprietary model. Customer-specific tuning could eventually create switching costs; the dossier does not establish them. I cannot identify an asset that becomes harder to reproduce after the ten-thousandth claim. Restricted learning rights are a concrete constraint beyond merely undisclosed technology.

The strongest argument against passing is that the founders may already have discovered a valuable workflow before establishing its defensibility. They report more than 10,000 investigated claims, roughly 80% lower handling costs, and one deployment reducing adjuster time by 82%. Those are meaningful pre-seed signals, even though they remain founder-reported and the customer is unnamed. An insurer that embeds Hesper deeply could become difficult to displace. My exceptions for distinctive prototypes and unusually capable founders matter here: a network need not exist before the first check. But deployment volume does not yet demonstrate durable dependence, and the dossier supplies no distinctive technical asset to substitute for it.

Hesper also falls short of my recurring preference for customer-created value. Another carrier brings another investigation workload; there is no demonstrated mechanism by which its participation improves another carrier’s results or distribution. This is a centrally sold enterprise product, with demonstrations, retrospective evaluations and conference outreach driving acquisition. The broader opportunity is credible: document verification, coverage checks and evidence reconstruction can address more claims work for carriers, MGAs and TPAs. But the cited $8.5 billion fraud-detection market includes substantial adjacent products. It establishes surrounding demand without establishing how much Hesper can capture, particularly against Shift and FRISS.

Usage-based pricing at least connects the bill to work performed. Yet neither the price nor paid revenue is disclosed, and the introductory offer includes 200 free claims and 90 days without a platform fee. The reported $15 million in potential fraud is a customer outcome, not Hesper’s revenue or recovered cash. I see an intelligible payer and charging mechanism, with little evidence yet of realized value capture. There is no documented integrity issue, financing pressure or demand for investor reassurance. Changing direction after unsuccessful sales is evidence of updating; it does not trigger my concern about founders who abandon direction whenever challenged.

I pass on Hesper AI today. The founders have earned substantial credit for execution, but their learning currently appears to compound primarily in themselves rather than in an asset the company owns. The single result that would reopen this is a documented, customer-verified comparison showing that a Hesper-owned investigation asset improves accuracy or cost beyond what a competitor with equivalent models and customer database access can achieve. That would turn useful automation into an advantage I could underwrite with my own money.
