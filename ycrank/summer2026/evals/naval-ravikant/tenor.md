# Tenor -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have built around the substrate, but not yet the exact problem. Muhtasham Oblokulov co-authored StarCoder and StarCoder 2 and operated production ML at Munich Re; Amgad Al-Zamkan researched adaptive decision systems; Hamze Al-Zamkan led TUM.ai and worked inside applied AI and startups. Those are real artifacts acquired through action. The missing link is equally specific: the dossier does not show that any founder previously deployed enterprise agents, sold AI-governance software, or measured token spend against business outcomes. Technical proximity is not yet proprietary knowledge of the buyer’s pain.

Tenor’s useful insight is that an AI worker needs an economic identity: responsibility, budget, system access, persistent context, and an attributed outcome. If the attribution layer works, token allocation becomes a control system rather than an expense dashboard. But attribution is the entire company, not a feature within it. No benchmark, audit method, customer deployment, or evidence of causal attribution across Slack and other systems is public. Without that proof, Tenor is assigning precise numbers to outcomes it may not be able to distinguish from human work, external demand, or another agent’s contribution.

The code-copying test is currently fatal. Relevance AI already builds agent teams, Artisan has reached $5 million ARR with task-specific AI workers, and Glean has reached $200 million ARR with governed agents built on an enterprise knowledge graph. Tenor names no exclusive data, proprietary model, hardware, workflow lock-in, or customer-created network. Persistent worker histories could eventually become switching costs, but a future database is not a present moat. As inference costs fall, implementation becomes cheaper for Tenor and every competitor simultaneously.

The strongest opposing case is the team plus timing. A StarCoder co-author, an adaptive-systems researcher, and an AI-community operator are attacking a market estimated at $5.23 billion in 2025 and growing 41.27% annually. Their cross-department outcome layer could sit above competing agent platforms rather than fight them directly. That is a plausible large company. It is not yet a known-exception case: there is no demonstrated prototype usage, obsessive user behavior, unusually difficult recruiting achievement, or self-reinforcing network that overrides the missing durability.

The commercial evidence does not resolve the uncertainty. Tenor asks for introductions to COOs, CIOs, and AI leaders through founder-led demos, but reports no payer, price, signed deployment, recurring revenue, or completed transaction. Its 392 LinkedIn followers show attention, not value capture. Nor does one customer improve the product or distribution for another; today each enterprise appears to require its own sale, integrations, permissions, and support.

I would pass. Within 36 months, the more probable outcome is a pivot from autonomous “AI workers” into AI-spend observability or agent FinOps, where attribution becomes reporting rather than closed-loop resource allocation. The verifiable flip condition is one cohort of at least ten paying enterprises showing that Tenor’s independently auditable attribution changes token allocation and improves measured business output for six consecutive months, while producing a proprietary cross-customer asset competitors cannot recreate from the same integrations.
