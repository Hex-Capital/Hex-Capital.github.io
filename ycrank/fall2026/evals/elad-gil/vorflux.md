# Vorflux -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Browser-capable cloud agents let engineering teams buy completed work rather than another coding assistant; Vorflux becomes a large company only if it reduces the fully loaded cost of an accepted code change, including human supervision. That is the controlling variable. Planning, implementation, review, and testing can all become cheaper while the final integration still consumes an engineer’s afternoon. The dossier establishes a product designed around this problem, but no measured reduction in supervision or cost per accepted change. I cannot yet underwrite the central market belief.

The timing is credible. Cursor’s February 2026 browser-agent release demonstrates that executing and checking software in the cloud has become commercially feasible. Vorflux combines parallel implementation, cross-model review, and browser QA into a pull-request workflow. That is a meaningful capability change. Its pricing—$0.2117–$3.3868 per compute hour plus model usage—does not establish the economic improvement, however. Without task success rates and intervention costs, inexpensive execution can still produce expensive engineering work. Cursor and Factory already offer overlapping capabilities, so this market is crystallizing around competitors with existing distribution.

The strongest affirmative evidence is architectural. Vorflux retains repository knowledge, supports configurable engineering workflows, and offers recurring Slack AutoBots. Better models could increase completed tasks while leaving the surrounding execution environment and workflow state with Vorflux. That fits my preference for owning an operational loop. The unresolved issue is whether this ownership creates customer dependence: persistent memory is useful, but the dossier supplies no reproduction-cost estimate or demonstrated switching cost. Cross-model review also needs a measurable advantage over the same models running inside an incumbent’s harness.

Customer evidence makes this more than a product diagram. Supermemory describes a two-day optimization session consuming $600 in compute, and TrashLab describes implemented customization controls. Those are concrete tasks for named users, although company-published testimonials deserve less weight than independently documented repeat usage. The reported trillion tokens could represent substantial experimentation; it does not distinguish successful delegation from repeated attempts. Nor is the $600 necessarily revenue retained by Vorflux. These signals justify more conviction than a waitlist, without establishing recurring product-market pull.

The bull case is uncomfortable to dismiss: Prasanna has documented prior founder and CTO experience, his co-founders bring enterprise infrastructure experience, and the reported $15 million July financing gives them resources to iterate. This fits my unstated pattern of backing experienced operators across a technology discontinuity. Prior startup experience earns credit; desperation cannot be inferred from credentials. I also checked the exceptions: there is no documented frontier-research asset warranting a research-stage override, or reconstructed market economics warranting an incumbent-displacement override. The workflow ownership reduces the thin-feature concern, but overlapping incumbent products leave value capture unresolved. I would pass on a personal check today. My single flip condition is a documented cohort of at least five independent teams sustaining eight weeks of production use with at least 30% lower fully loaded cost per accepted pull request than their previous toolchain, while Vorflux earns positive contribution margin.
