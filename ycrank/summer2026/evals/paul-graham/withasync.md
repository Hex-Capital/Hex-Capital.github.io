# Async -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what broke. Ben Smith and Connor Park moved from AI billing infrastructure in 2025 to legal agents in early 2026 and then to Async’s broader service-business product by August. That is motion, but motion is not yet resourcefulness. The dossier never identifies the observation that killed the billing route, the obstacle encountered in legal automation, or a workaround shipped within days or weeks. Four functioning legal deployments make the sequence look better than random wandering, but they do not prove how the founders behave when blocked. I leave the controlling founder test at its missing-evidence default.

The product is more convincing than the founder story. Async has deployed agents that perform intake, conflict checks, engagement-letter generation, task assignment, and legacy-system data entry. Approval queues, isolated customer infrastructure, and SOC 2 Type I certification are real artifacts, not a diagram of what the company plans to build. But the technical evidence is still company-reported. There is no reproducible reliability result, proprietary substrate, or threshold showing that these agents outperform the rapidly commoditizing combination of frontier models and integrations.

The strongest evidence is the narrow well inside small law firms. Martinez Legal, Clemente Mueller, Joshua Lowenthal, and Clifton Law are named users, and Clifton describes several connected procedures running without a system migration. “Thousands of hours” automated is imperfect but materially better than a waitlist. What is missing is the behavior after deployment: payment, repeat use, expansion, retention, or unsolicited referrals. Outcome pricing could align Async with customer value, but an undisclosed pricing formula is not revenue.

There is also a real schlep here. Someone must inspect a firm’s procedures, connect documents and email to calendars and case systems, design approvals, handle security, and maintain the automation when the underlying workflow changes. Smith and Park previously built legal agents and outcome-based billing infrastructure, so this is not pure accelerator brainstorming. Still, the dossier does not show that either founder personally suffered the problem before deciding to sell into it. Their prior work establishes proximity, not an organic founder-originated need.

The bull case stings. Async already has the kind of concrete pre-round proof I often find in unusually promising young technical teams: a working system, four named customers, regulatory precautions, and measurable work completed before hiring a larger organization. That nearly qualifies for the known-exception route in which a functioning product and unmistakable use substitute for missing conventional evidence. Nor does Async yet exhibit the classic big-launch, major-partner, or premature-hiring red flags; customers were operating before the August 2026 launch, and the two founders still perform the audits and deployments themselves.

But the exception is not complete. All published usage evidence comes from Async or its customers’ hosted stories, no revenue or growth rate is disclosed, and every named deployment is legal while the company markets simultaneously to healthcare, real estate, and childcare. The likely company is a useful legal-operations business disguised too early as a horizontal agent platform. My 36-month base case is that Async concentrates on law firms and reaches $3–8 million ARR, rather than establishing a defensible multi-industry platform. I would flip to Invest if one named law firm verified that a failed production workflow forced the founders to ship a correction within two weeks and then expanded its paid deployment.
