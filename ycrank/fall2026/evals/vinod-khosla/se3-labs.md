# SE3 Labs -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

SE3 Labs could attack a consequential bottleneck: the physical experiments required to make robots useful outside the laboratory. If each deployment failure becomes training data that reliably eliminates failures elsewhere, a robot workcell becomes a machine for producing capability. That would interest me. The dossier establishes an operating evaluation system, but supplies no quantified connection between that system and a collapse in deployment cost, human intervention, or training requirements. I cannot assign the economic consequence of general-purpose robotics to every supplier serving it.

The strongest evidence is actual hardware work. SE3 published 100 Dream evaluation episodes across 10 tasks on October 30, 2026, with recordings, telemetry, and scoring sheets. Its September study recorded 18 successful placements in 20 agentic-control attempts against six in 20 code-as-policy attempts. That is a 3x observed difference on a narrow experiment. It earns technical credit because the team has implemented and measured something real. It does not establish that SE3 produces better policies, or delivers evaluation 5–10x more effectively than Poke & Wiggle’s physical stations. Bifrost’s simulation approach creates a different comparison: physical testing can expose failures simulation misses, but the value of those additional discoveries still needs measurement.

The largest kill risk is whether collecting these failures produces transferable improvement cheaply enough to justify repeated purchases. SE3’s Dream report leaves fine-tuning and comparative retesting to a subsequent round. There is no disclosed experiment budget, pass/fail threshold, or decision about what happens if improvement fails to transfer. The completed evaluations establish a baseline; they do not yet resolve that uncertainty. Project-specific statements of work offer a way to charge for useful experiments, but no disclosed cost per usable trajectory, operator utilization, or customer alternative establishes scalable economics. Customer permissions also matter: the privacy policy requires written authorization for general-purpose training on raw customer content. A shared learning advantage therefore requires contracts and demonstrated transfer.

The bull case deserves attention. SE3 fits my observed preference for scarce scientific workflows that can become repeatable production platforms, and for AI that reaches physical work. A three-person team has already published inspectable experiments. Closed-source model APIs could let it test many developers’ policies without needing their weights. Those facts make this worth following. My exceptions for immature business models require an exceptional instigator or a sufficiently explicit consequence-of-success case; neither is established here. Founder identities and recruiting histories remain unverified, so I use the missing-evidence default rather than turn biography silence into a founder criticism. There is also no evidence of exit fixation, subsidy dependence, or rejection of disconfirming results. The concern is whether bespoke testing can become a compounding technical business.

I pass with my own money today. **My flip condition is a paid, customer-authorized, independently reproducible trial showing that SE3’s failure-directed training reaches the same held-out deployment reliability at one-tenth the customer’s existing total experimentation cost, including robot time, operator labor, and retraining.** That would connect its demonstrated ability to run experiments with the technical and economic discontinuity I need. Until then, SE3 has shown useful measurement; the investment depends on what those measurements enable.
