# Control Seat -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Battex Chile makes Control Seat an unsexy Wizard-of-Oz industrial operator with a real deployment wedge, while 341 company followers and no founder writing make Control Seat almost distribution-free. That high sleeve fit and low owned-community pairing is the analytical signature: Control Seat can become a sound, integration-led industrial business through execution, but nothing yet turns each installation into cheaper distribution for the next one.

Teamshares is the high rung on category fit because operational work is the wedge, software standardizes the work, and conventional SaaS investors misread the resulting asset as services-heavy. Control Seat shares that mechanism: Control Seat enters plants through systems-integration projects and deploys proprietary SCADA, historian, analytics, and Virtual PLC software during the engagement. The divergence is load-bearing—Teamshares made the unusual structure itself an advantage, while Control Seat has a standard YC financing path, only one named Battex Chile deployment, and no evidence that custom integration has become a repeatable economic system.

Fin is the low rung on founder-owned distribution: a Wizard-of-Oz process can generate learning without generating a customer flywheel, leaving institutional selling to carry the business when the initial product stalls. Warren Shepard and Jack Grodnick each have roughly 2,000 LinkedIn followers, but Control Seat has only 341 company followers, no identified Twitter presence, no public category writing, and no meaningful GitHub audience. Control Seat therefore owns technical access to plants, not an audience; that is acceptable in industrial software, but it removes the creator-style distribution asymmetry I disproportionately value.

Control Seat’s custom-priced managed-cloud, self-hosted, and integration deployments could compound over the next 18 months if every Battex Chile engagement produces reusable connectors, validated control logic, and a shorter commissioning cycle. Control Seat instead becomes founder-drainage if every Rockwell, Siemens, or CODESYS environment demands bespoke engineering while revenue remains attached to project labor. Software is worthless—fine, provocative overstatement—but the operational point is that Control Seat’s cap table becomes venture-shaped only when the tag layer scales faster than the integration headcount.

The $500,000 YC standard investment is the only financing datum, so Control Seat receives no value-seed credit for an undisclosed post-money price, tier-one passes, or unusual instrument. The counterfactual is straightforward: Control Seat moves into Invest territory if the angel entry is below the Summer 2026 batch median because industrial SCADA looks too services-heavy for competitive seed funds; Control Seat falls toward Pass if Demo Day produces a $25 million-plus post-money bidding process around “AI for factories.” The red-flag test is mixed rather than fatal: natural-language analysis appears in the bundle, but Control Seat is not a generic agent shell and industrial controls remain distinctly non-consensus.

Drop.io is the single useful founder parallel: technical conviction mattered because the founders built against entrenched free incumbents and kept turning the crank until the asset became strategically legible. Control Seat has a credible version of that missionary setup—Shepard worked on SpaceX Starlink manufacturing and flight software, while Grodnick worked on SCADA at both Tesla and SpaceX before the pair compared their industrial experiences. Control Seat does not yet match the Drop.io signal because Shepard has no identified public technical writing and Grodnick’s visible GitHub repository has zero stars; the domain history is real, but the asymmetric belief remains inferred rather than demonstrated pre-pitch.

Battex Chile anchors my 36-month forecast: Control Seat reaches $2 million to $4 million in annualized revenue across fewer than 20 industrial customers, with more than half of revenue still tied to custom integration, and remains independent rather than becoming a breakout software platform. Control Seat’s strong industrial sleeve fit should win a small number of valuable plants, while Control Seat’s weak owned distribution and brownfield commissioning burden keep growth lumpy and founder-mediated.

Ro is the Known Exception that makes the bull case sting: regulatory and operational complexity looked like capital inefficiency until fifty-state prescription rails became the moat. Control Seat could repeat that pattern because Shepard and Grodnick have SpaceX and Tesla controls experience, Battex Chile is already deployed, and the Virtual PLC reportedly beat CODESYS on nine of ten IEC workloads. Control Seat has not earned that override yet—the documentation keeps safety functions independent, the benchmark excludes I/O latency, scheduler jitter, motion, and certification, and no deployment count or revenue is disclosed. Three unrelated production customers each paying more than $100,000 of software ARR at greater than 70% gross margin without founder-led integration would falsify my forecast and flip the verdict to Invest.
