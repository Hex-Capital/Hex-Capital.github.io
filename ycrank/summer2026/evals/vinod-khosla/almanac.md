# Almanac -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence case is weak. Almanac addresses real organizational waste across Slack, Gmail, Docs, GitHub, calendars, and meeting notes, but the dossier never quantifies that waste or shows an order-of-magnitude collapse in the cost of retrieving expertise. A $15.36 billion knowledge-base market growing 8.57% annually describes an established software category, not a 100x societal consequence. This could become useful software. The evidence does not yet make it an asymmetric option on a radically different world.

The technical case is worse. Glean already has 250 connectors and more than $100 million ARR; Dust supports over 100 sources and 300,000 deployed agents. Against those named incumbents, Almanac offers a maintained wiki, iMessage access, and a managed browser. Those are packaging differences, not a measured 5–10x discontinuity in accuracy, task completion, latency, or cost. OpenAI and Anthropic already expose computer-use capabilities, so the dedicated computer is currently a dependency on commoditizing infrastructure rather than a proprietary architecture or learning loop.

The strongest bull case is the team’s behavior. Three technically credible founders shipped CodeAlmanac, earned 791 GitHub stars and 71 forks, and then expanded from coding knowledge into company-wide context. That resembles the known exception in **Block**: a modest initial tool can become an infrastructure platform when founders remain flexible about the route. The material difference is evidence. Block’s evolving product attacked a giant payment value pool; Almanac has disclosed neither current-product adoption nor a proprietary wedge inside enterprise knowledge. The prior project shows shipping ability and some pull, but it does not prove that the broader pivot followed customer evidence or that the team has recruited complementary owners for enterprise security, distribution, and agent reliability.

The dollars-versus-risk graph is missing. Almanac’s largest kill risk is whether an autonomous assistant can outperform incumbent retrieval systems while safely reading, transmitting, publishing, and deleting company data. Yet there is no reported benchmark, repeated customer usage, accuracy threshold, retention result, or experiment designed to kill that thesis cheaply. A three-day trial and a $250-per-user monthly price test willingness to pay only after the product works; they do not test whether users will trust it or whether its actions are reliably correct.

Economics are similarly unproven. The price is known, but inference, browser-operation, support, integration, and failure-remediation costs are not. At $250 per user, Almanac may support healthy margins, or it may be concealing substantial human and compute expense behind a premium early-adopter price. There is no bottom-up path showing that it can deliver company knowledge more cheaply than existing software plus employee labor at broad-market scale.

My base case is that by August 2029 Almanac has pivoted back toward developer knowledge tooling and remains below $3 million ARR. The painful counterargument is straightforward: only 1,000 seats at list price produce $3 million ARR, and the founders have already demonstrated an ability to attract a technical community. But a crowded category rewards measured superiority, not breadth of integrations. I pass because the dossier shows adaptive builders without the discontinuity their broader product requires. One verifiable condition flips me: a controlled deployment showing at least a 5x reduction in time spent retrieving and executing company knowledge tasks versus Glean or Dust, with no material accuracy deterioration and repeat paid use across three independent customers.
