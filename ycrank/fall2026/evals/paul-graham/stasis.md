# STASIS -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Wearing your own glasses for 30 days is a useful beginning. What I want to know is what broke on day three and what the founders changed on day four. STASIS’s dossier records the wear test, but no specific obstacle followed by a shipped workaround. That leaves my most important founder test unanswered. It does not establish that Soham Chakraborty and Neil Goradia are passive. It gives me insufficient evidence to bet on their resourcefulness.

There is something here beyond an application idea. Berkeley independently identifies both founders on GlaucoGlasses, and their backgrounds combine bioengineering, EECS, and ocular-device development. This gives STASIS a credible origin in work they have actually done. It does not establish that either founder encountered the particular eye-strain problem being sold here. The reported wear test earns provisional technical credit, but wearing a device is different from demonstrating that its fatigue measurements are accurate or that its automatic adjustments help. Neither the claimed patents nor the proposed combination of sensors, tinting, and Bluetooth closes that gap.

The strongest case for investing is that these founders have already learned to build together in an unusually awkward field. Their collaboration began during freshman year, rather than being assembled for an accelerator application. Combining electronics, optics, and something people will tolerate on their faces could conceal a worthwhile opportunity. This resembles my unstated preference for technical teams that build something difficult before asking investors to believe in a category. But the numerical proof spikes that make those bets compelling are missing here: 30 days measures the duration of the founder’s experiment, not its result. Nor can I treat the move from GlaucoGlasses to STASIS as evidence of intelligent adaptation without knowing what observation caused it.

Who wants these glasses badly enough to keep wearing an awkward early version? “Screen users” is too broad an answer. The 348 physician intent forms and seven distributor LOIs totaling $168,000 concern the separate glaucoma product. They demonstrate neither demand nor revenue for STASIS. Conversations with Kaiser Optometry leadership may help the founders learn, but they are not customer pull. Conflicting $95 and $240 preorder prices also leave the purchase proposition unclear. The optional $15 monthly subscription eventually requires a recurring benefit users can recognize. JINS MEME’s discontinuation makes that worth investigating; it does not prove that STASIS will have poor retention.

I would not infer the usual red flags from this thin record. There is no documented waiting for a partner, refusal to change course, or delegation of the hard work. But I also cannot invoke an exception to my founder gate. The exceptions require unusually convincing prototype evidence and founder correction, independent technical validation, or unmistakable user pull. STASIS has a self-reported test and relevant experience. Those justify attention, but do not yet replace the missing evidence.

I would pass today. One verifiable result could change that: a dated trial with ten unrelated heavy screen users documenting a specific failure, the founders’ shipped correction within two weeks, and continued voluntary use afterward. That would connect the things currently separated in this dossier—technical ability, action under resistance, and someone else’s need.
