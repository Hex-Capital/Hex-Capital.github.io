# Parasma -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. AI labs route some workloads through living neural substrates because those systems learn continuously without the energy appetite of conventional training clusters. To reach that world, Parasma must make stimulation policies reproducible, keep cultures stable under sustained use, replace cells without losing learned behavior, establish an ethical boundary around consciousness, and beat silicon on a measured workload. The approximately 200,000-neuron Doom experiment is a real leak from that future: Scientific American reported that the hybrid system outperformed the learning algorithm alone. But Parasma has not disclosed accuracy, latency, energy, cost, or substrate-lifetime results, so the artifact proves possibility—not the causal path to useful compute.

Sean Cole is the strongest part of this bet. Before forming the company, he studied spiking neural networks, earned an AI master’s with distinction, and wrote the code connecting Cortical Labs’ neurons to Doom. That is independently derived technical work, not a category pivot composed after fundraising. The solo-founder structure also leaves no ambiguity about who is accountable. Advertising a founding-scientist role requiring 11-plus years of experience shows that Cole understands the biological bottleneck, although a $1 million salary is not recruiting proof until someone exceptional actually joins.

Parasma is positioned beneath the visible AI wave rather than as another interface on top of it. Closed-loop stimulation, reward design, and decoding could become a training layer shared across token prediction, reinforcement learning, and future biohybrid machines. The problem is control. Its public demonstration ran on Cortical Labs’ substrate, the Doom implementation is GPL-3.0, and Cortical Labs, The Biological Computing Company, and Intactis Bio are already building integrated hardware or cloud platforms. Without proprietary response data or stimulation policies that transfer across substrates, Parasma may be a valuable experiment whose economics accrue to the hardware owner.

The working artifact is delightfully strange, but strange technology is not yet strange user behavior. The repository’s 255 stars and 29 forks show researcher attention, and independent coverage makes the technical claim more credible than a narrated demo. There is still no paid deployment, repeat-user cohort, or evidence that developers rearrange their work around the system. The same gap appears in the system economics: energy and hardware constraints are asserted, but Parasma does not quantify the rent imposed by GPUs, identify who captures it, or show whose agency biological compute returns.

The strongest case against waiting is the pre-revenue exception represented by CosmicBrain AI: both companies pair an obsessed technical founder with a functioning physical-AI prototype before commercial metrics exist. The material difference is that CosmicBrain had signed industrial proofs of concept, while Parasma has neither a named design partner nor a comparative benchmark. Parasma’s own research note also concedes cell degradation and the possibility that capability could become difficult to separate from consciousness. Avoiding architectures believed to support consciousness and consulting outsiders is responsible, but “we believe this architecture is safe” is not yet a limiting architecture that can survive commercial pressure.

I am not writing the check today. My 36-month base case is that Parasma remains research tooling or is absorbed by Cortical Labs rather than reaching $1 million in standalone ARR. The verifiable condition that flips me is one independently replicated, 30-day benchmark showing at least a 10× energy advantage over a GPU at matched task quality, including performance after culture replacement. That would turn Doom from a wonderful bottle cap found on the sidewalk into evidence that an entirely new compute stack has started leaking into the present.
