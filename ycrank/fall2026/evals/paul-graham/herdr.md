# herdr -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Can Celik built herdr because running agents across terminals had become a problem in his own work. That is a good origin for a developer tool. His AI engineering background explains how he encountered the problem; the shipped runtime shows he could do something about it. But I want to distinguish building something useful from being relentlessly resourceful. The dossier contains no specific episode in which an obstacle defeated the first approach and Celik found a workaround within days or weeks. That leaves my largest founder criterion at its missing-evidence default. It does not establish that he waits when blocked.

The product evidence is much stronger. Herdr has a background server that owns terminal processes, independently attachable clients, agent-state detection, and a socket API. These are inspectable capabilities in a public repository, rather than an architecture promised after financing. There is also an important boundary: disconnecting a client preserves execution, but restarting the server destroys the original processes. Herdr has solved a useful, bounded problem. It has not made agent execution indestructible. I give substantial technical credit for what exists.

The most interesting number is the 1,789 community plugins. The 46,194 stars and 1,644,092 cumulative installs measure attention and distribution, with substantial ambiguity about actual users. People building extensions reveal something more expensive than clicking a star. Raycast, Stream Deck, and iOS clients suggest that developers want to reach the runtime from places its original terminal interface does not cover. This fits my unstated preference for programmable interfaces that users bend into their own workflows. It earns strong demand credit, though plugin counts do not establish how many developers return every day.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that this combination already supplies an exception to an incomplete founder record: working software, substantial distribution, and users extending it without waiting for the company. My known exceptions permit that substitution. But they require the product evidence to replace the particular uncertainty. Here, the unresolved question is whether Celik can change routes when the attractive free product encounters a harder commercial problem. Herdr Cloud is forthcoming; the dossier documents no rejected hypothesis or customer observation that caused a material product revision. I therefore leave adaptability at its default. The $6 million financing does not answer that question, and the DigitalOcean and Fly.io demonstrations do not yet make distribution dependent on a large partner.

Herdr’s commercial difficulty is concrete. Its Apache-2.0 runtime can spread widely while another distributor captures the valuable additions; Herdr Extended already supplies an example. Connecting customer-owned machines without SSH configuration offers an adjacent service worth testing, with real operational and security work behind it. Unsandboxed plugins make that work more consequential. This is a plausible business, and the absence of revenue at this stage is not a strike against it. I am neutral because the strongest evidence concerns the artifact and its ecosystem, while the founder behavior I weight most remains unobserved. My flip condition is one documented Cloud customer episode: a specific adoption blocker, Celik’s shipped correction within two weeks, and that customer still paying and using the corrected workflow eight weeks later. That would test the missing mechanism directly.
