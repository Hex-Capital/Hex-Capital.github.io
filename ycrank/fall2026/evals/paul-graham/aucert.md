# Aucert -- Paul Graham Evaluation

What happened when these founders got stuck? Aucert’s dossier tells me where they worked, but gives no instance of a blocked release, a failed test, or a reluctant customer followed by a workaround they shipped. That leaves my most important question unanswered. It does not mean they lack determination. It means I cannot infer determination from PhonePe and PayPal. I would pass on the evidence here.

The strongest argument against passing is that these founders have spent years inside the problem. Vivek Soneja was a founding mobile architect at PhonePe. Rajesh Kumar spent 11 years at PayPal, including work on patented automated testing. Mobile QA pipelines and payments testing are unusually relevant preparation. Installed software also has an unpleasant constraint: correcting a defect can require another update and store review. There is a plausible opportunity in doing the tedious verification before that happens. This earns real credit for problem exposure, though the dossier does not document the particular encounter that led them to start Aucert.

The product could turn that experience into something useful. Agents explore an app, remember paths and approved expectations, and report findings across functionality, accessibility, design, and store policies. Organization-specific screen graphs are a more concrete proposition than simply asking a model to inspect screenshots. But these are company descriptions, not measured technical results. A public launch and an offered free scan provide some evidence of implementation; they do not establish how reliably the system explores an unfamiliar app or distinguishes a defect from intended behavior. I give the described architecture partial credit. I cannot give it the credit due an inspected working system.

Who wants the crude version badly enough to keep using it? Mobile engineering and QA leaders are a plausible audience, but Aucert has not identified a narrow group returning for successive releases. Its October 2026 launch, 317 LinkedIn followers, and coverage establish visibility. None establishes pull. Monthly subscription terms describe how customers might pay; realized revenue and repeat usage remain undisclosed. That is normal at pre-seed. It still leaves demand untested under my criteria. Likewise, there is no documented rejected hypothesis followed by a changed product, so adaptability receives the missing-evidence default rather than a judgment of rigidity.

Competition makes the first user’s behavior especially informative. BrowserStack already supplies mobile automation, accessibility tools, and testing agents; Drizz offers visual tests directed in English. Aucert could win by discovering important failures without requiring someone to specify each test. But that advantage must survive a customer asking whether reviewing its findings takes less effort than doing the testing. Unredacted screen content sent to AI providers adds another practical obstacle for sensitive apps. The founders’ experience gives them a credible chance of solving these problems. The dossier has not yet shown their response to them.

Aucert fits my preference for technical founders entering an ugly workflow, but not yet my observed pattern of backing a concrete proof spike or a developer tool users have already appropriated. Founder demos are a sensible acquisition method; nothing here establishes dependence on a ceremonial launch or a large partner. Unknown cofounder history is also not evidence of a hastily assembled team. I checked the exception case: an impressive working prototype, independently validated result, or unmistakable user pull could outweigh incomplete founder evidence. None is documented strongly enough here. My flip condition is one verifiable customer history: a named mobile team encounters a release-blocking limitation, the founders ship a workaround within two weeks, and that team pays to use Aucert on its next three releases. That would answer the question the résumés cannot.
