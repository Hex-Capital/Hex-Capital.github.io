# Elite -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Elite’s implied secret is that specialty practices will trust an autonomous agent—not merely a call-center interface—to execute consequential front-office work across voice, SMS, web, and the EHR. What would prove this wrong? Practices might accept automated answering but refuse durable delegation of refills, referrals, eligibility, and triage once errors touch clinical liability. The dossier reports no production usage or independent result testing that proposition. Worse, Assort, Hyro, and Syllable already pursue substantially the same belief. This is presently a consensus category, not a falsifiable secret Elite has uniquely discovered.

The product demonstrates execution breadth: protocols for 18 specialties, write-back into more than 19 EHRs, a public voice demo, audit logging, encryption, HIPAA compliance, and BAAs. But integration count is not monopoly. Assort claims 23-plus specialties and proprietary interaction data; Hyro has health-system deployments and explainability controls; Syllable covers the same communications channels. Elite has disclosed neither a sharply bounded specialty beachhead nor exclusive data, dominant share, exceptional retention, or a technical result its funded competitors cannot reproduce. These are useful features inside a commodity race.

The strongest counterargument is the team. Raj and Vihaan Jagiasi are brothers rather than co-founders assembled for YC, and their skills are complementary: Raj worked in GTM at Deel, while Vihaan was founding engineer number three at Cadastral before its acquisition and previously generated more than 1,000 campaign signups in 48 hours. A two-person team that can both integrate and sell may move faster than older competitors burdened by enterprise deployments. Outcome-based pricing also aligns Elite with completed work rather than software consumption. Those facts make the company more credible than the generic “AI receptionist” pitch.

But the chronology cuts against the thesis-led interpretation. The founders launched Stratum’s government application-review product on July 29, 2026; Elite’s healthcare HIPAA notice followed on August 22. That rapid migration suggests a general AI-agent capability searching for a market, not a company organized around a healthcare secret before fundraising. No clinical-practice operating history, current-product customer, usage, revenue, founder ownership, or explicit full-time status is documented. The sibling relationship supports long alignment, but it cannot establish economic exposure or durable commitment to this particular problem.

There is one coherent revenue engine—payment per resolved interaction sold directly to practices—but no public rate, gross margin, sales cycle, conversion evidence, or customer expansion arithmetic. The $13.7 billion conversational-healthcare figure says nothing about value capture. Direct demo-led sales may fit multi-workflow practice deployments, yet integrations and implementation can make small practices expensive to acquire and support. Elite could create a specialty dataset from resolved calls and escalation outcomes, but a possible future dataset is not a present moat.

The known-exception logic does not rescue the investment. There is no independently verified zero-to-one technical event and no persistent customer behavior overriding competitive or safety risk. Nor does Elite yet match the unstated pattern of company formation around a concentrated operating thesis: the recent government-to-healthcare pivot points the other way. I pass. One condition would flip the decision: an independently verifiable six-month cohort of at least 25 practices in one specialty showing more than 70% autonomous resolution, zero serious clinical-safety events, and expanding paid usage. That would turn breadth into a beachhead and a slogan into a testable secret.
