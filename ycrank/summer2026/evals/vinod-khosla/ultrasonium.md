# Ultrasonium -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The attractive option is not “metal 3D printing grows at 24.2%.” It is that acoustic control could remove the powders, high-powered lasers, and post-processing that make complex metal production slow and expensive. Ultrasonium claims 15× faster production at 75% lower cost across aerospace, defense, energy, and compute. If true, that is a discontinuity. But the dossier quantifies only a $7.73 billion metal-printing market, not the machining cost pool, energy saved, population served, or manufacturing capacity unlocked. This is potentially consequential infrastructure without a quantified 100× consequence case.

The mechanism is sufficiently non-consensus to deserve attention: a patented hemispherical phased array confines and shapes molten metal using controlled acoustic fields. That is not another laser configuration. Yet 15× and 75% remain company-reported figures without test methods, geometries, production volumes, or direct measurements against Seurat, VulcanForms, or Fluent Metal. A novel mechanism is not a benchmark. Until the founders publish comparable density, surface finish, tolerance, deposition rate, and fully burdened part cost, I treat the technical discontinuity as plausible but unproven.

The strongest bull case is the team. Jack Qiu brings MIT control hardware and LBNL research; Chris Carter brings metallurgy, additive steel, and melt-pool simulation; Hunter Brown brings ITER and CERN engineering; Alexander Urbanski brings DOE-funded invention and manufacturing-monitoring experience. They have already assembled 17 people around several distinct technical risks, obtained a $305,000 NSF award, and secured an issued process patent. This matches the pattern of combining scarce disciplines around a difficult production workflow. What is missing is evidence of belief revision: no failed experiment, changed architecture, or recruiting decision tied to a newly discovered weakness is documented.

The money-versus-risk graph worries me. The NSF program sensibly starts with low-temperature alloys before aluminum and copper and names submillimeter accuracy and greater than 99% density as targets. Those are real technical milestones. But they remain anticipated results, with no disclosed pass/fail threshold, experiment cost, owner, timing, or stop branch. Meanwhile Ultrasonium lists fourteen openings and describes several beachheads—machinists, research, dental, medical, aerospace, defense, energy, and compute. Building organization and multiple sales motions before establishing repeatable metallurgy would be precisely backward.

The economic claim is also incomplete. Seventy-five percent lower cost could pass the unsubsidized-price gate, especially because eliminating powder handling, lasers, and post-processing attacks real cost inputs. But there is no machine price, utilization assumption, feedstock consumption, yield, maintenance burden, throughput calculation, or service model. Competitors have raised between $5.5 million and $575 million, demonstrating both demand and the capital intensity of commercialization. An SBIR award is an appropriate temporary bridge; it is not evidence that production economics work without subsidies.

I would not write the check today. My 36-month base case is a technically impressive low-temperature-alloy pilot with less than $2 million in annualized commercial revenue, still short of a repeatable aluminum-or-copper production system. The one condition that flips me is independently reproducible testing on representative aluminum or copper parts showing greater than 99% density, submillimeter accuracy, and at least 5× lower fully burdened part cost than a named incumbent process at sustained production throughput. That single result would convert Ultrasonium from an unusually well-recruited science project into a credible option on rebuilding metal manufacturing.
