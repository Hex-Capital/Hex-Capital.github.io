# Induction Labs -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Induction Labs has built before asking to be believed. Jonathan Li worked on reasoning and reinforcement-learning infrastructure at Cohere, published first-author NLP research at ACL 2023, and—with David Li—trained a 106B-A5B model on 575 million frames. Photon-1 is not a résumé claim or a fashionable interface over somebody else’s API. It is evidence of specific knowledge expressed through action.

The technical question is what remains when a well-capitalized competitor copies the paper. The answer is meaningful but incomplete: an index of two billion public videos, roughly two million filtered screen recordings, a proprietary keyframe filter, more than 100× representation compression, and custom fused kernels. These are real artifacts, and the reported 30× reduction in pretraining compute and 3× lower serving cost could make the architecture economically important. But the corpus comes from public video, the benchmark is internal, and H Company already offers APIs and open-weight models. Reproduction may be expensive; it is not yet structurally prevented.

The strongest bull case is that this is precisely how a strange foundational company should look at pre-seed: two technical founders, little commercial packaging, and an ambitious working model that allegedly beats Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite before the market understands the architecture. The known-exception logic applies when demonstrated product language and capital-efficient execution arrive before network or revenue evidence. Photon-1 earns that exception serious consideration. The problem is that an internal benchmark cannot distinguish a breakthrough from an evaluation boundary chosen by its creator.

The market is large, but the path through it is still asserted rather than demonstrated. A $7.63 billion AI-agent market growing toward $182.97 billion says nothing about who pays Induction Labs. The present wedge appears to be model access for computer-use-agent developers; the adjacent path into broader world models is supported only by small checkers and billiards experiments. A huge parent market is not room to maneuver unless the same capability unlocks identifiable neighboring transactions.

The unstated pattern fit is strongest in the shift toward proprietary models and infrastructure bottlenecks. It is weakest in permissionless leverage: users do not yet contribute data, distribution, or utility for one another, and no customer feedback loop has been disclosed. There is also no price, payer, transaction, or recurring revenue. I see no evidence of the founder red flags that would veto the opportunity—unfair dealing, artificial deadlines, reassurance-seeking, or financing used as a substitute for adoption—but the unexplained rights to two million screen recordings could become a structural liability rather than a normal diligence detail.

My base case is that the research asset becomes more valuable than the standalone business: within 36 months, Induction Labs is more likely to be acquired by Cohere for its team and architecture before reaching $1 million ARR than to establish an independent model platform. I would not invest today, but this is a watch rather than a dismissal. One verifiable condition flips the decision: three unaffiliated agent developers paying for Photon-1 and showing that pooled deployment data measurably improves success rates across customers. That single result would validate monetization, a compounding proprietary asset, and the beginning of a user-created learning loop.
