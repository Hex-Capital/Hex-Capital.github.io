# Hundred -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Erica Diaz’s supplier troubleshooting in China and Mexico matters more to me than the names on her résumé. Five-and-a-half years designing consumer hardware puts her close to the moment when a damaged component becomes an expensive assembly problem. That is specific knowledge acquired through action. Gosserand’s production-software experience and Liao’s quantitative research complement it. But the dossier does not identify an inspection system they previously built, sold, or operated with measured results. Hundred earns substantial founder credit without yet reaching my highest evidence band ([founder profiles](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/hundred)).

The investment question is what remains after a competitor copies the inspection software. Hundred describes controlled illumination, whole-tray imaging, and three complementary comparison methods, targeting flaws below 100 μm. Those are concrete engineering choices. They do not establish an exclusive asset. Hardware creates defensibility when reproducing its performance is difficult; requiring a physical station does not establish that difficulty. Retained photographs could become valuable, but undisclosed ownership and hypothetical switching costs receive no moat credit ([technical description](https://hundred.systems/how-it-works)).

The strongest argument against passing is that Hundred may be standardizing knowledge currently trapped inside experienced factory engineers. Calibration from one acceptable part could make sophisticated inspection accessible across many suppliers. Deployment underway to three founding partners gives that possibility more substance than a presentation alone. This fits my allocation toward physical bottlenecks. The unresolved mechanism is whether each installation makes the next installation easier. Promised onsite monitoring and customer-specific hardware changes could help discover a repeatable product, but they do not demonstrate one. Nor does the dossier show one customer improving another customer’s inspection results. I assign the network dimension its missing-evidence default ([partner program](https://hundred.systems/partners)).

Hundred names sensible buyers: contract manufacturers, OEMs, and component vendors. The same incoming-inspection capability could serve different sides of a component transaction. That provides some market breadth, but the $25.3 billion machine-vision estimate includes applications this tray station does not address. Elementary’s camera-retrofit offering also means customers have an alternative to buying new imaging hardware. Preferential founding-partner pricing indicates commercial intent; it establishes neither a payer nor a completed transaction. Monetization receives the default, without treating an undisclosed price as evidence of bad economics.

My exceptions do not presently overturn the pass. Exceptional founder conduct can outweigh an unclear wedge, and a demonstrated, unusually capital-efficient prototype can justify investing before distribution develops. Hundred has relevant founder experience and a described product, but the dossier does not establish those stronger override signals. It also supplies no evidence of dishonesty, artificial financing pressure, or founder dependence on reassurance. This is a structural underwriting gap, not an adverse judgment about the founders.

I would pass with my own money today. The verifiable flip condition is a paid second-customer deployment demonstrating that contractually retained learning from the first customer improves inspection accuracy without customer-specific hardware redesign. That would show Hundred beginning to compound knowledge across factories. Today, the dossier establishes people who understand the problem more clearly than it establishes an asset that compounds.
