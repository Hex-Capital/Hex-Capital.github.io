# Shiraz AI -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Contract manufacturers now have robots that can adapt from video rather than task-specific programming, and the controlling economic variable is whether Shiraz AI can redeploy each system across weekly production changes quickly enough to keep utilization—and therefore customer ROI—high.

The dossier identifies the mechanism but not its threshold. One demonstration without retraining could turn automation from fixed capital into flexible capacity. Yet Shiraz publishes no changeover time, task-success rate, exception-recovery rate, cycle time, or labor-cost comparison. “Operational at customer sites” proves the system left the lab; it does not prove that high-mix factories can run it economically. The governing belief remains falsifiable but unvalidated: a customer must be able to assign a new task without Shiraz engineers becoming the scarce resource.

The technology shift is real, but its value capture is uncertain. A $37.8 billion industrial-robotics market growing 9.5% annually is large enough; it does not establish that Shiraz expands the market’s margins. Supplying the hardware, model, and on-site deployment may eliminate integration friction, but it also exposes the company to installation labor, support costs, and hardware gross margins. Skild S1 already advertises the same single-video, no-fine-tuning capability with more than $2 billion behind it. If the model capability commoditizes, Shiraz must win through deployment economics and workflow ownership rather than the headline technical feature.

The strongest bull case is the focused boundary. Skild and Physical Intelligence pursue general models, while Shiraz is concentrating on contract manufacturers whose lines change weekly. A narrow full-stack system can outperform a broader model because factory value lives in permissions, safety, exception handling, and reliable execution—not benchmark generality. Robots operating at customer sites and references to first customers are meaningful pre-seed signals. They are still company-reported and lack deployment counts, named buyers, repeat usage, revenue, or production metrics, so I cannot distinguish product-market pull from technically successful pilots.

Navid Aghasadeghi and Mojtaba Mozaffar are unusually credible builders for this problem: Boston Dynamics and Rethink commercialization on one side, Amazon Robotics manipulation and policy learning on the other. Navid’s prior GenZArt venture reportedly reached one million users, providing some second-time operating exposure. The dossier does not document the costly commitment, prior-company lessons, or desperation that would earn full founder credit, and market evidence remains more important than credentials.

I also checked the relevant exceptions and red flags. Exceptional technical talent can justify underwriting before demand when the team controls genuinely scarce frontier capability; direct overlap from Skild, Standard Bots, and Micropsi weakens that argument here. Physical-world integration can be attractive when technology reconstructs incumbent margins, but Shiraz has disclosed no price, gross margin, deployment labor, or utilization advantage. My decision is **monitor, not invest today**. The flip condition is verifiable: three customer factories independently reassign Shiraz robots every week for three months while sustaining at least 95% task success without a Shiraz engineer on-site. That would validate both flexible automation and a scalable delivery model.
