# Neuromorphic -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: robot models can now transfer skills across hardware, wet labs can buy automated workflows without bespoke multiweek integration, and Neuromorphic becomes large only if it can repeatedly move new workflows into reliable daily operation within roughly one week. That deployment-time variable is concrete, and one biotechnology customer reached daily operations on that schedule. But one deployment does not establish repeatability, safety, intervention rate, or economics across robot bodies. The controlling belief is visible; its validation threshold is not yet met.

The strongest evidence is unusually fast customer pull for a pre-seed robotics company. Neuromorphic signed its first contract five days after changing strategy and brought that customer to daily operation within one week. This is more informative than an LOI or a demonstration because a physical system entered a recurring production workflow. It remains company-reported evidence from one concentrated customer, with no disclosed revenue or retention, so I would not yet call it product-market fit.

The technical shift is real but the economic step change is still asserted rather than measured. Cross-embodiment models demonstrated zero-shot transfer in 2026, and Neuromorphic composes navigation, manipulation, inspection, and tool-use skills through natural-language instructions and sensor feedback. The prior $5,000–$10,000 monthly Robotics-as-a-Service price suggests meaningful contract value, but it bundled hardware, monitoring, support, and software. Without gross margin, deployment labor, or intervention data, this could remain a service-heavy robotics business whose onboarding is faster but whose underlying economics have not changed.

The bear case is market crystallization happening above Neuromorphic. FieldAI, Skild AI, Physical Intelligence, and Generalist AI have collectively raised more than $4 billion to build overlapping cross-embodiment intelligence. Neuromorphic has no documented proprietary data advantage, structural barrier, or benchmark showing that its software outperforms those platforms. A stronger robot foundation model could absorb skill composition while hardware vendors own distribution. The wet-lab focus helps only if the company owns the operational layer—permissions, validation, recovery policies, audit history, and responsibility for completed experiments—not merely the natural-language interface.

The team makes this worth monitoring. Ege Doğanay has relevant manipulation and policy-generalization research; Vatan Aksoy Tezer has navigation and deployment experience across PickNik, Rapyuta, KABAM, and Augmentus; and the founders reportedly worked on deployments involving more than 1,000 robots. Their high-school robotics history also reduces the usual co-founder-risk prior. But the dossier shows domain learning, not second-time-founder learning, costly commitment, or the desperation signal I specifically look for. Founder strength cannot substitute for unresolved market economics.

I would not write the check today. There is no applicable exception: this is not a scarce frontier-model team creating a foundational layer, and no evidence yet shows product-style margins overriding the physical-business risk. The flip condition is verifiable: within six months, deploy three wet-lab customers on at least two robot platforms, each reaching daily production within ten days while sustaining at least 70% gross margin and fewer than one human intervention per 100 workflow runs. Meeting that condition would validate both the controlling variable and the owned-workflow thesis.
