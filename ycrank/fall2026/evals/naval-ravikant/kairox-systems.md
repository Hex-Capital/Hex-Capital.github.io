# Kairox Systems -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Kairox Systems built its first prototype with a $14,000 grant. That tells me more than three MIT degrees. The founders have begun acquiring specific knowledge through physical construction: putting flight, pressure containment, underwater propulsion, and controls into one vehicle. Jorge Cavazos’s prior control-law and hardware qualification work adds relevant operating experience. This is meaningful founder evidence, although the reported prototype establishes construction, not successful execution of a military mission ([Tempest](https://www.kairoxsys.com/tempest), [YC founder profile](https://www.ycombinator.com/founders/jorge-cavazos)).

Copying the software would not reproduce Tempest. Its assembled hardware and pressure-vessel architecture give Kairox substance beyond an autonomy interface. But hardware creates an engineering hurdle; ownership of a durable advantage requires more. At TRL 4, with no published range, depth, endurance, or transition reliability, the dossier establishes neither superior performance nor an accumulated asset competitors cannot obtain. SubUAS already demonstrates fly-and-swim capability. The investment question is whether Kairox’s fixed-wing configuration delivers a useful mission advantage over that alternative. Proprietary testing data could eventually compound, but I cannot purchase that future asset as though it already exists.

The initial buyer and expansion mechanism are reasonably concrete. A military unit could use the same air transit and submerged operation across reconnaissance, mine countermeasures, and strike missions, with payload changes extending the platform. SubUAS’s reported Navy Phase III awards exceeding $4 million establish procurement spending in the category, not revenue for Kairox. NATO’s maritime infrastructure protection activity supplies another adjacent operational setting. These are better market evidence than the publisher’s internally inconsistent $3 billion estimate, but they do not yet establish a large accessible market for Tempest.

The strongest argument for investing now is that waiting for qualification could surrender the inexpensive option on an unusually resourceful hardware team. Kairox fits my recent pattern of backing physical bottlenecks, and its inexpensive prototype deserves the exception I sometimes make for demonstrated construction before commercial validation. Against that, this is an institutionally gated product with no demonstrated customer-created distribution or utility loop. One military customer has not been shown to make the vehicle better for another. Government engagement and showcase selection do not establish a purchasing program. No pricing or realized revenue is disclosed; the grant funded development and does not prove value capture.

I am neutral at this stage. Kairox has earned attention through action, but the prototype exception does not settle mission economics or competitive advantage. The dossier supplies no evidence of integrity problems, artificial financing pressure, or founders abandoning direction; those are not reasons to withhold a check here. The unresolved issue is whether this integration produces something a buyer needs enough to purchase repeatedly. My flip condition is a completed, paid Marine Corps field trial that meets predefined mission requirements through repeated air–water–air cycles and produces a follow-on vehicle order. That single procurement milestone would connect the engineering achievement to a repeatable business.
