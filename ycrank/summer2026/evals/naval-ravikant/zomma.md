# Zomma -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest asset is specific knowledge acquired through work. Jihyun Kim built products at Goldman Sachs and Endowus, then helped launch Arrowpoint Investment Partners as head of product. Cashel Fitzgerald worked on backend systems supporting more than $20 billion in annual transactions, while Varun Arumugam built verification infrastructure at Apple and ML agents at Bronco AI. This is not a team discovering financial operations through customer interviews. One founder knows the workflow; two know how unreliable automation behaves inside consequential systems.

The weakness is that the product currently compounds inside each customer, not across customers. Zomma stores operator corrections as firm-specific memory and maintains seven-year append-only audit logs. Those are useful product properties, but neither is yet a durable asset: no accuracy, retention, workflow-reuse, or switching-cost data shows that deployments make the general system harder to copy. Browser Use and Asteroid make the underlying computer-control layer broadly available. If a competitor copied Zomma’s code tomorrow, the dossier does not establish what unavailable model, data, or network it would still lack.

The market has room to maneuver. Hedge funds, asset managers, and fintech firms share the same underlying problem across reconciliation, onboarding, KYC, and reporting, while the broader financial-services agent market is projected to reach $5.71 billion by 2034. But a broad market is not the same as scalable leverage. Each Zomma engagement requires workflow mapping, supervised runs, and a dedicated engineer; each new customer currently creates more work for the company rather than more value for existing customers. There is no contribution, referral, liquidity, or shared-data loop. This is forward-deployed labor wrapped around promising automation.

The strongest bull case is that the deployment burden is temporary training data. Unquantified use by hedge funds, asset managers, and fintechs suggests the agents have crossed from demo to production, and approval gates, isolated credentials, and immutable logs address the realities of regulated operations. A team combining investment-product experience with verification engineering could turn early exceptions into a reusable financial-operations model before horizontal browser-agent vendors learn the domain. If that happens, today’s services are the cost of acquiring a proprietary operating corpus.

I cannot yet underwrite that transition. Zomma’s earlier Vercel accelerator product handled enterprise sales and application support, while the current positioning is investment operations; the change may reflect fast learning, but there is no dated evidence showing conviction earned through repeated finance deployments. The unstated pattern I want—a permissionless system in which participants create value for one another—is absent. The prototype-before-network exception also fails today because capital efficiency and obsessive repeat usage are not documented. I see no integrity, artificial-deadline, or morale red flag, but technical reliability, reviewer fatigue, SOC 2 completion, and slow heterogeneous deployments remain real structural risks.

My 36-month base case is that Zomma reaches $2 million–$6 million ARR as a finance-automation vendor but remains constrained by forward-deployment labor rather than becoming a compounding software network. That can be a good company without yet being the asymmetric angel option I want. I would reopen the investment if three independent customers document that at least 80% of a new workflow deployment runs from reusable cross-customer capabilities without a dedicated Zomma engineer. Until then, I pass rather than manufacture a moat from the possibility of future data.
