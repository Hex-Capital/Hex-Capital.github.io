# Roma -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Roma’s consequential possibility is making delegated work cheap enough that everyone can have an assistant. That could matter enormously. But the dossier measures the AI-assistant software category—$7.11 billion in 2026—not the human effort Roma can eliminate. There is no quantified baseline of hours, spending, or beneficiaries, and no demonstrated multiplier. I can imagine a large consequence. I cannot credit my imagination as company evidence.

The decisive uncertainty sits between starting work and finishing it. Roma connects email, calendars, files, and a cloud browser, then keeps results beside each task. That is a working product architecture. Its browser also stops at unsupported verification steps, blocked automation, and payments. Those boundaries matter because supervision can consume the time delegation saves. Motion and Lindy already address adjacent workflows; Todoist already connects external agents. Roma supplies no comparative completion-rate, accuracy, or throughput benchmark. Using external models is not itself disqualifying, but neither the integrations nor the interface establish a technical discontinuity. The wrapper red flag remains a diligence question, not a proven indictment.

The strongest argument against passing is the team. Senderek built Loom, acquired by Dropbox in 2014; Eichenwald built Senic, acquired by Gira; Osborne brings Dropbox creative experience. These founders have made products people wanted, and Roma already ships on Mac and iPhone. Nine US App Store ratings averaging 5.0 are a small, favorable response. My actual investing pattern includes execution-heavy consumer businesses, so requiring a scientific invention here would misread that pattern. A task record could become the persistent place where users delegate work across otherwise fragmented assistants. That is a credible bull case. However, previous acquisitions establish delivery experience; they do not document belief revision or difficult recruiting against Roma’s current risks. Nor do nine ratings establish a committed community or a proprietary learning loop.

I would spend the next dollar measuring supervision, not adding connectors. The largest kill risk is whether Roma reliably removes more work than it creates through checking, corrections, and handoffs. The dossier gives no experiment budget, threshold, or response to failure, so I assign the missing-evidence default rather than presume poor discipline. Economics receive the same treatment: a free download reveals neither inference costs nor support burden nor willingness to pay. There is no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, but there is also no disclosed cost per successfully completed task.

I pass at 25/100. The known exceptions do not rescue this decision: the dossier establishes neither an extraordinary quantified societal consequence nor a documented instigator attacking a giant existing value pool with an expanding platform mechanism. Small-team execution alone is insufficient. My single flip condition is an independently reproducible, four-week trial across 30 users showing Roma reduces total human handling time by at least 90% versus their existing workflow, with corrections and approvals included and positive contribution margin at the price those users actually pay. That result would connect consequence, capability, and economics. Today those connections remain unproven.
