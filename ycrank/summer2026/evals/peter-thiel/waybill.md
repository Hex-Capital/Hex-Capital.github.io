# Waybill -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The possible secret is that hardware procurement should become an accountable operating layer, not another workflow tool: one party should own the path from BOM to stocked shelf. What would prove this wrong? If customers continue splitting purchasing, freight, customs, and inventory among specialists because no general operator can control cost and reliability across the chain. Waybill has not yet run that test. Its two-week launch produced requests for an obsolete RF component, 20 tons of aerospace-grade titanium, and four Cessna aircraft, but requests demonstrate unusual demand, not repeatable execution. The dossier’s “why now” is the fashionable rise of agentic supply-chain software, not a contested truth that named peers reject.

The founders are the strongest evidence. Rishi Laddha has handled aerospace procurement, cross-border operations, and government relations; Tushar Goyal built inventory and forecasting systems used across more than 400 Indian D2C brands. Both attended Plaksha University, and the five-person company appears operationally committed. This is real founder-market fit, but not yet the stronger foundational proof: the dossier does not establish that they previously shipped together, that all core work remains in-house, or that their ownership and economic incentives are durable.

Waybill’s central weakness is monopoly, not usefulness. “Hardware teams” is not a narrow beachhead, and procuring RF components, bulk titanium, and aircraft actually suggests three different operating systems. A supplier network across India, China, and Southeast Asia plus accumulated price and lead-time history could become proprietary infrastructure, but no transaction volume, exclusive supply, dominant segment share, or measured advantage exists. Cofactr, Forge, Jiga, and Luminovo already occupy overlapping territory. Full-stack responsibility differentiates the service today; it does not prevent competitors, distributors, or freight operators from reproducing it tomorrow.

The bull case is sharper than the current score suggests. These founders lived inside both sides of the problem, and early users immediately submitted difficult orders that ordinary catalog software cannot handle. Owning payment, authenticity checks, customs, freight, delivery, and inventory could generate a uniquely valuable landed-cost and supplier-reliability dataset. If one difficult category—obsolete aerospace electronics, for example—produced repeated transactions, the apparent coordination burden could become the moat. This also fits the unstated pattern of software moving into physical production systems.

But Waybill presently displays the hard-technology coordination red flag without technical proof sufficient to override it. Supplier settlement, broker provenance, customs classification, international freight, last-mile delivery, and inventory must all work before the customer experiences a single successful order. The company publishes no pricing, gross margin, sales cycle, completed delivery, or revenue. “Book a call” and requests for introductions identify a buyer and channel, but not customer economics. A bundled payment may conceal several thin-margin services rather than reveal one power-law revenue engine.

My base case is that within 36 months Waybill narrows into a cross-border aerospace and electronics procurement concierge and remains below $2 million ARR, valuable to customers but structurally competitive. I would reverse the pass if Waybill documents $1 million in annualized gross profit from one narrowly defined hardware segment with at least 70% repeat purchasing. That would simultaneously test the secret, expose viable economics, and show the beginnings of market ownership. Until then: capable founders, real pain, but operator rather than monopoly.
