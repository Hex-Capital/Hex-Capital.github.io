# Vernius Systems, Inc. -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The useful number is not the $500 million of LOIs. It is eight weeks. The founders found two concrete obstacles—manual visual acquisition in the final kilometer and six-figure radar seekers—and built a 24 GHz alternative, tested it at the front, and sold 45 units to special forces. That is unusually compressed movement from resistance to a changed situation. The evidence does not reveal every intermediate failure, but it shows the behavior I care about rather than merely asserting that the founders are resourceful.

The technical proof is real but incompletely measured. Archimedes is functioning hardware, integrates with Ukraine’s STING interceptor, and has crossed from a laboratory artifact into a frontline field test. The automated engineering pipeline and eight-week prototype cycle also suggest that Vernius can iterate hardware faster than a conventional defense contractor. But the claimed 1, 3, and 12 kilometer ranges are company-reported against 0 dBsm targets. There are no independent clutter, jamming, or false-alarm results. This is a fierce technical team with a deployed proof, not yet a conclusively validated seeker.

Special forces are a sufficiently narrow well. Forty-five sold units are much stronger evidence than six Launch YC votes or an impressive pile of non-revenue LOIs. Someone accepted the crude product in an environment where failure matters. What is missing is evidence that the same operators reordered, used the seekers repeatedly, or improved interception outcomes. I therefore believe the initial pull while discounting the claimed $500 million pipeline heavily. An LOI can be a polite way to say “perhaps later”; a repeat order cannot.

The idea also grew near the founders’ actual work. Arthur Nguyen-cao brought more than six years of RF PCB experience and led engineers at Defense Tech for Ukraine. Joe Gonzalez architected secure Tomahawk-fleet communications at Sandia. Arthur and Hisham El-Halabi had already built together at Auctify. Radar integration, military procurement, frontline testing, and dependence on cueing networks are all genuine schleps. The dossier does not document the precise moment one founder personally encountered this guidance failure, so the origin evidence stops short of the highest band, but this is plainly not an “AI-for-defense” idea manufactured from a market report.

The strongest objection is architectural. Vernius depends on an external cueing network before Archimedes acts and on vision guidance or a human pilot afterward. Cambridge Aerospace and Mara can optimize complete interceptor systems, while Vernius may become a component trapped between systems it does not control. Its solicitation of airframe partners activates the dangerous verb “partner,” and $500 million of LOIs could disguise the absence of repeat demand. There is also no evidence yet that the founders change routes when evidence turns against them; the running-back score must remain at its default. The counterweight is that they have already sold directly to operators and completed an integration, so distribution is not merely a future partnership story.

This fits the pattern of a technical team assembling a difficult substrate before asking investors to believe in a platform. No known-exception override is required: the cofounders have prior shared work, relevant domain experience, a functioning product, and paying deployment evidence. I would invest, but not treat the LOIs as value. My base case is that by August 2029 Vernius remains a modular seeker supplier and reaches $15–40 million in annual hardware revenue through several interceptor integrations. The verifiable flip condition is simple: if the present special-forces buyers do not place a repeat order after operational use with independently logged acquisition performance under clutter or jamming, I would pass on the next round.
