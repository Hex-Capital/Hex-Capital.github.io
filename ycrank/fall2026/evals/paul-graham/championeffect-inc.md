# ChampionEffect Inc. -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Califrais agreed to buy before ChampionEffect Inc. launched. That is the most interesting fact here. The founders persuaded someone to commit before there was a finished product to inspect. But what happened next? Did employees bring it real tasks? Where did it fail, and what did Kevin Law and Emre Simdi do about it? The dossier stops before that sequence. For me, that is where the investment decision begins.

These founders deserve a serious hearing. Law co-founded Cambly and led its technology; Simdi led its GTM organization and reports helping grow that business from zero to $100M+ revenue. They have worked together in education, with complementary responsibilities. That substantially strengthens the case that they can build and sell a learning product. Yet those biographies contain no specific obstacle followed by a shipped workaround. I cannot turn an impressive previous business into evidence of how quickly these founders respond when ChampionEffect gets stuck.

The product contains a useful hypothesis: the best instructions for doing your job may already exist in a colleague’s head. Capture that colleague’s walkthrough and make it available when another employee needs it. Slack, Teams, and calls put the coach near the work. The hard part is preserving enough context to make the instructions correct for the next employee, including what that employee is allowed to access. ChampionEffect has announced a launch, which earns limited credit for shipping. The claimed ability to learn from walkthroughs remains self-reported, without an inspectable demonstration or measured result. My pattern of backing concrete technical proof does not yet have much to attach to here; neither does the pattern of developers appropriating a programmable tool for unexpected uses.

The strongest argument against passing is that this team may already know how to turn teaching into a product, and Califrais has supplied an unusually early opportunity to learn. Founder-led outreach also matters. They are doing the selling themselves, and the customer commitment preceded the launch announcement. That counters my concern about founders who substitute a ceremonial launch for recruiting users. But a buyer wanting employees to adopt AI is different from employees wanting this coach back tomorrow. The contract value, payment status, and repeat usage are undisclosed. The $100M+ figure belongs to Cambly. ChampionEffect’s funding supplies no evidence about that distinction.

Section’s overlapping coaching offering makes the distinction consequential. ChampionEffect could discover a narrow task where its accumulated walkthroughs become indispensable. It could also discover that employers buy encouragement while employees keep asking their colleagues. Prior education experience makes the first outcome plausible, but the dossier does not document a founder’s own collision with this problem or a neglected implementation burden they have already undertaken. Nor does it show a hypothesis rejected and a product route changed. Those dimensions receive their missing-evidence defaults. I see no documented obstinacy or dependence on a large partner. I also find no exception strong enough to replace the missing founder-response evidence: no independently validated technical result or unmistakable repeated user pull.

I would pass on ChampionEffect Inc. today. Its strongest evidence concerns getting an initial buyer; its weakest concerns what the founders do under resistance. The founder gate therefore fails, despite a credible team and a sensible first experiment. My verifiable flip condition is a documented Califrais rollout showing a named obstacle, a founder-built fix shipped within a week, and employees returning to the repaired workflow weekly for the following month. That would connect selling, building, and learning in the sequence I want to fund.
