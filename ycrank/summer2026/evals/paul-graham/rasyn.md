# Rasyn -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when something broke. Rasyn launched Marigold in August 2026 with 54 executable tools, but the dossier never names a blocker, the founders’ workaround, or how quickly it changed a technical or user outcome. The recent movement from Larmor and Marcus through a retrosynthesis product to Marigold might represent a good running back changing routes. It might also be repackaging. Without the observation that caused each change, I cannot tell, so both resourcefulness and evidence-driven revision remain at their defaults.

The strongest evidence is the artifact itself. Marigold is live, with 14 tools on hosted endpoints and 40 running on user-connected hardware. Integrating that many chemistry programs is real work, and offering a 1,500-compute-unit trial suggests the founders expect researchers to test the system rather than admire a diagram. But the measured evidence cuts both ways: Rasyn’s retrosynthesis benchmark had a 30.3% Top-1 exact-match failure rate, and its own terms require experimental verification. This is a fierce-nerd signal, but not yet a proof spike comparable to an independently reproduced scientific result.

Who wants the crude product now? The only answer is a cached claim that seven medicinal-chemistry teams used the earlier Larmor product. The page is now gone, and the dossier gives no frequency, retention, payment, or example of a team forcing an unexpected workflow. The $89 researcher plan, $249-per-seat lab plan, and $2,000 institution plan are sensible experiments in selling, but prices are not demand. A live product aimed at a plausible niche earns more than the missing-evidence default; it does not yet establish pull.

Daood Hashmi’s computational-chemistry work at Caltech gives the team authentic proximity to the problem, and connecting software to laboratory hardware exposes Rasyn to integration work that easier “AI for chemistry” products may avoid. But proximity is not an origin story. I do not know which workflow Hashmi personally needed, what failed in the existing tools, or why the founders first built this rather than discovering a fashionable market. Nor is there public evidence about how long the founders have worked together. Rasyn avoids several obvious red flags—it is not waiting for a large partner, a press launch, or a sales hire—but absence of bad behavior cannot substitute for observed founder motion.

The bull case is substantial. Rasyn resembles the programmable-interface companies I repeatedly fund: it gives technical users a primitive that can compose many workflows, and it has assembled the integration substrate before claiming adoption. The closest known exception is Dropbox, where a working prototype and rapid correction outweighed thin cofounder history. Marigold satisfies the prototype half of that exception, but the dossier does not show the rapid correction or unmistakable user response that made the override work.

I am passing now because Rasyn clears the technical-proof side of my gate but not the founder-action side. My one flip condition is inspectable evidence of a dated chemistry failure that the founders corrected within weeks, followed by repeated use of the corrected workflow by at least five named medicinal-chemistry teams. That single sequence would show what broke, what they did, and whether users cared.
