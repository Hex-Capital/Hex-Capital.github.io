# Pennant -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Regulatory pressure and capable language models now let governance teams replace standardized proxy advice with client-specific analysis; the economic variable is whether Pennant can make buying its audit-ready workflow materially cheaper and safer than building it internally. The dossier supports the mechanism but not its threshold. A 97% incumbent share for ISS and Glass Lewis demonstrates concentrated spending, while JPMorgan’s internal effort demonstrates that sophisticated buyers can substitute engineering for a vendor. Pennant has not quantified the analyst hours, error rates, or implementation costs that determine which side wins.

Demand is the strongest evidence. A large U.S. public pension plan, a Top AmLaw 100 firm, and governance advisors are already paying; a global asset manager is a design partner; S&P 500 companies are engaged. For a two-person pre-seed company, this is meaningful product-market pull from distinct buyer types, not a waitlist or an unconsumed pilot. The remaining ambiguity is depth: the dossier does not disclose revenue, renewal, recurring usage, or whether these customers have completed a full proxy season on the product.

The workflow is more durable than a filing-summary wrapper. Pennant applies client policies, routes uncertain cases to people, and retains sources, approvals, overrides, and rationale. Better models should improve policy interpretation while Pennant continues to own permissions and decision history. But the proposed moat is still a hypothesis. Public filings, general models, and policy rules are reproducible; no disclosed integration, exclusive dataset, or switching-cost evidence establishes how much time and money a well-funded competitor would need to recreate the system.

The bull case is that unusually precise founder-market fit converts early customers into a compounding governance system of record. Ryan has worked in proxy voting at State Street, Aladdin product at BlackRock, and board advisory at PJT; Tomas combines Aladdin experience with AI-agent development and a prior startup. They have known each other for 15 years. That team could use the first voting workflow as distribution into stewardship research, activism preparation, and issuer advisory. Yet biography does not establish costly commitment, prior startup learning, or the economic discontinuity required to overcome a crystallized market containing Tumelo, Minerva, Proxymity, and entrenched internal systems.

The December 2025 executive order may increase demand for transparent, customized voting processes, but it does not itself change unit economics. Pennant offers custom pricing, integrations, workflow configuration, and support—potentially an excellent enterprise product, but also a path toward services-heavy delivery. None of the known exceptions clears this gap: this is not frontier research with scarce model-building talent, an abandoned category requiring market reconstruction, or a technical asset valuable independently of workflow ownership.

My 36-month base case is Pennant reaching $3–7 million ARR as a focused governance-software vendor, without yet displacing the major proxy-advisory platforms. I would monitor rather than invest today. The one condition that flips me is two current institutional customers renewing after a complete proxy season at higher contract values while documenting at least a 50% reduction in analyst review hours; that would connect the existing pull to the market’s controlling economic variable.
