# Rex -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The useful obstacle is not “accounts receivable is inefficient.” It is that Synthesia’s finance team had to retrieve purchase orders across 30–40 customer portals while inbound AR email doubled. Rex was onboarded in under three weeks, then performed 22,000 autonomous actions in four months and retrieved more than $40 million in purchase orders ([Rex Synthesia case study](https://www.rex.inc/customers/synthesia)). That is a shipped workaround, not a diagram. But the dossier does not isolate which blocker the founders personally encountered, what failed first, or how they changed the implementation. I therefore give strong but certainty-discounted credit for resourcefulness.

The technical proof is clearer. A two-person company is operating against more than $500 million in receivables from Fortune 100 companies and has reduced manual follow-up cycles from weeks to hours ([Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/rex-inc)). The interesting threshold is not whether an agent can compose a collection email. It is whether it can preserve context while acting across inboxes, ERPs, and hostile procurement portals without creating financial errors. Twenty-two thousand actions under customer procedures and approval gates show that Rex has crossed from AI demonstration to working system. The remaining uncertainty is how many actions required hidden manual rescue.

Synthesia is also a real narrow well. Its team tolerated an early product because the alternative was absorbing twice the email and dozens of portals with more people. Four months of autonomous activity is stronger evidence than Rex’s Product Hunt rank, accelerator prize, LinkedIn following, or conversations with 50 finance leaders. Those latter numbers are startup attention. The customer behavior is demand. Outcome- and SLA-based pricing could align Rex with cash actually recovered, though the absence of disclosed revenue means I cannot yet tell whether the customer values this as software or as inexpensive outsourced operations.

Kafka and Blackwood did not manufacture an “AI for finance” idea after seeing a market report. They spent four years together at Sequence, where their work touched order-to-cash systems, more than 50 ERP integrations, and hundreds of finance teams. Before that, Blackwood was an early GoCardless engineer and Kafka worked at Tessian. They have lived inside both the financial workflow and the integration schlep. Their four-year working relationship also removes the application-assembled-cofounder risk. This is the strongest part of Rex’s case: they know where the supposedly trivial automation turns into portals, disputes, approvals, audit trails, and customer-specific exceptions.

The strongest objection is that Rex may be an excellent implementation disguised as a software company. HighRadius already serves more than 600 clients, Kolleno markets overlapping agents, and Tabs attacks the adjacent contract-to-cash workflow. Only one customer implementation is described in detail. Pending SOC 2 Type II certification could slow precisely the enterprise deployments Rex needs, while outcome pricing and editable procedures could conceal founder-heavy services. Most importantly, the dossier shows no rejected hypothesis or evidence-driven route change. The founders may know this workflow so well that they have not yet had to behave like running backs.

I would still invest. The concrete proof spike—three-week onboarding, 22,000 actions, and $40 million of purchase orders retrieved—outweighs the accelerator and market narrative, and no red flag contradicts the lean. My forecast is that Rex reaches $10–20 million ARR within 36 months by becoming the execution layer for enterprise receivables rather than a general finance-agent suite. The verifiable flip condition is the next three enterprise deployments: if they cannot each reach recurring autonomous production within six weeks without founder-heavy exception handling, I would conclude the apparent software leverage is really a services business.
