# Archangel Health -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Archangel Health is selling something potentially useful: the difference between an AI describing the right medical action and actually executing it in an EHR. But what does my check buy? The dossier supplies neither proposed post-money valuation nor investor competition, and no capital-funded experiment with a deadline and a pass/fail result. Those are missing facts, not evidence of an auction or a bad financing plan. They nevertheless leave my two largest underwriting gates unanswered. I would pass on the evidence available.

The product addresses a failure that better medical knowledge alone does not fix. Stanford reports **46% single-attempt completion on harder PhysicianBench tasks**, which makes physician-graded execution environments a credible need ([Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/measuring-what-medical-ai-can-actually-do)). Archangel’s combination of sandboxed EHRs, adversarial cases, and physician feedback also fits a pattern I like: translating between clinical operations and model developers. Its business need not depend on agent errors disappearing; persistent errors could sustain demand. The question is whether Archangel owns the clinical access and evaluation assets that buyers cannot reproduce.

The strongest argument against passing is that frontier labs and medical AI companies reportedly **already use the infrastructure** ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/archangel-health)). For a two-person pre-seed team, that deserves attention. If those users repeatedly expose failures, contribute workflow context, and purchase subsequent evaluations, the work could compound into proprietary assets. But the dossier establishes neither that feedback loop nor paid conversion, retention, or exclusive rights. Stanford publishes benchmarking infrastructure, while Centaur and iMerit supply adjacent expert services. My exceptions for AI businesses require demonstrated customer behavior or unusually strong proprietary access. Reported use is encouraging; it does not yet establish either exception.

The founders offer some operating evidence. Bhatia’s prior Origins Customs business reportedly reached **$70,000 in annual revenue**, which is more informative than a credential alone; Patel’s SimCare and Cedars-Sinai experience places him near the clinical and technical systems being joined ([Bhatia](https://www.ycombinator.com/founders/aryaa-bhatia), [Patel](https://www.ycombinator.com/founders/tej-patel)). That earns modest founder credit, without establishing a shipped clinical deployment or privileged buyer access. The economics remain separate: **$25–60+ per evaluated case** is advertised contributor compensation, not customer pricing. Without contract revenue, review overhead, or repeatability data, I cannot tell whether each engagement builds an asset or starts another labor-intensive project.

Archangel could become a useful specialist business. I cannot yet underwrite its ability to scale from customer cash, and I see no affirmative evidence of the auction tactics, equal-founder paralysis, or financing dependency that would warrant a harsher rejection. My verifiable condition for reopening is **a financing proposal below $10 million post-money that funds a dated, customer-contracted test of repeatable, positive-contribution EHR evaluations**. That would connect price, a finite experiment, and actual purchasing behavior. Today, the clinical problem is clearer than the investment.
