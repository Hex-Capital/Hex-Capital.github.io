# Mireye -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Mireye’s 175 provenance-bearing geospatial fields make Mireye a magically weird physical-world abstraction whose highest signal is non-consensus product shape and whose lowest is frontier commitment. Mireye gives agents terrain, hazard, utility, and environmental context, yet the artifact remains four HTTP endpoints and an MCP server rather than a camera, rocket, well pad, or regulated operating system. I am a professional daydreamer, but Mireye keeps one foot in the physical world and the other safely inside a browser tab.

Crusoe is the high rung: messy physical infrastructure transformed into machine-usable compute through an idea that initially sounded ridiculous. Mireye shares the translation mechanism—fragmented federal data from USGS, NOAA, FEMA, EPA, EIA, and NREL becomes structured input an agent can reason over. Mireye diverges where the analogy matters most: Crusoe possessed oilfield adjacency, hardware, and visible investor rejection, while Mireye exposes public datasets through MCP during the hottest agent-infrastructure cycle and names no sophisticated investors who passed. Mireye is unusual enough to attract me, but not yet strange enough to prove it is underpriced.

HQ Trivia’s low-rung failure was software virality that never hardened into a durable physical or regulated moat, leaving every softer weakness exposed when governance fractured. Mireye’s 175 fields sound physical, but Mireye does not own the terrain, sensors, or underlying observations; federal agencies supply the data, and Mapbox or CARTO can add competing agent interfaces. Eighteen months of normalization could become operationally painful and valuable, but today Mireye is an aggregation layer without the capital intensity or regulatory scar tissue that compounds into my favorite kind of moat.

Ansh Chokshi previously built Poker Pit to $250,000 ARR in four months and sold it, which gives Mireye real Biz and Tiz rather than résumé cosplay. Mireye still lacks the autobiographical hinge: Seismic AI/ML work explains why Ansh can build an agent tool, but nothing cited explains why he had to solve geospatial uncertainty specifically. The score flips upward if Ansh personally operated a location-critical business—or if Shashwat Kapoor spent years wrestling these exact datasets in production—but Mireye currently reads as technically adjacent discovery, not a problem that followed either founder home.

Ansh Chokshi is explicitly Mireye’s CEO, and that designation should accelerate product, hiring, and pricing decisions as Mireye moves beyond free early access. The mechanism fails if “CEO” is merely a LinkedIn label: no public evidence describes the co-founder relationship, equity split, vesting, or the tiebreaker when Ansh and Shashwat disagree. Multiple founders are a rebuttal, not automatically a hard no, but over the next 18 months ambiguous authority can turn a coverage dispute into a constitutional crisis. Mireye has named the driver; Mireye has not shown me the steering agreement.

Flock Safety gave me a camera and a Wi-Fi signal I could encounter in the wild. Mireye gives developers a public field catalog, four documented REST endpoints, and a hosted MCP server, so Mireye can at least be used rather than merely narrated. The gap is tactile: a normal person cannot photograph Mireye’s confidence metadata, and an API answer about flood risk is not the flood sensor, parcel, or building itself. Mireye earns partial physical-world credit because every response points back to observable land, hazards, and infrastructure, but the closest artifact remains a JSON response carrying dataset vintage and retrieval time.

CARTO has raised at least $61 million and already owns a cloud-spatial-analysis surface, so my 36-month forecast is that CARTO acquires Mireye for under $50 million after Mireye reaches roughly $2 million to $5 million ARR. Mireye’s provenance schema and agent-native packaging can become a useful distribution wedge, while public source-data dependency and technically buildable interfaces cap Mireye’s odds of becoming an independent physical-world platform. I’m never playing the game I seem to be playing: Mireye looks like an AI-agent infrastructure company, but the likelier outcome is a strategically valuable feature bundle for an established geospatial platform.

Poker Pit’s $250,000 ARR sprint and acquisition make the strongest opposing case sting: Ansh may be the DeepMind-style exception, a cross-domain polymath whose prior velocity matters more than a tidy autobiographical origin. Mireye already has design partners, 175 normalized fields, confidence metadata, and a working MCP endpoint; Mireye could become the trust layer that stops physical-world agents from hallucinating, with query feedback compounding faster than Mapbox notices the category. The bear evidence still wins today: Mireye has no proprietary dataset or published performance advantage, remains free, and faces Mapbox’s 28 billion daily map detections. Three named design partners each paying at least $100,000 annually for repeated mission-critical use would flip my verdict to Invest.
