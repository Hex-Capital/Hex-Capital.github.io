# Ekho Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what Alina Park did when Ekho Labs first failed. The dossier shows a technically relevant founder, a broad data system, and forecasts. It does not name a customer rejection, broken integration, bad prediction, or licensing obstacle followed by a founder-built workaround. That is not evidence against her at pre-seed, but it leaves my most important test at its default. I know she can build intelligence systems. I do not yet know whether she behaves like a running back when the first route closes.

The strongest evidence is the artifact. Ekho says it continuously integrates more than 10,000 public and licensed sources, turns them into PO-level routing recommendations, and produces a 12-day median warning lead time. Its Hormuz case study reports seven confirmed forecasts and a ceasefire call 21 days before the April 8 event. Park’s earlier work on multilingual geolocation and enterprise financial intelligence makes this more believable than a generic “AI for supply chains” claim. But all the performance evidence is company-authored. Without false-positive rates, timestamped prediction records, or customer-verified outcomes, this is a working technical proof with a large certainty discount.

Who wants the crude product now? Ekho refers to clients and enterprise customers, but names none and reports no revenue, repeated usage, retention, or reroutes actually executed. A freight team reading continuously updated intelligence is weaker evidence than one changing a booking because of it. The biweekly brief, RANE appearance, and ZEBOX Club may open doors, but attention and access are not pull. The demo-led enterprise model also creates a familiar danger: distribution quietly becoming “find the right partner” before the founder has personally learned why ten users act or refuse to act.

The bull case stings because the underlying schlep is real. Combining AIS, carrier EDI, sanctions, weather, labor, government, and social feeds—then inserting recommendations into TMS and ERP systems—is precisely the sort of ugly integration work that superficial competitors underestimate. Park has relevant technical history, and a 21-day forecast advantage could be worth far more than the software price when emergency freight or missed commitments are at stake. The pre-round proof-spike pattern is present in outline: a difficult data substrate and numerical outputs built before institutional permission. Yet Everstream already reported more than 200 enterprise customers, while Altana, Interos, and Prewave have collectively raised hundreds of millions. Ekho’s narrower PO-level action layer could be a wedge, but without verified customer behavior it could also be a feature those incumbents add.

I also see no evidence that the product route has changed after a falsified hypothesis. That receives the neutral default rather than a penalty. Park’s background establishes proximity to intelligence ingestion and risk detection, but not that she personally encountered disrupted ocean routing as a user. The schlep-heavy wedge earns credit; the founder-originated need remains only partly proved. No known exception rescues the failed resourcefulness gate: the dossier has neither independently validated technical proof nor unmistakable user pull strong enough to substitute for it.

I would pass today. The one condition that would flip me is a timestamped case study from a named freight team showing that, after a specific failed forecast or integration blocker, Park shipped the corrective workaround within weeks and the customer then used Ekho’s PO-level reroutes repeatedly for at least eight weeks. That single record would test the founder, the forecast, and the narrow well at once.
