# TrustAI -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

TrustAI maps 51 tests across six risk categories and four compliance frameworks, making TrustAI an unsexy regulated-control swing whose highest signal is regulatory terrain and whose lowest is physical-world verifiability. The artifact is an analyst-signed report, but TrustAI’s sample report admits reconstructed results reach the live agent only as hypotheses. I can inspect the paperwork; I cannot yet watch the agent obey. That is governance imagined more vividly than governance demonstrated.

Vigil Labs is the high rung: regulated financial software where other investors’ discomfort becomes part of the moat. TrustAI shares that load-bearing mechanism because ERP permissions, EU AI Act duties beginning August 2, 2026, and control evidence are ugly problems enterprises cannot wish away. TrustAI diverges from Vigil Labs because its obstacle is not yet a compounding regulatory fortress: SAP and Oracle are embedding authorization services, four competitors have raised more than $342 million, and TrustAI remains a software assessment layered over infrastructure owned by someone else. The category is attractive; the present position is less magical.

DeepMind’s low-rung failure mode on physical-world verifiability was abstraction: you could not ride, scan, or photograph the intelligence, but Demis Hassabis’s chess-neuroscience-game-design compounding supplied the Known Exception. TrustAI repeats the abstraction without establishing that exception. TrustAI connects to an MCP hub and produces a report, yet several controls remain unassessed and projected reconstruction results are not verified production behavior. HQ Trivia could override governance risk because I could feel the product pulling millions of people into the same moment; TrustAI offers no equivalent observable pull.

Hannah Chung studied computer science, economics, and data science at MIT and worked at Virtu, the World Bank, and IFC, while Medha Venkatapathy researched LLM post-training and Muon. Those are credible adjacencies, not evidence that either founder lived through an enterprise agent breaching an SAP authorization boundary. TrustAI’s earlier browser-automation product also makes TrustAI look discovered through iteration around a hot technology rather than surfaced from a wound. The founder score flips an entire tier if Chung personally owned regulated-system controls or Venkatapathy operated a production agent whose permission drift caused a specific incident; the dossier supplies neither story.

Crusoe was magically weird because gas flares, Bitcoin, and North Dakota operations repelled investors for reasons inseparable from the opportunity. TrustAI enters a category where Noma, WitnessAI, Zenity, and Patronus AI have already attracted more than $342 million, so TrustAI is differentiated mainly by analyst-reviewed assessments and ERP blast-radius mapping, not by inhabiting forbidden ground. The analyst layer could become a contrarian wedge, but 679 launch reactions and a crowded agent-security market make TrustAI look timely before it looks non-consensus. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing; here, unfortunately, the game looks much like the game.

Hannah Chung is explicitly named CEO, and TrustAI’s founders report building together for three years, which is better than shared titles or a partnership assembled during YC. TrustAI still discloses no majority operating authority, vesting protocol, or disagreement tiebreaker. Over the next 18 months, that omission compounds because TrustAI must choose between assessment services, compliance evidence, and runtime enforcement while larger competitors pull toward all three. The browser-automation pivot suggests someone can make a hard decision; it does not establish who wins the next irreconcilable one.

Noma’s $132 million war chest and SAP’s embedded authorization services will push TrustAI toward the work incumbents dislike: bespoke evidence collection and analyst-reviewed compliance. Within 36 months, TrustAI will have pivoted into a tech-enabled AI compliance-assessment service generating $1 million–$3 million ARR rather than becoming the runtime control plane for enterprise agents. TrustAI’s report-centric product and mandatory analyst review create that outcome mechanically: services close the trust gap faster than reconstruction-local tests can become autonomous enforcement.

TrustAI’s strongest bull case is that 51 preregistered tests, no-code MCP connectivity, four framework mappings, and the August 2, 2026 EU AI Act deadline form a narrow doorway into every sensitive agent deployment. TrustAI could also claim the DeepMind exception: Venkatapathy’s physics and LLM research may signal a polymath capable of turning assessments into a technical control layer. That case stings because pre-seed companies often look like services immediately before their data begins compounding. TrustAI’s current evidence still loses the argument: no disclosed revenue or customer deployment, projected reconstruction results, embedded ERP controls, and no demonstrated proprietary dataset. Three named SAP or Oracle customers renewing annual TrustAI contracts after production deployment would flip my verdict. Until then, this is a rebuttal, not a hard no—but it is not yet my check.
