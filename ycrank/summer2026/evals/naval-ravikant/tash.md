# tash -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have earned the right to work on this problem. More than $1 million in combined card transactions, years spent buying at card shows and tracking prices, and a prior exchange that processed 710 trades across 250-plus listings are action, not résumé theater. The $22,000 of volume is small, but it proves that the team shipped into the exact market. This is tash’s strongest evidence.

The problem is that experience has not yet become leverage. If a competitor copies the multi-card index structure, tash still depends on the same graded-card supply, broker-dealers, securities rules, and market data available to everyone else. Rally, Collectable, Alt, and Dibbs have already assembled pieces of issuance, custody, valuation, or trading with far more capital. Proprietary transaction data, sourcing relationships, and liquidity are possible future assets; none exists in the current regulated product.

The $13.51 billion card market growing at a forecast 7.9% is large enough to support a business, but tash has not shown the adjacent market that supports an outlier. A regulated card-index security is narrower than the underlying collectibles market. Expansion into other collectibles is plausible, but plausibility is not a causal path. Regulation also makes each new product less permissionless: SEC qualification, Rialto Markets, North Capital, custody, sourcing, and portfolio management add principals and agents before they add users.

The strongest bull case is real. These founders first operated an exchange, learned that individual card selection creates friction, and moved toward diversified exposure. Their history together since high school lowers coordination risk, while their finance and data backgrounds fit the mechanics of investment vehicles. If the index launches, each additional holder could deepen secondary liquidity for every other holder. But there is only a waitlist today. Collectable halted secondary trading in 2024, showing that issuing shares does not automatically create a network. Liquidity is an outcome, not a product feature.

The trust surface is also unusually important because tash will custody assets and market investment performance. The CL50 presentation uses interpolated intermediate points while the final chart remains to be generated from Card Ladder’s daily data, and the legal pages still describe the prior exchange. Neither proves bad intent. Together they show that the disclosure system has not caught up with the financial product. When investing other people’s money, unfinished evidence is not cosmetic debt.

I pass. The team has specific knowledge, but the current design converts that knowledge into a managed financial product requiring proportional sourcing, compliance, and liquidity support—not yet a compounding system. I would reopen the decision if an SEC-qualified tash portfolio sustains at least $1 million of monthly secondary volume for six consecutive months while bid-ask spreads narrow as the holder base grows. That would demonstrate the missing user-created network rather than merely predict it.
