# TrueMile -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

TrueMile addresses a $464.60 billion transportation market. That establishes an existing spending pool. It does not establish how much of that pool its technology can eliminate. Better dispatch can reduce empty miles and wasted driver time, but the dossier supplies neither the baseline waste nor the achievable reduction. I cannot price an option on an extraordinary consequence merely by multiplying a large industry by the word AI.

Owning the fleet gives TrueMile something useful: responsibility for what happens after the optimizer makes a decision. Its sourcing, planning, dispatch, and communications agents could learn from actual road outcomes rather than recommendations that somebody else executes. That fits my preference for technology embedded in physical work. But the proprietary learning advantage remains hypothetical. aifleet already combines fleet operations with AI planning; TrueMile provides no benchmark showing which constraint it overcomes better. Human approval of loads and rate confirmations is a sensible reliability measure, while leaving the achievable labor leverage unmeasured. I would not label this a generic wrapper, but I cannot yet identify its technical discontinuity.

The strongest argument against passing is Harpreet Dhaliwal. Managing his family’s fleet from 19 and reporting more than $7 million in that operation gives him direct exposure to the decisions the product must improve. Those dollars belong to the prior operation, not TrueMile, but the experience matters. A founder who understands cancellations, return loads, and driver constraints could build a substantially better operating system by controlling execution. My execution-heavy investment patterns leave room for precisely that possibility. The exceptions to my technical-breakthrough preference require a credible instigator and a route from the initial wedge to much broader infrastructure. TrueMile establishes relevant operating experience; it has not established that expansion mechanism, documented belief revision, or recruitment against a named technical weakness. Listing Daniyar Abykhanov as founding engineer does not answer those questions.

The immediate experiment should be on the two dedicated lanes TrueMile is seeking in Texas and the Sunbelt. The existential question is whether coordinated optimization improves fully burdened economics while preserving delivery reliability. The dossier gives no test threshold, budget, timing, or response to failure, so the partner solicitation receives no credit as completed risk removal. Industry operating costs of $2.336 per mile in 2025 make this consequential: fuel, equipment, insurance, driver time, and empty miles must all enter the calculation. TrueMile discloses no present cost or bottom-up route below the incumbent baseline. Eventual autonomous-truck integration cannot fill that gap; Aurora’s commercial launch establishes neither a TrueMile partnership nor TrueMile’s economics.

I pass with my own money today. Missing public revenue is normal at this stage; the unresolved issue is what extraordinary capability the business is buying with fleet exposure. The red flags around advertising, exit orientation, and resistance to evidence are not established here, and engineering job advertisements do not prove premature scaling. My single flip condition is an independently auditable, 90-day matched-lane trial showing at least five times the dispatcher capacity and 20% lower fully burdened cost per loaded mile than conventional dispatch, with comparable on-time delivery and no subsidized inputs. Those are proposed thresholds, not company claims. That result would turn the founder’s operating knowledge into evidence of a consequential technical and economic advantage.
