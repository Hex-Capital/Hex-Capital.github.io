# Speko -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Speko’s contested truth is that no voice-model provider will remain best across languages, latency, accuracy, and cost, so the durable control point will be the router rather than the model. What would prove this wrong? If developers can obtain comparable routing inside LiveKit, OpenRouter, or Vapi without adopting a separate vendor, Speko is a feature, not a company. Its benchmark of 23 speech-to-text models, with four different winners across nine languages, supports the first half of the claim. It does not establish the second.

The founder discovered the problem by manually comparing voice providers for four years and has worked in voice technology since 2019. That is substantially better than attaching a current model to an unfamiliar workflow. Beknazar Abdikamalov is now leading a four-person team after serving as Hupo’s CTO and working at Amazon; there is no evidence of part-time leadership, outsourced core engineering, or a convenience-matched co-founder. But founder ownership, compensation, employee equity, and the team’s prior working history remain unknown. The foundation is plausible, not proved.

The decisive weakness is value capture. Speko publishes the benchmark data, does not train on customer audio or transcripts, and has identified no proprietary technology, exclusive data, network effect, scale economy, or brand advantage. Meanwhile, LiveKit already routes inference among providers, OpenRouter supports speech and transcription with quality-aware routing, and Vapi permits provider switching while owning more of the deployment workflow. This is explicit commodity competition inside a fashionable AI category—the red flag is present, not merely hypothetical.

The strongest bull case is that complexity itself becomes the moat. Speko already offers one API across STT, LLM, and TTS, pre-response failover, open SDKs, LiveKit and Pipecat adapters, and a hosted MCP server. One commercial pilot and early voice-agent partners provide more evidence than a concept-stage benchmark site, while frequent model releases could make continuous optimization indispensable. The 5% routing fee, $0.09-per-minute managed service, and enterprise commitments also form one coherent revenue engine rather than several unrelated experiments.

But the same facts expose the problem: open adapters lower adoption friction and switching costs simultaneously. Public benchmarks can attract developers without giving Speko ownership of their workloads, and two GitHub stars on each principal SDK, 12 Launch YC votes, and one pilot do not yet demonstrate compounding distribution. The 5% fee is vulnerable to compression because better-capitalized platforms can bundle routing into telephony, runtime, or model access. A $35.2 billion category forecast cannot turn an interchangeable layer into a power-law outcome.

Speko superficially matches the unstated pattern toward AI-production infrastructure, but it lacks the proprietary control point that makes infrastructure durable. Nor does any known-exception logic override the failed monopoly gate: there is no independently verified zero-to-one technical event, persistent transaction behavior under adversity, or deployed proof that removes the competitive risk. My verifiable flip condition is twelve production customers each processing at least one million minutes monthly while retaining Speko for twelve months despite having access to equivalent bundled routing. Until then, I expect Speko to remain below $2 million ARR or pivot within 36 months toward managed voice infrastructure or observability. I pass: the secret may be true, but the monopoly does not follow.
