# Hemlock -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Generalist robot policies create a buyer for demonstrations containing information video cannot capture. Hemlock’s controlling variable is the cost of collecting a usable, transferable force annotation: if wrist EMG makes that materially cheaper than instrumented gloves or environments, robotics labs have a reason to buy repeatedly. The Bracelet offers a concrete mechanism—pair muscular signals with egocentric video without covering the hand. What remains unproven is whether that advantage survives calibration across workers and tasks at commercial collection volumes.

The strongest evidence is that adding the signal reportedly changes robot performance. Hemlock claims 10× better egg pick-and-place success and 2.7× better lightbulb installation in its October 2026 launch. Those results support a technical capability opening, which is more interesting than selling another motion-capture device. But relative improvements without absolute success rates, sample sizes, or collection costs cannot establish the economic step change. I give these company-reported experiments credit for technical feasibility; they provide no evidence that customers are pulling the product into their training pipelines.

The strongest bull case is that Hemlock becomes an upstream supplier to several competing physical-AI companies. Its opportunity resembles my pattern of funding infrastructure across a technology transition: the eventual robot winner need not be identifiable for a useful training input to sell. October 2026 FACT research independently supports interest in force supervision. The same research also introduces EgoForce, however, demonstrating that buyers have another path to the input. Hemlock needs to capture value from force data, beyond establishing that force data improves training. The broader $1.05 billion market estimate includes simulation and internal collection; it does not measure the budget available to this company.

Ownership is the unresolved issue. A proprietary Bracelet gives Hemlock control over collection hardware, but the dossier documents no exclusive collection agreements, recurring customer integrations, or evaluation loop tying new demonstrations to deployed robot failures. Better robot models might increase demand for its data; they might also become less dependent on this particular signal. The claimed four wearable patents have no identifiers or established Hemlock ownership. I would ask how much money, time, and worker access a well-funded lab needs to reproduce the pipeline before assigning a data moat. Collection labor and calibration could also leave this with service economics despite an attractive technical result.

Olschwang’s wearable-hand-sensing project, $25,000 Davidson Fellowship in 2024, and UCLA robotics-data work make the technical direction credible. They do not establish prior startup learning or the specific costly commitment my founder criterion rewards. Nor does the dossier establish the rare research team and credible capital path needed for my frontier-research exception to override absent customer pull. With no documented repeat usage, payment, or commissioned collection, I would decline a personal check today while continuing to track the experiments. One verifiable flip condition would be a paid customer renewing after independently demonstrating a lower total cost per successful held-out robot task using Hemlock data versus its existing collection pipeline. That would connect the technical advantage to a purchasing decision.
