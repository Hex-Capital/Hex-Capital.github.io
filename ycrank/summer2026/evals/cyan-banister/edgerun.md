# Edgerun -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Infantry still carries batteries, armor, ammunition, and sensors, but powered assistance has become part of the kit rather than a laboratory costume. Edgerun has two visible steps toward that world: one-pound actuators exceeding 30 Nm and 21700 cells approaching 450 Wh/kg made a ten-pound system possible, then more than 100 Ukrainian field tests since May 2026 put that system onto actual bodies. That is a meaningful leak from the future. The dossier does not yet explain the remaining path through reliability, doctrine, logistics, and procurement, so I cannot treat the future chain as complete.

The broken rule is painfully concrete. Doctrine says 48 pounds; personnel carry 90–140 pounds; the body absorbs the difference, and load carriage reportedly causes twice as many evacuations as enemy contact. Why is the soldier still the suspension system? Edgerun attacks the hip, which it says supplies roughly 60% of loaded-walking force, while keeping batteries swappable and removal under five seconds. This returns physical endurance to the individual, although it does not yet remove the procurement institution controlling whether that benefit reaches them.

The strongest evidence is behavior, not the claimed 30–45% assistance. Ukrainian infantry, special-operations, and National Guard operators have participated in more than 100 field tests in roughly three months, and Balistyka is integrating the device with existing combat kit. Wearing experimental powered hardware during military field exercises is costly behavior: it consumes time, creates operational inconvenience, and exposes defects immediately. Internal performance numbers awaiting third-party verification receive no extra credit, but repeated use in this environment is much more informative than a rendering or an unsigned promise.

David Venegas has relevant operating tuition from Durin, Hadrian, and Stoke Space, spanning industrial operations, manufacturing, and aerospace software. The daily feedback loop with Kyiv operators also suggests that he is willing to place the product where reality can injure the thesis. What is missing is the pre-company obsession: no dated personal builds, independent exoskeleton experiments, or recruiting history shows that this problem owned him before the company existed. Eight employees and one clearly identified CEO avoid the disclosed governance trap, but résumés are not first-believer proof.

Here is the bear case that stings: Edgerun may be an intelligently assembled defense product built from increasingly available components, not a defining enabling layer. The company itself says the actuators, composites, and CAN bus are “nothing exotic by design.” Hypershell, DNSYS, Verve Motion, German Bionic, and Mawashi demonstrate that wearable assistance is already crowded; Edgerun has no disclosed patent, contract, deployed fleet, revenue, or independently verified performance. Its Ukrainian tests could become excellent product consulting that never survives American procurement, while Taiwan manufacturing and Kyiv operations add supply-chain fragility to hardware that must work when batteries, seals, and connectors are abused.

I would still invest, but not at a consensus-defense premium. The combination of a working artifact, repeated frontline testing, and an unusually legible human cost clears my bar despite incomplete founder biography and weak lower-stack control. My expectation is narrower than the grand story: by August 2029, Edgerun either has $5–15 million in annualized equipment and support revenue from U.S. or allied defense buyers, or the military product has stalled and the team has moved into industrial load assistance. The verifiable flip condition is an independent loaded-march trial showing less than 20% assistance—or a material reliability penalty—under representative combat kit; that would turn this into a pass because the observed testing would no longer prove the mechanism being purchased.
