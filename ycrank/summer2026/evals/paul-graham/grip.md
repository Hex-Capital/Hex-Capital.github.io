# Grip -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important missing fact is not revenue. It is what broke. Grip has a four-person team, custom gripper hardware, and a founder asking facility operators to contact him, but the dossier never names an obstacle the team encountered, the workaround they shipped, or how quickly it changed the machine or a customer outcome. Founder-led outreach is better than waiting for a partnership, but outreach alone does not prove resourcefulness. On my controlling test, the evidence is absent rather than adverse.

Jonas Gruetter supplies the strongest evidence. He worked on reinforcement learning and perception for irregular industrial manipulation, and his excavation research reached 70% field-test success with a 12-ton machine ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.17683)). That is measured technical work in the real world, not merely a robotics résumé. But it is not a measurement of Grip. The company discloses no pick rate, uptime, contamination-removal rate, dataset size, or deployed cell. The closest known-exception mechanism is Bindwell: unusual founders earned an override through numerical validation. The material difference is that Bindwell’s validation tested its current product thesis, while Gruetter’s result tested an adjacent machine and task.

Organic-waste operators are a plausible narrow well. Wet, tangled material is harder to manipulate than the dry recyclables targeted by many suction systems, and removing plastic before compost reaches fields is a concrete job rather than a generic sustainability claim. It is also full of useful schleps: dirty hardware, retrofits, maintenance, variable feedstocks, and facility-by-facility selling. Yet Grip has not identified one operator using the crude product, much less returning to it or paying. A named niche is not user pull.

The bull case is stronger than the traction table suggests. AMP, Glacier, Recycleye, and EverestLabs have raised between $24.3 million and $195.85 million, but their deployments concentrate on conventional recycling lines. Grip may have found the awkward substrate their suction and jet systems handle poorly. EU packaging rules applying from August 2026 add pressure, and the founder’s prior field-robotics work could let this team solve a problem that looks small only because it is unpleasant. If the custom gripper works reliably in organic waste, the ugly integration burden may become the moat.

But Grip does not yet match the concrete proof spikes I repeatedly find in exceptional technical founders. Its learning loop—successful and failed grasps improving the policy—is still an architecture claim without disclosed data volume. Nor is there evidence of a rejected hypothesis and a changed route, so I cannot tell whether this team behaves like a running back or merely has a sensible first plan. The usual red flags are mostly absent: there is no dependence on a large partner, premature sales hiring, press launch, or delegated product work. The unresolved cofounder history is simply missing information. The problem is not a visible anti-pattern; it is failure to clear the affirmative gate.

I would pass today. My 36-month base case is that Grip has either shut down or pivoted from organic-waste sorting into manipulation systems for excavation or mining, where the founder’s measured proof is presently stronger. One result would flip me: a named composting or biogas operator publishing a 60-day pilot showing commercially useful uptime and contamination reduction, together with one specific field failure the founders corrected in under two weeks. That would simultaneously prove the machine, the user need, and the behavior I care about most.
