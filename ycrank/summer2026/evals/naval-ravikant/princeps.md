# PRINCEPS -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders know pieces of the problem through action, but none has yet demonstrated the whole act of underwriting it. Anya Trofimova worked in data-center project finance, and Wilhem Hector modeled uncertainty in energy systems; those are relevant encounters with the assets and failure modes. Conrad Frøyland Moe has sold events, not insurance. The dossier identifies no policy they designed, carrier capacity they secured, or compute claim they priced. Specific knowledge is present, but fragmented across adjacent domains.

The market is real and expanding. More than $20 billion of GPU-backed debt and projected 2026 data-center spending of $822 billion create outages, collateral impairment, and contractual liabilities that someone must absorb. PRINCEPS also has room beyond one policy: operators, neoclouds, AI labs, lenders, and residual-value transactions share the same underlying risk model. The $4.85 billion 2025 market estimate, projected to reach $74.14 billion by 2035, is imperfect top-down evidence, but the causal path from compute financing to insurance demand is credible.

The problem is that a large risk pool does not determine who captures its economics. PRINCEPS combines SLA terms, market prices, grid data, and failure modes in a workbench, but code can reproduce the workbench. Parametrix already monitors more than 7,000 cloud services and 750 data centers; American Compute claims more than 600,000 hardware-pricing datapoints; Forward Compute publicly sells the same outage, contractual-liability, and GPU residual-value products. PRINCEPS discloses no exclusive telemetry, claims history, carrier rights, or proprietary model whose advantage compounds with deployment. The future dataset is a possibility, not a present asset.

The strongest bull case is that scarce loss histories are precisely the opening. Trofimova understands infrastructure finance, Hector understands physical-system uncertainty, Ornn could expose live marketplace reliability data, and carriers may need an intermediary willing to translate novel compute risks into bindable language. If PRINCEPS binds the first policies, every claim could improve pricing while the expanding compute market supplies nonlinear upside. This is a legitimate pre-seed option.

But today the company is an agent between principals. Carriers own capacity, operators own telemetry, and customers create neither utility nor distribution for one another. Two advertised GTM roles imply proportional outreach, while the inferred MGA model has no published payer, commission, premium volume, or bound policy. This does not yet match the permissionless or customer-contributed systems I repeatedly favor. Nor does a known-exception case override the weakness: there is neither an already-forming network nor exceptional evidence that the founders have shipped through this exact problem. I see no disclosed integrity, morale, or artificial-deadline red flag, but the absence of misconduct is a gate passed, not an advantage earned.

I pass at the current evidence state. PRINCEPS may become a useful specialist brokerage or underwriting workbench, but useful software inside somebody else’s risk and data network is not automatically a defining company. One verifiable condition flips me: an exclusive Ornn underwriting-data and distribution agreement that produces at least ten bound policies through a named carrier. That would convert the relationship from borrowed credibility into proprietary distribution, realized transactions, and a dataset that can compound.
