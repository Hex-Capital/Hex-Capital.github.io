# IMPACT Drones -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: no proposed post-money valuation, no competing-term-sheet count, and no evidence that this is genuinely the first scarce check. YC affiliation does not prove an auction, but it does not earn the benefit of the doubt either. With the financing terms absent, I cannot tell whether IMPACT Drones offers cheap exposure to a non-consensus belief or whether civilian counter-drone defense has already been priced as the next fashionable defense category.

The potentially valuable card flip is visible but underspecified. Q3 2026 pilots could test whether HUNTER and turbine-powered SABLE can autonomously intercept different threat classes under GPS jamming. Yet the dossier gives no capital-use plan, dated performance threshold, false-positive ceiling, or customer commitment that converts a successful pilot into a purchase. “Deploy pilots” is activity. A venture moment would be something like: achieve a specified interception rate at a named Middle Eastern site, within a defined debris envelope, and secure a paid annual protection contract by a fixed date. IMPACT has not stated that experiment.

The founders are the strongest part of the case. Jasper Lortije previously co-founded AeroIntel, built GNSS-denied visual navigation for long-range ISR drones, and helped secure the 2025 NATO Summit. Alessandro Fiume led AeroIntel sales. That is a credible translation layer between autonomous flight engineering and institutional security procurement, not two résumés reverse-engineered around a hot market. The two-airframe design also addresses a real operational tradeoff: close-range interception and faster targets are not the same mission.

The bull case stings because the timing and technical problem are real. The counter-UAS market was estimated at $6.6 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $20.3 billion by 2030; the EU adopted a counter-drone action plan in February 2026; and initial Middle East pilots are reportedly forthcoming. If those pilots demonstrate reliable GPS-denied interception, the founders’ prior operating history could produce the kind of proprietary deployment data and procurement access that later entrants cannot download. That could override weak early commercial evidence.

But none of that dark pool exists yet. The 875 LinkedIn followers, two press mentions, and 27 job applicants do not show that owned access caused a pilot, purchase, or retained deployment. Meanwhile, Origin Robotics is already deployed by four European NATO states, Fortem reports more than 5,500 captures, and 9 Mothers reports $1.6 million in sales. IMPACT is entering a field with operating receipts while publishing no intercept rate, environmental testing, false-positive rate, customer LOI, or defensibility signal.

The subscription wrapper also outruns the economics. Hardware, reloads, and monitoring are bundled without a disclosed price, gross margin, burn, runway, utilization assumption, or route to break-even. Worse, the proposed civilian customer may not control the decisive input: U.S. private-site operators lack clear independent mitigation authority, and kinetic interception around airports and energy infrastructure creates debris and mistaken-engagement risks. My 36-month base case is therefore that IMPACT either pivots toward government-authorized base defense or remains a pilot-stage hardware supplier rather than reaching meaningful civilian-infrastructure subscription revenue.

I am passing today. The one verifiable flip condition is a named civilian-infrastructure pilot that, by a fixed date, meets disclosed interception and false-positive thresholds under GPS denial and converts into a paid multi-site contract under documented legal authority. That single receipt would establish the venture moment, customer action, proprietary workflow, and the beginnings of repeatable economics. Without it, I am financing several unresolved gates at once—and I still do not know the price.
