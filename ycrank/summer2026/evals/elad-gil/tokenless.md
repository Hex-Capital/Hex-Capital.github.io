# Tokenless -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Tokenless sits at a useful failure point: RL teams can spend compute optimizing a defective grader and mistake exploitation for progress. The buyer is the team responsible for training quality. The controlling variable is whether an independent audit prevents enough wasted training expenditure to become a recurring budget item. The dossier establishes the failure mechanism. It does not establish the economic threshold at which customers consistently buy the solution. I would pass on a personal check today.

The technical evidence deserves attention. EnvCheck inspects graders, probes tasks in separate sandboxes, shares hypotheses, and confirms findings through objective checks or human review. That is an operational loop with actions and verification. The company reports 110 reward hacks and grading bugs across DeepSWE, Terminal-Bench, and BFCL, with 66 published write-ups. The [public findings](https://github.com/tokenless-ai/envcheck-findings) make the work inspectable, although they do not independently validate the aggregate claim. Better models could improve this auditing loop, provided Tokenless can verify their discoveries reliably. Its acknowledgment that future models may exploit its own judge makes that qualification consequential.

The strongest investment argument is that environment auditing becomes mandatory as reinforcement learning expands. This fits my pattern of funding infrastructure across a technology discontinuity: increasing model capability creates additional failure modes and demand for tools that expose them. Vals reported in August 2026 that benchmarks become exhausted within months. But benchmark exhaustion does not quantify the savings from Tokenless’s audits, their delivery cost, or customers’ willingness to outsource them. A valuable technical activity can still produce a small services market, especially when the largest buyers have capable internal research teams.

The distinction between technical output and customer pull matters here. The October 20 report of unnamed partners exploring domain-specific reward hacking indicates conversations, without establishing repeated deployment or purchasing behavior. The prior Touchy product’s $14.99 in-app purchase and Product Hunt results tell me nothing about demand for environment audits. I am applying the absent-demand default, without treating a recently launched company’s lack of disclosed revenue as evidence of rejection. The missing affirmative signal is a buyer returning because an earlier audit materially improved training economics.

There is also a plausible distribution tradeoff. Published methods and an MIT-licensed grader can attract developers while making parts of the workflow easier to reproduce. Tokenless could eventually own private environment integrations, accepted fixes, and regression history, but those assets are not documented today. A future proprietary auditor model earns no present moat credit; I would want the time, money, and access required to reproduce its advantage. Agarwal’s alignment research and Wang’s SkyPilot and Eventual experience support technical relevance. They do not establish the documented founder sacrifice or prior startup learning required by the founder criterion. The research-stage and scarce-talent exceptions remain conceivable, but this dossier does not establish the exceptional research position and credible capital path needed to override absent commercial pull.

My single reopening condition is three independent RL teams paying for EnvCheck across two successive environment releases, with customer-verified savings exceeding audit and remediation costs. That would connect the working technical loop to a repeatable buying decision. Until then, Tokenless has demonstrated a problem worth solving more clearly than a market it can capture.
