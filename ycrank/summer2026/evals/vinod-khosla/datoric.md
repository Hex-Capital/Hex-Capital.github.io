# Datoric -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Nearly seven figures of revenue in 30 days from a two-person pre-seed company is extraordinary execution. It is not yet an extraordinary consequence. Datoric addresses a $3.2 billion training-dataset market projected to reach $16.3 billion by 2033, but the dossier does not quantify how consent records and private collection change model capability, data cost, or the population benefiting by an order of magnitude. The downside is one check; the upside currently looks like a successful data supplier, not a rewritten societal cost curve.

The product attacks a real failure mode. Open contributor systems admit manipulated submissions, uncertain consent, and defective provenance; Reddy learned this by automating 15,000 hours of annotation work while bypassing platform QA. Datoric now ties each sample to contributor, consent, license, collection, and review records. Its 250,000 computer-use traces, 15,000 hours of screen activity, 300,000 active contributors, and commercial revenue prove operational capability. They do not prove a 5–10x technical discontinuity. There is no measured comparison with Scale AI, Turing, Mercor, or Prolific on accepted-sample cost, fraud rate, rights defects, collection speed, or downstream model performance.

The strongest bull case is that I am applying a technology test to an execution platform. The founders moved from Arzule to Datoric within six months, found urgent demand, and assembled a contributor network at startling speed. My known exception for **Block** applies in form: an initially undistinguished product can become infrastructure when a tiny, adaptive team enters a giant value pool. The material difference is scale and platform evidence. Datoric’s disclosed market begins at $3.2 billion, versus a financial system-sized pool, and the dossier shows neither repeat purchasing nor a proprietary learning loop that makes every collection structurally better.

The company has also not stated its largest kill risk. Contributor fraud and broken rights chains are obvious candidates, yet there is no experiment with a numerical failure threshold, cost, timing, and stop-or-pivot action. Nearly seven figures in one month can conceal one unusually large project. Spending the next dollar on sales or contributor expansion before measuring customer concentration, repeatability, and rights-defect rates would be the wrong graph: revenue added without existential risk removed.

Reddy and Lin deserve credit for direct exposure to the problem and tactical flexibility. Reddy knows the attack surface because he exploited it; Lin adds robotics, ML, and benchmark experience. Their change of product suggests learning, but the missing transition explanation prevents me from treating it as demonstrated belief revision. There is also no evidence that they have recruited specialized owners for licensing integrity, adversarial contributor systems, or modality-specific quality—the risks that could destroy buyer trust.

This is likely to become a meaningful, fast-growing data-services business. It may even reach tens of millions in annual revenue within three years. But Scale, Turing, Mercor, and Prolific demonstrate that contributor aggregation and managed data operations reward capital, customer access, and labor orchestration; Datoric has not yet shown the technical or economic break that prevents commoditization. I pass today. One verifiable condition flips me: an independent buyer audit showing Datoric delivers accepted, rights-clean multimodal training data at least 5x cheaper than Scale AI across two successive projects.
