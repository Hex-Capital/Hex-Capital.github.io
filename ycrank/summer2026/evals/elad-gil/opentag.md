# OpenTag -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: models can now execute multi-step work inside Slack and Teams, operations teams can buy shared machine labor rather than another chat seat, and the controlling variable is whether customers delegate recurring workflows frequently enough for accumulated permissions, memory, and process state to create switching costs. OpenTag has built around that variable, but the dossier does not measure it. Ten teams entered early access in two weeks and six are displayed as users; there is no run frequency, retention, expansion, or threshold showing that experimentation has become operational dependence.

The product is materially deeper than a chat wrapper. Each run receives a sandboxed cloud machine, connected tools, visible plans, approval gates, audit logs, and source-linked wiki updates. Customer-specific skills, learned formats, permissions, and memory remain inside isolated workspaces. Better models should improve task completion while those workflow assets persist, and routing across more than 80 models reduces dependence on any single provider. This is OpenTag’s strongest evidence: it is attempting to own execution and state, not merely reformat an answer.

The problem is market structure. Anthropic launched the same tag-a-coworker interface in Slack approximately two months earlier; Dust and Lindy already connect shared agents to enterprise tools, while Glean brings an enterprise graph and $300 million of ARR. OpenTag’s model choice and Teams support are useful features, but the dossier does not show that either changes adoption, customer economics, or gross margins. The $50 monthly plan may accelerate trials, yet it also implies that the initial wedge has little economic distance from functionality a model vendor can bundle. The product shift is real; OpenTag’s value-capture mechanism is not yet distinct.

The strongest bull case is that the founders are covering an agent discontinuity before the winning application layer has crystallized. They previously worked together at Lilac Labs, Tony Kam already co-founded a YC company, and the team combines enterprise, infrastructure, security, and regulated-software experience. Ten teams in two weeks suggests the interface has pull despite an unfinished product. But the known exceptions do not apply: this is not a scarce frontier-model team, a research breakthrough, or a reconstruction of incumbent unit economics. The red flag is precisely the current claim to differentiation—model routing and retained workspace data remain reproducible by well-capitalized platforms, and no switching cost has been measured.

I pass today. My base case is that by August 2029 OpenTag has pivoted from a horizontal AI coworker into an agent-execution governance layer for Slack and Teams and remains below $5 million ARR. One result would flip the decision: five of the ten early-access teams each sustaining at least $1,000 MRR and 50 completed workflows per week for 12 consecutive weeks. That would establish recurring delegation as the controlling variable, demonstrate economic pull, and turn the stored permissions and workflow state into evidence rather than architecture.
