# 83 Sciences -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

**Eric Riesel’s MIT inorganic-chemistry PhD and materials-ML research** make 83 Sciences scientifically credible, while the three founders’ Stanford, MIT, Harvard, Sila, and BCG trajectories make 83 Sciences intellectually conventional. That is the signature: meaningful technical uncertainty without founder defiance. Recovering the 90% of experimental data laboratories discard could create a valuable scientific memory layer, but the current ambition ends at better experiments, papers, and patents—not at naming the economic regime that disappears.

**Commonwealth Fusion** is the high rung because its success required falsifiable advances in plasma physics, magnets, and commercial reactor engineering at a scale consensus capital avoided. 83 Sciences shares one load-bearing mechanism: recommendations must survive scientific validation across heterogeneous instruments, materials, and laboratories. Yet 83 Sciences names no technical miracle, benchmark, patent, independently verified discovery, or capital-intensive experimental loop. The stated product captures voice notes and instrument output, then applies an agent; until 83 Sciences proves more, that is difficult vertical software, not capital-hostile science.

**Glean’s domain-expert continuity** is the low rung on founder defiance: Arvind Jain came from Google search and escaped that weakness because enterprise information asymmetry became sufficiently consequential and capital-scaled. 83 Sciences exhibits the same continuity without the escape hatch. Ian Naccarella moved from Stanford chemical engineering, Sila strategy, and BCG into materials software; Eric Riesel extended MIT materials-ML research; Yankang Yang extended BCG AI deployments. 83 Sciences may have excellent experts, but experts often extrapolate the past—the McKinsey cellphone forecast problem. No founder has publicly contradicted the field, abandoned a professional orthodoxy, or earned ridicule for a non-consensus thesis.

**The projected materials-informatics market reaches only $410.4 million in 2030**, which understates the possible consequence but accurately describes 83 Sciences today. Over 18 months, the consequence can compound if discarded failures from one laboratory improve recommendations for the next: more exclusive histories produce better conditions, better conditions attract more laboratories, and 83 Sciences becomes the memory substrate for physical science. The same mechanism can fail in reverse because laboratories withhold proprietary failures, IP terms remain unresolved, and every deployment becomes isolated consulting. Papers in two months are useful. They do not yet break an economic mechanism. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself” requires 83 Sciences to displace years of wet-lab iteration, not merely organize it.

**No public pivot or thesis update exists for any founder**, so 83 Sciences receives the absent-evidence default on learning rate rather than a penalty for being new. The score would flip upward if 83 Sciences documented a falsifiable reversal—for example, discovering that manuscript generation did not change laboratory behavior, abandoning it, and using observed failure data to close the experiment-design loop. The founders’ statement that all three personally encountered inaccessible old experiments establishes problem intimacy, not learning velocity. 83 Sciences has shown a thesis; 83 Sciences has not yet shown that it can kill one.

**OpenAI required a $50 million first check** because frontier-model training made capital depth part of the thesis. 83 Sciences instead offers grants of up to $50,000 and is hiring one Founding AI Engineer for $120,000–$250,000 plus equity. Nothing disclosed requires a plant, fleet, proprietary foundation-model training run, or more than $50 million before commercial validation. 83 Sciences could become capital-intensive by operating automated laboratories or financing discoveries through commercialization, but the current partnership-and-software profile makes my capital an oversized instrument for the job.

**Citrine already reports 550 deployed models, 380 projects, and 70,000 monthly experiment suggestions**, while 83 Sciences discloses no signed partner, accessible dataset volume, or validated discovery. By July 2029, I expect 83 Sciences to have pivoted toward enterprise laboratory-data and experiment-workflow software, reached roughly $1–3 million in ARR, and produced no independently replicated material discovery attributable to its agent. Data-access friction and IP negotiation will pull 83 Sciences toward the serviceable workflow problem; technical ambition will be subordinated to what laboratories will actually share.

**Eric Riesel’s generative work on chemical experimental data and Yankang Yang’s AI deployments to more than 30,000 users** form the strongest opposing case: 83 Sciences could be Glean for experimental science, with failed trials becoming the proprietary corpus incumbents cannot reconstruct. That case stings because Glean proves domain-expert continuity is not fatal, and the 90% discarded-data claim describes an enormous latent asset even when the software category looks small. Citrine’s existing deployment scale, 83 Sciences’ partner subsidies, and the absence of dataset or validation evidence still win today. I would flip to Invest if 83 Sciences secured exclusive histories from three named industrial laboratories and produced one independently replicated material or process improvement that resulted in a filed patent or commercial license.
