# Zomma -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important obstacle is concrete: finance teams must move data through 10–15 systems whose APIs are missing or inadequate. Zomma responded by building agents that operate the existing screens, PDFs, spreadsheets, and desktop applications, with human approval around sensitive actions. This is a shipped workaround, not a diagram. But the dossier never names the moment a particular portal broke, what a founder changed, how long the repair took, or whether the customer’s workflow then succeeded. I see resourcefulness in the product’s shape, but not yet the dated founder behavior required to prove it.

The technical evidence is real but blurry. Hedge funds, asset managers, and fintech firms reportedly use Zomma for reconciliation, onboarding, KYC, and reporting. The system also has isolated runtimes, stored operator corrections, policy checks, and seven-year append-only audit logs. Those details suggest functioning software. Yet there is no task-success rate, intervention rate, deployment time, or accuracy benchmark. Even Varun identifies failure handling, reviewer fatigue, credentials, and 2FA as unresolved. A fierce technical team has built something difficult before receiving permission; it has not yet turned that difficulty into a measured proof.

The team fits the schlep unusually well. Jihyun Kim worked at Goldman Sachs, Endowus, and Arrowpoint Investment Partners. Cashel Fitzgerald built systems supporting $20 billion in annual transactions, while Varun Arumugam worked on verification infrastructure at Apple and agent systems at Bronco AI. They combine knowledge of the ugly workflow with the ability to manipulate unreliable software. Founder-led selling and dedicated engineers are also appropriate now. The problem is that their biographies establish proximity, not the originating collision: the dossier does not tell me which workflow one of them personally endured and then built Zomma to eliminate.

Demand remains provisional. “Hedge funds, asset managers, and fintech firms” is a useful niche, and reconciliation and KYC are urgent jobs. But no customer is named, no revenue is disclosed, and there is no repeated-usage or retention figure. A workshop waitlist, 169 LinkedIn followers, and an accelerator appearance are investor-legible activity rather than user pull. The inferred enterprise model could produce substantial contracts, but requiring workflow mapping, supervised runs, and a dedicated engineer could just as easily produce a consulting business whose revenue scales with headcount.

The unexplained route change matters most. In April 2026, Vercel described Zomma as automating enterprise sales and application support; by August it was focused on investment operations. This could be excellent running-back behavior. It could also be a team searching for a category after general computer-use agents became crowded. The dossier provides neither the falsifying observation nor the transition date, so the change earns no credit for adaptability. Browser Use and Asteroid already make the control layer accessible, while Gradient Labs, Concourse, and Hadrius are better-funded around adjacent financial workflows. Zomma’s firm-specific correction memory could become a moat, but there is no deployment-reuse evidence yet—the sort of concrete pre-round proof spike that would distinguish it from a capable integrator.

The strongest case for investing now is that working software plus unusually complementary founders can precede public metrics. The missing cofounder history might even be overridden under the same mechanism that justified Dropbox: an impressive artifact can outweigh uncertainty about the team’s formation. The material difference is that Dropbox’s prototype and rapid founder correction were observable; Zomma’s reliability, customer pull, and reason for changing markets remain unmeasured. I see none of the loudest red flags—there is founder-led selling, no dependence on one large partner, and no plan to hire away customer learning—but the quiet red flag is that both the pivot and the deployments are described without their outcomes.

My 36-month base case is a services-heavy investment-operations company at $1–3 million ARR, useful to a small set of financial firms but constrained by bespoke deployments rather than becoming a general computer-use platform. I would not write the check today. One verifiable condition would flip me: a dated production case study naming a customer, showing the founders restoring a broken no-API workflow within seven days, followed by eight weeks of paid repeated use with under 5% human escalation. That would prove the obstacle, the response, the artifact, and the narrow well at once.
