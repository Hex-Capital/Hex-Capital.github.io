# Caution -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders’ knowledge was acquired through costly action. Anton Livaja built security systems at Ledn, contributed to OpenZeppelin audits, and co-founded Distrust; Lance Vick worked across Fitbit, BitGo, Unit 410, and Turnkey before joining him there ([Caution](https://caution.co/about.html), [Distrust](https://distrust.co/company.html)). The team has now converted that experience into a working source-to-runtime verification system with production deployments across five workload categories. This is specific knowledge made legible through software, customers, and years of collaboration—not résumé theater.

The problem is real: AWS Nitro can attest to a running binary without proving that it came from reviewed source. Caution closes that chain through reproducible builds, independently comparable hashes, and verification of the live enclave against its source, kernel, configuration, and toolchain ([Caution documentation](https://docs.caution.co/guides/verify-an-app/)). Independent verification is permissionless in spirit. An operator need not trust Caution’s assertion when a third party can reproduce the proof. But a verification mechanism is not yet a network effect; the dossier provides no evidence that one customer’s deployment improves another customer’s security, acquisition, or utility.

The fatal question is what remains when a competitor copies the code. Today, the complete platform is available under AGPLv3, the hardware trust root belongs to AWS, and OPAQUE, Edgeless Systems, and enclaive already provide overlapping attestation and confidential-computing infrastructure. Caution owns neither the hardware nor a proprietary model, scarce dataset, or demonstrated network. Reproducibility makes the product trustworthy while making the implementation inspectable. That is excellent security architecture and weak investment architecture unless deployments accumulate a durable asset.

The bull case is that deep founder knowledge plus early production usage allows Caution to become the neutral control plane across Nitro, Intel TDX, AMD SEV-SNP, and TPM-based systems. The confidential-computing market was estimated at $5.5 billion in 2023 and projected to reach $153.8 billion by 2030, while 84.5% of surveyed organizations cited attestation-chain validation as a barrier ([Grand View Research](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/confidential-computing-market-report), [IDC](https://confidentialcomputing.io/wp-content/uploads/sites/10/2025/11/US53866125.pdf)). Production deployments in crypto infrastructure, lending, consumer hardware, post-quantum security, and private AI inference show more breadth than a single crypto enclave tool. The shared capability can plausibly extend into a large adjacent assurance layer.

The known-exception argument is capital-efficient founders with an unusually coherent prototype before the durable loop becomes visible. It does not yet override the structural weakness here because Caution depends on a cloud vendor’s substrate, supports only AWS Nitro, and faces funded competitors already spanning multiple clouds. Nor do I find a founder red flag: the team has worked together since Distrust in 2021, and there is no evidence of unfair dealing, artificial deadlines, or reassurance-seeking. The problem is not the principals. It is that the asset currently compounds for the ecosystem more clearly than it compounds for the company.

I am neutral at this price-independent stage. My 36-month base case is that Caution becomes a respected open-source security product with several enterprise deployments but remains below $3 million ARR unless it expands into multi-hardware attestation and turns approved workload baselines or cross-customer verification into proprietary leverage. One condition flips me to invest: evidence that at least ten paying organizations verify workloads across two or more hardware trust roots through a shared baseline registry whose value and switching cost measurably increase with each deployment.
