# Daqstra -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Darell Chua and Anchit Kumar built liquid rocket engines together in the Mojave Desert before Daqstra existed, yet neither is identified as CEO. That pairing is Daqstra’s signature: unusually authentic problem ownership attached to unusually ambiguous authority. Daqstra did not discover fragmented test infrastructure in a market memo; Chua co-authored work on cryogenic rocket data acquisition, while Kumar endured more than 10 physical test campaigns. I see plenty of Tiz and the beginnings of Biz. I cannot yet see who carries the tiebreaker when the static-fire countdown reaches zero.

Anduril is the high rung because Palmer Luckey arrived in defense after shipping frontier hardware from his parents’ garage; the biography and artifact were inseparable. Daqstra sits just below that rung: Chua’s Rivian systems architecture and Kumar’s Mojave campaigns directly produced Daqstra’s integrations across NI, Siemens, Modbus, OPC-UA, and custom TCP systems. The divergence is scale of prior consequence. Daqstra’s founders have operated real LOX/IPA static-fire campaigns, but neither has yet shipped a comparable platform at commercial scale. Daqstra’s origin is lived, specific, and wonderfully dusty—it simply has not acquired the scar tissue of an Anduril-sized prior act.

HQ Trivia exposed the low-rung failure mechanism: an irresistible product can postpone a founder-authority problem, but it cannot dissolve one. Daqstra names Chua and Kumar only as “Founder,” while UC San Diego previously identified four co-founders and Daqstra offers no explanation for the roster contraction. No single CEO, equity structure, vesting arrangement, or conflict protocol is public, so I apply the governance default. Daqstra may have handled the conversation privately, but the evidence reads like a rebuttal rather than reassurance. Safety-critical abort logic makes unclear authority more dangerous here than it was inside a trivia app.

Nominal’s $155 million and Sift’s $67 million make Daqstra non-consensus only in a narrow sense: the category is already blessed, but Daqstra’s vendor-independent control layer remains an awkward industrial wedge. Over 18 months, Daqstra’s weirdness compounds if every NI, Siemens, and custom-TCP deployment contributes reusable connectors, test context, and control patterns; it evaporates if each deployment becomes bespoke integration labor. Daqstra’s palm-oil pilot targeting more than $100,000 in annual savings is the important oddity—the rocket-test product may secretly be an industrial orchestration company. I’m never playing the game I seem to be playing, but well-funded neighbors mean this is not Crusoe-level mispricing.

Daqstra would move into my highest frontier category if Daqstra owned a safety-certified edge appliance, proprietary sensing hardware, or a regulatory approval that competitors could not reproduce. Today Daqstra orchestrates capital-intensive artifacts without itself being capital-intensive hardware: engineers retain control, AI actions require human approval, and the moat is integrations rather than physics. The counterfactual is concrete—three production deployments where Daqstra’s abort logic becomes the certified system of record would turn software adjacent to rockets into infrastructure rockets cannot operate without.

Flock Safety was observable in a Wi-Fi scan before it became an $8.4 billion company; the artifact announced itself outside the pitch. Daqstra has its own physical proof: SEDS UCSD used Daqstra during a Dispatch heat-shield static-fire campaign, and active aerospace programs reportedly use Daqstra day to day across multiple rocket-engine stands. Daqstra cannot be handed to a friend like Pokémon Go, but I can watch a valve sequence, inspect a redline, and see whether the engine aborts safely. That places Daqstra above an abstract developer API and below a product whose pull a normal human can feel within 48 hours.

Daqstra’s founder-origin strength and governance weakness produce a specific 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Daqstra reaches $4 million to $8 million in ARR across aerospace and energy test operations, but an institutional financing requires Daqstra to name Darell Chua CEO and document explicit founder-control authority. The mechanism is founder-earned access to test stands followed by connector and historical-context switching costs. The ceiling arrives when enterprise buyers ask who owns a safety-critical decision and Daqstra cannot answer with two names.

Nominal’s $155 million war chest is the bear mechanism that stings: Daqstra could be a feature-rich integration consultancy trapped between Nominal’s test stack, Sift’s telemetry analysis, and customers extending LabVIEW or PLC tooling themselves. Daqstra’s unexplained move from four reported co-founders to two amplifies that risk, while undisclosed revenue leaves the palm-oil pilot and aerospace usage commercially unproven. The evidence that wins—for now—is that Daqstra has already crossed from slides into live LOX/IPA campaigns and daily program use, territory generic AI wrappers rarely touch. I would invest a measured personal check, but if Daqstra cannot show one paid production customer, repeatable deployment economics, and a named CEO within six months, I flip to Pass.
