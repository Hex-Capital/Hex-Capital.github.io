# TrueMile -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, a shipper might book a lane and let software coordinate human drivers, autonomous trucks, cancellations, and return freight. TrueMile wants to operate inside that world. I can imagine the path: reliable dispatch first, autonomous capacity integrated later. But imagination is my contribution here. Aurora’s May 2025 Dallas–Houston driverless launch establishes that one transition has begun; it does not establish that TrueMile owns a necessary piece of the destination. The dossier supplies an eventual integration strategy, without a company-specific artifact showing that future arriving early.

Harpreet is the reason I would take the meeting. Managing his family’s fleet from nineteen puts him close to the cancellations, telephone calls, and empty return trips this product addresses. His reported $7M-plus in historical trucking revenue suggests experience with the business levers, although it belongs to the family operation, and the claim remains founder-reported. That is meaningful operating context. It earns more confidence than degrees and an AI job title, but the dossier does not document a sequence of pre-company experiments or independently corroborated commercial achievements that would establish exceptional first-believer proof.

Why is the truck coming home empty? That is the question I want Harpreet to unpack. TrueMile identifies manual coordination and competing commitments, then proposes mathematical optimization and agents operating under human approval. There is a useful job here. The missing mechanism is who captures a rent today and how the driver gains economic control when it disappears. An owned carrier could improve its own scheduling while leaving the driver’s position essentially unchanged. The industry’s reported $2.336 operating cost per mile in 2025 makes better planning consequential, but establishes neither TrueMile’s savings nor a transfer of agency.

The strongest case against passing is that operating the fleet could be the experiment itself. TrueMile can connect a planning decision to an actual road outcome, and Harpreet has lived inside the operating problem. That fits my unstated preference for mundane businesses that finance and teach a harder technical destination. Yet fleet ownership alone does not demonstrate a scarce enabling layer, and aifleet already combines fleet operations with AI planning. TrueMile’s solicitation of two dedicated-lane partners is an invitation to begin that experiment; it is not evidence of repeat shipper commitments or drivers reorganizing their work around a functioning product. The differing software-platform and owned-carrier descriptions also leave unclear which business accumulates the proposed advantage.

I would pass with my own money today. The pre-revenue physical-AI exception remains available, but TrueMile has not supplied the functioning prototype and signed industrial commitments that would make it applicable. Human approval of every load and rate confirmation is a sensible operating boundary; the unresolved issue is whether the system produces a valuable result repeatedly. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently checked, ninety-day paid-lane trial showing that TrueMile’s planning improves contribution profit against conventional dispatch on comparable routes without worsening driver hours or delivery reliability. That would turn the interesting feedback-loop story into something I could underwrite.
