# Glen -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Glen’s contested truth would have to be: organizational advantage will accrue not to the company with the best model, but to the company that remembers how its humans and agents actually work. The observable falsifier is straightforward: teams given persistent context and distilled skills should complete recurring work materially faster or more accurately than teams querying Slack, GitHub, and documents directly. Glen reports no such experiment. Three testimonials and production availability establish use, but not the rejected consensus, measurable result, or technical secret that would make the company necessary rather than timely.

The more serious problem is monopoly. “Unified organizational context” is not a narrow beachhead; it is the broad market already contested by Glean, Dust, Mem0, Zep, agent platforms, and potentially model providers. Glen’s automatic capture and skill distillation are differentiated features, but the dossier identifies no proprietary technology, exclusive data, network effect, or measured switching cost. Dust already reports 3,000 organizations, 51,000 monthly active users, and 300,000 deployed agents. Competition is not merely possible here—it has arrived before Glen has defined what small market it can own.

The founder is the strongest evidence. Nikos Dritsakos previously operated at Composio, built an AI sales-coaching company, and sold it for $500,000 after roughly 18 months. That is real proximity to agent integrations and B2B workflows, not résumé decoration. As a solo founder, he avoids the risk of a convenience-matched founding team, and YC plus his LinkedIn identification as Glen’s founder suggests full-time commitment. But ownership, salary, engineering control, and economic exposure remain undisclosed, so the founding structure is promising rather than fully proved.

The bull case is that Dritsakos is seeing the problem early from inside the agent-tooling ecosystem. Glen launched publicly on June 1, has production onboarding, and names users at Amorphic Labs, Zaplar, and Composio. Founder-led calls and design partnerships are sensible distribution for an immature enterprise product. If those customers repeatedly reuse captured decisions and distilled skills, their histories could become a compounding asset. Yet the dossier gives no paid conversion, usage frequency, retention, buyer, price, or sales cycle. Worse, private sessions are deliberately excluded, public Slack channels provide incomplete coverage, and recalled context may be inaccurate. The privacy and accuracy protections needed to sell the product also weaken the corpus from which its proposed moat must emerge.

There is likewise no demonstrated power-law engine. Paid B2B subscriptions are inferred from Stripe and the terms of service; pricing, margins, expansion, and revenue are absent. A $5.86 billion enterprise-search market does not solve this arithmetic, especially when the company depends on OpenAI, Vercel, Neon, standard integrations, and MCP rather than controlling a scarce layer of the AI production stack. The known exception for a crowded AI market requires an independently verified zero-to-one technical event. Glen has not shown one. My 36-month base case is a pivot toward narrower developer-facing skill or memory infrastructure while remaining below $3 million ARR.

I pass today. The team signal is stronger than the company signal: capable founder, real problem, commodity position. One verifiable condition would flip the decision—20 paid agent-native companies, at least 18 retained after six months, with independently measured evidence that Glen’s distilled skills reduce recurring task time or error rates by at least 50%. That would convert the inferred secret into technical proof and identify a beachhead worth dominating.
