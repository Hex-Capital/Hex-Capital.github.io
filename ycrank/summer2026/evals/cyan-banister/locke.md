# Locke -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Parth Badhwar worked inside the Biden-Harris White House, Deloitte’s policy team, the Harris campaign, and public-affairs software before founding Locke. Locke is an ugly, regulated service business wrapped around an AI product—strongest where other investors dislike compliance and human complexity, weakest where I want something I can touch before believing the pitch. Locke has Biz and domain access; Tiz is still mostly asserted, and Rizz cannot be read from a dossier. I see a founder who is *of* government affairs, but not yet the environmental signal—the oil field, dinner table, or Wi-Fi name—that makes me follow him before I know what he is building.

**Anduril** is the high rung because regulation, moral discomfort, and real-world execution formed the moat rather than merely complicating the sale. Locke shares that mechanism: lobbying campaigns, public contracting, and Lobbying Disclosure Act thresholds repel clean SaaS investors and reward operators who know the rooms. Locke diverges because Anduril shipped hardware while Locke publicly shows Slack alerts, citation-backed briefs, and proposal drafting, with no disclosed proprietary dataset or verification system. The regulation is load-bearing; the technology risk is not yet magically weird.

**The consensus YC AI-agent darling** fails my tactile test because the model performs behind a polished abstraction that can only be judged through founder testimony. Locke approaches that low rung: Locke’s output is Slack updates, stakeholder maps, compliance briefs, and consultation-led service, while no public app or deployed artifact is available to install or observe. A customer can eventually touch the work product, but I cannot ride Locke like Uber, play Locke like Niantic, or notice Locke operating in the wild like Flock Safety. Nothing here earns the HQ Trivia exception where overwhelming product pull overrides another weakness.

Parth Badhwar’s government, campaign, policy-consulting, and Clipbook GTM experience can compound over the next 18 months because each Locke engagement should teach Locke which signals become meetings, registrations, contracts, and wins. That lived network is more defensible than the current software. The missing piece is autobiographical specificity: the dossier shows a résumé intersecting the problem, not the moment Badhwar felt the existing system fail him. He is visiting fewer rooms than a market analyst, but Locke has not yet shown that he had to build Locke before deciding to start a company.

Quorum serves more than 2,000 organizations, FiscalNote reports $84.1 million in ARR, and Plural already sells AI legislative tracking. Locke therefore sits in an awkward middle: government affairs is unsexy, but “AI agents for government affairs” is no longer non-consensus. Locke would flip upward if two established firms had rejected its approach as legally dangerous or operationally impossible while named customers repeatedly chose Locke for execution those incumbents could not provide. “First few partners” and a growing unnamed pipeline do not establish underpricing; they establish that Locke has entered a busy bazaar wearing a more service-heavy coat.

**HQ Trivia** is my single warning on founder authority: tactile product love once persuaded me to tolerate governance ambiguity, and the bill arrived later. Locke publicly names Badhwar as “Founder,” but Locke has two employees, no disclosed co-founder history, and no evidence of equity, vesting, or tiebreaker authority. The singular title is better than co-CEOs, yet it does not answer who the second person is or what happens when a lobbying judgment, model error, or client conflict divides them. There is no explosive consumer pull here to justify granting the HQ exception twice.

Locke’s two openings offer policy hires $72,000–$200,000 and technical hires $100,000–$200,000, revealing the mechanism I expect to dominate: expensive humans delivering regulated outcomes with AI improving their leverage. By July 2029, Locke will be a service-heavy government-affairs boutique at $3 million–$7 million ARR, with respectable retainers but without becoming a venture-scale software platform. Locke can build a good business there; the high regulation and low physical verifiability make the consultancy outcome more probable than a new category-defining system.

Locke’s “first few partners,” growing pipeline, founder-policy fit, and active hiring form the strongest bull case: Locke could use services as a Trojan horse, capture proprietary policy-to-outcome data, and become the operating system that Quorum eventually has to buy. That case stings because I am never playing the game I seem to be playing, and Uber and Postmates also hid infrastructure beneath a service wrapper. The dossier still gives the decisive evidence to the bear: no customer names, no revenue, no documented dataset, no verification process, and five named competitors already offering AI-enabled analysis. I would flip to Invest if Locke documents ten paying customers, at least $1 million ARR, and software gross margins above 70% without growing policy headcount proportionally.
