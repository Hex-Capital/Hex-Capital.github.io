# Orgo -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Better computer-use agents create a new infrastructure buyer: developers and agencies deploying persistent workers. Orgo’s opportunity depends on whether eliminating desktop infrastructure work makes its managed environments worth paying for repeatedly. The dossier identifies the bottleneck—assembling virtual machines, streaming, storage, isolation, and scaling—but the claimed months of avoided engineering remain unmeasured. That is a plausible controlling variable, with incomplete evidence that Orgo captures the resulting value.

The strongest evidence is that people already use the product. Orgo reports more than 1,000 teams and thousands of production desktops; the founder identifies Peter Steinberger as its first paying customer, and a customer describes agents operating across three businesses. Those are meaningful pre-seed signals. I discount the company-reported totals, but I would not equate them with a waitlist. Conversely, the $29/$99/$399 plans establish pricing, not revenue, retention, or infrastructure margins. Followers and GitHub stars add little to this underwriting.

The product architecture is more interesting than a model interface. Persistent files, programmatic desktop actions, SDKs, MCP, and deployment templates put Orgo inside the execution loop. Better models should increase the work these computers can perform while customer environments retain useful state. This fits my pattern of backing infrastructure across a technology transition. However, documented functionality establishes a product; the dossier’s inferred switching costs do not yet establish durable ownership. The disclosed account freezes and MCP failures matter because continuity is part of what customers are buying.

Competition makes that distinction decisive. Daytona raised $60 million on October 20, 2026, and offers overlapping persistent environments; E2B already supplies graphical desktops, while Cua exposes components customers can deploy themselves. The market is crystallizing around several funded suppliers. OpenClaw-driven demand may expand consumption substantially, but the dossier does not quantify a capability breakthrough, cost reduction, or margin advantage specific to Orgo. More agent activity could produce a large infrastructure market while leaving individual providers with weak pricing power.

The strongest argument against passing is distribution. Vasilescu previously operated computeruse.agency, and the founders describe agencies, tutorials, and demonstrations as acquisition channels. An agency deploying agents across multiple businesses could become a repeat buyer and a channel for adjacent products. That could turn Orgo’s early usage into expanding infrastructure consumption before competitors reproduce its customer relationships. But the dossier establishes the channel’s existence, not repeatable acquisition economics. Likewise, the founders’ relevant experience deserves attention, while documented costly commitment or lessons from a prior startup are absent; I use the founder default rather than infer motivation from credentials.

I would pass on writing a check today. The concern is value capture within an attractive transition, rather than lack of early interest. The known exceptions do not change that decision: Orgo has no documented frontier-research advantage or demonstrated reconstruction of incumbent unit economics. Nor is there evidence of fundraising vanity or dependence on one frozen model; the relevant red flag is reproducible infrastructure presented as potential defensibility. My flip condition is a six-month agency cohort showing expanding paid desktop hours at positive contribution margins. That single cohort result would connect distribution, recurring demand, and economic capture.
