# Vorflux -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Delegating complete engineering tasks could make software expertise radically cheaper. That is a consequence worth financing. But Vorflux has not yet connected its product to that consequence: the dossier supplies neither an engineering-cost baseline nor a measured reduction in human supervision. The adjacent $9.35 billion AI coding market measures spending on tools, not the value Vorflux can eliminate or unlock. I pass today. The missing piece is evidence of a discontinuity, not evidence that developers want help.

Vorflux coordinates planning, parallel implementation, cross-model review, and browser QA. These are useful capabilities. Cursor already supplies cloud execution and browser verification; Factory supplies multi-agent workflows and model routing. Configurable review and reuse of existing subscriptions may win customers, but the dossier shows no benchmark establishing that they overcome a binding incumbent limitation. Persistent repository memory could improve performance over time; it has not yet demonstrated a proprietary learning advantage. I would not declare this merely a wrapper. Equally, I cannot purchase a technical breakthrough on the strength of an orchestration diagram.

The strongest argument against passing is the team. Prasanna Sankar helped build Rippling as co-founder and CTO; Anant Nag brings Databricks staff-engineer experience; Haritha Elango brings Databricks and cloud-protection experience. These founders understand complex enterprise engineering. The reported July 2026 seed of $15 million gives them room to experiment, and customer stories show actual work getting done. My execution-heavy investment pattern leaves room for a company whose initial product contains no scientific breakthrough. The relevant exception would be exceptional builders using a narrow wedge to capture a giant existing value pool. Vorflux has the credible builders, but the dossier does not establish the expansion mechanism. Nor do prior titles establish the specific belief revision or difficult complementary recruiting that my founder criterion rewards. The mission-scale exception likewise lacks a quantified consequence large enough to override the missing mechanism.

The economics remain unresolved. Supermemory’s reported two-day optimization session consumed $600 in compute. That could be extraordinarily cheap if it replaced weeks of expert work; it could be expensive if substantial supervision and rework remained. The testimonial supplies no counterfactual. Published compute prices of $0.2117–$3.3868 per hour, plus model usage, disclose customer charges rather than cost per accepted engineering outcome or Vorflux’s contribution margin. Similarly, company-reported consumption exceeding one trillion tokens demonstrates activity without establishing productive output. I give no economic-parity credit for inputs consumed.

The largest kill risk is whether Vorflux reliably converts those inputs into accepted work with substantially less human effort than competing agents. The dossier provides verification features, but no decisive experiment with a threshold, budget, or stop condition; that receives the missing-evidence default. My single flip condition is a customer-auditable, prospective comparison across at least 100 representative production tasks showing Vorflux delivers accepted changes at **one-tenth the fully loaded cost of the best incumbent workflow**, including inference, compute, supervision, and rework, with no increase in escaped defects. That would establish a mechanism for collapsing the cost of expertise. Until then, the founders are more convincing than the demonstrated advantage.
