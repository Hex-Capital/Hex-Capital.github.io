# Versine -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

By 2032, I can imagine telling an assistant, “Settle the cabin weekend,” and having everyone reimbursed within permissions they already chose. Versine is pointing toward that behavior. But the interesting question is where the assistant’s authority stops: who can move whose money, under what circumstances, and what happens when it misunderstands? The October terms describe passkey-controlled wallets and capped payment rules. Those are useful specifications; the unlaunched product has yet to demonstrate the transition from giving instructions to safely delegating financial judgment.

Why does someone still have to open a payment app after their assistant calculates a reimbursement? Some of that friction is wasted effort. Some is the moment a person checks the recipient and amount. Versine needs to distinguish the two. Account-free claim links could spare recipients another signup, but the dossier identifies no quantified fee, institutional restriction, or gatekeeper rent that this removes. Accounts, sending, and bank payouts are free; company revenue is undisclosed. I see a convenience proposition whose economics and transfer of control remain unproved.

The strongest argument for this team is quite practical. Harshith has worked at Stripe; Param has worked on Databricks vector search and Glean evaluations. Payment integrations and unreliable agent behavior are both relevant operating problems. Their public repositories establish some independent building, but the dossier supplies no dated history of payments obsession, recruiting exceptional collaborators, or learning this particular problem from customers. The September–October move from agent authentication to payments could represent discovery of a deeper need. Its timing alone establishes neither opportunism nor conviction.

I also recognize an attractive pattern: an ordinary consumer task can expose infrastructure that many other products need. Versine proposes MCP/API access alongside its consumer interface, and Stripe’s March 2026 Machine Payments Protocol announcement makes programmable payments a concrete ecosystem development. But Bridge handles verification and payouts, Coinflow/Brale support funding, and Coinbase Developer Platform supplies wallets, according to Versine’s disclosures. The dossier does not establish which scarce dependency this company controls. Calling it infrastructure cannot supply that evidence.

Privacy is where I become much less comfortable experimenting with my own money. The privacy policy says balances and transaction histories are public on Base; payment interpretation also sends contact information to OpenAI with consent. Consent and disclosed training restrictions are meaningful controls, but they do not establish protection against linking a person to a public payment history. Meanwhile, the terms acknowledge agent manipulation, and completed blockchain payments may be irreversible. The architecture could save a few taps while making an ordinary reimbursement substantially more revealing and harder to correct.

I would pass today. My exception for pre-revenue companies remains available: revenue is unnecessary when a working artifact and unusually deep founder evidence make the future tangible. Here, the payments product has not launched, and 1,076 LinkedIn followers supply no evidence of delegated payment behavior. The bull case could still sting: a team with relevant engineering experience might turn recipient links into habitual distribution and discover the permissions layer other assistants adopt. I would reopen the decision on one verifiable result: an independently assessed 30-day pilot in which 20 households repeatedly delegate real reimbursements within their chosen limits, with testing showing that recipients’ identities cannot be linked to public transaction histories through the product.
