# Insurf -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Only 11.5% of denied Medicare Advantage requests were appealed in 2024, while 80.7% of appealed denials were overturned. Insurf is therefore a regulated-sector data bet with a real economic wedge—but without a definite multi-year founder plan. That high-regulation, low-definiteness pairing is Insurf’s signature. Insurf understands the bureaucracy well enough to retain policy versions, physician attestations, submission records, and payer outcomes. Yet Insurf describes a one-workflow pilot, not an irreversible destination. Regulation can create a monopoly; it does not create one automatically. A labyrinth is valuable only if one founder possesses the map.

**Anduril** turned regulated complexity into a structural moat because Lockheed and Raytheon could not escape cost-plus contracting. Insurf shares the regulated mechanism: CMS requirements took effect January 1, 2026, payer policies are fragmented, and production PHI requires BAAs and technical gates. Every resolved claim could deepen Insurf’s institutional knowledge. The analogy breaks where it matters. Insurf has no issued SOC 2 report, production PHI remains disabled, and Amperos, Ember, Cofactor, and Red Sky already traverse overlapping workflows. Insurf operates in a regulated market, but Insurf has not shown that regulation excludes rivals rather than merely slowing everyone. This is closer to an emerging integration moat than an Anduril-style barrier.

**Quora’s** indefinite roadmap was tolerable only because Adam D’Angelo’s Facebook CTO pedigree and potential knowledge-graph effects supplied the missing conviction. Insurf displays the same weak dimension without the override: no two-to-five-year milestones, no architectural commitment that Bryan Chung and Chang Lu refuse to abandon, and no sequence from appeals to an inevitable decision infrastructure monopoly. Insurf’s one-workflow pilot preserves existing systems, staff, and submission channels—a sensible sales tactic, but also the language of optionality. “We will integrate gradually” is not a plan. Insurf has described how to begin; Insurf has not described what must be true in 2029.

Insurf’s proposed coverage-decision graph could compound over 18 months if every appeal joins payer, policy version, clinical evidence, physician attestation, submission, decision, and payment. That is the possible secret: insurers appear to make case-specific decisions, but their inconsistencies may be predictable from a proprietary longitudinal graph. Yet Insurf has not named the incumbent institution whose architecture prevents building that graph. Ember already combines payer-policy intelligence, prior authorization, documentation, and appeals; Amperos automates denials through portals and calls. Without a named blind spot, Insurf risks becoming “AI for healthcare appeals”—the mimetic form in which consensus disguises itself as contrarianism.

Insurf’s monopoly score would flip if the coverage-decision graph produced demonstrably superior outcomes unavailable from customer-local records—for example, materially higher overturn rates across three named health systems because cross-customer payer patterns predicted the winning evidence. Insurf currently publishes neither graph size nor production outcomes, pricing, or switching-cost evidence. The counterfactual is clear: if each new provider improves every existing provider’s appeal success, Insurf has network density and pricing power; if each deployment merely automates that provider’s documents, Insurf is a services-heavy SaaS vendor competing with $20.2 million-funded Amperos. One year of workflow advantage is a feature. Ten years of exclusive payer intelligence would be a company.

**Stripe** is the relevant founder-lineage parallel: Patrick Collison was a credentialed outsider with unusual technical ability before he was a shipped-to-scale operator, and developer-first payments revealed intellectual independence rather than résumé prestige. Bryan Chung’s Brown applied mathematics–computer science and physics work, 4.0 GPA, and two IEEE publications give Insurf a legitimate technical proxy; Chang Lu’s Brown BS/MD path and Emory Winship modeling research give Insurf clinical proximity. But Insurf has no prior scaled product, acquired project, public repository, or adjacent operating record. Chung and Lu may be unusually capable students. The dossier does not yet show that Insurf’s founders possess the non-mimetic identity that turned Stripe’s youth into an advantage.

CMS’s January 1, 2026 requirements make the next 36 months unusually legible: by July 2029, I expect Insurf to have narrowed from “decision infrastructure” into a specialty-practice appeals managed service with less than $3 million ARR, rather than becoming an independent payer-intelligence platform or being acquired. Insurf can sell the immediate recovery of denied revenue because the 80.7% overturn rate makes ROI visible. Insurf will struggle to make the graph proprietary because customers, competitors, and payer-policy sources overlap, while physician review and staff-controlled submission preserve labor inside the loop. Regulation will keep Insurf alive; absent a definite monopoly plan, regulation will not make Insurf powerful.

The 80.7% overturn rate supplies the strongest bull case: Insurf could capture an enormous pool of abandoned revenue, use signed services agreements to enter hospitals, and turn versioned payer outcomes into a Quora-like graph exception before better-funded competitors recognize that the data—not automation—is the product. That case stings because payer fragmentation means accumulated exceptions may compound faster than generic models. Insurf still loses the argument today: no customer count, graph size, production outcome series, issued SOC 2 report, or operator-pedigree override is public, while Ember and Amperos already attack the same institutional seams. I would flip to Invest if Insurf verifies three named health-system contracts and shows that its cross-customer graph improves appeal overturn rates by at least 15 percentage points over each customer’s prior workflow.
