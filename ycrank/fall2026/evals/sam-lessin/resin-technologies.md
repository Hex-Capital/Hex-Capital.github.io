# Resin Technologies -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Resin Technologies has published seat prices, but the price of its equity is missing. So are the competing-term-sheet count and evidence that my check would fund something otherwise unfundable. YC membership establishes none of those facts. I cannot call this an auction, and I cannot underwrite it as a cheap, non-consensus entry. With my own money, that leaves the most important part of the deal unresolved.

The product offers a plausible venture moment: can requirements become a working, fabricated board with materially less engineering effort, after counting verification and rework? Resin generates editable schematics, extracts symbols and footprints from datasheets, and supplies placement and routing. That creates something concrete to test. But the dossier gives no capital-funded experiment, deadline, or pass/fail threshold. I will not award the founders credit for a financing plan I invented. Building a complete editor could reveal an important technical truth—or consume the seed round maintaining a broad software surface.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ position between AI deployment and physical engineering. Anmol Jawandha’s enterprise AI experience at You.com complements Koosha Rezaiezadeh’s robotics and electronics work at A&K Robotics, Bosch, and Kodak. Their available product makes this more substantive than two résumés attached to an idea. It fits my pattern of backing people who translate between systems: model-generated suggestions on one side, fabrication consequences on the other. Still, the prior roles do not document a shipped operating result spanning both sides. The biography supports a credible attempt; it does not yet establish privileged access or execution superiority.

Resin’s potential durable asset is the engineering workflow around the model: trusted libraries, accepted edits, design history, and knowledge of what actually survives fabrication. None is demonstrated as a purchasing advantage here. The 176 LinkedIn followers, YC launch, promotional credits, and founder onboarding establish ways to solicit users, without documenting purchases or repeat use. Flux already offers a competing browser workspace and AI editing. Resin calls its router proprietary, but supplies no independent comparison. The opportunity is to own a useful engineering loop; the evidence currently establishes that the team has built tools to enter that loop.

The economics need the same distinction. Pro at $199 monthly and Team at $149 per user are offers, not realized revenue or evidence of a self-funding business. Resin discloses possible incorrect wiring, unsuitable components, and misread datasheets, with engineering review as a mitigation. That candor is sensible. It also means saved drafting time must exceed verification and rework costs before the customer has received value. There is no evidence that the founders assume errors disappear, so I do not assign that red flag. There is also no margin, burn, or runway evidence supporting a path without another round.

I pass today. My known exceptions to missing price or experiment gates require documented conversion, cash economics, or unusually strong external validation; Resin’s functioning product and relevant backgrounds do not yet supply that override. One verifiable condition would reopen underwriting: a sub-$10-million post-money financing proposal funding a six-month test in which three named paying teams repeatedly fabricate working boards through Resin, with measured engineering savings and positive contribution after inference and support costs. That would connect the scarce check to a result worth owning.
