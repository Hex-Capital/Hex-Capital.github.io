# Bayworks -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I want the mechanic’s explanation to become the repair record as the work happens. No second shift at a keyboard, no warranty administrator chasing a missing measurement. Bayworks has a sensible starting point: dictate against a repair-order line, answer follow-up questions, review the cause-and-correction narrative. But getting from that useful tool to intelligence running automotive operations requires reliable capture, trusted reimbursement outcomes, and durable access across dealership systems. The dossier establishes the starting workflow; it does not yet establish those transitions. My imagined future cannot earn the company points.

The little system worth investigating is the warranty narrative. Why does someone qualified to repair a vehicle have to spend billable time translating that work into prose? Warranty administrators require particular information, and incomplete explanations create another round of work. Bayworks prompts for missing findings while the technician still has the repair in mind. That could return productive time to the person holding the wrench. This is a specific institutional friction, although the dossier does not quantify minutes lost, rejected claims, or how much recovered value reaches technicians rather than dealership owners.

The strongest argument against my passing is that Bayworks may already be spreading through precisely this ordinary irritation. The company reports reaching **80+ dealerships in 110 days**, including an unnamed top-50 US dealer group. That deserves attention at pre-seed. The Android release dated **October 3, 2026** and employer-provisioned accounts also put a working artifact behind the story. But the dealerships’ **4,000 daily repairs** are their operating volume, not demonstrated Bayworks usage. I cannot turn that denominator into thousands of technicians repeatedly choosing the product. Nor is the customer’s $3B-plus revenue Bayworks revenue. The missing signal is behavioral depth, not a larger headline.

This fits my pattern of finding opportunity in unglamorous workflow repair. Whether it becomes concealed infrastructure is much less clear. Bayworks retrieves previous repair stories, but its privacy policy says dealership operational data belongs to the dealership; reusable learning rights remain unverified. Meanwhile, **Tekion already offers native technician voice documentation** and controls its own system of record. Bayworks needs DMS access to deliver its benefit, without demonstrated control over that dependency. Dealership ownership of records is not a privacy red flag by itself, and the dossier establishes no severe surveillance problem. It also does not establish technician consent or limiting architecture well enough for me to assume those questions are settled.

Bansal is identified as CEO and Goyal as CTO, so I have no evidence of a leadership shell game or equal-control deadlock. Goyal’s BITS computer-science education and backend work at Navi support implementation ability. They do not establish the pre-company automotive obsession, repeated experiments, or first-believer recruiting that would make me underwrite a much longer journey. My willingness to fund a pre-revenue prototype applies when that deeper founder evidence carries the bet; it is absent here. I would pass with my own money today. **One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an independently checked 90-day dealership cohort showing sustained technician use that measurably converts documentation time into additional paid repair work.** That would establish that Bayworks changes the economics of a working day.
