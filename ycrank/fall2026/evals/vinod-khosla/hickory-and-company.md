# Hickory & Company -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Hickory & Company could make regulated financial expertise substantially cheaper. That is a consequence worth examining: sponsor banks remain accountable for partners whose operations they cannot directly control. But the dossier does not quantify compliance labor, institutions reached, or the reduction in resources required. The adjacent $2.3 billion AML software estimate for 2026 is not a measurement of the cost pool Hickory could eliminate. I cannot price an extraordinary outcome from that number.

The decisive technical question is how much work disappears after accounting for verification. Analyst Studio retrieves source-linked information; agents collect documents and draft against acceptance criteria; employees approve decisions. Those are sensible design choices. They do not establish a discontinuity. Parcha offers configurable agents and audit logs, while Bretton AI works across existing investigation systems. Hickory supplies no comparative result showing that its institutional knowledge produces materially better accuracy, throughput, or cost. Human review could enable deployment—or consume most of the promised savings. The terms acknowledge inaccurate outputs, but that disclosure alone proves neither outcome.

The strongest argument for investing is the team. Joshua Howland has built inside banking, including as Seis co-founder and CTO. His reported 500,000 accounts and $10 million revenue within 16 months belong to Seis, subsequently closed; they still demonstrate experience operating financial products. Scott Grow brings more than 20 years of compliance experience and a CRCM credential, alongside Kevin Anderson’s banking-systems engineering background. That combination deserves affirmative recruiting credit. It gives Hickory complementary capabilities around the workflow. It does not yet document rapid belief revision or recruitment against an explicitly identified weakness.

My actual investing pattern includes execution-heavy financial businesses. Scientific novelty is not an absolute requirement. **Block** is the relevant exception: a small team’s initial product opened a route into a much larger financial infrastructure opportunity. Hickory shares the possibility of entering through a narrow banking workflow, but the dossier establishes neither a comparable expansion mechanism nor control over a giant existing value pool. The founder’s operating experience makes that possibility more credible; it does not supply the missing mechanism. There is also no documented scientific learning loop here that strengthens with every completed investigation.

The claimed live-bank integration is encouraging access evidence, although the institution is unnamed. It is not a demonstrated answer to the largest kill risk: whether reliable automation survives real institutional review at attractive economics. No disclosed experiment specifies a failure threshold, cost, or next decision. The reported September 2026 financing of $500,000 and 22 launch videos do not establish how capital is allocated, so I would not infer premature scaling from them. Nor do public prices, review costs, or deployment requirements establish unsubsidized cost parity. These are missing measurements, not evidence of founder misconduct or permanent economic failure.

I pass with my own money. Hickory & Company has more credible domain capability than technical or economic proof. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independently audited deployment at a named bank showing at least a 10x reduction in fully loaded cost per accepted compliance workpaper, including implementation and human review, with no deterioration in material-error detection against the bank’s existing process. That would demonstrate that Hickory removes expensive work rather than moving it into verification.
