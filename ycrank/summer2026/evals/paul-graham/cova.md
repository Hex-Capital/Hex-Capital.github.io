# Cova -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important move was not adding AI to home care. It was ceasing to be merely a software vendor. After a year building systems for agencies and hospitals, Jordan Ibe and Ansh Tandon converted Omnition into Cova’s internal operating system, obtained a Michigan provider license, and began operating the agency themselves. That is a real shipped workaround to the industry’s fragmentation. But the dossier does not identify the decisive failed deployment, how quickly the founders changed course, or whether the new structure shortened enrollment. I see resourcefulness, but not yet the complete obstacle-action-result sequence.

This is an unusually authentic schlep. The founders worked together in Palantir Healthcare, encountered healthcare workflows directly, and chose to bill Medicaid, managed-care organizations, long-term-care insurers, and families rather than selling a dashboard to someone else. They have accepted licensing, reimbursement, enrollment, insurance, and billing—the ugly parts most software founders try to place outside the product. Even the requirement that applicants complete four state forms and text photographs of every page is useful evidence: the difficulty is real rather than invented from a market report.

The technical proof is less complete. Cova is licensed and live, and says its agents run the back office without non-caregiver employees. A functioning provider built by two people is meaningful pre-seed evidence. But “end-to-end” is still a company claim, not a measured result. There is no disclosed enrollment time, automation rate, exception rate, claim-acceptance rate, or staff time per case. The manual form workflow makes the missing measurements more important. They have shipped a machine, but have not yet shown me its speed.

The narrow well is Michigan families trying to get relatives paid for care. That is specific and urgent. The June 2026 Reddit report of an applicant progressing through enrollment with a quoted Medicaid rate of $22 per hour is better than a waitlist, but it is still one prospective caregiver—not proof that anyone completed enrollment, received payment, returned, or referred another family. The reported $500,000 is financing, and 154 LinkedIn followers are noise. No revenue, payer contract, reimbursement rate, or repeated-use evidence is public.

The strongest objection is that Cova may be an ordinary home-care agency with an unusually small back office. Distribution currently depends on referrals from MCOs, hospitals, discharge planners, case managers, and social workers, without a named channel owner or contract. Aidaly already reports coverage across five states and 8.5 million lives, while Cova has no disclosed proprietary dataset or structural barrier. This also lacks the concrete proof spike I tend to find persuasive: the license proves permission to operate, not superior operation. The counterargument is that these founders have already crossed from software into regulated service delivery instead of waiting for partners to do the hard work. That keeps the company interesting, but it does not substitute for user pull.

I would not write the check today. My 36-month forecast is that Cova reaches $3–8 million in annualized billings but remains primarily a Michigan care provider rather than proving a repeatable multi-state technology platform. One result would flip me: ten families across at least two MCOs receiving their first caregiver payments within a median of fourteen days, with under two hours of founder intervention per case. That single cohort would show that the workaround, technical system, and user need reinforce one another.
