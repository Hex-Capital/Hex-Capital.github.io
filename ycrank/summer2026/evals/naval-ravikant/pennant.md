# Pennant -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Pennant’s strongest asset is knowledge acquired by doing. Ryan Stewart spent more than a decade inside the exact machinery being replaced: voting proxies at State Street, building institutional products at BlackRock Aladdin, and advising public-company boards at PJT. Tomas Taylor adds BlackRock product experience and hands-on AI-agent work. The founders have known each other for 15 years and worked on the same Aladdin desk. This is not résumé adjacency masquerading as insight; it is unusually dense founder-market fit.

The early sales evidence confirms that their knowledge has become a product someone will trust. A large U.S. public pension plan, a Top AmLaw 100 firm, and governance advisors are already paying customers; a global asset manager is a design partner, with additional S&P 500 engagements. In a workflow where a mistaken recommendation can affect fiduciary voting, these customers are more informative than follower counts. Custom enterprise pricing also proves direct value capture, although Pennant discloses neither contract values nor renewal behavior, so the evidence establishes willingness to pay—not yet attractive economics.

The weakness is what remains after a competitor copies the code. SEC filings are public, general-purpose models are available to everyone, and incumbents already own research, voting, or execution relationships. JPMorgan is building internally. Pennant retains client policies, approvals, overrides, rationales, and proposal mappings, but the dossier does not establish that these records can improve a shared proprietary model, move between clients, or create contractual switching costs. Client-specific context can make software sticky; confidential context trapped inside each client does not automatically become a compounding asset.

The bull case is sharp: the founders may be turning governance judgment into an institutional operating system. The same policy engine, ontology, audit trail, and human-review architecture can serve investors, corporate advisors, and public companies inside a $72.4 billion adjacent eGRC market. The paying pension plan and AmLaw firm suggest Pennant has already crossed organizational boundaries. If every deployment creates reusable governance precedents, today’s workflow wedge could become tomorrow’s proprietary decision substrate.

But that compounding mechanism remains inferred. Pennant has no demonstrated user-to-user loop: one asset manager does not make the product more useful or cheaper to acquire for the next, and each enterprise may instead require its own integrations, policy encoding, support, and liability controls. That produces a respectable vertical-software business, but not necessarily the permissionless leverage or nonlinear upside I need from a personal angel check. None of the known exceptions applies: there is neither an observable network already forming nor evidence of a uniquely non-copyable product language strong enough to override the missing substrate.

I would not invest yet. My flip condition is verifiable: Pennant must show that an exclusive, reusable corpus of reviewed governance decisions measurably improves recommendation accuracy across customers while remaining contractually usable across those customers. Without that, I expect expertise-led enterprise software with real revenue but linear implementation work; with it, Pennant becomes a proprietary governance model whose advantage strengthens while competitors merely reproduce features.
