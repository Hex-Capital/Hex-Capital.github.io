# Hundred -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Erica Diaz has spent over five-and-a-half years designing consumer hardware, including troubleshooting suppliers in China and Mexico. That is the most interesting fact about Hundred. She has worked where defective components become somebody’s expensive problem. But I want the next sentence: what went wrong, what did she build around it, and how long did that take? The dossier supplies no particular episode. I give the team credit for knowing the territory, while leaving resourcefulness at its missing-evidence default. Employment history cannot answer a behavioral question ([YC founder profile](https://www.ycombinator.com/founders/erica-diaz)).

The proposed wedge makes sense. Inspect incoming trays before bad parts enter assembly, and preserve photographs so engineers can establish what arrived. Controlled illumination and calibration from one acceptable part could make installation practical without collecting a large training set. The claim of detecting flaws below 100 μm is specific enough to test. It is still a company claim: there are no measured false-accept rates, false-reject rates, or production-throughput results. Hundred fits my pattern of technical founders attacking an unpleasant industrial problem, but it has not supplied the numerical proof that makes an unfamiliar technical bet comfortable ([technical description](https://hundred.systems/how-it-works)).

The strongest argument for investing is that the founders may already be doing precisely the work that reveals the right product. Hundred reports deployment underway to three founding partners and offers onsite setup, monitoring, and customer-specific changes. Early installation labor can teach them which defects matter, which variations are harmless, and what a factory will actually tolerate. Diaz’s hardware background, Gosserand’s production-software experience at Twitch, and Liao’s quantitative and ML research make that learning loop plausible. But three deployments underway do not yet establish three working installations or repeated use. There is also no documented product revision tied to an observation; I leave adaptability at default rather than infer either flexibility or stubbornness ([partners](https://hundred.systems/partners), [team](https://hundred.systems/about)).

The installation work becomes valuable if each deployment makes the next one easier. Otherwise Hundred could accumulate custom engineering obligations while Elementary sells software for cameras customers already own. Preferential founding-partner pricing reveals nothing about that tradeoff because neither prices nor installation economics are disclosed. Calling customers “partners” does not establish dependence on a large distribution partner; the proposed direct installation approach avoids that particular red flag. Nor does the dossier establish a hastily assembled founding relationship: Diaz and Gosserand met in 2017. The problem is the missing bridge from relevant experience to demonstrated execution ([partners](https://hundred.systems/partners), [Elementary VisionLink](https://www.elementaryml.com/product/visionlink), [YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/hundred)).

I would pass today. Hundred has a credible origin and a useful product hypothesis, but it does not meet my founder-action gate. I checked the exception case: there is neither independently validated technical proof nor unmistakable user pull strong enough to replace that missing evidence. My flip condition is one documented factory deployment where a named production obstacle forced a founder-built change within weeks, the change passed the factory’s measured acceptance test, and the customer continued using it in production. That single episode would tell me considerably more than another page of specifications.
