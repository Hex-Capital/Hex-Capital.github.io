# Kebra -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when something broke. Kebra’s founders have built a system that notices incomplete technician records, requests missing serial numbers or photos, and finishes work inside existing software. But the dossier never names a customer obstacle, the founders’ workaround, or the time between them. That matters more to me than Luca Hadife’s Microsoft multimodal-AI work or Mohamed Hosny’s Berkeley and Arista credentials. The product behaves resourcefully; I do not yet know whether the founders do.

There is nevertheless a real product here. Kebra is live with field-service companies in Florida, Pennsylvania, and California. Quality Air is a named customer whose technicians and back office use it for job capture and post-job automation. Connecting recordings, images, equipment identifiers, calls, claims, parts orders, and invoices is considerably harder than making an AI demo. It is measured only by deployment, however: there is no disclosed accuracy rate, job volume, time saved, or independently verified technical threshold.

Quality Air also identifies a plausible narrow well. HVAC offices have to reconstruct jobs from scattered records before they can invoice or file claims, and technicians evidently tolerate Kebra participating in that workflow. But “using” is an imprecise verb. The dossier supplies no payment, repeat-job count, retention, unsolicited referral, or evidence that work stops when Kebra is removed. Deployments across three states are encouraging; 395 LinkedIn followers and nine Launch YC votes are nearly irrelevant.

The ugly work is the opportunity. Incumbents own the systems of record, so Kebra must reconcile messy field evidence and operate across them without making costly mistakes. That overlay could accumulate customer-specific histories and integration switching costs. It could also be compressed into features by ServiceTitan, Jobber, or Housecall Pro, all of which already distribute overlapping AI capabilities. The founders have technical fit with multimodal and systems problems, but the dossier does not show that either encountered this problem through prior field-service work. This currently looks like a well-executed AI-for-an-industry thesis, not an idea forced on them by their own experience.

The strongest bull case is that the deployments themselves are the exception: two technical founders have shipped a difficult system before permission, found a named user, and entered precisely the integration and service work that larger software companies handle poorly. If Quality Air is running dozens of jobs through Kebra each week, correcting less data over time, and referring other contractors, the apparent weakness is merely missing public evidence. But no known exception replaces the founder-action gate here. There is also no documented rejected hypothesis or changed route, so I cannot tell whether this team will run around an incumbent response or defend the original overlay.

I would pass today. My 36-month expectation is that Kebra narrows from a general “AI-native” field-service overlay into one workflow such as HVAC warranty claims or parts authorization, rather than winning the whole back office. One fact would flip me: a verifiable account from Quality Air showing repeated weekly use and describing a specific customer failure that the founders personally worked around and shipped against within days or weeks.
