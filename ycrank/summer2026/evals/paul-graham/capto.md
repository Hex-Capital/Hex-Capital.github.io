# Capto -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Visiting approximately 50 contracting offices in Virginia is the most interesting fact about Capto. These founders went where the users were and made themselves available to be rejected. That is useful behavior in a market where understanding why someone refuses to buy may matter more than writing another feature. But the [August 7 founder profile](https://www.cs.umd.edu/article/2026/08/learning-when-pivot) does not establish how quickly a particular rejection became a shipped workaround. I give substantial credit for doing the work, withhold the highest resourcefulness scores, and give none for the internships.

There is also a credible reason these founders encountered this problem. Sankaralingam built RFP-search automation for a contractor. Searching fragmented portals and interpreting solicitations are concrete, unpleasant jobs. Capto could discover its advantage inside that work: learning which requirements actually disqualify a contractor, then making that judgment easier. Company-document grounding and source-linked extraction fit this wedge. The founder’s prior contractor work supports the problem’s origin; it does not establish that the current software performs those judgments reliably.

Who wants it now? Government contractors running capture teams are a sufficiently specific starting point. Paid pilots are affirmative evidence, even without disclosed amounts. Snovation’s report that Capto found an RFI its existing pipeline missed is better than generic praise because it identifies an actual job completed. But one recovered opportunity does not establish a recurring habit. The reported $250 million in obligated contract value belongs to customers, and the claim of tripled proposal output lacks a measurement method. Neither tells me Capto’s revenue or retention.

The strongest case against investing is that founder effort may conceal product substitutability. GovDash already covers overlapping workflows and post-award management. Capto could win pilots through unusually attentive service, then lose renewals when buyers consolidate tools. Its product mechanisms are plausible, but prospective PWin accuracy remains unvalidated. This falls short of the concrete technical proof spikes that recur in my investments. Announced partnerships also need scrutiny, although the founders’ own calls and office visits provide evidence that distribution does not currently depend entirely on partners.

The pivot deserves less credit than the fieldwork. Chromie had moved from browser-extension creation into government contracting by August 7. That establishes a change of direction. It does not identify the observation that falsified the old route, or demonstrate a stable goal across both products. I therefore use the absent-evidence default for evidence-driven adaptation. The founders’ high-school relationship and subsequent shared building do remove the hastily assembled cofounder concern. The documented exceptions do not justify a stronger verdict here: relationship uncertainty is already addressed, while independently validated technical results and unmistakable recurring demand remain unestablished.

I would keep talking to these founders, but I would wait before writing a personal check. Capto has an organically discovered problem, founders willing to recruit users manually, and some willingness to pay. Those are meaningful signals. The missing link is whether their close contact with contractors produces software customers keep pulling back into their work. **Five existing pilot customers converting to subscriptions and then using Capto weekly for eight consecutive weeks would move me to Invest.** That is one observable cohort result, and it would directly challenge the concern that attentive founders are carrying a replaceable product.
