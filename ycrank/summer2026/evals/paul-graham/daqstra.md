# Daqstra -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that Daqstra calls itself AI-native. It is that Darell Chua and Anchit Kumar spent more than ten test campaigns reconnecting the same ugly mixture of sensors, PLCs, controllers, scripts, and files, then built the missing layer themselves. Their response now operates live LOX/IPA static fires and multiple rocket-engine stands. This is real resourcefulness, but not yet the strongest version of it: the dossier does not name a particular failure, say how quickly they responded, or show the resulting before-and-after improvement. I can see that they built through the obstacle. I cannot yet see their speed when it bit them.

The technical proof is much clearer. Daqstra does not merely display telemetry. Its deployed system talks bidirectionally to NI, Siemens, Modbus, OPC-UA, and custom-TCP equipment, then executes version-controlled sequences, redlines, aborts, and valve commands. Software touching a live static fire has to work in a way a diagram does not. This matches the useful unstated pattern in recent investments: difficult claims become investable when young technical founders first turn them into an inspectable physical artifact. Daqstra has done that before asking the market’s permission.

There is also a narrow well of demand. Aerospace programs reportedly use the product day to day across multi-vendor environments; the team has deployed it across multiple rocket-engine stands; and an industrial-energy customer signed a pilot aimed at more than $100,000 in annual savings. The SEDS UCSD–Dispatch deployment is a named public instance, while the other aerospace users remain anonymous. That is stronger than a waitlist and weaker than disclosed payment, retention, or referrals. Still, users are trusting a crude pre-seed product with physical tests. That is unusually informative behavior.

The bull case is that every painful integration makes Daqstra harder to remove. Persisted configurations, calibration lineage, test histories, and automated procedures could turn a collection of adapters into the operating system for physical R&D. The bear case stings: Nominal has raised $182.5 million, Sift $67 million, and instrumentation vendors can extend upward, while Daqstra may become a three-person integration consultancy carrying safety liability across endless bespoke hardware. Its signed pilot targets customer savings, not Daqstra revenue, and the dossier provides no deployment duration or retention. The founders must prove that each installation makes the product more general rather than merely creating another customer-specific branch.

I do not see the usual red flags. Distribution is founder-led rather than dependent on a ceremonial launch or unnamed major partner. The cofounders met building rocket engines and later published the 2025 AIAA paper together, so this is not an application-week marriage. Nor is the technical work delegated. The missing evidence is adaptability: there is no rejected hypothesis, route change, or dated example of the team changing the product after contrary usage. That receives the default score, not a penalty. No known exception is needed to rescue the company; it clears the founder-action and technical-proof gate directly.

I would invest. Daqstra combines founder-originated need, a schlep-heavy wedge, working safety-critical software, and early daily use. The one verifiable flip condition is straightforward: if within twelve months it cannot show three paying third-party engineering teams repeatedly running tests through the same core orchestration product without substantial custom development, I would reverse the decision. That would mean the apparent platform is really a services business.
