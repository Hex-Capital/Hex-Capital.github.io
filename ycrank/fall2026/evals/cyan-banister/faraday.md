# Faraday -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a small manufacturer accepting a complicated order without someone spending the afternoon carrying exceptions between purchasing, engineering, and production. Faraday points toward that world by mapping dependencies and permissions into executable workflows. Two transitions must work: agents must resolve business-system exceptions reliably, and those decisions must reach physical operations without outrunning human authority. The proposed shared runtime connects those steps. Optional robot controllers remain a destination, however; the dossier does not show that bridge operating inside a factory.

The most interesting object here is the harness. Faraday reports a score of **96.2 versus 71.7** for the same GPT-5.5 baseline, at **2.6 times lower run cost**, across **74 synthetic episodes and 33 scenario families**. That suggests useful engineering below the model interface. It fits my recurring interest in the concealed infrastructure beneath a more visible product: dependable coordination could support purchasing, inventory, and production. But a company-designed development suite cannot establish that Faraday controls a scarce industrial bottleneck. Didero already executes procurement workflows, while Tulip supplies configurable frontline software. Faraday has to earn its place between those systems, one difficult dependency at a time.

Why does a quote wait? Who has the missing information, and who is allowed to act on it? Those are promising questions, and fragmented records and manual handoffs give the founders something concrete to investigate. The dossier does not yet identify a particular approval rule, quantify its cost, or show whose operating authority changes after deployment. Faster coordination could return real time to factory workers, but that benefit remains unmeasured. Subscription and pilot agreements establish a commercial mechanism; they do not establish customer economics.

The strongest argument against passing is the team’s relevant operating experience. Oscar Rangel built factory systems at **Worthington Steel** and shipped medical devices at **Stryker**; Datta Kaligotla brings enterprise engineering and **Handshake AI Enterprise** experience. Their benchmark is an actual technical artifact, and Fabtech conversations put them near the people whose exceptions they need to understand. My **CosmicBrain AI** exception matters here: both bets concern software enabling physical industry before revenue establishes the case. The material difference is that its lifelong robotics record and signed industrial proofs of concept supplied first-believer evidence. Faraday’s dossier supplies relevant work history, but no comparable record of independent obsession or industrial commitment. Missing revenue alone would not stop me.

I would pass on writing a personal check today. Faraday has more substance than a fashionable label, but its strongest evidence concerns synthetic execution; its weakest concerns people entrusting consequential work to the product. No repeated customer behavior is documented, so I assign the prescribed missing-evidence score there. The privacy disclosure deserves a concrete deployment review: separate authorization for writes is encouraging, while external-provider processing leaves custody dependent on customer terms. That is an unresolved question, not evidence of a severe privacy violation. My one verifiable flip condition is **a named manufacturer’s 90-day deployment record showing repeated paid use across an order-to-production workflow, reduced manual intervention, and an audit trail demonstrating that actions stayed within authorized boundaries**. That would make the imagined factory tangible.
