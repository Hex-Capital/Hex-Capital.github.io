# 83 Sciences -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Yankang Yang deployed AI programs to more than 30,000 users, while 83 Sciences discloses no plan beyond converting discarded laboratory data into manuscripts in under two months. That pairing is the signature: 83 Sciences is a credentialed, domain-native founder bet trapped inside an indefinite product thesis. The founders have encountered the problem personally; 83 Sciences has not yet shown that recovering the alleged 90% of discarded experimental data is a secret rather than merely an opportunity.

**Anduril** is the high rung on founder pedigree because Palmer Luckey had already shipped difficult hardware to a billion-dollar exit. 83 Sciences has a weaker but meaningful version of that mechanism: Eric Riesel developed materials-data ML at MIT, Ian Naccarella worked in materials commercialization at Sila, and Yang operated AI at 30,000-user scale. Yet 83 Sciences has no founder who has built and exited a company, owned a scaled scientific product, or demonstrated Luckey-level technical inevitability. Credentials are evidence of capability; they are not evidence of destiny.

**Quora** exposes the low-rung failure mechanism on definite planning: excellent pedigree can conceal an indefinite roadmap. 83 Sciences promises a co-authored manuscript in under two months, but that is a service deliverable, not a two-to-five-year architectural commitment. 83 Sciences names no sequence from laboratory ingestion to validated discoveries, cross-institutional learning, patent ownership, and commercial deployment. Without that sequence, “partner with us” means the market chooses the destination. Definite optimism requires a map.

Citrine’s 550 deployed models and 70,000 monthly experiment suggestions make the contrarian-secret test severe. Over the next 18 months, 83 Sciences can compound a secret only if failed experiments contain predictive information that Citrine’s conventional materials records systematically exclude—and if laboratories grant reusable learning rights. Otherwise each integration becomes bespoke data cleaning, while Citrine’s installed base compounds faster. 83 Sciences names unpublished data as the neglected asset, but 83 Sciences does not name the architectural or institutional reason Citrine cannot ingest it. “AI-native materials discovery” remains dangerously close to the mimetic “AI for X” pattern.

The $170.4 million materials-informatics market could support a useful business without supporting monopoly pricing. For 83 Sciences to move into an investable tier, partner agreements would need to permit a cross-laboratory failure-data corpus, recommendations would need measurable improvement with corpus breadth, and resulting patents would need to reinforce—not fragment—the platform. 83 Sciences currently discloses no dataset size, retention evidence, benchmark, or IP allocation terms. Proprietary data can become a moat; partner-by-partner confidentiality can just as easily become a collection of sealed rooms.

**SpaceX** owned the atoms, the capital intensity, and the technical roadmap; its software could not be separated from the rockets. 83 Sciences works near atoms but appears to own none of them: partner laboratories supply the instruments, experimental capacity, and physical validation. That makes 83 Sciences more exposed to scientific regulation and trust requirements than ordinary SaaS, but it does not yet create a regulatory or capex barrier. The 2025 review identifying failures in materials-ML generalizability, interpretability, and robustness raises the validation burden without granting 83 Sciences exclusivity.

The under-two-month manuscript claim predicts a narrower outcome: within 36 months, 83 Sciences will pivot toward enterprise laboratory-data workflow software and reach roughly $1 million–$3 million ARR, while abandoning or subordinating the broader claim of proprietary materials discovery. 83 Sciences will discover that unpublished data is heterogeneous, contractually encumbered, and expensive to normalize; the fastest revenue will come from organizing each laboratory’s records rather than pooling knowledge across laboratories. That produces a business, but competition is for losers—and workflow software without shared-data pricing power is competition.

The grants of up to $50,000 create the strongest bull case: 83 Sciences could seed a unique corpus before incumbents recognize that failed experiments are the valuable negative labels, while Riesel’s MIT chemistry depth and Yang’s 30,000-user operating history supply a Quora-like pedigree override. The case stings because a genuine cross-laboratory failure graph could become the materials equivalent of a proprietary knowledge graph. Yet 83 Sciences names no signed laboratory, corpus scale, validation advantage, or right to reuse partner data, and its promises of papers, patents, and commercialization leave ownership unresolved. I would flip to Invest if 83 Sciences secured five named industrial laboratories granting cross-institutional model-training rights and published a blinded benchmark showing materially better experiment selection than Citrine. Until then, the secret is asserted, not possessed.
