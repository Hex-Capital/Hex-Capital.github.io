# Instaplay -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Scrub Club’s 5,567 plays make Instaplay a tactile consumer toy with a frontier-shaped hole: Instaplay can be opened, played, shared, and remixed, but nothing in the dossier suggests the ugly technical, regulatory, or physical constraint that makes competitors stay away. The highest signal is physical-world verifiability; the lowest is capital-intensive or regulated difficulty. Instaplay is a real artifact wrapped around a replaceable technical stack—the sort of thing I can feel immediately but may forget just as quickly.

**HQ Trivia** is the high rung for physical verifiability because the product’s live, addictive pull temporarily overrode even terrible founder governance. Instaplay shares the load-bearing mechanism: a browser link becomes a multiplayer experience for four people, and Scrub Club reached 5,567 plays while Swordling reached 4,840. Instaplay diverges where HQ Trivia became a synchronized cultural ritual; Instaplay shows isolated play counts, but no repeat usage, creator retention, or evidence that people reorganize their evening around the product. The toy exists. The magic loop is not yet proven.

The consensus YC AI-agent darling fails because its moat is the model, its origin is analytical, and a polymath can rebuild the wrapper in a quarter. That failure mechanism appears inside Instaplay: Astrocade has raised $56 million and reports more than 20 million engaged users, while Rosebud AI and FRVR already combine generation with creation or distribution. Instaplay is software-only, depends on generally available code-and-asset generation, and has no proprietary-model evidence. Instaplay is not SpaceX hiding inside a game—it is standing in a crowded arcade where several machines already have distribution.

Gary Wu’s Harvard AB/SM, Salesforce Agentforce work, and prior LLM-evaluation startup Verita establish technical adjacency, not a lived relationship to games. Instaplay would flip this founder-origin score if Gary Wu had spent years building game communities, if Frank Liu had operated a multiplayer title, or if Akhil Aggarwal could name the autobiographical moment that made instant social play unavoidable. Instaplay instead presents three credentialed software builders who appear to have arrived through AI capability. Biz and Tiz are plausible; the dossier gives me no environmental evidence of Rizz and no **Niantic**-style decade of obsession.

Gary Wu is labeled “Founder” and Frank Liu “Co-founder & CTO,” but Instaplay names three founders without documenting equity, vesting, or tiebreaker authority. Over 18 months, that ambiguity compounds because consumer games demand rapid aesthetic decisions: what to moderate, which creators to privilege, whether Instaplay is a social network or a creation tool, and when to abandon a beloved mechanic. Instaplay receives some credit for the apparent CEO/CTO distinction, but the unidentified authority relationship with Akhil Aggarwal keeps the **HQ Trivia** scar visible. Three founders are not fatal; three founders without a named final decider are a rebuttal waiting to become a hard no.

**Crusoe** was magically weird because sophisticated people disliked the category itself—Bitcoin, flared gas, and rural infrastructure stapled together—and that rejection created the price. Instaplay has a mildly weird consumer expression in prompt-to-multiplayer games, but Instaplay occupies one of the hottest generative-AI categories and faces Astrocade, Rosebud AI, and FRVR with $56 million, $9.9 million, and $94.3 million raised respectively. No investor rejection, aesthetic taboo, or structural reason for underpricing is documented. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing, but here the game may be exactly what it looks like.

Astrocade’s more than 20 million engaged users point to my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Instaplay will have abandoned the standalone social-platform ambition, pivoted into embedded instant-game creation for Discord-style communities, and remain below $3 million ARR unless Discord acquires Instaplay for product and talent. Instaplay’s playable links provide the wedge for that adjacent market, while Instaplay’s absent model moat and competitor-scale disadvantage make an independent consumer network unlikely. The artifact survives; the destination changes.

Scrub Club’s 5,567 plays supply the strongest bull case: **HQ Trivia** proves that tactile consumer love can override weak governance and ordinary defensibility, and Instaplay already has several games above 4,400 plays at pre-seed. Instaplay could compound creation, remixing, discovery, and multiplayer sharing into a graph that general-purpose models cannot reproduce. The objection stings because consumer networks look trivial immediately before they become culture. Instaplay still shows play totals rather than retention, remix depth, or creator love, while Astrocade’s reported scale makes novelty a poor proxy for escape velocity. I would flip to Invest if Instaplay demonstrates at least 30% day-30 creator retention and shows that 25% of played games originate from user remix chains rather than first-party seeding.
