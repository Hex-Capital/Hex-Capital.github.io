# Yam -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine a woman creating a romantic world that other women inhabit, extend, and eventually pay her to keep alive. Yam already lets creators describe scenarios, edit characters, generate artwork, and publish playable links. But the distance between a playable fantasy and a creator-owned entertainment business matters. The dossier does not establish the intervening steps: how creators earn, how characters become durable original property, or why those worlds stay on Yam. That future is my hypothesis; I cannot score it as something the company has demonstrated.

The little object that makes me stop is the play counter: 1.8 million plays on “JJK Love island AU.” Another title shows 516,400. Somebody is doing something here. The reported three-plus hours per daily active user makes me want to sit beside a player and watch what she actually does. Does she rewrite a conversation, share an outcome, return to the same character? Public play counters establish activity, but they do not answer those questions, and the engagement claim lacks a measurement period or denominator. Yam earns credit for a functioning product attracting attention. Its $9.99 and $19.99 monthly subscriptions establish asking prices, not purchases or creator livelihoods.

The founders have relevant operating muscle. Yu Qin previously built TikTok AI creation tools and reports helping grow Effect House’s creator community to 800,000; Shan Zhong reports growing a previous consumer fintech to 100,000 users and 2 million ARR, with currency unspecified. Crystal Shan brings product-design leadership from Webex and early product experience at Amesite. Those are useful ingredients for making creation accessible. What I cannot reconstruct is the independent obsession preceding Yam. Personal participation in fanfiction and dating sims is a start, but the earlier design-first coding-tool positioning leaves the origin unresolved. A pivot can be discovery. It is not, by itself, evidence of either conviction or opportunism.

The strongest argument against passing is that this fits my recurring attraction to synthetic characters and devoted fringe behavior unusually well. Perhaps the creators and players have already discovered something the business description cannot explain. My willingness to back pre-revenue prototypes means missing revenue cannot settle this decision. But those exceptions require other evidence to carry the bet, and Yam has not yet supplied a deeply documented founder obsession or a controlled enabling asset. Nor does the dossier identify a specific institutional rent being transferred back to women creators. Easier storytelling is useful; economic independence requires an actual ownership and payment mechanism. Character.AI and Dorian also make overlapping interaction and authoring capabilities available, so access to those capabilities alone does not establish that Yam controls a scarce dependency.

I would pass with my own money today. The privacy architecture most changes how I interpret the apparent intimacy: romantic interactions may go to model providers and support model improvement, while the policy advises users against sensitive inputs. That does not establish severe misuse, but it leaves my consent-and-custody gate unresolved. Character rights introduce another tension: removing familiar fictional characters could remove the behavior attracting players, while preserving that supply requires more than assigning rights responsibility to users. Shan is identified as CEO; there is no evidence of the leadership shell game or equal-control arrangement that would independently stop me. My one verifiable flip condition is an audited 90-day cohort of returning, paying players in creator-owned original worlds where intimate chats are excluded from model training by default. That would connect the compelling behavior to a business whose essential supply and trust Yam can preserve.
