# TryNearby -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A neighborhood restaurant does not “run an influencer campaign”; it maintains a continuous local-media presence, while software recruits creators, learns who reliably travels and publishes, fills cancellations, and routes content into whichever discovery surfaces matter. Three things must happen between here and there: creator visits must become operationally dependable, restaurant outcomes must become attributable, and liquidity must reproduce city by city. TryNearby has a real leak from that future—an agent already handles more than 10,000 creator texts monthly—but the dossier has not yet mapped those transitions or shown that Southern California’s density travels.

The working artifact is much stronger than the future story. More than 120 restaurants pay $399 monthly, retention exceeds 90% month over month, and creators have produced 2,500-plus videos with roughly 35 million views. Restaurants are repeatedly spending money while creators travel, surrender schedule flexibility, and perform public labor; half of new restaurants reportedly arrive through owner referrals. That is costly behavior, not a waitlist wearing makeup. The reported revenue increases are exciting but not yet decision-grade because attribution is unpublished.

The founders make this more than a fashionable creator-economy wrapper. Yousef Abdelfattah entered FaZe Clan at fourteen and helped build it through a public listing. Obaida Albaroudi and Ahmad Ibrahim previously built Clipping Exe together; one reportedly grew a consumer product from $360,000 to $2 million ARR, while the other built infrastructure tracking more than 10 billion video views. That is pre-company obsession, complementary commercial and technical fluency, and evidence they have already lived inside the creator machinery. The mundane artifact I care about is not Yousef’s two million followers. It is the rejection, travel, availability, and rescheduling data accumulating while the system tries to make thousands of unreliable human calendars behave like infrastructure.

The broken system is recognizable but not yet fully decoded. A restaurant owner currently buys an agency, negotiates separately with creators, makes content personally, or hires someone full-time; TryNearby collapses that work into a $399 subscription and returns time to the owner. But the dossier does not quantify the agency rent, identify who captures excess economics, or prove that restaurants gain durable control rather than renting another marketing intermediary. The product repairs an irritating workflow. It has not yet exposed an artificial rule with the sharpness I want.

The bear case stings because this may be an excellent managed marketplace sitting on rented land. TikTok and Instagram control discovery and commercial-content rules; Hyper and Tablevert already automate restaurant creator campaigns; city expansion resets local liquidity; and 90% monthly retention can still compound into severe annual churn. TryNearby also stores sensitive preference, travel, availability, and rejection information through iMessage agents without a publicly documented consent or custody architecture. Three founders have named roles, but the dossier does not disclose control or a contractual tiebreaker. None of my known-exception patterns clears those gates: extraordinary usage should not excuse governance, and early traction should not excuse privacy.

I would invest a modest personal check because the highest-quality evidence is exactly where young marketplaces usually bluff: people are already paying, traveling, creating, renewing, and referring. My 36-month base case is that TryNearby reaches $6 million to $10 million ARR across at least eight metropolitan markets by August 2029 while remaining a creator-operations marketplace rather than becoming a general enabling layer. One result flips me to a pass: if its first non-California market retains fewer than 75% of restaurant cohorts through six paid months after excluding founder-led accounts, then Southern California was the product, not the beachhead.
