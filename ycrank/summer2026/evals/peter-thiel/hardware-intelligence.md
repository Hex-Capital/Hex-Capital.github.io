# hardware intelligence -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

What important truth does hardware intelligence know that Synopsys and ChipAgents do not? The implied answer is that verification engineers will abandon GUI-centered debugging for an auditable, terminal-native agent operating on-prem. The falsifying event is straightforward: pilot engineers continue using Verdi or ChipAgents after testing Wave because FSDB compatibility, established workflows, and broader design context matter more than terminal ergonomics. Hardware intelligence has shipped a demonstration, but it has not reported this substitution test or named a rejected industry belief. Today the secret is closer to a fashionable inference than a demonstrated contradiction.

The founders are the strongest fact. Athreya Anand and Rishov Sarkar met almost a decade ago, lived together throughout college, and bring complementary experience from Google agentic systems, AWS Inferentia, Tesla, AMD simulators, Siemens EDA, and the LightningSim project. This is not a team assembled around a YC application. It appears capable of building the core product in-house. But the dossier does not establish each founder’s full-time status, ownership, salary, or economic exposure, so long alignment cannot by itself satisfy the complete founding-structure test.

The market is narrow but not monopolistic. ASIC and FPGA waveform debugging is a legitimate beachhead, and Wave’s local execution, customer-supplied model key, evidence-linked transcript, simulator reruns, and waveform diffs form a coherent product. Yet these features do not presently preserve value. Synopsys already offers AI failure analysis, natural-language commands, signal tracing, waveform comparison, and the dominant FSDB workflow. ChipAgents has deployed across more than 120 semiconductor companies and raised a $134 million Series A; Silimate also performs AI root-cause debugging. Hardware intelligence enters a bounded market, but bounded competition is still competition.

The strongest bull case is that incumbency conceals the secret. A deeply aligned team with unusual compiler, simulator, chip-design, and agent experience could discover that engineers value auditable local agents enough to route around expensive license-server workflows. Founder demonstrations on multi-gigabyte waveforms show more substance than a generic EDA copilot. If Wave produces materially faster root-cause resolution on real customer designs, the lack of a shared proprietary dataset may matter less because workflow integration and accumulated audit history could create switching costs.

But demonstrations are not independent technical proof, and future switching costs are not a moat today. The on-prem architecture protects customer data while preventing that data from compounding across customers. VCD and FST support leaves the established FSDB ecosystem unresolved. The likely escape is to broaden Wave into a complete chip-design agent, but that converts a narrow contest with incumbents into direct competition with the better-funded ChipAgents platform. The current product is caught between feature and platform: too overlapping to be a secret, too narrow to support power-law arithmetic, and not yet entrenched enough to become the last mover.

I would pass, while preserving one verifiable flip condition: three named semiconductor design teams must pay for Wave, replace their incumbent debugger for a recurring production workflow, and document at least a tenfold reduction in median root-cause time over six months. That single result would simultaneously establish the disputed truth, a proprietary workflow advantage, and distribution appropriate to enterprise economics. Until then, hardware intelligence is an unusually credible founding team pursuing an increasingly consensus product.
