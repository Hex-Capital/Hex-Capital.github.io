# Risklytics -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Risklytics has taken action, but not yet inside the exact problem it now sells. Alexander Risio has built prior startups, conducted clinical-ML research, and passed an insurance licensing examination; the team also shipped Ember, a property-level catastrophe model, before rejecting it as a non-starter. That shows technical energy and willingness to update. It does not yet show that either founder has placed commercial insurance, negotiated an exclusion, won carrier access, or managed a claim. The learning is adjacent; the specific knowledge remains unproven.

The timing is real. ISO introduced optional AI-liability exclusions in 2026, creating variation across policy forms precisely as AI agents, robotics, and autonomous systems introduce unfamiliar liabilities. Risklytics can enter through frontier technology and reuse its form analysis, regulatory data, and carrier relationships across broader commercial insurance. But the cited $335.87 billion brokerage market is a parent category, not evidence that this wedge can capture it. Alton Risk already offers overlapping policy-gap analysis and carrier matching, while Corgi owns underwriting as a full-stack carrier. A new rule creates demand; it does not assign the demand to Risklytics.

The database of forms, endorsements, regulatory filings, and carrier treatment could become the durable asset. Today it is a claimed repository without disclosed size, exclusive inputs, placement outcomes, or evidence that it improves with usage. Copy the software and the remaining business is a young brokerage dependent on wholesalers, MGAs, and carriers that prefer placement history. The strongest unstated-pattern fit would be converting fragmented carrier appetite into a price-discoverable network. But this is not yet permissionless leverage: producer licenses are pending, coverage is not being placed, and every initial customer appears to require founder-managed service.

There is no demonstrated user-to-user loop. One insured company does not presently attract another carrier, improve another customer’s coverage, or distribute the product. Placement outcomes might eventually create proprietary routing data, but a future feedback loop is not a current network effect. Similarly, brokerage commissions provide a plausible direct business model, yet Risklytics publishes no pricing, bound policies, revenue, or completed transactions. The company has 123 LinkedIn followers and a July 2026 profile; those validate visibility, not value capture.

The bull case is that Ember was not abandonment but costly discovery: the founders learned that modeling risk was less valuable than controlling how emerging risk reaches insurance markets, then moved full-time in May 2026 and began licensing. A rapidly changing exclusion regime could let a small specialist accumulate carrier knowledge before generalist brokers react. This is the founder-first known exception in its strongest form. It still does not clear the exception: there is no extraordinary recruiting, prototype adoption, customer obsession, or commercial artifact that substitutes for missing domain history. I see no integrity, artificial-deadline, or reassurance-seeking red flag, and one evidence-driven pivot is preferable to stubbornness. The problem is not character; it is that the option currently contains too much brokerage labor and too little compounding substrate.

I would pass and reopen on one verifiable condition: Risklytics binds at least 50 frontier-technology policies and demonstrates that their proprietary placement history raises carrier-match or quote-conversion rates for later customers. That would simultaneously prove specific knowledge, direct value capture, and an asset that survives copied code. Until then, the company is a timely specialist brokerage hypothesis, not yet a network or data company.
