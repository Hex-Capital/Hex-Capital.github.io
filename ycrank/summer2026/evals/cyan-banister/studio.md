# Studio -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the attractive version is clear: consumer companies rehearse pricing, product, and campaign decisions against living simulations before spending real money, then those simulations learn from the outcome. Studio has a leak from that world—five enterprise clients, 200 million consumers modeled, and live Danone and Mila tests reportedly matching KPIs above 95%. But the dossier never supplies the bridge from promising pilots to enterprises routinely delegating consequential decisions. The future is imaginable; the causal itinerary is still missing.

The founders are the strongest part of this company. Nikita Mullangi researched human behavior and decision-making at MIT and previously built Genxl to a reported network of 51,500 students and 2,100 volunteers. Cameron Malloy built agent-based financial-contagion and society models before Studio. That is real pre-company convergence, not two people discovering “synthetic consumers” after a competitor raised $300 million. The five clients add first-believer proof, although I still want a longer trail of shipped artifacts and evidence that this team can turn technical accuracy into enterprise renewals.

Why are surveys broken? Studio’s answer—point-in-time responses miss interactions among cohorts—is technically specific. But who captures the rent? What measurable delay disappears? Whose economic agency is restored? The dossier never gets there. This appears to help enterprise managers make better decisions, not liberate a worker or customer from an artificial gatekeeper. It may become valuable analytics without changing the underlying system’s allocation of power.

The serious bull case is that the artifact already works. Studio reports 1,400 input sources, more than 300 cohorts per simulated society, five enterprise clients, and live tests rather than a waitlist. At pre-seed, that combination can matter more than disclosed revenue. The objection that stings is measurement: the 95% figure arrives without sample sizes, error distributions, frozen predictions, baseline comparisons, or decision categories. A model can “match” a broad KPI while being useless at choosing between two campaigns. Until customers repeatedly stake budgets or pricing decisions on prospective forecasts, the costly behavior belongs to Studio’s clients only weakly.

There is a possible enabling layer here: repeated forecast-versus-outcome data could become infrastructure for product, pricing, and marketing decisions across many downstream applications. Today, however, that loop is inferred rather than demonstrated. Customer CRM and loyalty data remain essential inputs, no structural barrier prevents incumbents or the heavily funded BluePill, Simile, and Fairgen from building adjacent systems, and no controlled technical bottleneck is established. Studio touches my synthetic-personhood pattern, but these synthetic consumers are currently a management instrument, not a new identity or companionship layer.

The privacy gate also stays open. Studio says PII is scrubbed and tenant data isolated, which is responsible, but modeling 200 million consumers from CRM, app events, reviews, and social conversations demands an explicit consent and custody architecture. My 36-month prior is that Studio narrows into CPG campaign and pricing measurement and remains below $5 million ARR rather than becoming a general enterprise decision engine. I am Neutral and would not write the check today. One verifiable condition flips me: three enterprises renew after twelve months while documenting that they committed real budgets or pricing changes based on Studio forecasts registered before the outcomes occurred.
