# IMPACT Drones -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: cheap hostile drones and a February 2026 European procurement catalyst are pushing airports, energy operators, and data centers toward counter-UAS systems, but the variable that determines whether IMPACT Drones becomes large is the all-in cost of a lawful, reliable intercept—including false launches, collateral damage, reloads, and monitoring. The dossier establishes the asymmetry—legacy effectors can cost more than $1 million against threats below $50,000—but does not yet show IMPACT’s cost or the reliability threshold at which civilian operators will buy recurring protection.

The product architecture is directionally right. A sealed cell, onboard visual navigation under GPS jamming, and separate HUNTER and turbine-powered SABLE airframes could convert episodic defense procurement into a managed service. This is more than software attached to commodity hardware because IMPACT assumes responsibility for detection, launch, navigation, and interception. But the economic step change remains asserted rather than demonstrated: there is no subscription price, gross margin, reload cost, intercept success rate, false-positive rate, or environmental-test result. Air Defense as a Service only creates attractive economics if autonomy reduces operating cost faster than physical deployment, maintenance, and liability consume it.

The strongest bull case is the same mechanism that made Anduril exceptional: product economics replacing expensive incumbent cost-plus systems. The material difference is that IMPACT has not demonstrated either half of that exception. It has not shown product-style margins, and civilian-site operators may lack authority to use kinetic mitigation under U.S. law. Ramming also creates debris and mistaken-engagement risk precisely around airports, grids, and populated campuses. A subscription wrapper cannot repair an unavailable or legally constrained outcome.

Demand evidence is presently the weakest part of the company. Q3 2026 pilots and forthcoming Middle East deployments are useful milestones, but no counterparty is named and there is no reported usage, contract, revenue, or conversion. The 875 LinkedIn followers, two press mentions, and 27 job applicants are awareness signals, not product-market pull. Meanwhile, Origin Robotics is deployed by four NATO states, Fortem reports more than 5,500 captures, and 9 Mothers reports $1.6 million in sales. The market is crystallizing around vendors with field evidence before IMPACT has published one representative intercept result.

The team is the best reason to keep watching. Jasper Lortije previously co-founded AeroIntel and built GNSS-denied visual navigation for long-range ISR drones; Alessandro Fiume led AeroIntel sales. Their prior working relationship joins directly relevant autonomy experience with defense-market selling. That is meaningful second-time learning, although the dossier documents neither a prior exit nor the costly personal commitment that would establish founder desperation. More importantly, founder quality cannot override the missing market proof when regulatory permission and physical reliability are themselves the market.

I would pass today and expect IMPACT, by August 2029, to concentrate on government-authorized bases and selected Middle Eastern energy sites rather than broad U.S. civilian infrastructure, reaching roughly $1 million–$3 million in annual recurring revenue if its pilots work. The single condition that flips me to invest is a named civilian infrastructure operator converting a regulator-authorized pilot into an annual contract after at least 100 representative engagements with a published intercept rate above 95%, zero false launches, and demonstrated service gross margin above 50%. That result would validate the controlling variable rather than merely promise it.
