# Bernard -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: appliance servicers can now deploy AI across intake, diagnosis, parts selection, and dispatch; they will buy if Bernard materially increases first-visit completion, and this becomes a large company only if that operational gain outweighs the distribution advantage of incumbent field-service platforms. First-visit completion is the controlling variable. Bernard explains the causal mechanism—identify the appliance and likely failure before dispatch—but publishes neither its current rate nor the improvement customers receive. Without that measurement, the central belief remains plausible rather than validated.

Demand is the strongest evidence. Bernard reports deployments with large authorized service companies and thousands of calls handled daily, while its privacy policy identifies Mr. Appliance of West Loop as an SMS customer. That is meaningful pre-seed product pull: operational teams appear to be entrusting the product with real customer traffic, not merely signing LOIs. Paid subscription terms and enterprise volume commitments support a commercial model, although no revenue, retention, expansion, or customer concentration figures are disclosed. The 100-plus Android downloads matter far less than production call volume.

The product is also deeper than an AI receptionist. Pre-ID combines symptom evidence, repair histories, manufacturer guidance, parts catalogs, and servicer knowledge, then carries the result into technician preparation. Integrations with Compass, Encompass, Marcone, Early Repair, and Samsung GSPN place Bernard inside a recurring operational loop, while completed repairs can improve later recommendations. Better models should improve diagnosis within that loop rather than automatically erase the company. The counterweight is that the supposed data moat is unquantified: there is no dataset size, diagnostic accuracy, first-visit resolution rate, or estimate of what ServiceTitan would spend to reproduce it.

The strongest bull case is that specialization wins before horizontal platforms can model appliance-level failures. A product already handling thousands of daily calls could turn its initial wedge into proprietary repair outcomes and then distribute scheduling, dispatch, warranty, and parts products through the same customer channel. But Bernard is entering a crystallizing market: ServiceTitan generated $961 million in FY2026 revenue and already bundles voice intake, dispatch, and customer data, while Probook has raised $40 million for an AI operating system. The relevant technology shift is visible, but Bernard does not quantify a corresponding change in labor cost, technician utilization, gross margin, or repeat visits. The market may reward this workflow while allowing an incumbent to capture most of the economics.

The founders have adjacent capabilities—Dan Katzman in operations and partnerships, David Goodfellow in enterprise automation and operational products—but the dossier documents neither appliance-service experience, prior startup learning, nor a costly personal commitment to this problem. That is missing evidence, not adverse evidence. More important, none of the known exceptions clears the market gate: this is not frontier research with a uniquely scarce technical team, and the dossier does not show a reconstruction of incumbent economics.

I would monitor rather than invest today. The verifiable flip condition is a cohort showing at least a 15-point increase in first-visit completion across three named service networks, with twelve-month net revenue retention above 120%. That would validate both the controlling variable and Bernard’s ability to retain value despite incumbent bundling.
