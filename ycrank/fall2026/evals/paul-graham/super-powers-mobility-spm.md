# Super Powers Mobility (SPM) -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important question about SPM’s September pivot is what made Jonathan Powers change direction. The company shifted from vehicles to battery packs and power export, then reported $421,815 in realized revenue in one month. That deserves attention. But the dossier does not identify the obstacle, what Powers personally did to overcome it, or which customer observation prompted the change. I can credit the sales. I cannot turn them into an invented story about founder resourcefulness.

There is something substantial underneath those sales. SPM demonstrated vehicle-integrated power systems to special operations forces in Nevada and Utah in July 2026. This is more informative than a rendering or an engineering résumé. Its advertised 15 kWh modules and 12 kW continuous AC output describe a useful product for operators who otherwise run generators or idle engines. The demonstrations earn technical credit; the claimed performance advantage remains unverified. SPM fits my pattern of backing industrial companies once they produce a concrete artifact, but only partway: functioning hardware and independently established superiority are different levels of proof.

The strongest argument for investing is that customers may already have identified the better business for these founders. A company can discover that the component inside its original product is what people actually want. SPM’s reported $421,815 month makes that possibility serious. I give it substantial demand credit, while keeping the $792,000 qualified pipeline and $769,000 of prior vehicle preorders separate. Neither is additional realized revenue for the new product. The missing distinction is whether buyers want a repeatable battery system or a succession of custom engineering projects. Inventus already supplies custom military power systems. SPM could become a useful engineering supplier without becoming the company its first revenue spike suggests.

Powers has relevant experience for discovering the answer. Cybertruck integration at Tesla, electric-truck brakes at Workhorse, and ATV/UTV engineering at Honda put him close to the physical problems SPM must solve. Integrating batteries, controls, and power electronics is precisely the sort of tedious work that can conceal an opportunity. That earns partial credit for an organically accessible wedge. It does not establish that Powers personally encountered this particular unmet need. Likewise, September supplies a date for the route change, but no account of the evidence behind it. I would leave adaptability at its missing-evidence default rather than mistake a pivot announcement for demonstrated learning.

SPM’s distribution plan also needs a careful reading. Prime subcontracting would worry me if growth depended on one large partner, but the dossier also describes direct quotations and evaluation units. The partnership red flag is therefore unproven. The prototype-led and independently validated technical exceptions are relevant to the weak founder evidence: SPM has demonstrated hardware and meaningful reported sales. Neither yet establishes the rapid founder correction, unmistakable user pull, or independent validation needed to override that gap. Its financing and founder credentials do not supply the missing evidence.

I would hold off on writing a personal check. This is a neutral decision about an unusually promising but incompletely explained result. The flip condition is one documented customer case: Powers encounters an installation blocker, ships a workaround within two weeks, and the customer subsequently places a paid repeat order. That would connect the founder’s behavior to both a working product and durable demand. SPM already has enough commercial evidence to make that a worthwhile test.
