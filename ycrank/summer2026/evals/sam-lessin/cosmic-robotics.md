# Cosmic Robotics -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with the deal because Cosmic Robotics is already behaving like a financed company, not a scarce pre-seed experiment. It raised a $4 million round led by Giant Ventures with HCVC and MaC Ventures, then accepted a $350,000 uncapped, undiscounted SAFE in July 2026. The dossier gives no post-money valuation or competing-term-sheet count, but this is plainly not the first lonely check into a discarded thesis. An uncapped SAFE also asks early money to assume early risk without being paid for it. That is a bad starting strike price even before knowing the next round’s valuation.

The product evidence is materially better than the deal evidence. Cosmic-1A has installed tens of thousands of panels, reportedly more than doubled labor productivity, and accumulated field cycles across mud, sand, weather, and rutted terrain. That is real physical-world work, not a robotics simulation or a follower count. Emerick previously deployed autonomous construction equipment at Built Robotics, while Jones shipped propulsion and space work across Relativity Space, SpaceX, and NASA environments. This team has unusually credible receipts for making heavy machinery function outside a lab.

But what purchase did that workflow cause? The dossier names no paid EPC customer, revenue, renewal, fleet contract, or exclusive data arrangement. Cosmic owns field data and has demonstrated usage, yet Gritt reports contracts covering 2.8 GW while accomplishing the same panel-positioning task with rented equipment and off-the-shelf arms. If the proprietary asset is merely that Cosmic has a custom 10,000-pound vehicle and more cycles, the market can compete around it. The defensible dark pool would be an EPC workflow that repeatedly buys deployment capacity and produces private operating data as a consequence; that causal chain is not documented.

The capital plan is similarly diffuse. The $149,521 NASA Phase I contract does define a finite TRL 4 experiment around lunar wire handling, power use, and error detection, but it does not explain what the equity round is supposed to prove on Earth. Solar-panel installation, cooling-pipe adaptation, hyperscaler construction, and lunar truss outfitting are four ambitions. None comes with a dated pass/fail milestone tied to the $4 million. Capital that funds a menu of technically impressive possibilities is still a capital factory unless one result dramatically changes the business.

The strongest bull case is that Cosmic has already crossed the hard-hardware threshold: tens of thousands of installations and a reported twofold productivity improvement are far more informative than normal pre-seed traction, and the same system adapted to cooling-pipe installation in ten days. That could be the beginning of a general construction platform. The problem is that custom-fleet manufacturing, charging, maintenance, worker assistance, and field service can consume every dollar of gross profit before the next robot ships. With no disclosed price per panel, gross margin, burn, or revenue, deleting the next financing from the model leaves no business model—only a functioning machine.

I am passing at the current evidence level. This is not a dismissal of the technology or team; it is a refusal to pay an unknown price for an already-financed, capital-intensive platform without knowing which commercial truth the next check buys. The one verifiable condition that flips me is a signed, paid multi-site EPC contract covering at least 100,000 panels, with disclosed per-panel contribution margin demonstrating that the existing fleet can finance its own expansion. That would prove purchase causality, constrain the venture moment, and show that Cosmic’s field data compounds inside a business rather than inside another fundraising story.
