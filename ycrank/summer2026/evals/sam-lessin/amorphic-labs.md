# Amorphic Labs -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and status of this as the first scarce check are all unknown. That is not evidence of an auction, but it leaves my primary gate unanswered. More importantly, Amorphic Labs names no card the financing flips: no dated reliability threshold, paid-customer target, or finite experiment whose success would materially reprice the business. “Build and sell an agentic demo product” is a company plan, not yet a venture moment.

The product does attack a concrete expense. It researches a prospect, populates the vendor’s application, operates a selected workflow, and produces a narrated video—work sales teams otherwise perform manually. But there is no documented customer, repeat usage, or purchase caused by that workflow. Subscriptions and metered fees are merely contemplated, with no public pricing. The proprietary execution traces and correction history are inferred future assets rather than an owned dark pool that has already changed customer behavior.

The strongest case is the team. Frank Li’s OpenAI technical work, NVIDIA ML-infrastructure experience, and earlier engineering roles plausibly cover the computer-use side; Dylan Kelly adds quantitative and AI-research experience. Public computer-use APIs also make this newly buildable. But the bridge has two shores, and the dossier shows no shipped sales product, operated RevOps workflow, or direct distribution access on the buying side. Technical ability can produce the agent; it does not establish that the agent can survive real customer applications, credentials, data states, and approval processes economically.

The bear case is sharper because the product’s labor substitution depends on reliability. Amorphic Labs itself warns that its agent may type, click, navigate, or modify information inaccurately and requires vendor review before distribution. If review and correction remain frequent, the automation becomes a service business with software rhetoric. If reliability improves through generally available models, Consensus, Reprise, and Walnut—funded with approximately $143 million, $82 million, and $56 million respectively—can pursue the same capability. The company presently owns neither the model layer nor demonstrated proprietary workflow data.

There is no Known Exception that rescues this. Amorphic has no viral adoption, early enterprise demand, rejected acquisition offer, measured conversion, retention, or operating accuracy comparable to the evidence that would justify overlooking price and model dependency. Delete the next financing and the dossier supplies no revenue, gross margin, burn, runway, or controllable cash metric from which to underwrite survival.

I am passing. The verifiable flip condition is ten paying software vendors renewing after six months; that would show the workflow causes durable purchases rather than producing impressive demos of demo production. Until then, I am being asked to finance an undifferentiated reliability curve without knowing the strike price or the discrete truth my check purchases.
