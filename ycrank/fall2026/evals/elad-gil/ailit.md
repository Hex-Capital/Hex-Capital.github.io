# Ailit -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Cheaper batteries could let North American railroads buy electric propulsion without replacing their locomotives or financing the equipment themselves. For Ailit, the controlling variable is delivered energy cost at actual corridor utilization: can it charge 20% below diesel while earning an adequate return on batteries, exchange sites and spare-pack inventory? The advertised discount establishes a measurable customer proposition. It does not establish the economics that make the supplier worth owning.

The market change is real but only partially quantified. Global battery pack prices fell 8% to $108/kWh in 2025. That helps procurement; it does not establish installed rail costs, particularly when North American packs cost more than the global average. Swapping also trades charging downtime for inventory and infrastructure expense. Higher utilization could spread those costs across more energy sales, but Ailit supplies no operating measurements that establish the crossover. I give the battery cost decline some credit without treating the proposed customer savings as demonstrated margin expansion.

The strongest bull case is that Superwagon removes several adoption obstacles together. It connects to an existing diesel-electric locomotive, charges batteries off-board and retains diesel backup. A railroad could therefore test electrification without committing its fleet to an entirely new propulsion system. Tagesson’s reported work on Koenigsegg batteries and Candela’s ferry certification and production makes that deployment path more credible. This fits my pattern of entering physical industries where relevant expertise could translate a technology shift into better economics. Banderet’s prior company-building at J12 and NOWO adds limited evidence of startup experience, although his operating role at Ailit is unclear; neither biography establishes unusual sacrifice or specific lessons applied here.

The distinction is between a plausible deployment path and an owned operating advantage. AilitOS is intended to coordinate packs, charging and departures, which could eventually give Ailit responsibility for a recurring operational result. Those systems remain under development, with the first full-scale prototype targeting summer 2027. I cannot score the proposed network as an existing moat. Dayton-Phoenix’s reported locomotive integration testing also shows that battery tenders are accessible to established suppliers. Ailit would have to win through delivered economics and dependable operation, rather than the tender architecture alone.

There is no documented customer pull to resolve that uncertainty. Rail trade coverage, at least SEK10M in previous financing and the separately reported $500K YC investment support visibility and development capacity; they do not establish railroad demand. I use the absent-evidence default for adoption, without treating pre-seed silence as failed demand. My physical-business exception requires demonstrated changes to incumbent unit economics, which Ailit has not yet supplied. The research-stage exception also does not apply: the dossier establishes relevant engineering experience, rather than frontier research and a credible financing path for the full infrastructure system. The red flag to guard against is attributing software economics to an asset-owning energy business before measuring its capital returns.

I would pass with my own money today. The objection to passing is substantial: if existing locomotives can switch energy sources cheaply, Ailit could acquire recurring energy demand through equipment railroads already operate. But that opportunity depends on the cost crossover, and the dossier does not establish it. My flip condition is one independently verified, paying corridor pilot demonstrating the promised 20% customer saving, positive contribution after battery depreciation and site costs, and preserved departure availability.
