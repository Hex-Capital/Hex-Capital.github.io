# Rational -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years from now, accounting firms may add clients without adding clerical headcount: software collects documents, prepares reconciliations, chases approvals, and sends only consequential exceptions to humans. Rational has one credible leak from that future. Its anonymized Global 500 customer reportedly doubled transaction volume with flat finance headcount while cutting close time 50%. But the dossier does not yet map the intervening steps from useful workflow automation to an enduring institutional layer. It describes what works now more clearly than what Rational uniquely becomes.

The working artifact is the strongest evidence. Tenace’s roughly 50-person team reports reclaiming about 20 hours per person each week, doubling its revenue run-rate, and achieving 98% accuracy on configured reconciliations. Another customer reports that Rational handles 80% of routine communications. Those are expensive behavioral changes, not waitlist theater: customers are entrusting financial work, approvals, and client relationships to the product. The weakness is provenance. Both studies come from Rational, revenue is undisclosed, and no retained cohort independently verifies that the gains persist after configuration and novelty wear off.

Jibril Moinuddin and Christ Xu can plausibly build and sell this. Jibril previously connected a reported 20,000 students through STEMplicity; Christ brings a $400,000 exit plus engineering experience at Tencent, Jane Street, and OpenAI. Their skills are complementary, and Jibril is clearly identified as CEO, so I do not see the leadership shell game that would stop me immediately. What I cannot find is the pre-company accounting wound: no years spent closing books, administering a practice, or building tax systems before this market became fashionable. The technical résumé is strong; the independently derived obsession remains unproven.

The broken system is visible but not fully decoded. Accounting firms lose scarce labor to inboxes, document chasing, reconciliation, and approval routing, sometimes rejecting paying clients as a result. Rational returns time and client capacity, yet the dossier does not identify a rule, incumbent rent, or artificial restriction that explains why this work remains trapped. Without that mechanism, automation may improve margins without changing who owns the customer relationship or where durable power accrues.

The bull case stings. A deterministic tax engine scoring 92% strict exact-return accuracy, 300-plus integrations, firm-specific rules, and human approval gates could become the operating substrate beneath many accounting services. The customer results suggest it may already be doing more than the better-funded competition. But Basis, Numeric, Digits, Truewind, and Fieldguide are converging on overlapping work, and Rational has not shown that it controls a scarce dependency rather than a good orchestration layer. The broad financial-data and communication permission surface also lacks a publicly inspectable custody and consent architecture. With my own money, that is a gate, not a footnote.

My 36-month prediction is that Rational reaches roughly $3 million to $8 million in ARR among accounting and advisory firms but remains a valuable workflow vendor rather than the defining accounting infrastructure layer. I am neutral and would not write the check today. One condition flips me: an independently referenceable cohort of at least ten firms showing twelve-month retention, approximately doubled client capacity without proportional headcount, and auditable permission logs from the same deployments. That would prove both durable behavior and that the accumulated workflow layer belongs to Rational.
