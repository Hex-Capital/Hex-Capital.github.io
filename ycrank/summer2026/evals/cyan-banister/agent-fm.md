# Agent FM -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the developer is no longer watching terminals; she is supervising a shifting fleet of software agents by exception, intervening when confidence drops, tools fail, or agents disagree. Agent FM has found a real leak from that future: Codex usage exceeded one million developers and doubled in roughly six weeks, while engineers are already running parallel, long-duration sessions. But the dossier stops at the leak. It does not show the intervening steps that make a separate audio-and-chat application the durable control surface rather than a feature absorbed by Codex, Claude Code, an IDE, or the operating system.

The working artifact deserves credit. Agent FM passively observes existing sessions without proxying them, detects both Claude Code and Codex, and has shipped on macOS and Windows 10/11. Cross-tool observation is more interesting than another single-agent wrapper because it preserves the user’s choice of models and tools. Yet availability is not behavior. The 809 LinkedIn followers, free credits, and waitlisted $48 and $144 annual plans do not tell me that users listen through a workday, interrupt agents from the shared chat, or become unable to return to terminal-hopping. There is no costly or weird ritual here yet.

The sharp bull case is that passive, vendor-neutral observation becomes the enterprise control plane before model vendors cooperate. Session, token, decision, and tool-error data could accumulate into a useful map of how agents fail across providers; Teams dashboards, centralized billing, SSO/SAML, and Privacy Mode point toward that destination. A small team could enter as an ambient radio and emerge as the record of agent work. But today that learning loop is inferred, no data scale or measured improvement exists, and Conductor, Omnara, and OpenAI already occupy adjacent supervision surfaces. Agent FM controls an interface, not yet the bottleneck beneath the wave.

The founders did encounter the problem personally, and Aniket Deshpande’s Meta engineering experience gives the team credible user fluency. What I cannot find is the deeper first-believer trail: dated experiments before the company, public technical artifacts, evidence of unusual obsession, or proof that either founder recruited the other around a long-held view. The economic wound is also modestly specified—attention is consumed by terminal switching and log reading—but its rent is not quantified, no institutional gatekeeper captures it, and the product has not yet demonstrated that it returns meaningful occupational control rather than merely presenting the same information more pleasantly.

The privacy issue presses harder than the feature competition. Limited session context may be sent to Gemini or OpenAI, which can include proprietary code, prompts, assumptions, and failures. “Enforced Privacy Mode” is a promise, not a documented custody architecture. Cross-provider support reduces dependence on one closed model owner, but it also multiplies compatibility and data-boundary surfaces; Omnara’s archived Claude wrapper shows how quickly upstream changes can erase product ground. I will not invent a 50/50 governance problem from missing information, but the dossier does not identify an accountable decision-maker or contractual tiebreaker either.

I pass today. The exception case would require the artifact to produce behavior strong enough to outweigh its thin stack position—teams voluntarily keeping Agent FM open, paying, renewing, and trusting it with sensitive sessions. My flip condition is one named 50-plus-engineer customer renewing a paid deployment after security review, with verified local or customer-controlled handling of session context. That single event would demonstrate both costly behavior and that the privacy gate can be crossed.
