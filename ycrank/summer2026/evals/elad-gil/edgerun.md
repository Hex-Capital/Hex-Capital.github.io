# Edgerun -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: lighter actuators and denser batteries now let infantry units buy powered load assistance that may survive operational use, and the controlling variable is whether a 10 lb system produces enough independently verified endurance improvement to justify procurement and battlefield complexity. Edgerun has identified the mechanism—hip torque contributes roughly 60% of loaded-walking force—but not the required performance threshold or the acquisition economics. Internal claims of 30–45% assistance are promising; third-party verification is still underway.

The technology shift is the strongest part of the case. During the preceding 18 months, one-pound actuators reportedly crossed 30 Nm while 21700 cells reached 450 Wh/kg. Those changes enable a 12-plus-hour, field-swappable system with sub-five-second removal. This is not software decorating an unchanged physical product. It potentially converts powered assistance from an impractical laboratory device into field equipment. The missing step is economic: the dossier provides neither unit cost nor evidence that deployment reduces injuries, evacuations, or operating costs enough to support product-style margins and scaled purchasing.

The 100-plus Ukrainian field tests since May 2026 matter more than a generic pilot count because infantry, special-operations, and National Guard operators are testing the device in the intended environment. Balistyka integration and a daily Kyiv feedback loop also show that Edgerun is designing around combat kit rather than producing an isolated robotics demonstration. But tests are not product-market pull. There are no disclosed orders, contracts, prices, revenue, repeat-unit purchases, or deployed fleet. The evidence demonstrates access and iteration, not yet demand.

Durability will depend on whether those field loops become proprietary system knowledge: gait-control data, equipment integrations, failure histories, permissions, and procurement relationships. Today the components are intentionally non-exotic—quasi-direct-drive actuators, composites, and CAN bus—and several funded companies already produce adjacent hip-assistance or industrial exoskeletons. A well-capitalized competitor could reproduce the hardware architecture. Edgerun must make the integrated operational system, rather than the actuator package, the product.

David Venegas has unusually relevant execution experience across Durin, Hadrian, and Stoke Space, spanning operations, industrial manufacturing, and aerospace software. That reduces hardware-development risk, and H.R. McMaster’s backing may improve defense-market access. It does not establish the specific founder signal I weight: no prior startup outcome, personal need, prolonged unpaid commitment, or costly sacrifice is documented. The eight-person team and multi-jurisdiction footprint across California, Washington, Kyiv, and Taiwan may also be premature complexity before performance and procurement are proven.

The bull case resembles the mechanism that made Anduril exceptional: a physical defense product can escape commodity economics when technology enables a new operational capability and government demand converts quickly. The material difference is that Edgerun has not yet demonstrated independent performance, product margins, or purchasing behavior. My verifiable flip condition is a paid U.S. Department of Defense order issued after independent testing confirms at least a 30% improvement in loaded-march endurance without increasing mission failure or injury rates. Until then, I would monitor rather than invest.
