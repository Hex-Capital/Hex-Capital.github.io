# Codag -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Codag has proved a useful discontinuity without proving a consequential company. Its 5.9–7.5× reduction in agent tokens directly attacks a new constraint in production debugging, and 98–99% incident-answer rates suggest compression need not destroy the evidence an agent needs. But cheaper context for coding agents is still a workflow improvement. The dossier quantifies neither the operational spending displaced nor a path by which this becomes an orders-of-magnitude expansion of reliable expert debugging.

The technical case is the strongest part. Raw logs plus grep is a named incumbent workflow, and Codag tested 51 incidents, two agent backends, and three window sizes across 102 paired runs per window. Preserving source lines and nearby context also makes the output auditable. This is substantially more than an interface wrapper. It is not yet top-quartile proof because the incidents were synthetic, the evaluation was internal, and the deterministic layer adapts published Drain3 work that Codag releases under an MIT license.

The largest kill risk is therefore generalization, not token compression. Capital should test heterogeneous production logs from real failures before funding sales or a broader observability surface. Codag published artifacts and a reproducible deterministic benchmark, which is good experimental behavior, but supplied no customer-defined pass threshold, experiment cost, deadline, or stop condition. A benchmark demonstrating that the mechanism can work is not yet a plan for killing the thesis cheaply.

Michael Zhou has relevant infrastructure experience at Okta and Shopify, built a prior developer tool that reached 655 GitHub stars, and reportedly made five pivots in six months. That creates the strongest opposing case: one technical founder with demonstrated open-source distribution may use compression as a wedge into the enormous observability value pool, much as Block’s narrow initial device evolved into broader financial infrastructure. The material difference is that Codag’s current repositories have only 45 and 20 stars, no disclosed revenue or repeat usage, and no complementary recruit owning enterprise security, data infrastructure, or go-to-market risk.

The economics are attractive only in the narrow software sense: a $19 Pro plan and $499 Team plan can sit far below enterprise observability spending. Yet no bottom-up model connects inference cost, log volume, gross margin, and customer savings. Worse, Dash0, Honeycomb, Sentry, and Cribl already control telemetry workflows and can bundle similar compression. Open-sourcing the reproducible portion while retaining no demonstrated proprietary learning loop leaves Codag exposed to precisely that response.

I expect Codag to pivot within 36 months toward an agent-observability or telemetry-control product and remain below $1 million ARR; compression alone is too copyable and too bounded. I pass today. The one condition that flips the decision is a reproducible production benchmark across three named enterprise customers showing at least 5× lower end-to-end debugging cost than their existing Sentry, Cribl, or equivalent workflow while sustaining paid renewal.
