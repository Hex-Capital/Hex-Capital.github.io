# Spacesheep -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Makarov has built something close to a problem he encountered at Anthropic: agents can produce work faster than people can comfortably review it. That is a promising place to find an idea. But I want to know what happened when a particular user could not use Spacesheep. What broke? What did he change? How long did it take? The dossier establishes relevant experience and a shipped product, but contains no such episode. I cannot infer resourcefulness from having worked at Anthropic, Pinecone, or Twitter ([founder profile](https://www.ycombinator.com/founders/michael-makarov)).

The strongest evidence is the software itself. Spacesheep publishes interactive HTML into shared spaces with comments, agent edits, version history, and semantic search. Connectors and browser extensions make this more than an architectural proposal ([product](https://spacesheep.dev/), [getting started](https://spacesheep.dev/start)). This fits my pattern of backing programmable interfaces through which technical users can invent workflows. The important qualification is that accepting multiple agents does not yet establish that users are inventing anything indispensable on top. The functioning product earns substantial technical credit; its existence cannot also establish founder behavior or demand.

James Briggs is the most useful demand evidence. He uses Spacesheep for coding plans, explanations, and reviews—a specific job for an identifiable user ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/spacesheep)). I would learn more from watching him work than from the launch ranking. The October 27 snapshot of 180 Product Hunt upvotes and live rank #4 measures attention. The Chrome extension’s 22 users measures installations. Neither tells us whether a team returns when the novelty wears off. Briggs supplies some evidence of use, but the dossier gives no frequency, payment, or dependence. At this stage the small numbers are perfectly acceptable. Their limited meaning is what matters.

The strongest case for investing is that Makarov helped build internal deployment and the first CC Artifacts version before starting Spacesheep. He has unusually direct knowledge of both the problem and the machinery required to solve it. That supports the origin of the idea independently of the current product. Deployment, permissions, and collaboration also contain useful, unglamorous work. But Notion already supports external agents and interactive HTML blocks ([Notion, July 2026](https://www.notion.com/releases/2026-07-01)). Spacesheep therefore needs a small group that finds its particular workflow substantially better. Team pricing of $10 per member per month gives that group a straightforward way to demonstrate preference; published prices alone establish no willingness to pay ([plans](https://spacesheep.dev/plans)).

I would remain neutral and keep my money for now. The working-prototype exception deserves consideration, but there is no documented rapid founder correction or unmistakable user pull strong enough to override the missing resourcefulness evidence. Nor does acknowledging criticism of the launch video establish an evidence-driven product change. These are gaps, rather than evidence of obstinacy. I see no affirmative sign of dependence on a major partner, premature delegation, or a plan that ends at launch day. My flip condition is one documented customer episode: Makarov encounters a blocked workflow, ships a workaround within two weeks, and that customer’s team subsequently uses it weekly for six consecutive weeks. That would connect the impressive software to the founder behavior and persistent need on which I would actually bet.
