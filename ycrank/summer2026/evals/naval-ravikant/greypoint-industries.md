# Greypoint Industries -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have learned this problem by touching it. Darcy Zhang served as a Canadian Armed Forces infanteer; Nic Chu and Jordan Khoo worked on defense systems at General Dynamics; Jordan also built controls at TerraSense; Steven Xu worked on embedded systems at Bosch and autonomous boats at UBC. More importantly, the team converted that experience into a working aerial geolocation test, a Canadian Armed Forces demonstration, and a signed government contract. Credentials describe potential. Those artifacts reveal specific knowledge.

Greypoint is also building where copied code is insufficient. LEGION must detect and fuse physical radio signals, while ARROW must turn that picture into reliable interception. A competitor can reproduce the software architecture faster than it can reproduce RF performance, flight hardware, military integrations, and manufacturing experience. But the durable asset remains embryonic: Greypoint discloses no proprietary emitter dataset, contested-environment benchmark, patent, production volume, or feedback loop showing that each deployment improves the next. This is real hardware leverage, not yet a demonstrated moat.

The market is large, but the path through it is less developed than the headline. Counter-UAS spending is projected to grow from $14.41 billion in 2026 to $55.25 billion in 2034, and Canada’s July 2026 Defence Drone Initiative creates an unusually direct procurement opening. Yet that is a top-down category, not a causal adjacency map. Greypoint has not shown whether LEGION becomes a broader electronic-warfare sensor layer, whether ARROW expands into other autonomous missions, or whether procurement remains confined to a narrow Canadian counter-drone program.

The contract matters because a government has crossed from interest into a transaction. It does not yet prove pricing power: the value, gross margin, repeat order, interceptor consumption, and support burden are all undisclosed. Nor is this a permissionless system. One military customer does not make the product more useful to another customer, and every deployment may require more sales, integration, certification, and service labor. Greypoint fits my recent preference for physical infrastructure, but not my recurring pattern of users contributing supply, data, or distribution without proportional company effort.

The strongest bear case is technical rather than fashionable. Passive RF detection can miss silent or waypoint-controlled drones; the public record contains only one geolocation test and no range, accuracy, contested-spectrum, or swarm-scale interception results. Anduril, Fortem, and Epirus already possess validated systems, capital, and procurement access. In 36 months, I expect Greypoint to reach roughly $3 million to $8 million in annual contracted bookings while remaining a specialized Canadian defense supplier rather than a compounding platform—but one failed contested-environment trial could reduce the system to an integration project inside a better-capitalized prime.

I see no evidence of the integrity, artificial-deadline, or reassurance-seeking red flags that would veto the founders. The known-exception case is that unusually capable builders can outweigh an immature market wedge, and this team has done enough to keep that possibility alive. It has not done enough for me to price the option confidently with my own money today. I remain neutral. The single flip condition is a repeat government order supported by published contested-spectrum results showing reliable multi-emitter geolocation and autonomous interception at operational range; that would prove both repeatable value capture and a substrate competitors cannot copy from a demo.
