# OpenRelay -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Prashant Patel helped build Amazon Bedrock and orchestrated clusters exceeding 10,000 GPUs, while Jaden Wang worked at Voltage Park, TensorDock, and founded Heaviside Compute. OpenRelay is a founder-authentic infrastructure bet trapped inside an unresolved two-founder structure: its highest signal is that both founders lived GPU orchestration before OpenRelay existed; its lowest is that neither founder is identified as CEO or tiebreaker. Biz and Tiz are visible. Rizz and authority remain hidden.

**Niantic** is the high rung for lived experience because John Hanke spent a decade building location systems before Pokémon Go made that history legible. OpenRelay shares the mechanism: Patel productionized inference at IBM, helped build Bedrock at AWS, and later operated 10,000-plus-GPU clusters; Wang independently accumulated HPC experience across Voltage Park, TensorDock, and Heaviside Compute. OpenRelay diverges because the dossier gives me professional adjacency, not the autobiographical moment when either founder personally felt idle capacity or unreliable inference break something they owned. OpenRelay sits closer to Niantic than to a market-analysis founder, but I have observed the résumé, not yet the human outside the pitch.

**HQ Trivia** is the low rung because Yusupov and Kroll never resolved who could break the tie, and product magic eventually became hostage to founder conflict. OpenRelay names Patel “Founder and CTO” and Wang simply “Founder,” with no CEO, equity structure, operating agreement, or conflict protocol. That is not a missing pre-seed metric; it is the exact red flag my unstated pattern tells me to treat as a near-term blow-up risk. OpenRelay’s shared Voltage Park history may create trust, but trust is not governance, and OpenRelay has supplied no evidence that one person can make the ugly decision when supply reliability, security, and revenue pull in different directions.

RunPod reports $120 million in annual revenue run rate and serves 500,000 developers across 31 regions, so OpenRelay is not entering a category sophisticated investors consider absurd. Over the next 18 months, OpenRelay’s “magically weird” quality will either compound through heterogeneous consumer GPUs and automatic failover or disappear into a price-comparison table beside RunPod, Vast.ai, and Lambda. OpenRelay currently shows no provider count, utilization data, public customer, or investor rejection on aesthetic grounds; the consensus-AI red flag therefore wins over the modest weirdness of coordinating NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, and CPUs. This is technically difficult, but difficult is not automatically non-consensus.

$0.18-per-hour dedicated GPUs make OpenRelay infrastructure, but the hardware belongs to independent providers and the failed VM must still be restarted or replaced. OpenRelay would move into my frontier-infrastructure tier if provider recruitment required it to finance, retrofit, or physically operate stranded compute in places hyperscalers ignore—something closer to turning wasted energy into a new asset class than brokering another cloud instance. OpenRelay instead owns secure tunnels, QEMU isolation, scheduling, and billing: meaningful systems engineering with capital intensity one layer removed. The score flips upward when OpenRelay’s control plane becomes inseparable from difficult physical supply rather than merely portable across it.

**Flock Safety** could be noticed in a hotel Wi-Fi scan and then encountered as a camera in the world; OpenRelay offers a dashboard, live Stripe billing, and 89 documented API operations. I can use OpenRelay, but I cannot yet observe OpenRelay independently of what its own control plane tells me. The third-party GPUs are physical, yet the customer experience remains a developer-tools abstraction whose reliability, isolation, and data deletion claims require founder testimony until real workloads expose them. OpenRelay is more tangible than an AI-agent wrapper, but it does not yet give me the tactile serendipity that made Flock Safety impossible to unsee.

RunPod’s $120 million revenue run rate makes the likely 36-month outcome legible: OpenRelay pivots from a general GPU marketplace into orchestration software for independent regional data centers, reaches $2 million–$6 million ARR, and remains a specialized control plane rather than displacing RunPod or Lambda. OpenRelay’s founders possess the domain depth to find that adjacent product, while OpenRelay’s absent network-effect evidence and unresolved authority make a clean two-sided-market breakout less likely. I am arguing with my future self: technical survival looks more probable than category leadership.

Patel’s Bedrock history and Wang’s TensorDock experience form the strongest bull case: OpenRelay could make fragmented hardware behave like one dependable cloud, use $0.29 RTX 4090 pricing to attract developers, and compound every new provider into better routing and availability. **HQ Trivia** is the known exception that stings—explosive product pull can temporarily override bad founder governance. OpenRelay has no equivalent override yet: no disclosed revenue, customer count, active-node count, utilization figure, or repeat workload proves that its network is pulling itself into existence. One verifiable condition flips my verdict: OpenRelay publicly names a single CEO with documented final tiebreaker authority. Until then, I am a warm bubbe with one foot in love and my own money still in my pocket.
