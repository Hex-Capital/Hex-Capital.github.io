# Litmus -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence case is too small as currently stated. Litmus can replace artificial coding screens and interviewer-heavy work trials with asynchronous, repository-specific assessments. That may improve hiring accuracy and save engineering hours, but the dossier quantifies only a $2.30 billion assessment-software market—not the cost of bad engineering hires, the interviewer capacity released, or an order-of-magnitude change in access to technical talent. This is a useful workflow improvement. It is not yet a 100x societal consequence.

The product is more substantive than a generic AI wrapper. Employer repositories and tickets become native-environment work trials; deterministic sandbox tests evaluate correctness while configurable LLM graders assess architecture and process. That is meaningfully different from standardized libraries and hosted IDEs. But different is not 5–10x better. Litmus provides no measured comparison against HackerRank, CodeSignal, or Karat on predictive validity, interviewer hours, candidate completion, false positives, or cost per successful hire. Worse, those incumbents already possess customer data, ATS distribution, and adjacent functionality sufficient to copy repository-derived tasks and workflow recording.

The strongest evidence is execution: 3,000 assessments and $60K ARR before or during Summer 2026, with Neo Scholars, Autumn AI, and Sleuth identified as users. Normal usage may also generate a proprietary corpus of candidate activity, submissions, and grading outcomes. The bull case is that a narrow assessment wedge becomes a learning system for engineering talent—one that understands how real work predicts later performance better than any standardized test. Execution-heavy software can justify an exception to my technical-discontinuity rule when it opens a much larger infrastructure value pool. Litmus has the wedge, but not the proof of that expansion: there is no longitudinal quality-of-hire study, demonstrated grading advantage, or disclosed link between assessment data and employee outcomes.

The largest kill risk is therefore validity, not customer acquisition. An LLM can generate plausible assessments and plausible scores while being systematically wrong about who becomes an excellent engineer. Repository confidentiality, extensive candidate recording, and ownership terms compound the adoption risk. Yet the dossier identifies no controlled experiment, pass/fail threshold, budget, or stop condition for validity or demographic performance. Capital spent building more assessments or acquiring more employers before answering that question would produce activity without removing the existential risk.

Shaivi Rau and Elena Zhao have relevant exposure across recruiting, startups, Meta, Two Sigma, and engineering research. Building a three-person company to 3,000 assessments and $60K ARR is evidence of speed. It is not evidence of belief revision or risk-tuned recruiting: there is no documented strategic change after disconfirming data and no complementary expert recruited to own psychometrics, employment validation, security, or bias. My 36-month base case is that Litmus remains below $2 million ARR and pivots toward internal engineering-performance analytics as incumbent assessment vendors converge on its hiring features.

I pass today. The red flag is not that Litmus uses AI; deterministic tests and employer context give it more substance than a prompt interface. The red flag is that its claimed accuracy—the entire economic mechanism—remains unbenchmarked while well-funded incumbents can reproduce the visible features. One result would flip me: a preregistered, independently audited study across at least 10 employers and 500 hires showing that Litmus predicts 12-month engineering performance at least twice as accurately as a named incumbent while reducing interviewer hours by at least 70%, at equal or lower cost per hire.
