# Kara -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Aarin Jhaveri has sold more than $5 million of diamonds, Anuveer Chadha built diamond quantum-sensing systems at Berkeley Lab, and Kara grows diamond in its own reactors. Kara is therefore a high-schlep, low-hacker-trail company: unusually native to a difficult physical process, but without the public artifacts that normally prove the founders are animals rather than merely well matched to a field. That pairing is attractive. It is also why I would invest without yet calling Kara a black swan.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness because Brian Armstrong ran toward custody, compliance, and banking friction everyone else avoided. Kara shares the important mechanism: Kara combines reactor growth, impurity control, finishing, characterization, and custom fabrication instead of treating diamond as a catalog item. The divergence is that Coinbase had a working wallet and a concrete user path, while Kara publishes no wafer diameter, yield, defect density, throughput, or customer qualification. Kara has chosen an excellent schlep, but has not yet proved it can survive the schlep.

The polished AI-wrapper company from my pass example is the low rung for hacker genealogy: credentials substitute for code, and hiring engineers is mistaken for building. Kara does not quite fit that failure pattern, because Chadha worked on diamond sensing and Kara says it operates its own reactors. But Kara leaves surprisingly few hacker tracks: no public repositories for Jhaveri or Chadha, and Lakhanpal’s `video-integrity-sdk` has zero stars. Kara sits above the low rung because operating reactors is itself a technical artifact, but below the **Dropbox**-style demo rung because no public result demonstrates what Kara’s artifact can actually do.

Aarin Jhaveri’s $5 million in diamond sales is Kara’s best evidence of resourcefulness, but resourcefulness compounds only when it is redirected into the startup’s immediate bottleneck. Over the next 18 months Kara must turn trade relationships into unusual feedstock access, customer introductions, or manually won qualification programs. If Jhaveri personally persuades one skeptical RF or laser customer to qualify an imperfect early part, Kara’s commercial knowledge becomes an animal advantage. If Kara merely benefits from knowing the diamond business, the $5 million is biography rather than a cereal-box stunt.

Anuveer Chadha’s Berkeley Lab diamond work and Jhaveri’s fifth-generation diamantaire background make Kara unusually organic. **Dropbox** began with Drew Houston repeatedly losing his USB stick; Kara begins with two founders who already lived on opposite sides of diamond, one selling it and one engineering with it. Kara’s origin is less narrowly personal than Dropbox’s, but it is plainly not the result of a market-sizing exercise. The idea-to-founder distance is short enough that Kara should notice process and customer constraints outsiders would discover only after burning several years.

All three Kara founders attended UC Berkeley, but the dossier does not say whether Kara’s founders built together for years or merely converged around the opportunity. The counterfactual is simple: evidence that Jhaveri, Chadha, and Lakhanpal had previously operated a reactor, shipped an instrument, or survived a failed project together would move Kara’s configuration into the top tier. Without that, Kara gets credit for being a complementary trio—diamond sales, chemical engineering, and software—but not for the long-tested chemistry of **Airbnb** or **Stripe**.

Element Six already reports reproducible 3-inch wafers, Diamond Foundry markets 100-mm wafers, and Kara publishes no qualification results. My 36-month forecast is that Kara reaches $2 million to $5 million in annualized revenue from custom diamond heat spreaders, submounts, and engineered layers for RF and laser customers, while Kara remains short of competitive wafer-scale production. The mechanism is specialization: Kara’s integrated reactors and fabrication can win ugly, low-volume jobs before Kara can beat incumbents on diameter or volume yield.

Element Six’s 60-plus patents and Diamond Foundry’s $350 million raised make the bear case sting: Kara could be three bright Berkeley founders entering a capital-intensive field where incumbents already have the wafers, patents, and qualification history, while Kara has no disclosed revenue, yield, or notable public hacker artifact. Kara’s answer is founder-problem fit plus willingness to own the whole process—Jhaveri’s $5 million of diamond sales, Chadha’s diamond-sensing work, and Kara’s in-house growth through fabrication—which is stronger evidence than a conventional materials pitch. I would flip to Pass if Kara cannot show, within 12 months, both measured yield on a specified component and a paid repeat order from a semiconductor, RF, or laser customer.
