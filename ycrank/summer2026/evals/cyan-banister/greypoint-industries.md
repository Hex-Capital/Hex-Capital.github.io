# Greypoint Industries -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Small units no longer wait for a remote counter-drone operator to identify one aircraft at a time; distributed sensors locate emitters, merge observations, and send autonomous interceptors toward operators, vehicles, and command nodes. Greypoint Industries has drawn a credible path into that world: passive LEGION detection, a shared battlefield picture, and ARROW effectors. The present-day leak is unusually concrete for pre-seed—a Canadian-government contract, a Canadian Armed Forces demonstration, and an aerial geolocation test. What keeps this below the highest future score is the unnamed distance between one target and a contested swarm: range, accuracy, latency, electronic-warfare performance, and coordinated interception remain undisclosed.

The founders have paid more operating tuition than their student biographies initially suggest. Darcy Zhang served as a Canadian Armed Forces infanteer; Nic Chu and Jordan Khoo worked inside General Dynamics systems engineering; Jordan also built controls at TerraSense; Steven Xu brought UBC Sailbot, Bosch embedded-systems, and engineering-physics experience. Three founders worked together through UBC’s Venture Founder program, and the team converted those ingredients into hardware that reached a military demonstration and contract. That is real first-believer proof. It is not yet evidence of a years-long private obsession, exceptional recruiting reach, or commercial command over a procurement organization, so I cannot give them the full Rizz–Tizz–Biz credit.

The broken rule is operational rather than economic. Existing defenses often chase an individual drone while the operator, jammer, or command post remains hidden; LEGION attempts to return location intelligence and response control to the soldier. That is a meaningful transfer of agency, but Greypoint has not identified the rent embedded in the current system—procurement cost, interceptor cost per kill, response delay, personnel burden, or the incumbent beneficiary. Without that accounting, I cannot tell whether this architecture changes defense economics or merely adds another box to an already crowded counter-UAS stack.

The strongest bull signal is costly institutional behavior. A government signed a contract and the CAF allowed the team into a demonstration environment; those acts carry more information than 1,107 LinkedIn followers or two competition prizes. The artifact also successfully geolocated an aerial target. But one demonstration is not yet weird, repeated user behavior. There are no disclosed redeployments, operator requests, paid follow-on orders, swarm interceptions, or performance measurements. The product is real; enduring pull is still unproven.

Here is the part that stings. Passive RF detection can miss waypoint-controlled or otherwise silent aircraft, while Anduril, Fortem, and Epirus already field well-funded alternatives spanning kinetic interception, net capture, and high-power microwaves. Greypoint may therefore be integrating commodity sensors and effectors into a procurement window created by Canada’s July 2026 Defence Drone Initiative, rather than controlling a scarce enabling layer. Deployment-derived signatures and manufacturing learning could become the asset underneath multiple effectors, but today those are hypotheses: there is no proprietary dataset, patent, production record, or demonstrated C2 dependency. Passive emitter collection also raises a custody and surveillance question that the dossier does not resolve.

I would invest a small personal check, with the conviction concentrated in the team and the contract-backed artifact—not in the market forecast or “autonomous swarm warfare” language. My verifiable flip condition is a contested CAF test showing repeatable geolocation and autonomous cueing against at least ten mixed targets, including RF-silent aircraft, with disclosed range, false-positive rate, and interception latency. Failure there would reveal LEGION as a narrow emitter detector inside a fashionable defense round; success would show Greypoint owns the battlefield-intelligence layer rather than merely another interceptor.
