# Aucert -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Aucert has credible builders, but I cannot yet identify the investment I am being offered. Post-money valuation: undisclosed. Competing term sheets: undisclosed. Whether my money would be the first genuinely scarce check: unknown. The conflicting $500K and $125K funding reports establish none of those things. YC membership does not prove an auction, and I will not pretend it does. Equally, I cannot underwrite cheap entry from silence. There is also no stated use of proceeds that names a finite experiment, a deadline, and a result that materially changes Aucert’s value.

The founders are the strongest reason to keep reading. Vivek Soneja’s founding mobile-architecture work at PhonePe and Rajesh Kumar’s 11 years at PayPal, including payments infrastructure and patented automated testing, connect building consequential mobile applications with checking whether they work. That fits my preference for founders who can translate between operating systems they have actually inhabited. PhonePe’s 500M-plus users belong to the employer, however; they are neither Aucert’s distribution nor evidence that these founders can sell this product. The CEO/CTO split identifies leadership roles without establishing an equal-founder decision problem.

The potentially valuable asset is Aucert’s accumulated understanding of each customer’s application: screen graphs, approved expectations, remembered paths, and historical verdicts. If that knowledge becomes part of every release, the product could own an important workflow even as models improve. Customer ownership of the underlying knowledge is sensible, but it leaves an economic question: what makes Aucert indispensable once the customer has taught it the application? BrowserStack already supplies mobile infrastructure and testing agents; Drizz offers visual tests directed in English. Autonomous exploration needs to produce something customers repeatedly buy. The October 2026 launch, free scan, and 317 LinkedIn followers do not yet demonstrate that causal link.

The strongest case against passing is that these founders know which mobile failures matter and can encode that judgment into a recurring verification service. They need not invent a better foundation model to build a useful business. But Aucert’s own terms acknowledge missed defects and retain human release responsibility, while its security disclosure says screen content reaches AI providers without redaction. Those facts make trustworthy operation and customer acceptance central to the product. My exceptions to AI skepticism have rested on measured conversion and retention; my exceptions to a conventional finite experiment have required an explicit capital plan and demonstrated founder outcomes. This dossier supplies relevant operating experience, but neither override is established.

I would pass with my own money today. Monthly subscription terms establish a billing structure, not a profitable base business: prices, inference and device costs, human review costs, burn, and a seed-funded break-even plan are all undisclosed. Those are missing facts, not proof of bad economics. My single condition for reopening is an independently referenceable 90-day paid deployment showing repeated release usage and positive contribution after device, inference, and human review costs. That would turn Aucert’s promising workflow into an observed economic asset. I could then price the remaining option.
