# Super Powers Mobility (SPM) -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

A parked vehicle running its engine just to power a radio is a wonderfully irritating object. Why carry fuel, make noise, and advertise your heat signature when the wheels aren’t moving? Super Powers Mobility (SPM) attacks that dependency with battery modules and power-export electronics: advertised specifications include 15 kWh modules, 12 kW continuous AC output, and 14 kW DC output. That gives me a concrete system to investigate. It returns some operational freedom to the person using the equipment, although the dossier does not quantify fuel savings, runtime, or the resupply burden actually removed ([SPM PEK](https://spm.us/pek)).

Move the clock to 2032 and I can imagine field equipment operating around interchangeable electrical power rather than an idling engine. But that is my daydream; SPM has not yet supplied the bridge. The dossier does not establish how its modules become interoperable across platforms, how qualification becomes repeatable, or which deployment proves that transition has started. Vehicle-mounted demonstrations establish that hardware exists. They do not establish that a shared power platform is emerging. This distinction matters to me: a useful battery business and the enabling infrastructure for a different way of operating require different bets.

Jonathan Powers has paid relevant engineering tuition. Cybertruck integration, electric-truck brakes at Workhorse, and ATV/UTV engineering at Honda put him inside the messy interfaces this product must survive. His off-road racing experience adds proximity to the user’s world. I give that substance more weight than accelerator membership. Still, the biography does not document a sequence of independent experiments or recruiting accomplishments that reveals an obsession preceding the company. The September 2026 pivot is not evidence of opportunism; the technical continuity is clear. It also cannot substitute for that missing founder history ([Pitt profile](https://www.engineering.pitt.edu/contentassets/696d570857a14aa485808233ce296f31/news_elpe_fall_091825_fnllo.pdf)).

The strongest argument for writing a check is that somebody has already spent meaningful money. SPM reports **$421,815 in realized revenue in one month** after shifting toward packs and power export. That is distinct from its **$792,000 qualified pipeline** and the earlier **$769,000 in vehicle preorders**. Together with the physical demonstrations, it makes dismissing SPM as a speculative hardware concept untenable. But the revenue disclosure does not identify repeat customers or distinguish recurring pack sales from custom engineering. The exception I make for compelling prototypes before revenue does not resolve that question: SPM already has sales; what remains uncertain is what those sales are teaching the company to reproduce ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/super-powers-mobility-spm)).

SPM does fit my pattern of looking underneath the visible vehicle for the enabling machinery. It controls system design across batteries, electronics, drivetrains, and controls. Yet Inventus already supplies custom military and industrial power systems, and SPM’s claimed performance advantage remains independently unverified. The consumer battery could eventually help finance harder development, but an early-2027 shipping target does not establish that financing loop. I would keep my money in my pocket today, with real interest. **My flip condition is a documented production reorder from one customer deploying the same core pack-and-export design across two vehicle platforms, with measured operating savings.** That would show that SPM’s engineering is accumulating into a reusable asset, rather than beginning again with every quotation ([SPM development](https://spm.us/custom-development), [Inventus](https://inventuspower.com/h-i-g-capital-invests-in-inventus-power/), [Defence Blog](https://defence-blog.com/pittsburgh-startup-pitches-ev-kit-to-elite-troops/)).
