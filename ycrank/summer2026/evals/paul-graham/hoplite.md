# Hoplite -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Ryan Morrissey and Bence Redmond are a strong pair attached to an idea that does not yet read as organic. Hoplite’s best signal is that Morrissey and Redmond studied together and built Blueprints before building Hoplite; its weakest is that Hoplite appeared one month after their unrelated prediction-market product, with no evidence either founder first built cloud migration to cure his own pain. This is a founder bet more than an idea bet. I would make it, but I would expect the founders to outlast the current product.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for co-founder configuration: two brothers with years of shared hacking and complementary ability, building the commerce tool they already needed. Morrissey and Redmond have the important part of that mechanism: Hoplite is their second product together, after Blueprints reached more than 250 public-beta users and processed over €500K of trading volume. Hoplite diverges because a shared university program and one prior product are not the Collisons’ lifetime of building together. Still, two founders who have already survived one product are much better than two founders paired for an application.

**Kiko** (2005) is the low rung on organic origin: a plausible product without documented founder pain, followed eventually by an eBay sale when the product failed to become indispensable. Hoplite shows the dangerous part of that pattern. Morrissey and Redmond were publicly operating Blueprints in June 2026 and launched Hoplite in July, while the dossier never says either founder personally suffered the environment-migration problem or built Hoplite for himself. Hoplite may be a fast observation about a fashionable market rather than an organic idea. With no stronger evidence, I apply the absent-evidence default.

Blueprints’ €500K-plus trading volume is evidence of resourcefulness, though not €500K of revenue. Morrissey and Redmond found more than 250 users, abandoned the product quickly, shipped Hoplite, and obtained a business installation that turns ignored Sentry errors into pull requests. That sequence can compound over 18 months because founders who repeatedly put artifacts in front of users learn faster than founders polishing decks. Hoplite has animal tracks. It does not yet have an Airbnb cereal-box moment: no paid stunt, rejected-customer reversal, or quantified current-product usage that proves Morrissey and Redmond will go through walls.

Hoplite handles repositories, authentication, environment variables, MCP servers, dependencies, and isolated cloud sandboxes. That can become a real schlep because credentials and source code turn a convenient developer tool into security infrastructure. Hoplite would move into Coinbase territory if Morrissey and Redmond personally endured security reviews, bespoke enterprise installations, and incident-response obligations that competitors avoided. Today Devin already offers local-to-cloud handoff and Factory accesses local environments, so Hoplite has not proved that the unpleasant work belongs uniquely to it. The schlep exists; ownership of the schlep does not.

**Dropbox** (2007) is the useful hacker parallel. Drew Houston’s demo was a technical artifact that made the founder legible before the business was legible. Hoplite likewise exists as a working credit-billed product, and Morrissey and Redmond previously shipped Blueprints rather than merely describing it. But Hoplite’s public hacker genealogy is thin: Morrissey has no public repositories, Redmond’s most visible repository has three stars, and Stripe and AWS internships are credentials rather than hacks. Hoplite passes the builder test without yet passing the tribal-marker test.

One business installation and three enormous converging competitors make a product pivot more likely than a straight line. By July 2029, I predict Hoplite will have stopped selling a general-purpose “cloud software factory,” repositioned around secure agent-environment and credential orchestration, and reached $1M–$3M in ARR. Hoplite’s durable asset is Morrissey and Redmond’s ability to build together; Hoplite’s fragile asset is the current framing. Strong pairs often find the company after they discover the first product was merely reconnaissance.

Cursor has more than $1B in annualized revenue, Cognition reported $73M ARR, and Factory has raised over $220M. The strongest bear case is brutal: Hoplite has no identified moat, its first installation is founder-reported, Devin already hands local sessions to the cloud, and the founders may have pivoted from Blueprints into the loudest market of 2026. Hoplite survives that objection narrowly because Morrissey and Redmond have shipped two products, attracted 250 prior users, and already placed the new one inside a business; this resembles determination more than slide-chasing. If Hoplite has fewer than five unaffiliated paying businesses using it repeatedly by January 2027, I would flip from Invest to Pass.
