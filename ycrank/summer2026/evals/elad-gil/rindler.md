# Rindler -- Elad Gil Evaluation

MCP made agents easier to connect, operations teams now want them to act inside closed web portals, and Rindler becomes large only if each maintained mapping produces enough successful authenticated runs across enough tenants to amortize verification and repair. That is the controlling belief. Reusable mappings and centrally applied route repairs establish the causal mechanism, but Rindler has not disclosed the mapping count, repair frequency, or run volume at which the economics compound. The market thesis is plausible and falsifiable, not yet demonstrated.

The strongest evidence is architectural. Rindler retains encrypted sessions, converts sites into verified actions and typed records, executes write operations, and repairs routes centrally. A stronger model could improve navigation while Rindler continues to own permissions, workflow state, verification, and structured outputs. This is meaningfully more durable than a browser-control wrapper. The counterweight is market structure: Kernel and Browserbase already supply its underlying browsers, while Browserbase, Browser Use, and Deck could move upward into schemas and managed repairs. Rindler has not shown that reproducing its mapping library would require significant time, money, or privileged access.

The November 2024 emergence of MCP is a real distribution change, and Rindler’s 38-run benchmark reports 4× faster execution, 3× lower cost, and 2× fewer failures than Browser Use. Those figures point toward improved automation economics, but two grocery tasks are too narrow to establish reliability on authenticated, bot-defended, or regulated portals. The $100 and $1,000 monthly plans demonstrate monetization design, not willingness to pay. Security procurement also constrains the adoption mechanism: there is no SOC 2, HIPAA, or PCI certification, and authenticated sessions can traverse several external browser and proxy providers.

The bull case is that centrally maintained mappings behave like shared infrastructure: every additional tenant lowers repair cost per run, and existing production coverage of Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, and state registries provides the beginning of a reusable distribution surface. If customers repeatedly delegate consequential actions, the route library and failure history could compound faster than browser vendors can productize them. That case stings because the product is already technically coherent. But “in production” is not accompanied by a named customer, paid account, repeat-use cohort, retention figure, or externally observed capacity constraint. Today the dossier proves task execution, not product-market pull.

Arthur De Los Santos and Michael Serrano have credible technical preparation through MIT CSAIL, computer science, physics, and Roblox machine-learning engineering. That supports their ability to build the verifier and repair system, but it does not establish costly commitment, prior startup learning, or personal exposure to the operational workflows being automated. Nor does their research record qualify for the rare-team exception used when demonstrated demand is absent: the dossier shows relevant engineering ability, not field-defining research or an unusually defensible technical asset.

My 36-month base case is that Rindler narrows from horizontal web translation into recruiting and ATS automation and remains below $3 million ARR because concentrated workflows are easier to sell and maintain than a universal mapping layer. I would monitor rather than invest today. The single flip condition is twelve months of evidence that at least ten paying enterprise customers each run 1,000 or more authenticated tasks monthly while centrally repaired mappings sustain at least 99% successful completion across interface changes.
