# CONUS -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

CONUS’s strongest asset is learning acquired through building. Paffrath started in 2022, Becker and Schulz joined in 2024, and the app shipped in January 2025. The team now reports more than 20 live European hospitals. That is substantially better evidence of specific knowledge than the founders’ university affiliations. Schulz’s biomedical instrumentation work also puts technical experience near the problem. I give the founders substantial credit for getting into real hospitals, while leaving the unspecified “six-month launch” milestone unresolved ([company history](https://conus-med.com/en/about-us), [YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/conus)).

The question for my money is what those deployments accumulate. CONUS reports 500,000 papers and 2,000 manufacturer documents, with answers linked to original pages. Useful retrieval is a product capability. A collection becomes a durable asset when competitors cannot obtain it, or customers cannot readily replace what they have built around it. The dossier establishes neither exclusive document rights nor demonstrated switching costs. Hospital-specific protocols could eventually supply that advantage, but their presence alone does not establish it. If another team copied the software, the surviving advantage remains unproven ([CONUS Intelligence](https://conus-med.com/en/conus-intelligence)).

The strongest objection to that caution is commercial access. Relationships with Johnson & Johnson, Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, and B. Braun could give CONUS an unusually valuable position between manufacturers and hospitals. Manufacturers add portfolios; hospitals supply the context that makes those portfolios useful. This could organize fragmented knowledge into shared infrastructure—an unstated pattern I favor. But the dossier does not show that another hospital improves an existing hospital’s experience, or that another manufacturer measurably brings users into the system. Twenty installations establish reach; they do not establish a compounding user loop. The bull case is credible enough to watch closely, but it has not yet supplied the mechanism that would make me invest.

CONUS does have a payer. Its institutional terms identify paid manufacturer services as a financing source, and indexed subscription prices run from €9.99 to €49.99 monthly. That earns direct monetization credit without inventing revenue or margins. The expansion path from surgical document retrieval into device field-team support and hospital inventory workflows is also intelligible: the same device-to-document mapping can serve adjacent buyers. Its economic breadth is less established. No adjacent spending estimate is supplied, patient-specific selection is excluded under current terms, and C8 Health and Incision already address neighboring workflows. I see room to maneuver, with limited evidence of how much value CONUS can capture there ([institutional terms](https://conus-med.com/en/institution-terms-of-use), [user terms](https://conus-med.com/en/terms-of-use)).

I sometimes accept missing monetization when users are building a network, or missing distribution when an exceptional prototype and unusually efficient founders justify the option. Neither exception resolves CONUS’s central uncertainty. Its shipping record is strong; capital efficiency and self-reinforcing usage are unestablished. There is no documented integrity or financing-pressure red flag. Manufacturer funding does create a principal-agent tension, although prohibitions on paid sorting and manufacturer access to hospital usage data are sensible safeguards. My position is **Neutral**: capable founders have built something useful, but I would hold my check. One verifiable flip condition would be a documented cohort of hospital renewals showing that customers pay specifically for manufacturer-authorized knowledge unavailable through competing products. That would turn access into an asset.
