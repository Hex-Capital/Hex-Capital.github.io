# BioDelivera -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A therapeutic developer chooses the diseased cell and payload, and delivery becomes a configurable component rather than another research program. BioDelivera is working on something that world needs. But “precisely targets only diseased cells” contains an enormous promise inside the word *only*. Naming HER2, PDGFRβ, and PD-L1 gives me something concrete to investigate; it does not yet establish selective delivery at a useful dose. The dossier supplies a plausible destination, but not the demonstrated intermediate steps that make the six-year journey legible.

The founders are the strongest reason to keep looking. Ikeda and Orikeda Trashi conducted doctoral research on the delivery technology they are commercializing; the company attributes five years of research to the platform. That is substantially more interesting than two people discovering drug delivery during accelerator applications. The reported Gassensmith Lab grant collaboration adds evidence of institutional engagement. I see sustained technical commitment, although the record does not yet establish their ability to recruit beyond themselves or turn capital into specific commercial progress. Their 2025 UT Dallas competition win is encouragement, not a substitute for that evidence; neither conflicting prize figure, $12,000 or $15,000, changes my judgment.

Bacterial self-assembly without subsequent modification is the detail I would circle. If reproducible, it could remove a manufacturing step while making a carrier useful across genetic medicines and small molecules. That fits my recurring preference for the concealed enabling layer beneath more visible products. But BioDelivera has not publicly established exclusive rights or comparative manufacturing performance, and Aera already pursues programmable protein nanoparticles among other delivery approaches. Aera’s $193 million financing does not invalidate this team. It does mean “programmable particle” alone cannot tell me which scarce capability BioDelivera controls.

Why are developers stuck? The dossier identifies difficulty reaching tissues beyond the liver. That is a biological constraint worth solving, but it does not identify an institutional rule, the party collecting its rent, or a specific transfer of control to patients or practitioners. I cannot award the same targeting claim again as evidence of economic liberation. Likewise, reported efficacy in tumors, muscle, and brain is a technical assertion, not evidence that an outside developer repeatedly commits laboratory time, payloads, or money to this carrier. Revenue is unnecessary here; costly outside use would be informative.

The strongest argument against passing is that I could miss the enabling technology precisely because its first believers are still in the laboratory. The sisters have relevant research depth, and the manufacturing approach could support several therapeutic categories. My exception for pre-revenue technical companies applies in principle. What supported that exception was a working prototype accompanied by concrete outside commitments; neither is sufficiently documented here. The founder-record discrepancy also deserves resolution, but a historical third founder is not evidence of concealed leadership. Ikeda is identified as CEO, and nothing establishes equal voting control, a fundraising-title shell game, or a severe privacy problem.

I pass on investing my own money on this dossier. BioDelivera fits the physical-enablement pattern, but its strongest evidence concerns the researchers rather than an externally demonstrated delivery capability. One verifiable result would flip my decision toward investing: an independent therapeutic developer reproduces functional payload delivery to a prespecified tissue beyond the liver, at tolerable exposure, across repeat BioDelivera production lots. That would make the particle a credible leak from the future I want to inhabit.
