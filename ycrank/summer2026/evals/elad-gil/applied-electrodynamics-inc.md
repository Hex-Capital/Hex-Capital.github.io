# Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: construction teams facing more than $20 billion in claimed annual rework may buy a room-distance wall-imaging system if one economic variable holds—WaveSight must produce sufficiently reliable spatial data that contractors can act without destructive verification. Applied Electrodynamics states the relevant technical threshold clearly: a scale-native point cloud accurate to 1 mm across sheetrock, tile, marble, quartz, brick, and insulation. That is a falsifiable proposition, not generic hard-tech language. The problem is that every performance fact comes from the company; there is no independent benchmark, field-validation dataset, specification sheet, disclosed price, or measured reduction in rework.

The technology could matter substantially. A scanner operated from across a room would change labor and adoption relative to the $199.95 Walabot, which must move against the wall and penetrates roughly four inches. But the dossier does not yet show the economic step change. I cannot tell whether WaveSight replaces destructive inspection, shortens a documented workflow from hours to minutes, or merely gives crews another indication they must verify manually. The cited $134 million through-wall-radar market, growing 6.3% annually, is too small and slow to carry the outcome by itself. Applied Electrodynamics must demonstrate that better imaging expands the category into construction-quality spatial documentation, rather than winning share in an existing instrument niche.

Demand remains unproven. Preorder registration has no unit count or price; 148 LinkedIn followers and “hundreds of conversations” at the Nashville Build Expo establish interest, not product-market pull. The team is also requesting introductions across contractors, industrial inspection, and defense. These buyers have different validation standards, procurement cycles, and required outputs. At this stage, the cross-market scope looks less like reusable distribution and more like uncertainty about which market actually pulls the product.

The strongest bull case is the unusually concentrated technical team. Bilgehan Avser led antenna work across four iPhone generations; Brian Huppi worked on iPhone multitouch and the first Nest thermostat; Paul Leutheuser shipped Apple hardware; George McLean worked on semiconductor imagers, retinal implants, and optical sensing. The four founders have worked together for more than eight years, and the company has already combined custom radio hardware with reconstruction algorithms. This could be a legitimate rare-team exception in a difficult physical category. Yet the cited 250-plus patents are not shown to belong to Applied Electrodynamics, and experience shipping sophisticated products does not prove desperation, startup learning, or ownership of a defensible technical asset.

The product is deeper than a superficial software attachment, but it does not yet own the construction workflow. RGB and thermal fusion remain planned, while integrations, permissions, historical building state, verified scans, and downstream actions are absent. Existing RF vendors prove that penetration alone is not the discontinuity. The company’s advantage must be trusted geometry that flows into inspection records, BIM systems, work planning, or autonomous tools. Otherwise improved sensing becomes a hardware feature exposed to Vayyar’s capital base and other radar suppliers.

I pass today because the combined market and technology evidence does not clear the primary gate. The physical-company exception requires documented reconstruction of incumbent economics; this dossier currently offers a striking performance claim without field proof, customer pull, or unit economics. One condition flips the decision: an independent contractor deployment showing that room-distance scanning maintains near-1 mm accuracy in real walls and reduces a named inspection or rework workflow by at least 10×, accompanied by repeat paid use. Without that result, my 36-month base case is a pivot toward narrower, higher-value industrial or defense inspection rather than a large construction platform.
