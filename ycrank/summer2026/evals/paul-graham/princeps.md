# PRINCEPS -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when PRINCEPS first tried to insure something. There is no named carrier that refused a risk, no operator whose contract exposed a modeling failure, and no workaround the founders shipped in response. The Ornn relationship and four advertised roles show motion, but not resourcefulness under resistance. I cannot infer the most important founder quality from Oxford credentials or a competition final. With no adverse behavior either, this criterion stays at its missing-evidence default.

The technical evidence is slightly better. PRINCEPS says it has a workbench combining SLA terms, market prices, grid data, failure modes, and contract structures ([PRINCEPS mission page](https://www.princeps.dev/mission)). That is more than a diagram, but less than a measured proof. There is no priced policy, quoted loss probability, backtest, live telemetry threshold, or carrier acceptance. Parametrix already monitors 7,000 cloud services and 750 data centers, while American Compute reports 600,000 hardware-pricing datapoints. Against those concrete substrates, PRINCEPS has described the machine but has not shown it running.

Who wants the crude product now? Ornn is a named marketplace relationship, so this is not pure market brainstorming. But the dossier does not say Ornn repeatedly uses the workbench, refers neoclouds, pays PRINCEPS, or has produced a bound policy. YC, NVIDIA Inception, London Tech Week, and a projected $74.14 billion market are institutional or category signals, not user pull. More worrying, the company is advertising two GTM roles before the dossier shows that a founder has personally learned why the first operator, lender, or carrier says yes. Distribution also depends on Ornn and unnamed carriers. Those are versions of the partner red flag until an actual transaction proves otherwise.

The strongest case for PRINCEPS is the schlep. Trofimova worked in data-center project finance, and Hector studied energy systems and uncertainty. They are not tourists choosing “AI insurance” solely from a market report. Sparse loss histories, changing GPU prices, SLA interpretation, carrier capacity, and contract-specific exposure are precisely the ugly problems that can hide a valuable company. This is the company’s best evidence. But the dossier only infers that the founders’ prior work produced the idea, and it discloses no prior underwriting or actuarial experience. Hiring an actuary may fill a skill gap; it does not prove the founders have already crossed it.

The bull case stings because these inputs could be complementary: infrastructure finance supplies the customer problem, energy modeling supplies one technical component, and Ornn could supply distribution and reliability data. Bindwell is the relevant exception mechanism: limited conventional credentials can be overridden by numerical technical proof tied to direct problem exposure. The material difference is that PRINCEPS has no comparable measured result. Its unstated-pattern fit therefore fails at the decisive point: no proof spike, no accumulated proprietary dataset, and no working product whose use arrived before the financing story.

I expect PRINCEPS within 36 months to have pivoted from carrier-bound insurance into risk-scoring software for GPU lenders, with less than $1 million ARR. The founders may have found a real neglected problem, but they have not yet shown that they can make the insurance route work. I would pass today. The single flip condition is verifiable: show one bound policy for a named operator or lender, including the carrier, insured exposure, premium, and a documented pricing or placement blocker the founders overcame themselves.
