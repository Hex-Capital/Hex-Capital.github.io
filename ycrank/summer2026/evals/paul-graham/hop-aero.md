# Hop Aero -- Paul Graham Evaluation

A $1.25 million U.S. Air Force contract makes Hop Aero a schlep-heavy company with an unusually consequential gap: Hop Aero has proved more about enduring government and hardware friction than about whether Jacob Balaj and Matija Milenovic form the durable founder pair required to survive it. The highest signal is not “rocket cargo.” It is that Hop Aero built and tested a prototype in under 30 days, hot-fired its own engine, and persuaded the Air Force to pay before proving the full vehicle. The lowest signal is the missing co-founder history. Rockets magnify every crack between founders.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung on schlep blindness because compliance work that looked like an obstacle became the asset. Hop Aero shares that mechanism: containerized launches, hypersonic re-entry, unprepared-surface landings, environmental approval, and military procurement are five schleps hiding inside one sentence. Hop Aero diverges from Coinbase because regulatory endurance alone cannot make Rook work; Hop Aero must still bridge the enormous distance between a tethered small-scale flight and carrying 250 kg over 750 km in 15 minutes. I like the unpleasantness. I do not confuse unpleasantness with physics.

**Loopt** (2005) exposed the danger of relying on one unusually forceful founder when the surrounding configuration was not yet the source of conviction. Hop Aero names two relevant founders, but Hop Aero gives no evidence that Balaj and Milenovic worked or built together before starting it. Their skills fit—Balaj worked on Falcon 9 and LauncherOne, while Milenovic scaled Ukrainian loitering-munition production from 10 to more than 100 units monthly—but complementary résumés are not the same as founder chemistry. A rocket startup can survive an engine failure more easily than a founder divorce.

A prototype built and tested in under 30 days puts Hop Aero closer to **Dropbox** (2007) than to a deck-first aerospace project. Drew Houston’s video mattered because it compressed a claim into an artifact; Hop Aero’s tethered flight and engine hot-fire do the same, though at a much earlier fraction of the promised machine. Hop Aero also converted the artifact into a $1.25 million contract. That is animal behavior: ship something partial, use it to open a door, then ask for the next impossible thing.

Jacob Balaj’s Marine Corps avionics work and Matija Milenovic’s defense-production experience make Hop Aero look organic, but the dossier never says either founder personally needed 250 kg delivered 750 km in 15 minutes. Hop Aero would move into the top rung if Balaj could point to a specific failed resupply operation he experienced, or if Milenovic built Rook because Ukrainian operators had asked for it while he was scaling munitions production. Without that causal story, Hop Aero may be an expert-selected opportunity rather than a tool its founders could not stop themselves from making.

No public GitHub repositories matter less for Hop Aero than they would for a software startup, because aerospace hackers leave scorch marks instead of commits. Over the next 18 months, Hop Aero’s genealogy will compound only if Balaj’s SpaceX, Virgin Orbit, Masten, and SpinLaunch experience produces rapid test cycles rather than aerospace ceremony. Milenovic’s prior propulsion IP sale and 10-to-100-unit production ramp are stronger evidence than an aerospace degree. Hop Aero has hacker ancestry in hardware, but the next untethered failure-and-rebuild cycle will reveal whether it has hacker speed.

Within 36 months, Hop Aero will have accumulated $8 million to $20 million in Air Force and Navy development contracts but will not yet operate the promised reusable 250-kg, 750-km Rook service; Hop Aero will narrow the first deployment to one-way contested resupply over a shorter range. The mechanism is the signature: Hop Aero is unusually willing to attack procurement and regulatory schleps, while its unproven founder history and immense technical gap make a narrower operational wedge more likely than the complete machine arriving on schedule.

**Inversion Space** has raised $54 million, Grid Aero has raised $26 million, and Sierra Space has raised at least $1.95 billion, so the stinging bear case is that Hop Aero is undercapitalized in a category where a tethered model and hot-fire are almost theater compared with hypersonic re-entry and autonomous landing. Hop Aero’s answer is speed and paid validation: a sub-30-day prototype plus a $1.25 million Air Force contract is more persuasive than a large financing announcement. I would write the check because animals leave tracks, and Hop Aero has left three. I would flip to Pass if Hop Aero has not completed an untethered flight carrying at least 50 kg over 100 km by July 2027.
