# Zaplar -- Paul Graham Evaluation

More than 100 hotel interviews make Zaplar a schlep-rich company with animal-poor evidence. Zaplar is volunteering to replace PMS, POS, booking, messaging, and payments—the operational plumbing other founders wisely avoid—but five waitlist signups in 48 hours are not the equivalent of forcing a working artifact into users’ hands. Zaplar’s analytical signature is therefore unusual: the founders found an excellent swamp, but the dossier does not yet show that they are animals capable of crossing it.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness because Brian Armstrong built the wallet and then embraced the compliance grind everyone else avoided. Zaplar shares the important mechanism: every property brings migrations, fiscal rules, payment failures, staff training, and ugly configuration work. Zaplar diverges where it matters most. Coinbase had an artifact before conviction; Zaplar has no disclosed installation, revenue, retention, or completed migration, while Oracle already supports hospitality operations across more than 200 countries and territories. Zaplar has identified the schlep, but has not proved it enjoys the schlep.

**Kiko** (2005) is the low rung for resourcefulness applied to company survival: capable hackers reached an eBay sale before they found a way to force the product into demand. Zaplar does not show that acquihire instinct, and interviewing 100 hotels is better than writing a plan in a room. But Zaplar’s five waitlist hotels joined around the earlier event-booking product, not the later full-stack hotel operating system. There is no Zaplar demo story, manual installation, paying property, or cereal-box stunt. Animals leave tracks. Zaplar has left research notes.

Axel Andersson Lingbert worked in hotels and restaurants, which gives Zaplar an organic starting point rather than a market discovered in a spreadsheet. Over the next 18 months, that origin compounds only if Zaplar keeps narrowing each hotel complaint into software Axel personally recognizes from the floor. The danger is already visible: Zaplar moved within months from event-booking automation to replacing nearly the whole operating stack. Organic ideas usually get sharper as founders use them; Zaplar’s has become broader after interviews, which can mean the founders are collecting requests rather than solving their own pain.

Jon Milles worked as a security engineer at 17 and joined Lund Formula Student’s first autonomous-driving group, while Douglas Solberg studied physics and mathematics and shipped software at Newscoop. That puts Zaplar above credential-only founders, but no public repository, open-source contribution, competitive-programming result, or independently visible technical artifact establishes hacker genealogy. The score would flip upward if Zaplar showed a property-model engine running a real hotel—or even an ugly public demo proving that Jon’s agents can reconcile bookings, payments, and room state. Until then, Zaplar has plausible hackers by biography, not hackers who have left a trail.

**Airbnb** (2009) had three founders whose complementary abilities became visible through doing unscalable work together. Zaplar has four co-founders and eight people already, including a CEO, CTO, CPO, and CFO, but no public source establishes how long all four worked together. Axel and Jon both attended Lund, which is a modest positive; the early executive specialization is a modest warning. Zaplar may have assembled exactly the coverage a hotel replacement requires, but Zaplar may also be practicing manager mode before one property has been shown to run on the product.

Mews’s 15,000 customers and $300 million Series D make the likely 36-month outcome fairly specific: by July 2029, Zaplar will have abandoned the full PMS/POS replacement claim and narrowed into an event-booking or operational-workflow layer for hotels, with less than $1 million in annual recurring revenue. Zaplar’s schlep insight should produce something useful, but Zaplar’s missing artifact and expanding scope make a complete operating-system migration improbable. Full-stack hotel software is several companies hidden inside one sentence.

Five hotels joining Zaplar’s waitlist in 48 hours form the strongest bull case, especially when combined with Axel’s hotel experience, Jon’s security work at 17, SEK 3 million already raised, and four founders willing to leave university or conventional careers. **Airbnb** is the Known Exception that stings: I initially mistook an operationally absurd idea for a bad business because I had not yet noticed the founders. But Zaplar’s dossier contains the hotel interviews without the cereal boxes—the demand claim without the act that makes disbelief impossible. Three named hotels fully migrating to Zaplar, operating on it for 12 months, and renewing would flip my verdict. Until Zaplar produces that evidence, the schlep is more convincing than the founders’ response to it.
