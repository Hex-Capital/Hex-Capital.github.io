# Decawork -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The proposed secret is that enterprises will govern AI agents as a new workforce—each with its own identity, permissions, supervision, and retirement—rather than absorb them into existing identity and security systems. The falsifying event is straightforward: IT departments standardize on Okta or another incumbent and treat agent controls as features, not a separate control plane. Decawork has built the right experiment—scoped credentials, gateway-mediated calls, approval gates, attributed logs, and reported SOC 2 status—but has not reported a current-product deployment that tests the proposition. This is a plausible secret, not yet a demonstrated one.

The larger problem is value capture. Decawork enters a market where Okta already registers agents, assigns credentials, governs access, and connects to an established integration network. Astrix, Noma Security, WitnessAI, and Zenity have each raised more than $55 million around overlapping controls. Deployment and maintenance workflows may be better than identity discovery or interaction monitoring, but a feature difference is not a monopoly mechanism. There is no exclusive data, measured technical advantage, network effect, or bounded customer segment Decawork already dominates. The category may become important while this particular layer becomes commodity infrastructure.

The founders provide the strongest opposing case. Sarthak Aggarwal worked on AI systems at NVIDIA reportedly deployed at OpenAI and Meta and on enterprise AI at Ema; Aman Raj built compliance tooling reportedly used by more than 240 Barclays teams. Their previous autonomous-IT product was live across multiple enterprises in India and the United States, and achieving reported SOC 2 status by July 2026 suggests unusual execution speed for a two-person YC company. This is not a generic team attaching an interface to a fashionable model. They have lived near both the technical and institutional problem.

But prior-product deployments do not validate the new control plane. The dossier provides no current customers, revenue, price, repeat usage, sales cycle, founder ownership, or history of the two founders working together. LinkedIn’s seven-employee count against YC’s team size of two also leaves the actual founding and engineering structure unclear. Missing evidence is not refutation, but founding structure cannot be inferred from résumés, and enterprise-security distribution cannot be inferred from conference visibility.

The economic case remains indefinite. An $11.14 billion non-human-identity market and a governance category growing from $308.3 million describe demand, not Decawork’s share of it. Founder-led demos and outreach to production-agent operators name a plausible buyer and channel, yet no contract value or implementation burden shows that this route can support enterprise acquisition costs. Nor is there evidence that one revenue engine can compound into a fund-returning outcome rather than become a useful product acquired before it establishes an independent position.

None of the known exceptions rescues the case: there is no independently verified zero-to-one technical event, persistent transaction behavior, or deployed physical asset that overrides the failed monopoly gate. I pass. The single condition that would flip me is ten named enterprises running production agents through Decawork for twelve months, with expanding contracts and documented operational data that makes its policies materially better than Okta’s controls. That would convert the thesis from slogan to secret and the gateway from dependency to proprietary infrastructure.
