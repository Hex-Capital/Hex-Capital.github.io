# Canos -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Canos has a credible molecular measurement. It has not yet demonstrated a consequential change in cancer care. Combining cfDNA fragmentation, copy-number variation and native methylation could make otherwise invisible disease measurable between veterinary visits. That matters. But the adjacent $985.24 million U.S. veterinary oncology diagnostics market measures spending, not the benefit Canos can create. The dossier supplies no quantified reduction in diagnostic cost, treatment delay or mortality. I cannot price an extreme-outcome option from the market estimate alone.

The strongest evidence is technical: 341 dogs, 896 sequencing runs and a 303-dog diagnostic analysis reporting 90.6% sensitivity at 95.1% specificity under nested cross-validation. This is substantive research, beyond a convenient interface around an AI model. It still leaves the important comparison unanswered. CanCan already offers sequencing-based canine liquid biopsy; Canos has no demonstrated 5–10x advantage against that alternative or conventional monitoring. And diagnostic classification is not longitudinal relapse detection. If the reported specificity transferred to screening, roughly five of every hundred cancer-free dogs would receive a positive signal. The subsequent investigations belong in the clinical and economic calculation.

The founders provide the strongest objection to passing. Ming-shan Tsai brings animal-health diagnostics experience and previously helped build a pathology product reportedly distributed to 10,000-plus clinics. Bin Li brings biomedical engineering and computational pathology research. Those are relevant, complementary capabilities, and the prior distribution belongs to their earlier company. Canos also fits a pattern I like: a scientific workflow that could generate better models through repeated measurements linked to outcomes. But ownership of that longitudinal dataset and the resulting learning advantage remain unverified. Their biographies establish domain competence; they do not document belief revision or difficult recruiting against a named weakness.

The next dollar should test whether a molecular warning changes veterinary action early enough to help. Origin is processing samples, while OncoTrack offers free trial testing, but neither establishes a completed, decisive experiment against that uncertainty. The dossier gives no prespecified clinical threshold, experiment budget or stop condition. That receives the missing-evidence default, not a penalty for being pre-seed. Likewise, $699 including the veterinary visit is a selling price, not a unit-cost model. Sequencing, collection, repeat testing and confirmatory work could consume the apparent savings. Free research participation provides no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, but it provides no evidence of unsubsidized parity either.

I pass at this evidence level. The documented science and founder fit make Canos worth following; they do not yet establish the magnitude or economics that would justify my check. My exceptions allow an extraordinary instigator or an overwhelming consequence to outweigh missing milestones. Neither override is established here. Nor does the dossier establish adverse founder behavior or premature spending on scale. The decision turns on what the assay changes. One verifiable result would reopen it: an independently evaluated, prospective multicenter study showing at least a fivefold reduction in total cost per clinically actionable relapse detected versus standard veterinary monitoring, at noninferior detection sensitivity and including confirmatory investigations. That would connect the molecular capability to an economic consequence.
