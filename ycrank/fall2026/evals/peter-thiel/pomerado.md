# Pomerado -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Pomerado’s candidate secret is that agents should discover a website’s operations once and then execute compiled integrations without repeatedly reasoning through the browser. The test is concrete: do those integrations remain faster, cheaper, and more reliable after authentication changes and website redesigns, including the cost of repairs? The published architecture makes the proposition testable. But the dossier supplies neither independently verified results nor a prevailing belief that named competitors reject. Browser automation may contain an important engineering opportunity; the secret remains unestablished.

The more decisive problem is ownership. Browser Use offers reusable scripts, Browserbase offers generated Stagehand scripts, and Skyvern describes compiling and repairing browser paths. These competitors already accept substantial parts of Pomerado’s premise. Its MIT license is not itself disqualifying, but the dossier identifies no exclusive data, measured technical advantage, or network effect that would let Pomerado dominate a bounded market. Insurance, healthcare, logistics, and internal systems describe several markets to pursue. They do not identify one market the company can already monopolize. This is affirmative competitive overlap, rather than merely missing moat documentation.

The founding structure is more promising. Manglik and Ma previously collaborated on SafeSip; they have done something together before assembling this company. That earns more confidence than a convenient pairing formed for YC. Their Scale AI and McKinsey backgrounds also make the enterprise integration problem intelligible to them. Pomerado fits my less public preference for infrastructure companies formed around operating knowledge. However, shared university attendance and one prior project establish only part of the foundation. Full-time status, ownership, and economic exposure remain undocumented. I would neither invent alignment nor infer a founder red flag from those omissions.

The strongest objection to passing is that the integration generator may be the acquisition mechanism for a much more valuable maintained service. Customers could prefer paying Pomerado to handling credentials, broken workflows, and deployment themselves, even when the underlying code is freely available. There is some evidence that developers will notice the product: 1.1k GitHub stars, 100 forks, and Product Hunt’s #5 daily position on October 16, 2026. Free signup, open source, and enterprise demos provide identifiable routes to buyers. But the dossier does not connect that attention to production adoption, conversion, or repeat purchasing. A maintained integration library could become valuable; customer dependence on it has not yet been demonstrated.

The economics leave the same distinction unresolved. Premium costs $50 a month, or $600 annually. At that price, $100 million of ARR would require roughly 167,000 paying accounts. That is illustrative arithmetic, not evidence of a reachable customer base. Custom Business contracts could support a different scale equation, but their prices, margins, and expansion behavior are unknown. In particular, Pomerado promises commercially reasonable repairs without a universal restoration deadline. Whether maintenance becomes scalable software revenue or recurring engineering labor matters much more than the size of the agent market. Missing revenue is normal at this stage; the available evidence still does not establish a power-law engine.

I pass because the competitive mechanism is already visible while the mechanism for escaping it is not. My exceptions for independently demonstrated technical breakthroughs or persistent transaction demand do not apply: company performance claims and launch attention establish neither. The explicit red flag is shared functionality among several capable suppliers, not the founders’ age or the company’s stage. One result would reopen the decision: an independently audited six-month deployment study in a single named customer segment showing at least a tenfold advantage in total operating and repair cost over Browser Use, Browserbase, and Skyvern, including a self-hosted Pomerado fork. That would begin to distinguish a proprietary maintained service from an integration generator others can reproduce. Until then, I would keep my money.
