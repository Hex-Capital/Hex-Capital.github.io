# Deploy -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Deploy promises to charge less than replacement labor while absorbing hardware, integration, and human assistance into one monthly station price. That puts the central underwriting question in exactly the right place: can the company deliver useful work for less cash than it collects? The proposed post-money valuation and competing-term-sheet count are undisclosed. YC and Afore backing establishes neither an auction nor scarce capital. I cannot establish the price of this bet, and the dossier names no funded experiment with a deadline and measurable result.

The strongest reason to take Deploy seriously is the founders’ experience. Polson worked on deploying autonomous airport vehicles at AeroVect; Chada researched vision-language-action and world models at Honda. That combination fits my preference for people who can translate between technical systems and messy physical operations. It is more substantive than two robotics degrees. But the operating bridge is incomplete: the dossier documents robotics experience, without demonstrating ownership of a warehouse workflow or direct access to fulfillment buyers. That earns meaningful founder credit without establishing the entire advantage.

The strongest argument against passing is that Deploy has already made the proposed loop tangible. Packing **800-plus pens with approximately 30 minutes of fine-tuning data** is a concrete demonstration. Human intervention also gives the company a way to deliver service before autonomy is perfect; this need not depend on errors magically disappearing. The bull case is that deployment generates exceptions, exceptions improve performance, and improved performance lets one operator support more paying stations. My unstated preference for software reorganizing difficult physical operations applies here. What remains unproven is the economically decisive connection between those steps: exhibition throughput establishes neither a customer purchase nor a declining cost of intervention.

The same fallback that makes the product usable can consume its margin. Below-labor pricing leaves Deploy responsible for teleoperators, equipment, maintenance, and installation recovery. None of those costs is disclosed, so I cannot call the service uneconomic, but neither can I underwrite a profitable base business beneath the AI option. Likewise, intervention data could become proprietary access; its existence alone does not establish exclusive rights or customer conversion. Manifold already offers overlapping robotic labor without upfront equipment spending. Deploy needs an advantage in delivered economics or embedded customer access, beyond a different billing unit. The 718 LinkedIn followers add no evidence on either question.

I would pass with my own money today. This is a plausible operational business whose most important investment claims remain untested, rather than an active mismatch with robotics. I find no documented auction pressure, forced future financing, or equal-control founder arrangement; those red flags cannot be inferred from silence. I also checked the exceptions to my price and venture-moment gates: Deploy supplies neither measured paid conversion and retention nor a prior-exit-backed financing structure that substitutes for those gates. **One condition would reopen underwriting: a documented 90-day paid warehouse deployment producing positive contribution margin after teleoperation, hardware depreciation, installation, and servicing at the advertised below-labor price.** That would establish the functioning business I could then price.
