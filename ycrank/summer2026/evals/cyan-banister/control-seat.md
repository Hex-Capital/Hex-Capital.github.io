# Control Seat -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Warren Shepard built Starlink manufacturing and flight software while Jack Grodnick worked on SCADA at Tesla and SpaceX, making Control Seat a lived-problem industrial bet whose weakest dimension is founder authority. Control Seat did not discover factories in a market memo; both founders have touched the machinery and inherited software they now want to replace. But “CEO” and “CTO” are labels, not a tiebreaker protocol. The analytical signature is authentic domain ownership paired with an unanswered two-founder question: excellent Tiz, credible Biz, and Rizz I cannot infer from LinkedIn.

**Anduril** is the high rung because Palmer Luckey arrived in defense after shipping Oculus from a garage; the biography was already the product thesis. Control Seat shares that mechanism through Shepard’s SpaceX manufacturing work and Grodnick’s Tesla and SpaceX SCADA experience. Control Seat sits just below Anduril because internships and engineering roles are contact with the problem, not yet proof that either founder previously carried an adjacent product from invention through scaled deployment. Battex Chile moves the evidence well above pedigree theater, but it does not yet make Control Seat’s origin inseparable from Warren or Jack’s life story.

**HQ Trivia** is the low-rung warning: intoxicating product energy temporarily concealed a partnership without durable tiebreaker authority. Control Seat names Warren Shepard CEO and Jack Grodnick CTO, which is better than shared titles, yet Control Seat discloses no equity split, operating agreement, founder-vesting structure, or conflict protocol. Meeting in first year at Dartmouth and comparing internships explains affection; it does not explain who wins an irreconcilable argument about becoming an integration business versus a software platform. I am a bubbe with one foot in love, but two founders still need one steering wheel.

Battex Chile is the mechanism that can make Control Seat magically weird over the next 18 months: integration work gets Control Seat through the factory door, the overlay captures tags and histories, and each successful deployment can convert an unsexy services wedge into sticky operating infrastructure. Ignition, AVEVA, Rockwell, Siemens, and CODESYS make the category crowded, but they also make replacing the entire stack nearly impossible; Control Seat’s willingness to sit above those systems is the game behind the game. What is missing is evidence that sophisticated investors have rejected Control Seat for being ugly, dangerous, or too industrial. YC acceptance can create consensus faster than weirdness creates underpricing.

Control Seat’s safety documentation keeps safety functions independent, and that clause is the frontier boundary. Control Seat would jump a full tier on the hard-technology dimension if its Virtual PLC demonstrated production-grade scheduler jitter and I/O latency, earned safety certification, or controlled a materially consequential process rather than stopping outside the safety loop. Today the self-reported nine-of-ten benchmark win against CODESYS shows technical ambition, while the exclusions for motion, I/O latency, and SIL3 certification show where ambition has not become moat. This is unsexy industrial software with physical consequences, not yet a SpaceX-class frontier artifact.

**Flock Safety** was observable before it was legible as a venture investment: a camera and even a Wi-Fi name left evidence in the physical world. Control Seat has a weaker but real version of that quality because Battex Chile consolidated MQTT brokers, virtual machines, databases, dashboards, and APIs onto Control Seat. An ordinary person cannot try Control Seat within 48 hours despite the seven-day hosted trial; a plant operator can watch a dashboard, inspect a tag, and observe whether an alarm arrives. That places Control Seat above abstract AI middleware but below a product I can ride, play, scan, or photograph in daily life.

Battex Chile makes my 36-month forecast concrete: Control Seat reaches $3 million to $6 million in ARR across 10–20 industrial sites, but Warren Shepard becomes the unmistakable operating tiebreaker while Jack Grodnick either accepts a narrowly bounded CTO mandate or leaves. Control Seat’s lived SCADA experience should win deployments that generic software founders cannot survive, while Control Seat’s weakest dimension makes founder-role clarification more likely than effortless co-leadership. The services wedge produces the first revenue; reusable integrations determine whether it becomes software.

CODESYS already offers containerized virtual control and certified SIL3 safety, while Rockwell and Siemens own installed relationships that Control Seat must preserve while competing against them. The stinging bear case is that Control Seat becomes a talented two-person systems integrator: every Battex Chile deployment is bespoke, the nine-of-ten benchmark proves little in a live plant, and founder conflict erupts when services revenue competes with product development. The dossier’s answer is the overlay strategy—Control Seat can enter brownfield plants without demanding replacement—and ownership of the SCADA, historian, analytics, and Virtual PLC layers creates a plausible compounding path. I would flip to Pass if Control Seat cannot show a second production customer within 12 months whose deployment reuses the Battex architecture with materially less engineering effort.
