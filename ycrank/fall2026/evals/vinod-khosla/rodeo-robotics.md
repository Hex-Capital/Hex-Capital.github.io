# Rodeo Robotics -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Rodeo Robotics could make scarce maintenance expertise available across many more fleet vehicles. That is a consequence worth investigating. But the dossier’s $599.7 billion automotive aftermarket figure includes parts and services Rodeo does not address. It supplies neither the technician-hours consumed by its target workflow nor the reduction its robots can deliver. I cannot price an option on transforming maintenance using the spending baseline for the entire aftermarket.

The interesting mechanism is collecting demonstrations through teleoperation and instrumented hand tools. Human assistance can finance learning while keeping vehicles moving. It can also conceal a labor-intensive service behind a robot. The distinction is whether each deployment reduces the human work required on subsequent vehicles, including unfamiliar models. Rodeo describes the collection mechanism; it provides no measured learning curve or performance comparison with technician-led oil changes. PitPro’s tire automation and UVeye’s inspections establish adjacent activity, not proof that Rodeo has solved manipulation underneath a vehicle.

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders have relevant experience building autonomy systems, and they have chosen a narrow task inside an existing, necessary workflow. Michael Hazard spent four years at Applied Intuition; Colby Duke led neural simulation there. Their shared background could help them turn messy field observations into useful training data. This fits my preference for AI applied to physical work and for scarce workflows that can become repeatable platforms. But simulation experience does not establish rapid belief revision, and the dossier documents neither complementary recruiting nor a proprietary learning advantage. Those remain open questions, not founder defects.

My central concern is dollars spent against risk removed. Rodeo acknowledges vehicle variation and retains human intervention, but the dossier gives no decisive experiment, intervention threshold, test budget, or stop condition. Its reported $500,000 financing and October 13 launch coverage do not answer that question. Neither does the absence of public revenue count against a company this young. The economic uncertainty is specific: technician assistance, equipment utilization, installation, and rework must together cost less than the service being replaced. No such model is disclosed. Human assistance is a plausible bridge; there is no evidence yet that the bridge ends.

I pass at present. The exceptions I would consider require an extraordinary founder commitment or demonstrated technical trajectory paired with a quantified, enormous consequence; this dossier establishes neither combination. I also see no evidence of exit-first behavior, subsidy dependence, or scaling sales before resolving technical risk. This is missing underwriting evidence, not an active thesis violation. One verifiable result would change my decision: an independently audited fleet pilot across 500 oil changes on multiple vehicle models, showing at least 90% fewer technician minutes than the existing process, comparable service quality, and fully loaded cost below that process—including equipment and human intervention. That would connect Rodeo Robotics’s learning mechanism to both consequence and economic advantage.
