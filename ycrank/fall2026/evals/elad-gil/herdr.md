# herdr -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Long-running coding agents move the developer’s problem from issuing commands to supervising work across machines. Herdr sits directly in that transition: its server owns terminal processes, and clients can disconnect without stopping execution. The market question is whether teams adopt that independent control layer deeply enough to pay for managing it across machines. The dossier establishes the operational need and a credible product mechanism. It does not yet establish the conversion from useful infrastructure to a large business.

The distribution is substantial for a company this young. Herdr had 46,194 GitHub stars and 3,691 forks on October 27; the company also reports 1.64 million cumulative installs. Those are different measurements, and none establishes retained users. More persuasive is the surrounding activity: 1,789 reported plugins, third-party interfaces, and DigitalOcean and Fly.io plugin demonstrations. People are extending the product beyond the founder’s original implementation. That earns meaningful demand credit, although I would not call free downloads product-market fit for the forthcoming Cloud service.

The strongest case for investing now is that herdr already has distribution before its commercial market crystallizes. A free runtime could become the channel through which the company sells shared administration and remote connectivity. Its $6 million September seed also includes practitioners associated with Shopify, Cloudflare, and fal. This fits my pattern of entering several layers around a technology discontinuity: the runtime can matter regardless of which coding agent wins. Waiting for revenue could mean waiting until the distribution advantage is obvious to everyone.

The counterweight is unusually concrete. Herdr Extended already distributes upstream-based binaries with additional functionality under the Apache-2.0 license. That demonstrates reproducibility rather than merely suggesting it. Warp, Conductor, and Paradev can also compete for the surrounding workflow. The plugin ecosystem therefore cannot yet be underwritten as proprietary data or customer lock-in. Herdr Cloud’s proposed customer-owned-machine model might avoid hosting the underlying compute, but the dossier supplies no pricing, support costs, or margin comparison. Agents running for hours or days explain why the category is emerging; they do not quantify the economic improvement herdr captures.

The product has more substance than an output formatter. It owns live processes, exposes state and controls through a socket API, and supports independently attachable clients. More capable agents could increase the work flowing through that runtime. But stronger agents could also require less supervision, while their vendors bundle remote execution. Server restarts currently destroy the original processes, and unsandboxed plugins complicate the path toward organizational deployment. The architecture earns durability credit; reliable enterprise control remains unproven. Can Celik building herdr for his own multi-agent workflow is also meaningful founder evidence. His previous AI roles establish relevant experience, but they do not establish prior startup learning or costly personal sacrifice.

I would monitor rather than write a personal check today. The demand evidence makes this interesting; the economic mechanism remains below my investment gate. None of my known exceptions resolves that gap: this is already a distributed software product, without evidence of a scarce frontier-research asset or a demonstrated reconstruction of incumbent margins. My single flip condition would be a verified cohort of at least 100 unaffiliated teams paying $100 or more monthly for Cloud, with at least 90% of those teams still paying six months later. That would directly test whether herdr’s existing distribution produces a recurring business.
