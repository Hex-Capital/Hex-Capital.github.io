# Jinko -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI agents create a new buyer for travel infrastructure: developers who need to complete transactions without building a travel operator. Jinko’s controlling variable is contribution profit per completed agent booking after search, supplier, payment, and servicing costs. Its pricing identifies the tension: a 1% flight-booking fee produces $10 on a $1,000 booking, with excess-search charges beginning above a 1,500:1 search-to-book ratio. That is a concrete economic mechanism. The dossier does not establish where it becomes profitable or whether developers will generate enough completed bookings to support it.

The technology shift is credible, but its economic magnitude remains unproven. Jinko documents discovery, live pricing, and execution through API, MCP, and CLI interfaces, making travel transactions accessible to agent developers. Advertised coverage of 800 airlines and 2.6 million hotels describes available supply, not incremental demand. More consequentially, Etraveli launched its Flight MCP on October 28, 2026, including payment, ticketing, and servicing. The market is already crystallizing around competing transaction providers. Jinko needs an advantage beyond making inventory accessible to models; the dossier supplies no measured improvement in conversion, delivery cost, or customer acquisition.

The strongest bull case is that the team understands precisely where a simple supplier interface stops being sufficient. These founders previously built Mowgli together before its acquisition by Hopper; their experience spans airline operations, booking engineering, and travel distribution. Jinko also assumes contracting travel-operator responsibilities and documents post-booking management and professional liability coverage. Those are substantive commitments to completing the workflow. Better models could generate more transactions while Jinko retains responsibility for execution and servicing. That earns meaningful architectural credit, although documented responsibility alone does not establish switching costs or attractive margins.

The Exa Connect listing offers a plausible route to developers, and the July 2026 builder event shows deliberate ecosystem participation. Neither establishes product-market pull. There are no disclosed repeat booking cohorts, paying infrastructure customers, or realized revenues for the current product. I would assign the absent-demand default rather than treat inventory coverage, followers, or the previous consumer app’s recognition as adoption. The pivot documented by August 25 also means the infrastructure business should be evaluated on its own evidence.

Jinko fits my pattern of backing infrastructure across a technology discontinuity, and the founders’ prior startup experience makes this more interesting than a generic agent integration. But broad exposure to a wave does not resolve value capture. The relevant red flag is a service-heavy business earning transaction fees while retaining operational costs; the dossier raises that question without proving the adverse outcome. The research-stage exceptions do not apply: there is no documented frontier research advantage, scarce model-building capability, or incumbent withdrawal. Here, incumbents are entering the channel. Founder experience cannot substitute for the missing market economics.

I would monitor Jinko and keep my money uncommitted today. Its strongest evidence concerns the team’s accumulated learning and its ownership of transaction responsibilities; its weakest concerns actual demand. **My flip condition is a verifiable 90-day cohort of at least three independent agent customers making repeat bookings with positive contribution profit after all search, supplier, payment, and servicing costs.** That would connect the new distribution channel to a business worth scaling. Until then, the product is more convincing than the investment case.
