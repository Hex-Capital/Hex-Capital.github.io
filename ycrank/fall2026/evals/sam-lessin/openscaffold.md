# OpenScaffold -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

OpenScaffold asks me to underwrite autonomous deployments before I can underwrite the check. Post-money valuation: undisclosed. Competing term sheets: undisclosed. Whether my money funds one finite experiment: undisclosed. Those are missing facts, not evidence of an auction or a bad capital plan. But I cannot establish either of my primary investment gates from this dossier. “Superagent for Deployment Teams” describes a product ambition; it does not tell me what truth my capital buys.

The interesting mechanism is generating a scaffold from customer discovery, business objectives, and previous deployments. If OpenScaffold owns the resulting implementation knowledge and customers repeatedly entrust execution to it, the workflow could become a proprietary asset. Today, that ownership and customer behavior are hypothetical. There are no documented purchases, pilots, retained deployments, or data rights. Auctor already connects discovery to delivery artifacts, Tandem maintains account context, and June implements enterprise systems. The question is what OpenScaffold retains after those vendors can generate scaffolds too.

The strongest argument against passing is Param Mehta. His biography describes leading an AI platform for transaction-error handling at JPMorgan, which is substantially more relevant than a generic claim to understand agents. Both founders also worked at USUL, giving them shared operating context. This could be a team that understands the ugly translation between software intentions and operational reality. That fits my preference for founders who can bridge ecosystems. It earns affirmative founder credit, but the dossier does not establish that either founder owned customer implementations or has privileged access to deployment buyers. I can recognize relevant operating experience without inventing the second side of the bridge.

Autonomous operation also creates a specific economic question: who pays when the scaffold misunderstands the customer? OpenScaffold provides no business model, margin, burn, or remediation-cost data. I therefore use the economics default rather than assuming the business is uneconomic. Still, a deployment agent can appear productive while moving expensive human work into exception handling. June’s reviewed, sandbox-tested changes illustrate an existing competitive approach to execution risk; OpenScaffold supplies no comparable benchmark. My AI red flag becomes decisive if profitability requires errors to disappear, but the dossier does not establish that assumption.

I pass. The exceptions in my framework require something concrete to override missing gates: observed enterprise conversion, durable customer economics, or an explicit financing structure that changes the capital requirement. None is documented here. The October 26, 2026 directory change from Sigma’s lakehouse positioning makes attributing any earlier product success especially inappropriate; it does not itself prove a bad pivot. My one verifiable condition for reconsideration is a named customer renewing a deployment contract at positive contribution cash after all human remediation costs. That would begin to demonstrate both workflow ownership and a functioning base business. Until then, I see relevant builders and a plausible product mechanism, without evidence that this is the scarce check I should write.
