# Micora -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Battery prices are falling, remote industrial operators already buy expensive electricity, and Micora proposes to remove installation labor from solar deployment. The controlling variable is delivered cost per reliable kilowatt-hour after freight, installation, storage, and maintenance. Micora’s claimed deployment rate of 100 kWp per day offers a measurable mechanism for improving that cost. But installation speed alone does not establish an economic advantage: the dossier contains neither a comparable conventional installation cost nor the utilization and storage assumptions needed to connect that speed to customer savings.

The strongest why-now evidence is the reported 45% decline in stationary-storage battery pack prices to $70/kWh in 2025. That changes a real input cost. It also helps every competing integrator. BoxPower already offers prefabricated microgrids, so Micora must capture an additional advantage through deployment automation. Forklift-placeable modules could simplify field work, but the October 21, 2026 LAKI collaboration concerns planned robotic deployment. I would distinguish the currently documented installation process from the autonomous system being developed. Neither the broader $43.47 billion microgrid market nor cheaper batteries establishes that Micora can earn attractive margins.

There is some evidence of a business beyond a prototype. Micora reports deployments at Pacific islands and manufacturing sites, which deserves modest demand credit despite the absence of named customers, capacities, or contract values. Its purchase and 12- or 24-month lease offerings include engineering, delivery, assembly, and commissioning. That gives the product responsibility for a useful completed result and provides some workflow depth. However, site-dependent final pricing and the exclusion of ongoing operations leave open whether each deployment becomes repeatable product delivery or another engineering project. I cannot yet credit a proprietary operating feedback loop or distribution channel that compounds across installations.

The strongest argument against passing is that this team has unusually relevant experience for making the physical system work. Choo has deployed solar/BESS at Tesla and handled drivetrains and manufacturing at Serve Robotics; Akemoto previously shipped heat pumps as Normal Robotics CTO. Mareels adds founding-team experience at Pear Suite. Those are substantive operating backgrounds, and prior startup work earns some credit for learning. They do not establish personal sacrifice or a prior founder outcome. Micora also fits my unstated pattern of entering physical markets where software can change delivery economics. The physical-business exception would apply if automation demonstrably changes those economics; relevant résumés and a planned robotics collaboration do not establish that override today.

I would pass on writing a personal check now, while keeping Micora on my list. The concern is specific: its proposed differentiation targets installation labor, while its actual margin structure remains unproven. That leaves unresolved my red flag around physical products retaining integrator economics despite automation claims. The vacancies alone do not establish undisciplined hiring, and I see no evidence of founder liquidity or fundraising for status. My single flip condition is a customer-verified deployment comparison showing that Micora’s current system delivers equivalent reliable power at materially lower fully loaded cost than a conventional prefabricated microgrid, while earning a positive contribution margin. That would connect the technical claim to the market variable that matters.
