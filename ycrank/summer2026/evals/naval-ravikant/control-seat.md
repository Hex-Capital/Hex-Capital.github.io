# Control Seat -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders possess the right kind of knowledge: knowledge acquired while touching the machinery. Jack Grodnick worked on controls and SCADA at Tesla and SpaceX; Warren Shepard worked across Starlink networks, manufacturing, and flight software. They then connected those experiences to a working industrial-control product and a named Battex Chile deployment. This is more valuable than the Dartmouth credentials. The action is specific, corroborated, and inside the exact problem.

The difficult question is what compounds. Control Seat connects PLCs through OPC UA and MQTT, sits above installed SCADA, and combines control, history, anomaly detection, forecasting, and natural-language investigation. That is useful integration. It is not yet leverage. The dossier identifies no proprietary model, exclusive operational dataset, hardware advantage, or customer-created network. Seeq, Litmus, and Augury have collectively raised at least $567.6 million around overlapping industrial analytics and data infrastructure. If they copy the interface, Control Seat’s remaining advantage appears to be implementation labor.

The strongest bull case is that industrial deployment itself becomes the asset. Every plant could contribute reusable configurations, failure patterns, and operational knowledge; years of control history could create switching costs. The intelligence layer also avoids demanding immediate SCADA replacement, which is a sensible wedge into a $13.87 billion market projected to reach $26.59 billion by 2034. This matches my recent preference for infrastructure bottlenecks over disposable application software. But the dossier labels the compounding asset as inferred, not observed. A possible feedback loop is not a network effect.

Control Seat also has direct value-capture machinery—custom deployment pricing, managed-cloud and air-gapped self-hosted configurations, support, and SLAs—but no disclosed price, revenue, or evidence that Battex paid. Integration services may help the founders learn and sell; they may also make each new customer require proportional expert labor. The product can scale while the project organization fails to scale.

The known exception for unusually capital-efficient founders with a demonstrated prototype is the best reason to preserve optionality. The Battex installation gives that argument substance. It does not yet override the missing durable substrate because there is no observed user-distribution loop, repeated deployment pattern, or evidence that plant knowledge transfers into a proprietary asset. I find no documented integrity, morale, or financing-pressure red flag; the objection is structural, not personal.

I pass today. My verifiable flip condition is five paid production plants showing that each successive deployment requires materially fewer engineering hours because Control Seat reuses proprietary configurations or operational models. That would turn a promising industrial integrator into a compounding system.
