# Simantic -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Simantic’s founders have earned the right to work on this problem. Hong worked in firmware at Tesla and robotics companies; Shahriar worked on Neuralink embedded software and Tesla AI hardware. More persuasive than the employers is the public SDK and October 2026 Cloud Sim beta: they have converted their experience into an inspectable product. That is specific knowledge expressed through action. The dossier does not establish which prior systems they personally shipped or how reliably customers use this one, so I stop short of exceptional founder conviction.

The decisive question is whether Simantic accumulates knowledge of silicon that a competitor cannot cheaply reproduce. Firmware generated instantly still needs somewhere trustworthy to run. That makes simulation a plausible bottleneck in a world of abundant code, and fits my recent preference for infrastructure tied to physical constraints. But serving hardware companies does not itself confer hardware defensibility. The SDK defaults to Renode, an MIT-licensed simulator that already supports peripheral modeling and CI. Privately developed engines and a Rust backend establish implementation effort; they do not yet establish a durable advantage. The proposed library of silicon-validated models could become that advantage. Its accumulated fidelity is still unproven.

The strongest argument for investing now is that waiting for complete proof could miss the inexpensive entry into an important infrastructure company. These founders encountered the testing delay themselves, built the product, and attracted Embedder, BoardRepo, and WATFlight partnerships. BoardRepo’s proposed testing before board manufacture gives the wedge a concrete expansion path from firmware developers into board development. Adjacent IoT and automotive virtualization also has demonstrated spend: Corellium reported $16.1 million ARR at year-end 2025. That supports market possibility, not Simantic’s demand. The capability transfer into those adjacent workloads remains to be demonstrated.

Simantic currently sells access to a tool rather than participation in a compounding network. Nothing establishes that one customer’s regression tests improve another customer’s results, that users contribute reusable peripheral models, or that adoption generates referrals. Confidential firmware and private enterprise hosting could further constrain shared learning. This fits my infrastructure pattern more closely than my permissionless contributor pattern. Partner announcements are encouraging distribution experiments, but they do not demonstrate a user-to-user loop.

The $100 monthly Developer plan and custom Enterprise offering provide a direct route to payment. They establish an asking price, with no disclosed paying customers, realized revenue, or contribution margins. The roughly $600,000 raised by July is financing, not customer validation. Likewise, the SDK’s warning to defer production pipelines identifies a specific adoption obstacle, rather than a reason to punish ordinary pre-seed immaturity. There is no documented integrity violation, manufactured investment deadline, or repeated abandonment that triggers a founder veto.

I would keep watching rather than write a check today. My exceptions for prelaunch products require unusually convincing product evidence or capital efficiency; exceptions for missing monetization require observable compounding behavior. Simantic has a credible artifact, but neither override is established strongly enough. One result would change my decision: an independently reproduced comparison across two unrelated customer workloads showing that Simantic’s proprietary peripheral models predict silicon failures Renode misses. That would turn the central claim from hosted convenience into scarce technical knowledge. Until then, the founders are more differentiated than the business.
