# OpenTag -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Are companies operating through persistent machine coworkers that remember decisions, maintain institutional knowledge, and execute work across systems? Probably. But OpenTag has not mapped the intervening steps or shown me the artifact that belongs uniquely to that future. Its clearest timing fact cuts the other way: Anthropic launched Claude Tag on June 23, 2026, and OpenTag entered roughly two months later with model routing and Teams support. That is a reaction to the present wave, not yet a view of what comes after it.

The product is real enough to inspect. OpenTag executes tasks in sandboxed cloud machines, connects to tools, maintains source-linked wiki pages, and places approval gates before external actions. Ten teams entered early access in two weeks, with six organizations displayed as doing real work. That earns credit beyond a concept or waitlist, but I cannot see what those users actually surrendered—money, recurring labor, credentials, or a workflow they would now fight to keep. A $100 trial and $50 monthly plan establish availability, not devotion.

The founders are the strongest part of the case. Tony Kam, Shelden Shi, and Wilson Nguyen studied at Berkeley, worked across Tesla, Intel, Flatiron Health, Roche, CertiK, HPE, and Vestible, and already operated together at Lilac Labs. They have relevant technical range and survived one company together. Tony is explicitly CEO, so this is not presently a leadership shell game. What is missing is the first-believer story: no dated pre-company experiments, operating wound, or independently discovered obsession explains why this team had to build OpenTag. The dossier instead shows prior products named Lilac Labs and Gini, then a launch closely following Claude Tag.

The bull case stings because workspace memory can compound. Skills, learned formats, permissions, approval history, and an evolving wiki could become difficult to remove, while pooled workspace pricing and Microsoft Teams support may reach customers poorly served by Claude-only Slack tooling. If those ten teams are already delegating recurring cross-system work, the apparently thin interface may be concealing a valuable operational substrate.

Today, however, the substrate is still hypothetical. OpenTag depends on Conifer, external models, and outside infrastructure while Anthropic, Dust, Glean, and Lindy control models, enterprise graphs, distribution, or larger agent platforms. The company has not identified a scarce technical asset it owns. Nor has it named the broken rule and rent underneath stale documents: who captures economic value from this dysfunction, how large is the cost, and whose autonomy changes when OpenTag removes it? “People repeat work in chat” is true, but it is a nuisance description rather than system economics.

The privacy gate is not fatal: OpenTag documents tenant isolation, TLS 1.3, AES-256 encryption, audit logs, approval controls, a DPA, and failure-stop behavior. SOC 2 Type II remaining in progress matters because the product touches messages, files, credentials, and connected applications, but the team is treating custody as architecture rather than hand-waving it. No known-exception pattern overrides the larger weakness; ten early teams are not yet strange or costly behavior powerful enough to excuse a consensus application layer. My 36-month expectation is that OpenTag abandons the general model-agnostic coworker pitch and pivots toward approval and audit infrastructure for enterprise agents, remaining below $1 million ARR by August 2029. I would flip if three named customers each paid at least $1,000 per month and let OpenTag execute the same cross-system workflow weekly for eight consecutive weeks.
