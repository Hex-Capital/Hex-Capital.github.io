# Faraday -- Exit Evaluation

**Action:** Watch  
**Evidence confidence:** Low

### Company case

**Strategic acquisition** is Faraday’s strongest private-company exit path in Fall 2026. An industrial ERP vendor could buy Faraday’s runtime and workflow tooling to automate work across its own suite, legacy applications, and factory equipment. The memo documents Infor shipping agents for orders, items, and invoices, which establishes that an incumbent is investing in these workflows. Ownership could give a buyer control over integrations and let it reuse deployment tooling across its installed base. That advantage depends on Faraday proving that the tooling works across customers and that the assets producing the benefit can transfer. (The Idea, Defensibility, Key Risks)

Faraday reports that its harness raised benchmark accuracy from 38% to 94% with the base model held fixed. The founder also reports working around a remote-desktop blocker in one unnamed customer deployment. The milestone is repeatable paid production use across distinct legacy systems, with measured reductions in exception-handling costs after integration and support. The reported deployment gives Faraday a starting point. Paid usage, live savings, deployment cost, and runway are unverified. Write-enabled workflows require separate customer authorization and agreed controls. IP ownership and rights to transfer customer-specific tooling, data, and contracts are also unverified. (Defensibility, Market & Traction, Key Risks, Key Facts)

**Proceeds bridge.** With no check terms disclosed, assume one $500,000 check at a $10 million post-money valuation, with 5% initial ownership diluted to 3% and no follow-ons. The eight-year 5× objective requires $2.5 million in gross cash. Under a simple pro rata waterfall, that takes $83.3 million in distributable equity proceeds at 3% ownership, $125 million at 2%, or $62.5 million at 4%. The memo supplies no buyer economics or applicable transaction prices to support a range. (Market & Traction, Key Facts)

The sale scenarios assume zero applicable net debt and no other price adjustments, so enterprise value equals equity consideration. They assume all cash at closing, no preference adjustments, and zero stock, escrow, earnout, rollover, or transaction-linked compensation.

| Illustrative outcome | Timing and equity proceeds | Investor cash at 3% |
|---|---|---|
| Modest sale | Year 5, $40 million | $1.2 million, 2.4× |
| Strong sale | Year 7, $100 million | $3 million, 6× |
| Prolonged private | Through year 8, growth plateaus, buyers stay away, public scale remains out of reach, and cash is reinvested | $0, 0×, with shares retained |

The strong sale clears the objective at 3% ownership and falls short at 2%, where it returns $2 million. The memo provides no usable evidence of an independent route that could pay this holder. (Market & Traction, Key Facts)

### Countercase

Infor already bundles overlapping workflows, and a buyer could license Faraday for the remaining legacy integrations. If customers still need bespoke setup and separate write authorization at each site, owning Faraday may save too little to justify the hurdle price. The reported benchmark comes from test environments, and the reported live deployment encountered a customer-specific integration blocker. That supports Watch. The memo establishes overlap but leaves open whether bundling or licensing can match Faraday’s performance across legacy systems, so the structural Pass override does not apply. (Defensibility, Market & Traction, Key Risks)

### Decisive diligence question

What would an industrial ERP vendor pay to own Faraday’s transferable tooling after seeing it reduce exception-handling costs in repeatable, paid deployments across distinct legacy systems? A buyer-validated comparison with building or licensing, grounded in those production results and supporting a price that clears the holder’s cash hurdle, would move the action to Reach out.
