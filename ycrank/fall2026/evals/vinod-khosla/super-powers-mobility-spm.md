# Super Powers Mobility (SPM) -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Silent power can change what a military unit can do. Super Powers Mobility’s proposed replacement of idling engines and generators could reduce acoustic signatures, thermal exposure, and fuel resupply. Those are consequential benefits. But the dossier does not quantify fuel displaced, mission endurance gained, or the cost of delivering equivalent power today. A $1.6 billion military-battery market establishes a spending pool; it does not establish an orders-of-magnitude consequence. I cannot price the upside merely by calling the battery strategic.

Jonathan Powers gives me the strongest reason to keep looking. His Tesla integration, Workhorse braking, and Honda powersports experience maps directly to building hardware that must survive outside a laboratory. More revealing is SPM’s September 2026 shift from vehicles toward battery packs and power export, followed by company-reported realized revenue of $421,815 in one month. That is an observed change in direction with a commercial result. It supports tactical flexibility, although the dossier does not establish what evidence prompted the change or show complementary recruiting against specific technical weaknesses.

The strongest objection to passing is that customers may already be identifying a valuable platform before public benchmarks catch up. SPM has demonstrated vehicle-integrated systems to special operations forces, reports another $792,000 in qualified pipeline, and combines batteries, controls, drivetrains, and power electronics. My actual investing pattern permits difficult physical systems and scarce engineering workflows that become repeatable products. A four-person team producing this much reported revenue deserves attention. Nevertheless, the pipeline is not sales, the earlier $769,000 in vehicle preorders belongs to the prior product, and one revenue month does not establish repeatable production economics.

The decisive technical question remains unanswered. Fifteen-kWh modules, 12 kW continuous AC output, and 14 kW DC output describe capability; they do not measure superiority against Inventus, Bren-Tronics, or a specified generator. Defence Blog explicitly says the claimed power-density advantage was not independently verified. Bench and vehicle testing establish that engineering work exists, but the dossier supplies neither a comparable incumbent test nor a measured discontinuity. I would not label this commodity integration without evidence. Equally, I will not award breakthrough credit for an unresolved performance claim. The disclosed milestones also lack a ranked kill risk, numerical acceptance threshold, test budget, and if/then response.

Economics could overturn the decision, but the $1,799 consumer battery price cannot answer the military system question. There is no disclosed system cost, cycle-life model, production yield, utilization assumption, or delivered-energy comparison including charging and logistics. Procurement restrictions on Chinese suppliers could help SPM sell; they do not demonstrate globally competitive economics. The known exceptions to my technical and economic gates require an exceptional instigator confronting a quantified enormous consequence, or compelling evidence of exponential capability improvement. Powers’ relevant background and early sales make a credible business case, but do not yet establish either override. There is also no basis to accuse the team of premature scaling or permanent subsidy dependence.

I pass with my own money at this point. SPM could become a useful, profitable defense supplier; the missing link is evidence that its integrated system changes the underlying cost curve enough to justify breakthrough underwriting. My single flip condition is an independently audited customer pilot showing a 10x reduction in total mission power cost against a named generator system at equivalent load, uptime, and operational requirements, including charging, transport, replacement, and production-representative costs. That result would connect demonstrated engineering to a quantified consequence.
