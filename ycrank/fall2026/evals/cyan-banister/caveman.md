# Caveman -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years from now, I can imagine people giving agents a spending limit and a job, then letting the machines negotiate the computational budget. Caveman could become part of that world. But that is my daydream; the dossier establishes a present-day optimization workflow, not the intervening steps toward that future. Recording, replaying, and approving changes is useful. What remains unproven is whether those actions accumulate into something customers increasingly depend on. I would keep my money in my pocket today, with enough curiosity to try the product.

Why do teams keep buying expensive inference? Caveman identifies an understandable institutional failure: nobody wants to save money by quietly making the agent worse. Workload-specific evaluations, shadow testing, and canaries give engineers a way to challenge that spending without betting production on a benchmark. That returns some control to the customer. But the dossier identifies neither an artificial restriction nor a quantified rent Caveman can reliably remove. Its own consumption charges also enter the calculation. The customer needs a smaller total bill for successfully completed work.

The strongest argument against my hesitation is wonderfully concrete: people installed the peculiar little thing. The earlier browser extension reached 20,000 users and 67 ratings averaging 4.6/5; the repository attracted approximately 115,500 stars and 6,700 forks. That fits my pattern of finding consequential infrastructure underneath an initially modest consumer artifact. Installing software and forking code involve more commitment than liking a launch announcement. Yet Caveman Cloud and version 4.0 arrived on October 13, 2026. The earlier product earned that attention; the dossier does not establish that users carried their workloads into the new business. JetBrains’ July test makes the distinction consequential: output tokens fell 8.5%, while aggregate billed cost rose 11.6% because of an outlier. That does not invalidate the new product, but it makes verified billing outcomes central to the bet.

Julius Brussee has relevant building experience: Stacklink agent infrastructure, retrieval systems, and token-efficiency tooling. I give that more weight than his university affiliation. I cannot establish the longer biography of obsession, recruiting, and commercial judgment that would make me trust him through several changes of product. My exception for a pre-revenue technical founder remains available, but this dossier supplies neither the independently documented lifelong pursuit nor the signed industrial commitments that made that exception persuasive. Caveman does occupy an enabling position beneath agent applications, and its Apache-2.0 code is a tangible asset. It has not demonstrated control of a scarce dependency. LiteLLM and Edgee already sit near the traffic and could distribute overlapping capabilities.

The privacy disclosure is the other place I stop: free-plan routing may retain submitted context to improve routing. Disabling routing stops transmission, which is a meaningful limiting mechanism, but does not establish explicit consent or satisfactory custody for sensitive workloads. I see no documented founder-control conflict or dependence on one closed supplier; I would not invent either. My one verifiable flip condition is an independent 90-day production audit across three paying Caveman Cloud customers showing at least 20% lower total costs, including Caveman’s fees, without regression on agreed task-success evaluations and with explicit consent for retained context. That would show this strange little tool becoming infrastructure people trust with both their money and their work.
