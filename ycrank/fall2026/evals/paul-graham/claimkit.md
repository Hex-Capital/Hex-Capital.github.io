# ClaimKit -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Rachel Huang has encountered the work ClaimKit proposes to replace. At KPMG she assembled the technical evidence and expenditure records needed for R&D claims. That matters more than the phrase “AI-native tax firm.” Evidence collection, eligibility judgments, and responsibility for a lodged claim are precisely the unpleasant work that makes this an organic startup idea. The strongest signal is the founders’ proximity to the problem ([founder background](https://claimkit.co/en-au/about)).

But knowing the obstacle and overcoming it are different facts. The dossier does not show a specific customer problem, what Huang or William Mahon personally built around it, and how quickly the situation changed. Mahon’s Google and trading-firm experience cannot substitute for that episode. Nor can I treat the retirement of Grants on April 22, 2026, as demonstrated learning: we know what they stopped doing, but not what customer observation falsified the original route. Both resourcefulness and evidence-driven revision remain at their missing-evidence defaults ([product history](https://claimkit.co/help)).

There is nevertheless something here that customers appear to want. ClaimKit reports 80+ customers within six months, and Edexia’s COO describes switching from accountants during an engagement and getting the work completed faster and more cheaply. Switching halfway through a tax project is more informative than signing up for a demo. It identifies a narrow well: companies already struggling to complete a claim. I give this substantial demand credit, while keeping the company-reported customer count separate from verified payment, retention, or weekly growth ([customer count](https://www.linkedin.com/company/claimkitco), [testimonial](https://claimkit.co/en-au)).

The product deserves intermediate technical credit. Connected engineering and accounting workflows, documentation generation, and historical claim records describe functioning software, but do not establish how much work the software performs. The upfront software charge and minimum-or-percentage service fee could support a useful business even if specialists still do most of the preparation. That is the central uncertainty: does each engagement improve a system, or mostly consume another specialist’s time? Overlapping automation from Neo.Tax and Onshore makes the answer consequential ([workflows](https://claimkit.co/help), [terms](https://claimkit.co/terms), [competition](https://www.neo.tax/)).

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders have already combined relevant experience, software, and a concrete customer benefit. Manual service can teach them what to automate. ClaimKit also avoids several obvious red flags: the dossier shows neither a plan dependent on Airwallex nor evidence that the cofounder relationship was assembled for the application. But my recurring preference for measured technical proof and user-shaped programmable tools is only partly satisfied. The known exceptions require an unusually convincing prototype, validated technical result, or unmistakable real-world pull. Eighty reported customers and one testimonial warrant attention; they do not yet justify overriding the missing founder-action evidence. The advertised 100% claim success rate lacks the denominator and review period needed to strengthen that case.

I would remain Neutral and withhold a check today. My flip condition is one documented episode in which a substantial integration or substantiation obstacle blocked several customers, the founders personally shipped a workaround within two weeks, and those customers then completed their claims using it. That would connect ClaimKit’s strongest asset—knowing this ugly problem—to the founder behavior I am actually buying.
