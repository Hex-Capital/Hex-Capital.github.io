# Egoist Machines -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. People expect every AI application to know them, but no model provider permanently owns the person underneath that context. Applications ask for pieces of memory, users grant access for a purpose and a duration, and receipts make those exchanges inspectable. Egoist Machines has found a plausible leak from that world: ChatGPT and Claude already retain context inside separate ecosystems, forcing users either to repeat themselves or accept provider custody. What is missing is the causal bridge from today’s Passport to a standard that independent applications will actually honor.

The broken rule is unusually clear. AI providers decide where personal context lives and how portable it is; Egoist returns field-level approval, revocation, duration limits, and read receipts to the user. That is real agency, particularly when the proposed context includes health, identity, fitness, and OAuth credentials. But the dossier does not quantify the rent imposed by re-explanation or captivity. Worse, the consent architecture may replace one irritation with a recurring inbox of permission chores. Defaults can reduce that burden, but defaults are exactly where nominal user control quietly becomes ceremonial.

The founders make the idea more credible than the traction does. David Khachaturov brings a Cambridge PhD, OpenAI PaperBench work, AI-security experience at FrontierOne, and a shipped open-source artifact with 4,700 GitHub stars. Erin McGurk brings a first-place Cambridge result and distribution experience reaching more than one million followers across platforms. That is complementary technical and recruiting capacity, but not yet evidence of pre-company obsession with portable identity: the dossier shows credentials and audience rather than years of experiments, prior custody wounds, or a trail of memory products built before this market became fashionable.

The strongest bull case is that a security-trained builder and a consumer educator are arriving just as native AI memory makes consent visible to ordinary users. The Passport is also beneath the application layer rather than another chat interface. But an enabling layer must control something others need. Egoist discloses no integrations, active users, proprietary asset, or structural barrier, while Mem0, Supermemory, and Letta already supply model-agnostic memory infrastructure and OpenAI and Anthropic control the relevant platforms. Today, the product is a proposed coordination standard whose most powerful participants can decline to coordinate.

The decisive absence is behavior. The waitlist opened July 13, 2026, general availability was promised for later that summer, and there are no disclosed users or application partners. YC selection, 268 company followers, press coverage, and founder reach do not show anyone repeatedly approving a pass, trusting Egoist with sensitive context, or refusing an AI app that lacks Passport support. A privacy product becomes real when users entrust it with something costly and developers surrender some control to obtain that trust. Neither side has done so publicly.

I am neutral, leaning pass. My 36-month base case is a pivot from the free consumer Passport into a developer-facing consent or identity SDK, remaining below $1 million ARR because application distribution—not memory storage—is the bottleneck. The verifiable flip condition is three independent consumer-AI applications integrating Passport and at least 1,000 users completing repeated, revocable context grants across more than one app. That would show the strange new behavior: people treating their AI memory as property they carry, rather than a feature supplied by whichever model they opened first.
