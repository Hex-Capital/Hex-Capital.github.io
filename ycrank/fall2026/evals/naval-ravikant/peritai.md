# Perit.AI -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Perit.AI has shipped something buyers can use: 4,000 audio hours and a public dataset containing 45 conversations. That is more informative than the founders’ employer names. Kshitij’s previously profitable ZeoAuto and Poorva’s engineering experience suggest they can operate, but neither establishes deep speech-model expertise. The strongest founder evidence is the production system they are building now. I see demonstrated execution, with specific knowledge still being established.

The strongest argument for investing is that customers may already be paying substantially for that knowledge. Six major AI labs and “multiple millions in ARR,” even self-reported, are unusually meaningful at pre-seed ([CEO announcement](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kshitijdixit_i-am-excited-to-share-that-perit-ai-yc-f26-activity-7502597870538366976-3u6g)). Perit names a payer and a direct transaction: custom data programs priced by locale, annotation depth, turnaround, and exclusivity. This could become a substantial business before it becomes a defensible one. But annualizing project deliveries can resemble recurring revenue without having its durability. Contributor compensation is separately itemized; retained gross profit and renewals remain unknown.

The decisive question is what Perit keeps after delivering the work. Its 650-person annotation bench and calibration gates are operational assets. They take effort to assemble. Yet contributor access is not shown to be exclusive, and non-exclusive licensing is the default ([Pricing](https://perit.ai/pricing)). That permits reuse; it does not establish that Perit owns scarce reusable rights. Mercor or Turing could recruit practitioners and reproduce the workflow without copying Foundry’s code. The substrate here is human expertise, which fits my interest in infrastructure bottlenecks, but expertise only compounds for shareholders if the company retains something competitors cannot readily purchase.

There is room to maneuver. Speech collection leads naturally into alignment, grading, multilingual evaluation, and live caller simulations using related contributor and quality-control capabilities. Perit already describes those products, and the wider labeling market supplies evidence of substantial spending. Still, the quoted $27.6 billion market includes work this company does not address. Its current wedge provides a credible expansion path, rather than proof that it can capture the parent market.

Perit also resembles the permissionless expert networks I like: outsiders contribute useful work to frontier systems. The missing mechanism is one contributor making another contributor—or another customer—more productive without equivalent company effort. The public dataset’s 350 monthly downloads demonstrate discovery, not that loop. I can make exceptions for an absent network when an exceptional prototype and capital efficiency supply the conviction. The released 5.10-hour, scripted English sample establishes an artifact, but not that exceptional technical advantage. Nor do I see evidence of founder misconduct or artificial fundraising pressure; unnamed customers are uncertainty, not an integrity finding.

I would hold off on a personal check. The commercial evidence makes dismissal expensive: these founders could build a valuable supplier while investors debate whether services count as technology. But my money needs a clearer path from deliveries to an accumulating asset. **My flip condition is a customer-verified deployment showing that a Perit-owned, reusable evaluation corpus improves a second lab’s model outcomes while reducing human delivery hours per dollar of revenue.** That would connect today’s sales to tomorrow’s compounding advantage.
