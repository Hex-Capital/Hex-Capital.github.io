# HillStar -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

HillStar has unusually credible founders and an unpriced investment proposition. The proposed post-money, competing-term-sheet count, and whether my money fills a genuine financing gap are all unknown. Announced backing from YC, Liquid 2 Ventures, and Pioneer Fund establishes neither an auction nor scarce capital. I would pass on writing a personal check today. Utility expertise can make this a good business; it cannot tell me whether the security is a good purchase.

The founder evidence deserves substantial credit. Nooshin Behroyan founded a utility-services business and reportedly exited in 2025. Kenny Stone co-founded PlanGrid, acquired by Autodesk for $875 million in 2018, and subsequently led software at Bedrock Energy. These are operating receipts across utility infrastructure and software, precisely the translation layer I look for. HillStar also fits my willingness to tolerate physical-world complexity when software reorganizes a neglected operating process. The team has credible access to the problem rather than just a fashionable explanation of it.

The most useful product evidence is the procurement committee confirming that two of 14 bidders lacked a mandatory certification, including the lowest-priced bidder. That connects the software to an actual buying decision. The company-published claim of evaluating 22 submissions and approximately 19,800 pages in nine minutes is less decisive: what matters is whether the findings survive expert review and influence awards. These anonymous case studies earn credit for observed workflow use, but they establish neither a purchase of HillStar nor repeat adoption. Capturing evaluator judgments and supplier outcomes could create proprietary institutional memory; advertising that capability does not establish ownership, exclusivity, or a durable distribution advantage.

The strongest objection to my pass is that these founders may already possess the relationships needed to turn useful deployments into a compact, profitable utility business. Committee review and source-linked findings also make the product more credible than an automation pitch that assumes errors disappear. But the exceptions I make for AI businesses require demonstrated conversion and economics; exceptions based on repeat founders still require an explicit capital plan. HillStar supplies neither paid conversion nor pricing, margins, burn, or a route to break-even. Its professionals-plus-software offering could support attractive contracts, or consume the savings in delivery labor. The dossier cannot distinguish those outcomes. Fairmarkit and Keelvar make that distinction consequential: document analysis alone is unlikely to remain scarce.

What does the check prove? HillStar has not disclosed a proceeds-funded hypothesis, deadline, or pass/fail metric. I will not invent that experiment from its case studies, nor mistake missing information for evidence that management has no plan. My verifiable condition for reopening the decision is a signed financing plan below $10 million post-money that funds a six-month test of three repeat, paid utility procurements at positive contribution margin, including expert-review labor. That would connect price, a finite experiment, and cash generation. Today, the founder bridge is proven far more convincingly than the investment mechanism.
