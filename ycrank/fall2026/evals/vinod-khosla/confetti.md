# Confetti -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Confetti could make the ability to build physical objects available to people who cannot use CAD, select compatible electronics, or write firmware. That is a worthwhile consequence. But the dossier does not establish how much expert labor disappears, what that labor costs today, or which population gains a previously unaffordable capability. I can imagine an enormous outcome. I cannot credit my imagination as company evidence.

The decisive technical question is whether the product reliably produces working hardware. Confetti advertises conversational design, component selection, printed-part ordering, and project remixing. Each step can appear successful while the assembled object fails. Zoo already offers geometry inspection and debugging; Flux covers electronics, sourcing, and manufacturing outputs. Confetti supplies no measured advantage against either. This leaves technical discontinuity at the absent-evidence default. It does not establish that the team is merely wrapping models, but it leaves that red flag unresolved.

The strongest argument against passing is that successful assemblies could become the training infrastructure for subsequent designs. Mechanical fit, connector compatibility, firmware behavior, and manufacturing failures are valuable feedback. A consumer project community could collect that feedback across many configurations. This fits my interest in physical workflows that become repeatable learning platforms. However, the dossier labels this learning loop an inference: there is no demonstrated collection system, improvement curve, or repeated successful manufacture. Community remixing alone does not establish proprietary technical learning.

Gu’s Penn CS/math background and research at Wolfram and Stanford, together with Yuan’s EngineLab work accepted at an ICML workshop, give the pair credible software research foundations. Their collaboration is concrete. Neither credentials nor coauthorship establishes fast belief revision or complementary recruiting against hardware risks. The inferred transition from Asteria to Confetti could reflect adaptability, but its cause and results are unknown. I would override an early technical-evidence gap for a documented exceptional instigator addressing a quantified giant value pool. Neither element is established here, so the known-exception route does not change my decision.

The first capital should test end-to-end reliability before expanding the project catalog. The dossier supplies no founder-defined experiment, threshold, budget, or response to failure; that receives the process default, rather than a deduction for presumed bad spending. Economics is similarly unproven: pricing and transaction fees are unverified, and there is no delivered-cost model incorporating failed prints, replacement components, shipping, and human intervention. The 58 LinkedIn followers establish neither demand nor its absence. A two-person pre-seed team needs no revenue history to interest me; it needs evidence against the particular uncertainty that makes its ambition valuable.

I pass with my own money. My flip condition is an independently replicated paid pilot showing that novices can complete at least 90% of 20 previously unseen hardware projects, with tenfold less expert intervention than a documented Zoo/Flux-assisted workflow and no higher fully loaded delivered cost. That would demonstrate a mechanism for removing expertise rather than shifting it into support and rework. Today, Confetti presents a plausible destination without evidence that its route changes the underlying cost curve.
