# Pango -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Pango’s 100-plus integrations make Pango a schlep-rich company resting on a relationship-poor founding record. Twelve months embedded with 30 brands means Pango has already entered the swamp of carriers, warehouses, refunds, and merchant-specific rules. But there is no public evidence that Steve Rahimi and Lukasz Reszczynski built together before Pango. The highest signal is willingness to absorb ugly operational complexity. The lowest is whether the two founders are a durable pair when that complexity stops being novel and becomes exhausting.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness: compliance was not incidental to the product; enduring compliance was the product advantage. Pango shares that mechanism. Pango’s agents issue labels, approve returns, coordinate refunds, cross EEA data boundaries, and connect more than 100 systems. Mainstream founders see integration maintenance and autonomous-action errors and turn away. The divergence is that Coinbase faced an existential regulatory grind, while Pango still faces ordinary commercial substitutes such as Narvar and parcelLab. Pango has found a real schlep, but not yet one that competitors cannot choose to endure.

The two-MBA AI wrapper in my pass case is the low rung because a pair assembled around a pitch is merely two solo founders sharing stationery. Pango is not that caricature: Rahimi brings seven years in e-commerce and Reszczynski built delivery infrastructure and a patented configurator used for more than $1.5 billion in quotes. But Pango supplies no evidence of how long the founders knew each other, what they built together, or how they divide decisions under pressure. Two complementary biographies are better than one founder. They are not evidence of the Stripe brothers’ kind of bond.

Pango’s $550,000 pre-seed round matters less than what Pango did around it: 30 brands, 60% month-over-month growth, five Shopify reviews at 5.0, and 12 months spent embedding into merchant operations. Over the next 18 months, resourcefulness compounds through the integrations because every new carrier exception teaches Pango how to sell and deploy the next merchant faster. But I cannot find the cereal-box moment—the improvised act that proves Rahimi will manufacture an escape when the obvious route closes. Pango has shipped unusually much before YC; Pango has not yet left unmistakable animal tracks.

Steve Rahimi’s reverse-logistics thesis and seven years building e-commerce businesses make Pango substantially more organic than a market-map startup. Lukasz Reszczynski’s logistics-infrastructure work strengthens the connection, and Pango’s 99% self-service result for Switch Nails looks like something learned from operating reality. The score would fall a full tier if Rahimi’s earlier businesses never personally encountered returns administration and the thesis merely identified an attractive category. The score would rise toward Dropbox territory if Pango showed that its first workflow was built to solve Rahimi’s own recurring problem before any merchant requested it.

**Stripe** (2010) is the useful hacker parallel because the Collisons converted prior commerce hacking into a small interface over enormous backend ugliness. Reszczynski’s patented configurator and delivery infrastructure give Pango a credible version of that technical lineage: Pango is executing money-and-shipment actions, not merely generating customer-service prose. But Pango has no public GitHub trail, Hacker News artifact, competitive-programming history, or open-source contribution from either founder. Pango hits the shipped-systems half of my unstated hacker pattern while missing the tribal evidence that usually makes the pattern unusually predictive.

Thirty global brands and 60% month-over-month growth lead me to predict that by July 2029 Pango will remain independent at $8–15 million in annual recurring revenue, serving 300–600 brands primarily through returns, shipping exceptions, and refund automation. Pango’s integration schlep should produce a respectable business faster than an incumbent can standardize every merchant’s rules. Pango’s unproven founder history and the absence of a uniquely difficult technical artifact make a Narvar-scale category victory less predictable than the first $10 million of recurring revenue.

Narvar, parcelLab, and AfterShip make the bear mechanism sting: Pango may be maintaining 100 commodity integrations while funded incumbents copy the agent layer, and Pango’s free Checkout+ pricing leaves the 30-brand count disconnected from disclosed revenue. No known exception supplies a free pass. Pango has neither Airbnb’s cereal-box proof nor Loopt’s Altman-level force-of-personality signal, while the missing GitHub trail and missing cofounder history contradict two of my unstated patterns. What keeps Pango above Pass is the harder evidence: 12 months embedded, 60% monthly growth, and Switch Nails retaining 19% of returns as exchanges or store credit. If fewer than 20 of Pango’s current 30 brands become paying customers with at least 80% twelve-month gross retention, I would flip to Pass.
