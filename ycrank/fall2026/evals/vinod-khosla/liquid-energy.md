# Liquid Energy -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Four years waiting for a grid connection is an infrastructure bottleneck worth attacking. If Liquid Energy can deliver useful inference in eight weeks, it could make compute available where conventional facilities cannot get built. But deployment speed is only the beginning of the consequence case. The dossier identifies a $29.04 billion modular-data-center category; it does not establish how much capacity NanoDC can unlock, whose computing costs collapse, or by how much. I see a consequential problem. I cannot yet price the upside as an extraordinary outcome.

The engineering question is whether carrying the power plant improves the economics of the computer. NanoDC combines compute, cooling, networking and an unfolding solar array. That creates a specific tradeoff: avoiding the grid can accelerate installation while variable generation reduces GPU utilization or requires expensive storage. Eight weeks to first token does not establish competitive lifetime inference delivery. Rune uses existing solar facilities; Crusoe offers modular infrastructure across power sources. Liquid Energy needs a measured advantage against those alternatives. Proprietary-module and cooling descriptions supply no benchmark, so technical discontinuity receives the absent-evidence default.

The strongest bull case is that factory completion makes deployment repeatable enough to turn stranded compute demand into a large infrastructure business. JLL’s January 2026 grid-delay findings support the need, and Crusoe’s reported contracted inference ARR above $100 million supports commercial demand in the surrounding market. Those contracts belong to Crusoe. Nevertheless, this is the kind of physical infrastructure opportunity where I will tolerate manufacturing risk and long development cycles. A sufficiently capable instigator attacking an enormous existing spending pool could also justify investing before conventional milestones. Here, the dossier establishes neither a quantified exceptional consequence nor the technical evidence needed to make that exception persuasive.

Ravikumar brings software-building experience from Jelly.fm and Lotus; Khosa has documented commercial initiative through three brokerage signings at his prior company. These are useful starting capabilities. They do not demonstrate rapid belief revision or recruitment of complementary owners for power electronics, thermal systems and manufacturing risks. The older storage-and-waste-heat description might reflect learning, but without the evidence behind the change I cannot count it as learning. Similarly, Velocity participation since 2024 and NVIDIA Inception affiliation do not establish that a NanoDC has passed a decisive operating test. Founder adaptability and risk-removal process therefore receive their defaults.

My next dollar would buy a test of utilization and delivered cost. The dossier provides no ranked kill risk, budgeted experiment or pass/fail threshold, and no bottom-up economics covering solar, storage, cooling, GPU depreciation and availability. That is missing evidence, not evidence of subsidy dependence or reckless spending. The company’s terms also qualify its advertised capabilities; I would not mistake that language for demonstrated failure. But I will not assume a cost breakthrough simply because the energy source is solar.

I pass on Liquid Energy today. There is no documented exit fixation, harmful business category or refusal to confront contrary evidence; this is a failure to establish the investment gates, rather than an active contradiction of them. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independently audited NanoDC customer deployment delivering a matched inference workload at its contracted availability, commissioned at least five times faster than the buyer’s documented conventional alternative, with fully costed, unsubsidized inference at or below that alternative. That would connect the deployment promise to an economically useful machine.
