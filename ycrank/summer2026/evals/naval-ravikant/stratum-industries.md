# Stratum Industries -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have acted, but not yet inside the exact problem. Vihaan was founding engineer number three at document-oriented AI company Cadastral before its acquisition by Legora, while Raj averaged 220% of quota over two years at Deel and built several automation projects. That is real technical and sales output, not résumé theater. But neither founder has disclosed selling to government, navigating procurement, or operating an adjudication system where errors affect patents, benefits, or drug approvals. Their specific knowledge is adjacent; the costly learning unique to this market has not yet been demonstrated.

The product reads applications, applies agency-selected rubrics, routes steps among models, requests missing information, and gives a human officer a cited summary. Useful workflow design is not durable substrate. Stratum discloses no proprietary dataset, model, hardware, exclusive rights, or deployed feedback system. Meanwhile, GovWell already serves more than 150 agencies across 5,000 processes, and Polimorphic and Clariti sell overlapping government automation. If a competitor copies the orchestration code tomorrow, the dossier identifies nothing Stratum still uniquely owns.

The opportunity is broader than a single permit workflow. The same adjudication capability could move from licenses into grants, benefits, patents, and regulated approvals, against a $34.4 billion global smart-government market growing at a reported 20.2% CAGR. Federal policy adopted in April and May 2025 also creates a real procurement opening. But breadth can become abstraction: patent examination, disability benefits, and drug approval do not share identical evidence standards, risk tolerances, or buyers. A platform spanning all of them before mastering one may accumulate exceptions faster than leverage.

The strongest bull case is the pairing of Vihaan’s shipped document-AI experience with Raj’s demonstrated enterprise-selling ability. Government is fragmented supply trapped behind legacy interfaces, and a two-person team that lands one difficult agency could turn reviewed cases and rubric integrations into a compounding asset. Pre-seed companies do not need revenue history. They do need one artifact showing that deployment creates something the next deployment cannot cheaply reproduce. Stratum has disclosed no customer, pilot, accuracy benchmark, pricing, or proprietary case corpus, so that bull case remains a plausible mechanism rather than observed evidence.

The actual operating model currently runs against my revealed preferences. Agencies do not create value or distribution for one another; each appears to require direct sales, custom rubrics, compliance work, and integrations. This is not yet a permissionless network, and it risks becoming a high-service software business whose labor grows with every deployment. Nor does a known-exception case rescue it: missing monetization can be tolerated when a working prototype, extraordinary founder-specific evidence, or self-reinforcing user behavior is already visible. None is publicly demonstrated here. I see no integrity, morale, or artificial-deadline red flag—the problem is structural, not personal.

My 36-month base case is that Stratum narrows to municipal permitting and is acquired by GovWell for less than $30 million because the market is real but the horizontal application layer is copyable. I pass with my own money today. One result would flip the decision: a second agency deploying in under 30 days from a proprietary reviewed-case corpus created by the first, while sustaining below 1% material error across 10,000 adjudications. That would show the product becoming more defensible with use rather than merely becoming more customized.
