# Veltha -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the founders’ résumés; it is the work they already did. Roland Jaloszynski converted mortgage-underwriting guidebooks into executable rules at a $642 billion servicer. Edin Citaku deployed AI inside Germany’s federal criminal police. Veltha applies the same learned skill—turning regulated text into auditable decisions—to insurance claims. That is specific knowledge acquired through costly action, not an AI thesis assembled after the market became fashionable.

The product has also crossed the line from demonstration to consequence. An unnamed $2 billion-plus specialty carrier is running live claims, review time reportedly fell from one hour to five minutes, and the team has encoded more than 500 FCIC, LAM, and PPSH checks. Reading handwritten files and connecting a recommendation to the exact source span and regulatory provision is harder than producing a summary. In a market where 24 states had adopted the NAIC AI Model Bulletin by August 2025, auditability is part of the product rather than compliance decoration.

But difficult implementation is not yet a durable substrate. CLARA, Five Sigma, Sprout.ai, and EvolutionIQ already process claims documents or guide adjusters. If one of them copies provision-level citations, Veltha has disclosed no exclusive data rights, proprietary model, contractual workflow lock-in, or demonstrated learning loop from reviewer overrides. The 500 encoded checks are a real asset and evidence of execution, but today they resemble accumulated expert labor more than an advantage that compounds automatically.

The market supplies room to maneuver. Crop and workers’ compensation claims are wedges into carriers, MGAs, TPAs, and self-insured employers, and the same rule-execution capability could extend across a claims-automation market estimated at $3.85 billion in 2025 and $14.88 billion by 2034. The limitation is that this adjacency is still asserted through shared workflow and top-down market research; the dossier does not show a second insurance line adopting the product or a repeatable path between buyers.

The bull case against my hesitation is strong: two founders with unusually exact experience have already placed software into live, regulated decision-making and produced a twelvefold reduction in review time before publishing pricing or hiring a larger team. The known-exception pattern for a capital-efficient product preceding an observable network applies partially. Yet Veltha lacks the decisive part of that exception: evidence that usage creates an increasingly distinctive product language or asset. Each carrier currently appears to require sales, integration, rule encoding, and support from Veltha; users do not create utility or distribution for other users, and no revenue, price, take rate, retention, accuracy, or override rate proves direct value capture.

I see no disclosed integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-morale red flag. The structural red flag is subtler: a fashionable AI company may confuse a valuable workflow with leverage. My 36-month base case is that Veltha reaches $3 million to $8 million in ARR and is acquired by Guidewire as a regulatory-decision layer rather than becoming an independent network-scale platform. I would not write the check yet. One verifiable condition flips me: three unaffiliated carriers paying for production deployments while Veltha contractually retains reusable override-and-outcome data that measurably improves accuracy across customers.
