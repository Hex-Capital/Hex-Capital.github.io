# Stoa -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Compute Exchange already aggregates procurement across more than 100 compute providers, which exposes Stoa’s signature: Stoa has its greatest potential in the consequence of turning AI hardware into a transparent, financeable commodity, but its weakest dimension is the absence of technical or capital risk that would repel consensus investors. If Stoa wins completely, opaque broker quotes and OEM allocation games give way to clearing prices, auditable execution, and liquid collateral. That is meaningful market infrastructure. It is not yet civilizationally distinct. Stoa currently looks more like a market-structure optimization than a system that breaks an economic regime.

**OpenAI** (2019) is the high rung on consequence because its success-state reorganized labor, knowledge, and potentially $15 trillion of GDP before it had revenue. Stoa shares one load-bearing mechanism with OpenAI: Stoa could make a scarce foundational input broadly priceable, letting lenders, operators, and funds allocate AI hardware with less friction. The divergence is magnitude and evidence. Stoa discloses no transaction volume, longitudinal price dataset, or named institutional liquidity; its public signal is 146 LinkedIn followers while a direct competitor launched the same physical-GPU market in July 2026. Stoa may improve how GPUs trade. OpenAI changed what economies can produce.

**DoorDash** (2013) occupied the low rung on technical risk: no scientific miracle, no $50 million prerequisite, and an execution model any generalist could finance. DoorDash escaped that weakness because consumer-commerce flow became enormous. Stoa has the same missing-risk pattern without the demonstrated escape hatch. Stoa structures PDFs, routes RFQs, performs KYB, and tracks settlement, while users retain hardware inspection, conformity, and counterparty risk. Those are useful workflows, not falsifiable breakthroughs. The hard problem is marketplace liquidity, and capital can buy sales coverage but cannot manufacture trusted bilateral flow.

Kaan Yigit’s commodities-desk experience will help Stoa imitate commodity-market mechanics over the next 18 months, but it also makes founder defiance Stoa’s conformity problem. Berat Celik brings fintech software experience, and Eren Berke Saglam holds Series 7 and 63 registrations; all three biographies point toward applying established trading infrastructure to GPUs. Stoa could compound execution knowledge as quotes accumulate, yet Compute Exchange and the $33 million financing for Ornn show that treating compute like a commodity is already a funded consensus. Experts extrapolate the past. Here, Stoa’s founders appear to be importing the past deliberately.

Berat Celik previously co-founded ForgeMind and XFunds, but Stoa’s dossier provides no documented pivot, prior exit, thesis reversal, or “we were wrong” moment. Stoa therefore receives the absent-evidence default on learning rate rather than a penalty for being young. The score would flip upward if Stoa showed that an initial brokerage or compute-capacity product failed, explained why, and converted that learning into the present transaction-data model. Credentials show capacity. A specific discarded thesis shows learning rate.

**Commonwealth Fusion** (2019) required a $115 million Series A because capital was inseparable from proving the technology. Stoa presents the opposite capital profile: a three-person marketplace, no disclosed hardware ownership, no inspection function, and no roadmap requiring more than $50 million before a commercial milestone. Stoa may eventually finance inventory or underwrite settlement, but Stoa’s current terms leave title, disputes, export compliance, and product diligence with users. I am over-resourced for a software marketplace whose central bottleneck is participation rather than capital-hostile invention.

Stoa’s 0% platform fee for eligible data partners reveals where Stoa will be in 36 months: pivoted primarily into GPU pricing, benchmarking, and transaction-data subscriptions, with the physical marketplace secondary and annual recurring revenue below $2 million. The mechanism is straightforward. Stoa must subsidize flow to obtain proprietary data; direct transaction fees then weaken precisely where the best data enters. Without enough liquidity to become the exchange, Stoa will sell the price signal generated by whatever flow it can collect. The market behind AI hardware becomes a data product about the market.

Compute Exchange’s more than 100-provider footprint creates the strongest opposing case: Stoa could be the **DoorDash** exception, where ordinary technology compounds into extraordinary distribution and transaction data becomes a clearing-price network effect. Kaan Yigit’s commodities background, Stoa’s firm RFQs, delivery-gated payment release, and privacy-preserving data program are real ingredients for that outcome. The case still fails today because Stoa discloses no completed transactions while Compute Exchange already claims aggregated procurement, and Stoa refuses the inspection and counterparty risk that could make its venue uniquely trusted. I would reverse the pass if Stoa verifies at least $250 million of annualized GMV, repeat transactions from three named neoclouds or institutional buyers, and clearing-price data that participants cannot obtain from Compute Exchange.
