# Zomma -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Computer-use models crossed a useful threshold in 2026, investment-operations teams can now buy automation for systems without APIs, and the controlling variable is whether Zomma can drive exception handling and human review low enough to deploy each additional workflow with software economics. The dossier identifies that variable indirectly through reviewer fatigue, failure handling, and slow heterogeneous deployments, but supplies neither a target intervention rate nor evidence that deployment labor declines. That leaves the primary market gate unresolved.

The product is more substantial than a browser-agent wrapper. Zomma operates portals, desktop applications, PDFs, and spreadsheets; applies policies; obtains approval before sensitive actions; retains operator corrections; and maintains seven-year append-only audit logs. These are actions, permissions, workflow state, and feedback loops around a completed operational process. Better computer-use models should expand what the system can execute. The limitation is that the claimed learning effect—successive workflows becoming faster—has no accuracy, reuse, or implementation-time data behind it.

The 2026 capability shift converts previously inaccessible interfaces into addressable workflows, but it has not yet produced a demonstrated economic discontinuity. Every engagement still requires workflow mapping, supervised runs, and a dedicated engineer. The team itself describes enterprise deployment as slow. That may be appropriate at pre-seed, but without measured reductions in implementation time or reviewer load, Zomma could remain a technology-enabled services business in a market projected to grow only from $1.96 billion in 2026 to $5.71 billion in 2034.

The bull case is real: hedge funds, asset managers, and fintech firms reportedly use the product for reconciliation, onboarding, KYC, and reporting. If one deployment creates trusted access to 10–15 fragmented systems, the first workflow can become distribution for several adjacent workflows without reacquiring the customer. But the usage is unquantified, with no named customer, revenue, retention, paid expansion, or capacity constraint. A workshop waitlist and 169 LinkedIn followers do not establish product-market pull, while the move from enterprise sales and application support in April 2026 to finance operations by August leaves open whether the vertical emerged from demand or repositioning.

The team has unusually relevant coverage: Varun Arumugam brings ML and verification infrastructure, Cashel Fitzgerald worked on systems supporting $300 million in annual revenue and $20 billion in transactions, and Jihyun Kim operated inside Goldman Sachs, Endowus, and Arrowpoint. That is strong founder-market fit, but the biography documents neither prior startup learning nor costly commitment or personal desperation, so I do not convert credential quality into motivation credit.

My strongest concern is market crystallization. Hadrius already reports more than 500 financial firms, while Gradient Labs, Concourse, Browser Use, and Asteroid occupy adjacent application and infrastructure layers with substantial funding. Zomma’s broad coverage of a technical discontinuity fits an attractive pattern, but no rare-team or frontier-research exception overrides the missing economics and demand evidence. The red flag is the combination of bespoke forward deployment and an asserted data moat whose reproduction cost is unknown.

I would pass today, while viewing this as a credible company rather than a broken thesis. One verifiable condition would flip the decision: three paying customers each deploying a second workflow in under ten engineer-days with human review required on less than 5% of actions. That would validate reusable distribution, improving margins, and operational pull through one measurement rather than five narrative virtues.
