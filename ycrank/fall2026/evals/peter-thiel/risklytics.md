# Risklytics -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Risklytics’s interesting proposition is that some robotics companies rejected by insurers are insurable once someone correctly describes their operations and negotiates the exclusions. The founders report obtaining coverage for three previously declined businesses in August 2026. That is a small experiment with an observable result. The proposition would fail if the resulting policies still excluded those customers’ principal deployment risks. These placements deserve credit, but the dossier names neither a dissenting underwriter nor independently verified coverage terms. A useful underwriting insight is emerging; a well-established secret is not.

The decisive question is who owns that insight once it works. Risklytics has compiled 736 AI-exclusion filings across 49 states and 33 insurers. That demonstrates work and specialization. It does not establish exclusive information, proprietary underwriting authority, or an advantage that strengthens with every placement. RiskCube already advertises exclusion reviews, and Koop serves overlapping insurance needs. Application automation and careful brokerage can win customers while leaving the economic position open to replication. Physical autonomy is a credible beachhead, but narrowness alone does not create a monopoly.

The strongest argument against passing is that conventional brokers’ mistakes could become Risklytics’s distribution engine. Three rescued placements could produce referrals precisely when insurance blocks deployment; almost 50 application users suggest the problem extends beyond those initial cases. This fits my less conspicuous interest in financial infrastructure supporting new industries. My exceptions for demonstrated customer behavior deserve consideration here. But the behavior resolves a demand question, not the ownership question: customers needing coverage does not show that they must obtain it through Risklytics. There is no independent technical discontinuity or realized exceptional economics to override that weakness.

Gold and Risio provide more substance than a conveniently assembled founding team. They were college roommates and worked together on wildfire modeling before switching to brokerage. That establishes a relationship and actual shared work. Gold’s Harvard leave supports commitment; Risio’s producer license makes the brokerage operationally credible. His continuing degree description does not establish part-time work, so I would not invoke that red flag. Nevertheless, both founders’ current full-time status and economic ownership remain unverified. Their shared history earns credit without pretending the founding structure is fully established.

The business has one understandable revenue engine: commissions of 10–20% of premiums. However, the adjacent $4.8 billion premium forecast translates into only $480–960 million of aggregate brokerage commissions if those rates apply across it, before considering Risklytics’s attainable share. That forecast cannot establish a fund-scale outcome. YC and Harvard networks identify an initial acquisition route, while wholesale and MGA relationships identify access to supply. Neither comes with conversion, sales-cycle, premium-size, or servicing-cost evidence. The channel is concrete enough to investigate; its economics remain unproved.

I pass on writing a check. Risklytics may become a valuable specialist brokerage, but the current evidence supports competent intermediation more strongly than durable market ownership. My verifiable flip condition is an executed, exclusive, multiyear underwriting agreement for a defined robotics deployment class, already used to bind customer policies. That would turn carrier access from a dependency into a potentially protected position. Until then, the founders have shown that they can find coverage, not that they can own the market for finding it.
