# Operon -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Two hundred drawings uploaded in three weeks is the fact I would start with. Someone was willing to put industrial documents into a crude product. But the most important question remains unanswered: what broke when they did, and what did Anderson Chen do next? Operon has evidence of building and customer contact. It has no documented instance of a founder encountering a specific obstacle, inventing a workaround, and getting the customer moving again. That leaves my most heavily weighted criterion at its missing-evidence default.

The product deserves more credit. A P&ID prototype followed by engineering agents developed over six months with design partners is a concrete sequence of artifacts. Operon reports 97%+ detection accuracy on symbols, tags, and lines. That earns intermediate technical credit, with a substantial discount for an unreproduced company benchmark. Finding the symbols is only part of reconstructing a plant. A missed connection across sheets could matter much more than several correctly detected valves. Source references, confidence scores, and engineer approval make the approach sensible, but do not establish its reliability.

There is a plausible narrow well here: engineers repeatedly reconstructing equipment connections for safety reviews and changes. The 200 uploads suggest willingness to try the product; the 100 industry conversations show contact with the problem. Neither tells me how many engineers returned. Six months with unnamed design partners adds some evidence of engagement, but leaves its intensity unclear. The claimed $2.8 million in average annual customer savings would be powerful if a customer explained the calculation. The advertised upfront deployment payment and invoiced licenses describe how Operon intends to charge, rather than establishing that customers have paid.

The strongest case for investing is that Chen has chemical-engineering training and process-engineering experience, and has chosen work that requires embedding engineers and connecting disagreeable industrial systems. This could be the sort of neglected work from which an important company grows. Operon also resembles my recurring interest in teams that assemble a difficult substrate before offering a broader interface. But the verified facility graph is still a claim here, and the dossier never connects the idea to a specific problem Chen personally encountered. I give the origin criterion its default. Likewise, HVAC being a second product establishes expansion, not an evidence-driven correction of a failed hypothesis; adaptability remains at default.

I would pass today. The working product makes the prototype-based exceptions worth considering, but neither independently validated technical proof nor unmistakable repeated use is strong enough to override the missing founder evidence. I would not treat the launch announcement or private-equity offering as proof of a launch-dependent or partner-dependent distribution plan. The more immediate risk is that custom deployment work keeps producing separate projects while Cognite and other vendors improve the common software. Manual service is valuable when it teaches the founders what to build repeatedly. Operon has not yet shown that learning loop. My flip condition is one verifiable customer case: a named plant engineer confirms that Chen fixed a blocked cross-sheet workflow within two weeks, after which the engineer used the repaired product weekly for a month. That would supply both the founder behavior and the user pull missing from this decision.
