# Fabraix -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Every consequential company runs agents across messages, files, websites, permissions, and financial systems; attackers continuously train against those agents, and defensive testing becomes part of deployment rather than an annual ceremony. Fabraix has found a leak from that world: Nyx already places payloads across multiple surfaces, adapts over several turns, and reportedly finds its first exploit in under an hour. But the dossier stops there. It does not map the intervening transitions, explain how agent autonomy changes the security boundary, or identify what Fabraix must control when today’s models and attack techniques commoditize.

The working artifact is the strongest evidence. Nyx reports a 78% AgentHarm attack-success rate against GPT-5.6 Sol’s 67%, maintains more than 10,000 strategies, and has exposed failures in public-facing agents at dozens of Fortune 500 companies. More than 50,000 attempts against the first Playground challenge show people spending real effort inside the product’s world. That is wonderfully weird behavior. It is also researcher behavior, not yet buyer behavior: the Fortune 500 companies are not identified as customers, the benchmark has not been shown to transfer into production, and the Product Hunt listing has one review despite 934 followers.

Ahmed Aly and Ibrahim Abdu have unusually relevant operating tuition. Ahmed led international-payments fraud at Monzo and worked on systems processing more than $1 billion annually; Ibrahim built production-debugging agents at Meta and previously built compiler and database systems. They worked together before Fabraix, so this is not a conference-weekend pairing. What I cannot see is the first-believer act: no dated pre-company red-teaming experiments, founder-built public artifacts, or evidence that either became obsessed with this exact problem before AI-agent security became a crowded category. Their résumés establish capability, not yet independent inevitability.

The present-day leak also contains a concrete economic wound. Manual red-teaming can take weeks and cost six figures, while every change to a model, prompt, tool, permission, or data source can invalidate the result. Black-box testing without source integration could return release control to engineering and security teams. But Fabraix has not identified who captures that six-figure rent, how much continuous testing removes, or whether customers will purchase recurring prevention instead of occasional scans. The $0 researcher tier and custom pricing describe packaging; they do not yet prove the institutional rule has been broken.

The bull case stings because this could become the adversarial regression layer beneath every agent deployment. The strategy corpus, adaptive loop, indirect-injection environments, and Playground submissions could compound into proprietary attack data while larger vendors remain trapped in broad compliance suites. That is the pre-revenue exception I take seriously: functioning technical proof plus founders who have lived near the problem can outweigh missing sales. The bear case is that Virtue AI, HiddenLayer, Garak, and PyRIT reproduce the capability while Check Point and Zscaler bundle it into existing distribution. Fabraix then becomes an excellent attack engine with no independent company around it.

I would not write the check yet. There is also no identified accountable CEO or contractual tiebreaker between two founders, and a system attacking customer agents across messages, files, and permissions needs an explicit consent and data-custody architecture before enterprise deployment. My verifiable flip condition is two named enterprises renewing the continuous tier after six months while contributing proprietary regression cases that measurably improve Nyx against previously unseen production agents. That would turn 50,000 curious attacks into evidence of a controlled enabling layer.
