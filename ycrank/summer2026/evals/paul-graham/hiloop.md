# hiloop -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The strongest fact about hiloop is not a credential or a diagram. The founders ran 4,188 agent-directed experiments on 50 B200 GPUs in two days, then checked the result across 25 paired runs with interleaved controls. They produced a 0.9016 median validation score and a separate 12.54% NanoGPT runtime improvement. This is a real artifact built before anyone granted them category leadership. It puts them near the top of the technical-proof dimension.

But what broke first? The dossier never names a specific obstacle the founders encountered, the workaround they improvised, or how quickly it changed a user’s situation. Their system presumably required solving difficult orchestration and reproducibility problems, but difficulty is not evidence of resourcefulness unless one can see the collision and response. The unfinished request-time secret-delivery mechanism is actually the clearest named blocker, and it remains unfinished. I therefore cannot infer the people-first signal from the benchmark.

The same distinction applies to demand. “Teams with measurable objectives” is a plausible niche, not a narrow well pulling the product back. Erdos Miller appears under “Trusted by,” but hiloop publishes no use, payment, repetition, or workflow details. Seven votes on the YC launch and no revenue data add nothing. Offering to run initial campaigns personally is exactly the right kind of unscalable motion, but it is still an offer rather than evidence that users need the crude product now.

The origin is more convincing. Brar worked on ML infrastructure at DynamoAI and Reducto; Boser worked on ML and platform systems at Reducto and several other engineering organizations. They met while doing the work from which hiloop emerged. Forkable compute, experiment lineage, customer-cloud deployment, secrets, and reproducibility are all genuine schleps. What is missing is a dated account of a particular failure that forced this product into existence. There is also no evidence yet that the founders rejected one route and rapidly shipped another, so flexibility remains untested rather than disproved.

The bull case stings. A two-person team has already assembled the kind of concrete pre-round proof spike I often find more informative than investor enthusiasm. The product is a programmable primitive, the result was numerically confirmed, and founder-run campaigns could expose the scalable system hiding inside the service. But the known-exception logic does not rescue the investment yet: the proof is the founders’ own campaign, not independent validation or unmistakable external pull. Meanwhile Prime Intellect reports more than 10,000 beta training jobs and raised a $130 million Series A. If customers do not pull hiloop into a distinct workflow, the experiment machinery can be absorbed into better-distributed platforms.

My 36-month base case is that hiloop narrows from horizontal “recursive self-improvement” infrastructure into managed post-training and inference-optimization campaigns, reaching roughly $1–3 million ARR if it survives. I would not write the check today because the operational gate fails: impressive machinery has not yet shown me either the founders’ response to a named external obstacle or a user who repeatedly demands it. The flip condition is one named external team paying for and completing three campaigns over eight weeks, with a documented blocker that the founders solve by shipping a product change during those campaigns.
