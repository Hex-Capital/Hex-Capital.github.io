# Petrichor -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Petrichor’s most useful founder signal is the earlier Qokedas instrument-data business. These three founders were working on scientific data before offering wet-lab execution; September 2026 sources document that continuity. That earns more credit than credentials alone. But a public offering does not establish what they personally delivered, who used it, or what difficult laboratory lesson they learned. I see relevant action, with limited evidence of its depth. Their age, missing educational biographies, and three-person team do not concern me.

The investment turns on who keeps the learning. Petrichor proposes recording photographs, raw instrument files, timestamped actions, and failures while scientists execute experiments ([product workflow](https://petrichorbio.com/how/)). Intermediate failures could be valuable training inputs. Yet customers own the commissioned data, and Petrichor promises against sharing it or training on it ([company website](https://petrichorbio.com/)). That protects the customer while closing off the most obvious shared-data advantage. An internal execution system could still compound across projects, but the dossier establishes neither that asset nor a measured improvement from using it. Physical work survives copied code; durable company advantage requires something more.

The strongest bull case is that Petrichor can serve experiments too variable for existing automation. Scientists could accommodate changing model requests while detailed recording turns their work into machine-readable feedback. Lila’s reported hundreds of thousands of AI-driven experiments in September 2025 demonstrate that this workflow has moved beyond a thought experiment. Petrichor also fits my recent preference for physical infrastructure bottlenecks and tools that turn knowledge into output. The difficult question is whether human flexibility becomes the bridge to scalable infrastructure or remains the product indefinitely. Every commissioned experiment currently calls for scientist execution; there is no demonstrated contribution or distribution loop through which one customer improves another customer’s experience.

Frontier AI laboratories are a concrete initial buyer. The adjacent market is less concrete. Petrichor has not established which additional buyers its existing capabilities unlock, or the spending attached to them. Emerald Cloud Lab and Strateos demonstrate established alternatives for remote experimentation, while Lila combines laboratories with its own scientific agents. Their financing is evidence of competitive resources, not Petrichor’s addressable revenue. Likewise, YC participation and October founder coverage establish visibility, not purchasing behavior. With no disclosed payer, price, or completed transaction, direct value capture receives the missing-evidence default.

I would pass with my own money. The known exceptions do not carry this case: there is no observed self-reinforcing adoption that justifies overlooking monetization, and no demonstrated prototype performance or exceptional founder execution that overrides the structural uncertainty. Petrichor’s infrastructure direction fits, but permissionless participation and aggregation of independent supply are not established. I see no documented integrity violation, artificial fundraising pressure, or repeated abandonment; the move from instrument data to execution alone proves none of those. The decisive concern is that the customer may accumulate the valuable training asset while Petrichor accumulates staffing obligations.

One verifiable result would reopen the decision: comparable experiments across multiple paying customers showing that a reusable execution system reduces scientist-hours per experiment while maintaining reproducibility. That would demonstrate that Petrichor retains an improving productive asset even when customers retain their data.
