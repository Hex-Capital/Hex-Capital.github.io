# OpenHack -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI-generated code increases the security workload; engineering teams become the buyer; the controlling variable is the total labor required to get from a code change to a trustworthy security decision. OpenHack wins if exploit verification reduces that labor enough to justify deploying another security product. Its published reproduction of Papermark authentication bypass CVE-2026-36755 gives this hypothesis technical substance. But one reproduced vulnerability does not establish the operating threshold: how much investigation, environment setup, and checking remains across ordinary customer applications?

The product has more architectural substance than a scanner that generates explanations. OpenHack’s repository describes application modeling, vulnerability discovery, and browser or sandbox exploitation. Those actions give stronger models useful work to perform. This is the most attractive part of the company: improving model capability could increase completed security investigations. However, sandbox/browser verification remains beta and requires Docker. Customer-specific workflow state and remediation switching costs are inferred in the dossier. I give credit for the implemented execution machinery, with limited conviction about ownership of the recurring operational loop.

The economic discontinuity remains unproven. The dossier supplies no measured reduction in review hours, inference costs, or cost per verified finding. Its $0 entry plan includes five PR reviews monthly and an initial full scan; Enterprise pricing is negotiated. Those terms reveal an acquisition path without establishing delivery economics. Meanwhile, Aikido already combines code security with autonomous pentesting and reportedly exceeds $40 million ARR. That revenue belongs to a competitor, but it matters to market structure: the category is crystallizing around platforms with distribution. OpenHack needs verification economics strong enough to overcome the convenience of an existing vendor.

The strongest bull case is that Arora has built a credible wedge unusually early. His security research and bug-bounty history fit the problem, the scanner has 430 stars and 65 forks, and the website displays six named team logos. An open-source entry point could become reusable distribution for paid security work, matching my pattern of backing products that establish a channel before expanding. Still, stars and displayed logos establish attention more clearly than recurring demand. Apple employment and technical credentials also supply no evidence of prior startup learning or costly personal commitment. The rare-team research-stage exception does not resolve this: the dossier documents practical security expertise, without the frontier research and capital path that support that override.

I would pass on a personal check today. The decisive gap is demonstrated economic leverage in a market where competitors already reach the buyer. The relevant red-flag test is whether apparent automation conceals substantial human work; beta verification makes that unresolved, rather than proven adverse. I also assign no proprietary-data premium to an MIT-licensed scanner with no documented exclusive feedback asset. One result would flip my decision: a paid, 90-day cohort of five independent customer teams demonstrating at least a 50% reduction in fully loaded security-review cost per deployed change, including environment setup, inference, human verification, and checks for missed vulnerabilities. That would connect the working architecture to the market variable that matters.
