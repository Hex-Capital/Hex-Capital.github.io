# Risklytics -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The opening is institutional: new AI-liability exclusions create coverage uncertainty just as robotics companies need insurance to deploy. Risklytics sells access through that uncertainty. The variable I would underwrite is commission revenue per hour of placement work: can the company repeatedly resolve these coverage gaps without adding broker labor proportionally? Carrier-paid commissions of 10–20% provide a clear monetization mechanism. Application automation helps, but insurers still control underwriting and licensed brokers still negotiate coverage. The dossier establishes a market opening more clearly than an economic engine.

The best evidence is the three companies reportedly helped after previous declines, disclosed on August 26, 2026. Robotics rental, bridge-collision technology, and teleoperation are specific customer problems, and obtaining coverage is a substantially stronger signal than generating an insurance quote. Almost 50 application users add evidence of interest, although they are not 50 bound policies. I give these company-reported claims meaningful, discounted credit. For this stage, the absence of a revenue history is ordinary; the successful placements are the reason to keep paying attention.

The strongest bull case is that coverage becomes a deployment bottleneck and Risklytics becomes the specialist through which physical-AI companies must pass. Its wholesaler and MGA relationships could help aggregate scarce underwriting capacity, while the first policy creates opportunities to sell additional coverage. That fits my pattern of investing around a discontinuity before the category settles. But the dossier does not establish preferential capacity, repeat purchases, or exclusive distribution. Koop already serves this market, and RiskCube advertises exclusion reviews and policy management. The regulatory change creates demand for expertise; it does not yet establish who captures the economics. Deloitte’s $4.8 billion forecast concerns global premiums, of which brokerage commissions are only a fraction before geography, segment, and market share.

Risklytics also deserves a more precise durability test than simply calling it an AI wrapper. Customers confirm applications, and brokers review wording before binding: there is responsibility for a consequential outcome. However, that responsibility currently sits with the brokerage operation. The dossier does not document software integrations, persistent renewal state, or a placement-feedback loop that improves subsequent decisions. The tracker covering 736 filings across 49 states and 33 insurers demonstrates useful compilation work, but its source material is public. Better models could improve intake and comparison for Risklytics and its competitors. The unresolved issue is whether the technology changes service economics or merely makes a conventional brokerage easier to operate.

Gold taking leave from Harvard is concrete commitment. The founders’ move from wildfire modeling to brokerage, together with Risio’s producer licensing, shows practical learning and willingness to do operational work. Those facts earn credit; university prestige and follower counts do not. I find no evidence of status-driven fundraising or premature liquidity. I also find no applicable exception that would justify underwriting absent market economics: this is neither frontier research requiring patient capital nor a demonstrated technological reconstruction of incumbent margins.

I would pass on a personal check today while continuing to track placements. Risklytics has an informative demand signal and a plausible distribution wedge, but it falls short of my combined market-and-economic-change gate. My single flip condition is a six-month bound-policy cohort showing commission revenue per broker hour at least doubling from its initial baseline while maintaining explicit coverage for the customers’ relevant AI exposures. That would connect the current customer need to a business that can scale.
