# Control Seat -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important obstacle is not building another dashboard. Industrial operators retain years of configuration in PLCs, SCADA systems, and historians, so replacing the installed stack is slow and dangerous. Control Seat responded by building above that stack: it supports OPC UA, MQTT, databases, and APIs, can sit over existing SCADA, and has already remotely monitored and controlled a Battex Chile battery. That is a real workaround with an observable result. But the dossier does not say how quickly the founders built it after encountering the blocker, so the evidence of resourcefulness is strong but incomplete.

The Battex deployment also proves there is functioning software, not merely an AI-industrials diagram. A system that controls physical equipment through MQTT has crossed a more meaningful threshold than a prototype dashboard. Still, this is one company-reported deployment. FactoryTalk and Siemens WinCC remain “coming soon,” and there is no uptime, latency, commissioning-time, or independent reliability measurement. The fierce-nerd signal is present; the measured-proof spike is not yet as sharp as the company’s scope implies.

The founders’ origin is the best evidence. Jack Grodnick worked directly with controls and SCADA at Tesla and SpaceX. Warren Shepard worked on networks, manufacturing, and flight software at SpaceX. They met in their first year at Dartmouth and later recognized the same problem through separate industrial experience. This is not a generic “AI for manufacturing” thesis. They encountered an ugly system and chose to absorb the integrations, networking, commissioning, on-premise deployment, and safety burden that easier startups avoid.

That burden is both the opportunity and the trap. Custom pricing, integration services, and direct participation in PLC and SCADA projects are sensible ways to learn. They also make it possible to reach several million dollars of revenue without discovering a repeatable software company. Battex is a named user doing a concrete job, but the dossier supplies no payment, repeat usage, retention, or second deployment. The 341 LinkedIn followers add almost nothing. The question is not whether one battery can be controlled; it is whether the second plant becomes substantially easier to deploy than the first.

The bear case stings because Seeq, Litmus, and Augury have raised at least $567.6 million collectively, while Control Seat has no demonstrated moat. AI may reduce the founders’ development burden, but it also makes overlapping investigation and analytics features easier for incumbents to add. Control Seat does avoid several worse red flags: its distribution does not depend on a ceremonial launch, a hypothetical major partner, or prematurely hired salespeople. Nor does this require a special exception to rescue weak founders or a nonexistent product. It clears the gate on lived experience and a deployed system; what remains unproven is whether that system compounds.

I would make a small personal investment. The combination of founder-originated need, genuine technical work, and willingness to enter the integration schlep is unusual enough at pre-seed. My decision would flip to Pass if diligence cannot verify that Battex has operated Control Seat in production for at least 90 consecutive days. Without that, the strongest evidence collapses from user pull to a successful demonstration.
