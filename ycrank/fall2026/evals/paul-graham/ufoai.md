# UFO.ai -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most useful fact about UFO.ai is that its agent remembered a correction and still used the wrong repository. That is a real obstacle. The team’s September 23 engineering account describes manual recovery and an exact-string correction mechanism that addressed the case. This earns more credit than a founder biography: something broke, and they changed the system. But the account does not establish how quickly they shipped the repair or whether it prevented recurrence across different tasks. I see a concrete instance of resourcefulness, with limited evidence about its reach.

The technical work is stronger than the evidence about founder behavior. UFO.ai has published an Apache-licensed runtime with execution, identity, authorization, accounting and extension layers. This is an inspectable artifact, not merely a proposal to connect a model to Slack. It fits my recurring preference for programmable interfaces that let other people build things. But the other half of that pattern is people actually building with them. The repository’s 99 stars and 13 forks do not establish that. Nor do the September context-management experiments provide an independently validated numerical proof of reliable long-running work.

Who needs this badly enough to keep using it when it makes mistakes? The dossier does not identify that user. Reporting, follow-ups and software maintenance are examples of work, not yet a narrow well of demand. Prepaid task billing, unlimited workspace members and customer-supplied model keys make experimentation possible, but they do not tell us which experiment became indispensable. I assign the absent-evidence default for demand. This is not a penalty for having no disclosed revenue at pre-seed. It is the distinction between shipping a useful-looking machine and discovering who cannot do without it.

The strongest argument for investing is the founders. Alex Graveley created Copilot, directed Comet and built Hackpad before its acquisition by Dropbox. Marshall Kiely brings AI engineering and operating experience from Notable Health and Perplexity. These are unusually relevant backgrounds for discovering what an agent workspace should become. With Glean reporting $300 million ARR and Dust already selling shared agents, business demand for adjacent products plainly exists. The founders might find the important missing piece faster than an outsider could. But their previous work does not document the personal collision that produced UFO.ai, and an exact-string bug fix does not demonstrate a material change of product route after contrary evidence. Those two dimensions remain at their defaults.

I would hold off on writing a check. There is no evidence that the founders are waiting for a partner, delegating away the product or mistaking launch coverage for growth; the hackathon and October 2 coverage alone establish none of those red flags. Their shared Perplexity background also leaves cofounder history unresolved rather than adverse. A working-product exception can excuse uncertainty about a new team, but this dossier’s artifact and modest public attention do not justify overriding my resourcefulness gate. **My flip condition is a documented customer episode in which a founder ships a workaround to a named deployment blocker within two weeks, and that customer then uses the repaired workflow weekly for a month.** That would connect the promising machinery to the founder behavior and persistent need I would be buying.
