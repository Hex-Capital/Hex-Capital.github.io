# Nines -- Paul Graham Evaluation

What happened the first time Nines tried to patch a real fiber connection and failed? That is the story I want. The dossier gives me the intended tasks, but no obstacle followed by a founder-built workaround and an observable result. Eom’s software background and Li’s robotics experience make that story worth asking for. They cannot supply the answer. Resourcefulness stays at the absent-evidence default.

The strongest case for Nines is its choice of starting point. Commissioning fiber connections is a specific job. Li’s reported work on large-scale inference at Meta gives the team relevant context, and the CTO’s autonomous-underwater-vehicle experience suggests some acquaintance with physical systems. Site adaptation, cable handling, and failure recovery contain precisely the ugly work that can conceal an opportunity. But relevant employment does not establish that these founders personally encountered this problem. The dossier leaves the idea’s origin unanswered.

The decisive gap is between selecting that job and demonstrating that a robot can do it. Nines identifies its website sequence as simulated inspection. That disclosure is useful; it is not evidence of deception. It also supplies no measured fiber-patching result. Microsoft’s November 2024 manipulation prototypes establish that the technical direction is credible, while its account of adjacent-cable disturbance explains why an impressive movement may still be an unusable operation. Nines needs evidence about its own machine. My pattern of backing difficult technical companies after concrete proof does not activate here, nor does the pattern of developers already using a new programmable tool.

Demand is similarly untested in this record. The November 13 launch attracted approximately 252,000 views, but no identified operator is reported using the product repeatedly. The request for operator introductions is a sensible beginning to founder sales. It does not establish dependence on a large partner, and a public launch alone does not establish the launch-centered red flag. The commercial question is concrete: will an operator tolerate a crude robot because waiting for technicians costs more? The broad $16.2 billion market estimate cannot answer that, and Nines has disclosed neither pricing nor purchasing behavior.

I would pass today. There is no documented product revision to distinguish adaptability from rigidity, so that dimension also stays at default. Unknown cofounder history is likewise an unanswered question, not evidence of a bad relationship. I checked the exceptions: this dossier supplies neither a working product, independently validated proof, nor unmistakable user pull strong enough to replace the missing founder evidence. One verifiable flip condition would reopen the decision: an operator-confirmed commissioning deployment documenting a named failure, the founders’ dated fix, measured successful patching, and the operator’s subsequent request to use it again. That would turn Nines from a plausible project into evidence about the people building it.
