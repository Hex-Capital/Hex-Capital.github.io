# HyperBench -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

HyperBench closes an important loop: diagnose a failure, change the software, and test the repair on actual hardware. If that becomes reliable, scarce engineering expertise could become much cheaper. But the dossier does not quantify how much engineering expenditure disappears, how many systems become economical to build, or what limits that expansion. I can see the mechanism for useful automation. I cannot yet price the option on an extraordinary consequence. Missing evidence earns a low prior; it does not establish a small ambition.

The strongest evidence is the product’s actual engineering. An evidence-linked system graph connects repositories, datasheets, layouts, and interfaces; agents can then verify changes on existing benches. This is substantive application-level work. The October 8 comparison reports **117.7 versus 237.2 seconds of solver time**, approximately a 2x improvement, with both attempts passing. That establishes a narrower result than replacing engineering labor. Single attempts cannot establish repeatability, and solver time excludes integration and human supervision. The September FPGA demonstration—approximately one million parameters at 10,500 decode tokens per second on an AMD Kria KV260—shows hardware execution, but supplies no comparative measure of debugging performance. Neither result establishes a 5–10x discontinuity against an incumbent workflow.

The strongest argument against passing is the combination of builders and feedback. Justin Leong worked on Anduril’s Roadrunner GNC firmware; Ivan Ge worked on software infrastructure at OpenAI. Those are relevant, complementary experiences for a product crossing physical devices and software systems. A two-person team publishing executable demonstrations deserves attention before revenue appears. HyperBench also fits my pattern of turning scarce technical workflows into repeatable platforms: a verified repair could potentially improve subsequent investigations. However, the dossier does not establish that accumulated repairs produce that learning advantage, nor document belief revision or difficult recruiting. Relevant experience supports taking the team seriously; it does not substitute for those particular founder signals.

The largest uncertainty is whether useful autonomy survives unfamiliar customer systems at an acceptable deployment cost. Private-beta enrollment provides no evidence resolving that question. The published experiments demonstrate execution, but disclose no ranked kill risk, budgeted decisive test, or pass/fail threshold. Likewise, unpublished pricing and absent support, inference, and integration costs leave economic parity unproven. Nominal’s investigation agent and custom-agent interfaces make this consequential: HyperBench must deliver enough verified work to justify a separate product. Its custom retrieval and physical verification keep me from labeling it a generic interface wrapper, but platform overlap remains unresolved.

I pass at this evidence level. My exceptions allow incomplete milestones or economics when an exceptional instigator and an enormous consequence are already established; this dossier does not establish that override. There is also no evidence of exit fixation, resistance to contrary results, or permanent subsidy dependence. This is a promising engineering mechanism with limited evidence on my other gates. **One verifiable condition would reopen the decision:** an independently audited customer deployment showing at least a 10x reduction in fully loaded cost per correctly verified repair across previously unseen hardware/software faults, including integration and supervision. That would connect the technical mechanism to an economic consequence worth underwriting.
