# Kebra -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence case is too small and too vague. Kebra targets administrative reconstruction after field-service jobs: gathering photos, diagnoses, parts data, calls, and texts before filing claims or invoices. That is useful work inside a $5.10 billion FSM market, but the dossier quantifies neither the labor cost being eliminated nor a mechanism that makes field service 10x cheaper or more available. This is workflow compression, not yet infrastructure reconstruction.

The technical case is weaker. ServiceTitan, Jobber, and Housecall Pro already possess the system-of-record data and distribute overlapping AI functions. Kebra’s overlay advantage—working across the customer’s existing stack—reduces adoption friction, but it is systems integration until proven otherwise. There is no measured improvement in claim cycle time, invoice accuracy, technician utilization, or office labor against a named incumbent. The Company Brain may accumulate useful context, but no retention, proprietary-data scale, or learning-rate evidence establishes a technical discontinuity.

The strongest bull case is real. Two founders with multimodal-AI, signal-processing, and data-center-systems experience have put software into operating field-service businesses across Florida, Pennsylvania, and California. Quality Air is named, and both technicians and its back office reportedly use the product. At pre-seed, live workflow adoption is more informative than the absence of published revenue. An overlay can also penetrate fragmented software environments faster than a replacement platform and then expand from documentation into claims, parts, invoicing, and customer communication.

But that bull case does not resolve the central risk. The dossier acknowledges incomplete or inaccurate AI output and describes review steps, yet supplies no experiment, error threshold, cost, or stop condition for the automation-accuracy kill risk. Nor does it show that the founders changed a material belief after deployment or recruited a domain operator to own claims, warranty, or trade-specific workflow risk. Their technical backgrounds are relevant; their risk-removal behavior remains undocumented.

The economics are similarly unproven. There is no pricing, gross-margin model, implementation burden, or quantified savings per contractor. A quote-based sale plus customer-specific integrations can become a services-heavy business whose apparent automation is subsidized by founder labor. Software does not require a physical Chindia-price curve, but it still must beat the incumbent bundle after integration, review, and support costs. Kebra provides no evidence of that parity.

My 36-month base case is a pivot toward a narrow HVAC claims-and-warranty workflow with less than $2 million ARR, because the broad “AI-native field-service company” layer is easy for installed FSM vendors to bundle. I pass today. One result flips the decision: an independently verifiable deployment showing at least a 10x reduction in claims-processing cost or cycle time, with below-incumbent total cost and the improvement sustained across multiple unaffiliated contractors.
