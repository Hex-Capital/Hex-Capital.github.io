# Verdant -- Paul Graham Evaluation

More than 200 local-government interviews make Verdant a schlep-first, pain-second company. Verdant has gone farther into the municipal maze than most hackers would willingly go, but neither Aidan Ng nor Jason Yi is described as having personally suffered through a land-use permit. The strongest signal is their willingness to learn an ugly system; the weakest is that permitting appears selected through research rather than built from an organic wound. That combination often produces a competent government-software business. It does not yet produce the inevitability of Dropbox.

Coinbase is the high rung for schlep blindness: Brian Armstrong built the wallet and then ran toward the compliance grind everyone else avoided. Verdant shares the essential mechanism. Verdant must encode jurisdiction-specific ordinances, survive municipal procurement, process property plans across AWS, Google Cloud, Supabase, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Reducto, and keep government personnel legally in control when the AI is only 80–90% accurate. But Verdant diverges from Coinbase at the point that matters: Coinbase had a working artifact from Armstrong before conviction, while Verdant has no named deployment and launched only marketing-site v0.1 on July 3, 2026. The schlep is real. The evidence that these founders can metabolize it is still preliminary.

Loopt is the low rung on organic necessity: the product arrived before mobile-location infrastructure made it genuinely usable, and Sam Altman’s animal quality had to override the weakness in the idea’s immediate pull. Verdant has adjacent biography rather than direct necessity. Ng worked around government and commercial real estate; Yi built local-government HR software at Holly and researched public-benefits automation. Those experiences make Verdant less synthetic than a consultancy-created AI wrapper, but Verdant’s March 2026 grant-management positioning followed by a July permitting launch suggests exploration, not a tool the founders had already needed for years. Verdant could discover the organic idea by working inside planning departments, but it has not shown that discovery yet.

The March-to-July product change is the animal test in miniature. Verdant interviewed 200-plus government employees, discarded grant management, and moved into permitting; over the next 18 months that behavior compounds only if Verdant turns interviews into manual implementations, ordinance cleanup, and embarrassingly unscalable applicant support. Interviews are tracks, but they are not cereal boxes. Verdant has no named customer, revenue figure, signed LOI, or working-demo adoption comparable to Dropbox’s Hacker News video. I give substantial credit for the search effort and withhold the rest until the founders force one municipality across the line.

Jason Yi’s 24 public repositories put Verdant above the technical-by-credential-only pattern, and his work on Holly, Aidy, public-benefits automation, data security, and rules-as-code is relevant. But Formulate and cloud-fpga-frontend each have two stars, while Ng has no public GitHub footprint; Verdant therefore lacks the hacker genealogy that made the Collisons legible before Stripe. The score would flip upward if Yi released a non-trivial ordinance compiler, plan parser, or rules engine that municipal technologists adopted independently of Verdant. A private SaaS implementation may still work, but hackers usually leave artifacts before they leave sales collateral.

Airbnb is the useful single-anchor parallel for Verdant’s founder configuration. Airbnb’s three founders had complementary abilities and enough shared history to survive years of rejection; Verdant has a complementary pair, with Ng covering government and land-use context while Yi covers government software and implementation. Both Verdant founders attended Georgetown and connected through a shared interest in government software, which is better than two strangers paired for an application. Verdant does not disclose how long they built together or any prior joint artifact, so the pair looks structurally sound without yet showing Airbnb-level cohesion.

GovWell’s 150-plus agency footprint suggests that, within 36 months, Verdant will narrow into an ordinance-grounded intake and review copilot that integrates beside incumbent systems rather than replacing them, reaching roughly $1–3 million in ARR from 10–30 municipalities. Verdant’s highest signal—the appetite for jurisdiction-specific schlep—supports a useful implementation business. Verdant’s lowest signal—the absence of an organic founder wound—makes another customer-led narrowing more likely than a clean march toward the current end-to-end vision.

Clariti’s CivCheck acquisition and Accela’s AI expansion form the strongest bear case: Verdant could spend three years educating slow buyers while better-distributed vendors copy application checking and bundle it into existing contracts. Verdant has no moat, no named customer, and already changed product scope once, so that failure mechanism stings. But Verdant’s 200-plus interviews, Yi’s prior local-government software work, and Ng’s government background make outright naïveté less likely; they are enough to predict adaptation, not dominance. Three named municipalities paying Verdant, with repeat permit volume and founders personally handling ordinance onboarding, would flip my verdict to Invest because it would convert interviews into animal evidence.
