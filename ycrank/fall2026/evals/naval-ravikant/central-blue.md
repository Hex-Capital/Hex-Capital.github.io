# Central Blue -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Central Blue’s founders have worked on machines that must function outside a software demo. Brian Wu’s JPL visual-navigation and robotics work, alongside Jeewoo Choi’s Boeing thermal and environmental testing, gives them relevant knowledge acquired through action. That earns my attention. But spacecraft autonomy and aerospace testing do not yet establish mastery of sustained optical power delivery. The nearly completed Seattle prototype is where their adjacent experience must become specific knowledge of this problem.

The investment turns on what survives copying the code. Central Blue is attacking a physical bottleneck: collecting energy, pointing a beam, receiving it and managing heat. A competitor cannot reproduce that system by generating software. Yet difficulty alone does not give Central Blue an advantage. The dossier establishes no demonstrated power-delivery performance, proprietary hardware advantage or accumulated operating asset. HELION’s 300 kWh storage and 10 kW delivery for four to five hours are targets. The planned Wave Motion demonstration could produce evidence; its announcement does not supply it. The 2027 Blackwing demonstration tests CORE software, a different link in the chain.

The strongest argument for investing now is that waiting for complete validation could surrender the opportunity to back capable founders building genuinely scarce infrastructure. This fits my recent preference for embodied bottlenecks. But Central Blue has not yet earned the exceptions I make for an unconventional working prototype, extraordinary founder behavior or an already compounding user network. Star Catcher reports an October 1, 2026 demonstration-payload launch and offers compatibility with existing arrays. Central Blue’s dedicated receivers introduce an adoption dependency: a customer must integrate hardware before receiving the proposed benefit. The founders need to show what that integration buys which an existing-array approach cannot deliver.

“Power grid” also implies more economic compounding than the evidence currently supports. Satellite operators are identifiable initial buyers, but the dossier does not establish a path into independently evidenced adjacent spending. Nor does another customer demonstrably improve service or acquisition for existing customers. More receivers could eventually improve fleet utilization, but orbital geometry and scheduling could also require additional capacity. Until measured, that is a hypothesis. My pattern of backing networks assembled from fragmented supply does not yet apply; Central Blue currently proposes to build the supply itself. Mission-specific contracts offer a plausible payer, while pricing, completed transactions and contribution economics remain absent.

I would pass on this evidence. There is no documented integrity problem, artificial financing deadline or reassurance-seeking behavior, and the move from standalone CORE toward power infrastructure does not by itself establish indecision. The issue is narrower: relevant founders have not yet demonstrated the asset or economics that would make this my bet. One milestone would reopen it: a paid customer pilot with independently measured receiver-side power, duty cycle and thermal performance, showing that the contracted service price exceeds the fully allocated delivery cost. That would connect the engineering accomplishment to something Central Blue can own and sell.
