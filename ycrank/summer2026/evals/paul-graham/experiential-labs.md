# Experiential Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Kion Fallah led mixed-reality simulation at Waabi, and Silen Naihin contributed to AutoGPT before building Experiential Labs. Experiential Labs is therefore a hacker-rich, schlep-light company: the founders have unusually strong technical lineage, but the unpleasant work protecting the business remains mostly hypothetical. A 216-star optimizer, a public endpoint, and a research paper are real artifacts. They are not yet the equivalent of personally dragging customers through broken integrations, sensitive traces, and unreliable reward functions. Experiential Labs passes the hacker test more clearly than the animal test.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for hacker genealogy because the Collisons had been building commerce software since Auctomatic and possessed both technical taste and an organic reason to use it. Experiential Labs shares the technical mechanism: Fallah has a machine-learning PhD and simulation research, while Naihin has continual-learning research and an AutoGPT contribution. Experiential Labs diverges because Stripe's hackers had already encountered the commercial problem themselves; the dossier shows research continuity and 216 GitHub stars, but no production agent operated by either founder whose failures forced them to invent continual learning. These are genuine hackers. The missing piece is the scar tissue.

**Kiko** (2005) exposed what happens when pure software has no sufficiently protective schlep: smart founders can build quickly, yet speed alone does not make the product hard to copy or urgent to buy. Experiential Labs currently shows some of that pattern. Trace ingestion, reward construction, and verifier maintenance could become a valuable grind, but Experiential Labs has disclosed no named customer whose sensitive production data had to be cleaned, labeled, and converted into a functioning simulation. OpenPipe, Together AI, and Fireworks AI can all add overlapping workflows. The schlep exists in theory; Experiential Labs has not shown that it has entered it.

Experiential Labs has shipped a hosted platform, an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, a PyPI path, and an optimizer with 26 forks. Those artifacts make Experiential Labs more than a paper company, and over the next 18 months they could compound animal resourcefulness by pulling the founders into support calls, hand-built evaluators, and customer-specific training failures. But 216 stars are not cereal boxes. There is no revenue, named design partner, manual deployment story, or stunt showing Fallah and Naihin refusing to take no for an answer. I see builders, but the dossier does not yet let me call them animals.

Fallah's Waabi simulation work and Naihin's continual-learning research make Experiential Labs intellectually organic, but the decisive counterfactual is personal use. If Experiential Labs had shown that the founders operated an agent themselves, watched distribution shift destroy it, and built the optimizer to stop their own production losses, the organic-idea score would move into Dropbox territory. Instead, Experiential Labs appears to grow out of the founders' research fields rather than an explicitly documented pain they suffered as users. That is much better than spotting a fashionable market, but research adjacency is not quite an organic idea.

**Reddit** (2005) benefited from founders who had lived and built together long enough for their working relationship to survive the first idea being rejected. Experiential Labs has the right numerical shape—two technical founders with complementary simulation and agent backgrounds—but Fallah and Naihin had conducted research together for only six months before starting Experiential Labs. Experiential Labs avoids the solo-founder red flag and does not resemble a pair assembled merely to pitch. Six months is nevertheless too short to reveal whether the pair will still like each other after a failed training run, a lost customer, and six weeks without sleep.

OpenPipe's $6.7 million head start and Together AI's $130 million of 2024 revenue make the likely 36-month outcome unusually concrete: by July 2029, Experiential Labs will have narrowed from continual learning for agents to trace-driven model routing and will be producing between $1 million and $3 million in ARR. Experiential Labs can reach that range because the 40% cost-reduction promise is immediately legible and the OpenAI-compatible endpoint lowers adoption friction. Experiential Labs is less likely to become a standalone continual-learning platform because customers will buy reliable cost savings before they buy a new theory of how agents learn.

Fireworks AI has raised more than $1.827 billion, and the strongest bear case is that Experiential Labs is an excellent research project sitting inside a feature roadmap owned by much larger inference platforms. Fireworks AI, Together AI, or OpenPipe could copy trace ingestion and routing, while sparse or incorrectly scored traces prevent Experiential Labs from delivering its promised quality. That objection stings because Experiential Labs has no disclosed revenue and reports 27% savings on RouterBench while advertising at least 40%. The evidence that wins for now is the founder pair plus a shipped optimizer, public endpoint, and nontrivial research result—three times the final pass rate with half the forgetting on the disclosed adversarial task. I would flip to Pass if Experiential Labs has no three named paying customers repeatedly retraining production agents by January 2027.
