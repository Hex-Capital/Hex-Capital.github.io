# Fleety -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

A plumbing shop’s dispatch board is a surprisingly interesting place to look for the future. By 2032, I can imagine a small contractor coordinating calls, equipment, technicians, and return visits without an owner spending the day moving little boxes around a calendar. Fleety connects several of those pieces today: call transcripts, equipment records, technician skills, and changing schedules. But getting from better assignments to a shop that largely coordinates itself requires reliable outcome feedback and authority to act across systems. The dossier does not establish those transitions. That future is my hypothesis; I cannot yet underwrite it as Fleety’s destination.

The founders are the strongest reason to keep looking. Ben Zhou founded B&A Roofing and permitting startup Timrep, which reached six-figure revenue and raised $540,000. Ariyaman Molanorouzi previously ran property-operations automation covering 3,000-plus units and built a six-figure-ARR SEO business. Both worked as dispatchers before building Fleety. These are useful operating scars, and the prior businesses show some ability to turn effort into commercial activity. I give that substantial founder credit, while keeping every dollar of their earlier revenue out of Fleety’s traction. The chronology establishes relevant experience more clearly than a long, independently pursued obsession with this particular problem.

Then I get stuck on the advertised $500,000 annual revenue gap between the best and average technician. Why does that gap exist? Better diagnosis? Different jobs? More aggressive selling? Matching skills to equipment could recover genuinely wasted capacity, but the number alone does not explain which mechanism Fleety fixes. The dossier identifies scheduling complexity; it does not identify a gatekeeper collecting a rent or a restriction that Fleety removes. An owner may get time back. I still need evidence of how dispatch changes give the shop or technician more control over their working day.

The strongest case against passing is sitting inside the customer shops. Fleety names Albert Nahman, Ace Plumbing, and Apex Plumbing, and describes spending weeks onsite configuring the product. That fits my pattern of finding consequential companies inside unglamorous workflow repair. Compatibility with ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, and Jobber also gives the team a plausible opening across different operating systems. Those facts deserve product credit. They do not establish repeated paid use, dispatcher reliance, or measured assignment improvements; the subscription terms establish a billing arrangement, not collected revenue. I would happily consider a pre-revenue exception for a sufficiently convincing working artifact. These references do not yet supply that level of behavioral proof.

ServiceTitan’s existing Dispatch Pro makes the distinction consequential. Fleety needs a reason to remain valuable after the incumbent improves its own matching. Shop-specific configuration might become that reason, but exclusive outcome data, demonstrated learning effects, and a controlled technical bottleneck remain undocumented. Its CRM integration dependency is concrete; it does not establish the more severe red flag of dependence on one closed model supplier. Likewise, the dossier supplies no affirmative evidence of deceptive leadership or severe privacy abuse. My pass rests on the investment mechanism: experienced operators have found a useful application, while the enabling asset and next-world transition remain unproven.

I would pass with my own money today. My one verifiable flip condition is a six-month deployment cohort across at least two supported CRMs showing dispatchers repeatedly accepting Fleety’s assignments and a measured increase in completed jobs per technician-hour against each shop’s prior baseline. That would show the product changing how shops work and give substance to the cross-platform advantage. The founders have earned attention; the dispatch board still needs to reveal something harder to replace.
