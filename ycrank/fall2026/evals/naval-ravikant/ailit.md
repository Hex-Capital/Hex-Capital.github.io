# Ailit -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Emil Tagesson has worked on batteries at Koenigsegg and reports taking Candela’s electric ferry through certification and production. That is specific knowledge acquired where engineering meets physical consequences. It earns Ailit meaningful founder credit. It does not establish that the team has solved locomotive integration, railroad procurement, or battery exchanges around freight schedules. Christian Lind’s physics education supplies little additional evidence on those questions. The strongest founder signal is the machinery Tagesson has helped bring into service.

Ailit’s design makes the opportunity credible: put interchangeable batteries behind an existing diesel-electric locomotive, use its electric drive, and retain diesel backup. The railroad could adopt incrementally. But hardware earns durability through demonstrated performance, manufacturing knowledge, or installed infrastructure. Ailit’s first full-scale prototype is still under construction, with deployment targeted for summer 2027. Dayton-Phoenix already reports integration testing of a competing battery tender. Copying Ailit’s software would not reproduce an operating rail system; the dossier has yet to establish which difficult part Ailit uniquely owns.

The strongest bull case is that Tagesson’s deployment experience makes this integration tractable, and a useful first route finances expansion into neighboring routes. More operators sharing exchange sites could improve battery utilization and make coverage increasingly valuable. This fits my interest in infrastructure bottlenecks and networks assembled from fragmented physical demand. It also exposes the decisive uncertainty: an additional railroad might require another dedicated installation rather than improve the system for existing customers. A proposed network receives no credit for effects that have not occurred.

The commercial offer is direct: railroads buy energy, Ailit owns the equipment, and advertised pricing is 20% below diesel without an upfront equipment purchase. That identifies a payer and a reason to switch. It leaves the return on Ailit’s capital unresolved. Batteries, spare inventory, charging sites, electricity, and maintenance must fit inside that discount. The reported SEK10 million in earlier funding and separate $500,000 YC investment provide resources to test the proposition; neither establishes customer demand or profitable energy delivery. There is no disclosed transaction or operating margin to underwrite yet.

Class I and II railroads supply an identifiable initial customer set. Expansion across their routes is plausible, but the dossier does not establish adjacent buyers, addressable spending, or the infrastructure reuse needed to reach them economically. I would not invent those advantages from the size of freight transportation. Nor would I treat missing revenue at pre-seed as misconduct. No documented integrity, pressure-selling, or founder instability red flag appears here. My exceptions for extraordinary founder behavior, an already forming user network, or a demonstrated capital-efficient prototype also lack supporting evidence in this dossier.

I pass with my own money today. Ailit has relevant engineering experience and a sensible integration hypothesis, but too much of the investment case depends on assets and operating behavior that remain prospective. My flip condition is a verified paid corridor deployment in which an additional operator shares existing swapping capacity and improves gross profit per delivered unit of energy after battery depreciation and site costs. That would demonstrate the compounding mechanism I am being asked to finance.
