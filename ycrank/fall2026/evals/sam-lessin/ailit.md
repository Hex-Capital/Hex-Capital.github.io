# Ailit -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Ailit is asking its balance sheet to absorb the equipment cost that railroads do not want to pay upfront. That is the investment question. Selling energy at 20% below diesel could make adoption attractive while leaving the supplier financing batteries, spare inventory and swapping sites before collecting enough cash to replace them. The dossier reports at least SEK10 million of prior financing plus $500,000 from YC, but no proposed valuation or competing-term-sheet count. Those checks establish funding, not cheap entry or an auction. I cannot underwrite my personal return from the customer’s advertised savings.

The Superwagon prototype, targeted for summer 2027, could reveal something valuable: whether interchangeable batteries can power an existing locomotive without compromising its operating schedule. Retaining diesel backup makes the transition more practical, but it also makes a successful demonstration ambiguous. How much diesel actually disappears? What does continuous availability cost? Ailit has a development date; the dossier does not connect round proceeds to a funded experiment with a measurable pass/fail threshold. Building hardware is an activity. Establishing that one route produces cash after supplying reliable energy would be a venture moment.

The strongest reason to take this seriously is Emil Tagesson. Taking Candela’s electric ferry through certification and production, alongside experience on Koenigsegg’s battery team, is meaningful operating evidence. Ailit fits my pattern of backing teams that reorganize difficult physical infrastructure rather than avoiding it. The proposed bridge between electric vehicle engineering and freight operations is credible on the engineering side. Direct railroad operating experience remains unestablished. Dayton-Phoenix’s reported locomotive integration testing also means the battery-tender architecture itself cannot carry the differentiation; Ailit must win through exchanges, availability and delivered economics.

But what if the team is right? Preserving existing locomotives and removing upfront equipment purchases could unlock customers who would otherwise refuse electrification. A working route might create valuable operational relationships and scheduling data. That is a serious bull case. Today, however, those are prospective assets: the dossier identifies no customer purchase, signed pilot or operating deployment. Trade coverage and fundraising do not establish that owned access causes demand. My exceptions to missing financing gates require documented conversion, cash economics, proprietary access or unusually strong operating receipts. Tagesson supplies a real reason to listen, but not evidence that overrides both gates. Nor does the record establish auction tactics, confused founder control or an AI-dependent moat; I would not invent those red flags.

I pass on the evidence available. Gross margin, burn and runway are undisclosed, so I cannot conclude that another financing is necessary—or that the business can avoid one. The specific concern is that every new customer may require Ailit to buy substantial assets before the operating surplus is known. I would reopen on one verifiable result: a paid single-route deployment showing positive cash contribution after electricity, battery depreciation, spare-pack inventory, swapping operations and diesel backup costs. That would begin turning an attractive railroad proposition into an asset I can finance.
