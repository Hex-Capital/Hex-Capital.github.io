# Hundred -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Hundred’s interesting possibility is catching defective components before factories spend more material, labor, and machine time assembling them. That could multiply the value of inspection beyond the inspector’s wage. But the dossier gives no defect baseline, downstream loss per escaped component, or measured reduction in scrap. The $25.3 billion machine-vision market includes many unrelated applications. I cannot turn that number into a quantified consequence of Hundred succeeding. I pass at present.

The technical approach deserves attention. Controlled illumination, ambient-light exclusion, and whole-tray imaging address physical measurement conditions; reference-part, within-batch, and known-defect comparisons provide several ways to identify anomalies. This is substantive engineering. But detecting flaws below 100 μm says little about production usefulness without throughput, false accepts, and false rejects. Elementary offers inspection through existing cameras. Hundred must demonstrate that its dedicated station removes a constraint that the retrofit cannot economically remove. No such comparison is disclosed. The architecture earns limited technical credit; a 5–10x discontinuity remains unproven.

The strongest argument against my pass is the combination of Erica Diaz’s supplier troubleshooting in China and Mexico and deployment underway with three founding partners. Diaz has encountered the factory conditions that can defeat an elegant laboratory system. Gosserand brings production-software experience; Liao brings quantitative and machine-learning research. Those complementary disciplines fit the work. Whole-tray inspection calibrated from one acceptable part could also reduce the setup burden enough to make comprehensive inspection practical. This is a credible path to a repeatable industrial product. However, the biographies do not establish fast belief revision or difficult recruiting against named risks, and the partner announcement does not establish completed installations or repeatable performance.

My first experiment would attack discrimination under real manufacturing variation. Can Hundred accept legitimate differences in finish and material while catching economically important defects at production speed? Run a blinded comparison on partner trays with customer-agreed error limits and a fixed engineering budget. The disclosed deployments have no such thresholds, cost, or stop condition, so I cannot credit them as demonstrated risk removal. Onsite setup and customer-specific changes might accelerate learning, but they also create a question about whether each installation needs another engineering project. Pricing, hardware cost, utilization, and service requirements are undisclosed. Unsubsidized customer economics remain unestablished.

Hundred fits my pattern of combining disciplines around physical work. It has not yet demonstrated the repeatable production platform or proprietary experimental learning loop that makes that pattern especially valuable; retained photographs alone do not establish either. Nor does the dossier establish the exceptional combination of an instigator and quantified enormous consequence that would justify overriding weak technical and economic evidence. There is no evidence of premature organizational scaling or permanent subsidy dependence, so those red flags do not support a harsher verdict. **One verifiable result would reopen my decision:** a customer-audited trial across the three founding partners showing that the same station configuration reduces fully loaded inspection and attributable downstream defect costs per shipped part by at least 10x, while meeting each factory’s throughput and quality limits. That would connect Hundred’s engineering to an economically consequential result.
