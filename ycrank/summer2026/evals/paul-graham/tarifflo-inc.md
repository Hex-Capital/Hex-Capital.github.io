# Tarifflo Inc. -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Bryce Judy clearly encountered this problem rather than manufacturing an “AI for customs” thesis. He classified imports at KPMG, obtained a customs-broker license, published HTS benchmarking research, and then entered a market full of regulatory updates, filing deadlines, and liability. That is unusually strong founder-origin evidence. But the dossier never names the obstacle that stopped Tarifflo, what Judy personally built around it, or how quickly the workaround changed the result. A difficult business is not itself proof of a relentlessly resourceful founder.

The artifact is more persuasive than the founder story. Tarifflo reports an ABI filer code, a direct CBP connection, more than 1 million products audited, and intelligence spanning over 10 million shipment records. These are concrete pre-round proof spikes of the kind I take seriously. They also distinguish Tarifflo from a classification demo constructed for an accelerator application. I discount them somewhat because they are company-reported and the ABI product remained in private beta in August 2026, but there is plainly a functioning technical and regulatory substrate here.

Orly is the narrow well. Tarifflo audited more than 10,000 items and reports that the work recovered $1.3 million, with Alba Wheels Up filing the petitions. That is much better than a waitlist or a compliment: a named importer obtained a measurable financial outcome. Public companies were reportedly signing onto the earlier classification product by February 2025 as well. What remains unknown is whether importers repeatedly use Tarifflo, whether the company was paid on those recoveries, and whether Alba or another broker—not Tarifflo—owns the durable customer relationship.

The economics contain both the opportunity and the trap. Free filing removes adoption friction, while contingency audits convert compliance work into an immediately legible promise: pay only when money comes back. But refunds can depend on 300-day correction windows, liquidation deadlines, and broker review. Tarifflo could therefore become a good customs-recovery practice with software inside it rather than a software company with compounding economics. My 36-month base case would be $3–7 million in annualized revenue, still concentrated in contingency recovery rather than a dominant filing platform.

The strongest argument against that cautious forecast is already in the dossier. A founder with direct domain exposure has assembled a difficult regulatory connection, a large shipment substrate, and a seven-figure customer outcome before the market has become tidy. That combination could overwhelm the missing narrative about blockers. It also fits the unstated pattern of unusually concrete proof preceding the round. Yet the known-exception logic does not fully rescue the investment case: working product can override conventional-looking red flags, but here the missing fact concerns my primary test of the founder, not press, age, or market respectability.

The partner red flag matters because Tarifflo routes filings through in-house or partner brokers, lets brokerage partners retain the filing relationship, and relied on Alba for the named petitions. Zonos, KlearNow, and Altana already possess customs or government integrations, so Tarifflo cannot win merely by having assembled one. I would not write the check today, though this is Neutral rather than a Pass. One verifiable fact would flip me: a dated account of a specific ABI, broker, or customer-acquisition blocker that Judy personally worked around within weeks, producing repeat paid use by at least three named importers.
