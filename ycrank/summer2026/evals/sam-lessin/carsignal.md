# CarSignal -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely the first scarce check. CarSignal may be cheap and ignored, but the dossier does not establish either. The same problem applies to the capital itself. There is no named experiment, deadline, or pass/fail milestone—only a broad operating system spanning reception, diagnostics, estimates, parts, scheduling, communication, and payments. That is a product roadmap, not yet a venture moment.

The strongest fact is the founders’ translation-layer position. Both reportedly grew up in mechanic shops, and they have shipped software now live across 14 states. The product preserves one vehicle record from the first call through payment, directly addressing a fragmented workflow they observed firsthand. But firsthand exposure is not the same as documented shop operation, and Michael Muzzin’s public record shows coursework rather than a prior deployment, exit, or scaled operating result. They understand both languages; I cannot yet prove they have operated both systems.

The bull case is that CarSignal has found a sensible wedge into a stubborn physical-world business. Its 1% fee on paid invoices aligns revenue with realized shop cash, caps the bill at $200 per month, and removes per-seat and feature-tier friction. Moreover, every shop that reportedly began migration completed it, and the product is deployed in 14 states. Those facts could describe an unusually effective workflow replacement hiding behind an undisclosed denominator.

But they do not yet establish the dark pool. CarSignal reports no paid-shop count, invoice volume, repeat usage, retention period, or causal evidence that its receptionist or diagnostic copilot creates incremental purchases. The $500 referral payment and Meta attribution suggest distribution is being rented or purchased, while PartsTech, Stripe, Plaid, and QuickBooks own important rails underneath the product. Repair histories could eventually create switching costs, but a possible future data asset earns no credit today—particularly when CarSignal says shared-model training requires consent.

The AI layer also fails the exception test. CarSignal explicitly warns that personnel must verify diagnoses, estimates, messages, and parts decisions because outputs may be inaccurate or outdated. That can be responsible product design, but it leaves the economics unresolved: how much labor is removed, what error burden remains, and whether the 1%-of-invoice model supports implementation and support. Ando overcame this kind of concern with measured accuracy, complete pilot-to-paid conversion, and retention; CarSignal offers migration completion with no denominator and no equivalent operating receipts.

My 36-month base case is that CarSignal either remains below $1 million ARR or narrows into an AI intake and estimating add-on rather than displacing established shop systems. The verifiable flip condition is ten named shops retained for six months, processing at least $10 million in aggregate invoices, with documented positive contribution margin and a single seed-funded milestone that does not require another round. Until then, I am being asked to finance several commoditizable features, an undefined experiment, and unknown round terms. I pass.
