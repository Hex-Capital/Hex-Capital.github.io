# Kebra -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Kebra’s implied truth is that the incumbent field-service record is too fragmented for the incumbent field-service system to automate the work: an independent agent must reconstruct reality from calls, photographs, equipment identifiers, and technician behavior. What would prove this wrong? If ServiceTitan, Jobber, or Housecall Pro can use their existing data and distribution to produce equally reliable post-job automation, the overlay becomes a feature rather than a company. The dossier never states that contested proposition explicitly, identifies why incumbents cannot act, or reports a result that could have falsified it. This is closer to a plausible observation than a demonstrated secret.

The best evidence is behavioral. Quality Air’s technicians and back office use Kebra for job capture and post-job automation, and deployments span Florida, Pennsylvania, and California. This matters more than 395 LinkedIn followers or nine Launch YC votes: geographically dispersed operators have permitted a two-person startup to touch claims, parts, invoices, and customer follow-up. Founder-led demonstrations and operator referrals therefore constitute an intelligible early distribution route, although the absence of pricing, conversion rates, sales-cycle length, and revenue prevents it from becoming a proven economic channel.

But distribution into a workflow is not ownership of a market. Kebra’s Company Brain may accumulate equipment histories, communications, images, audio, and repair decisions, yet there is no disclosed retention, exclusive data, dataset scale, or measured improvement with continued use. Meanwhile, three capitalized incumbents already bundle overlapping AI functions into installed systems; ServiceTitan alone reported $614 million in fiscal-2024 revenue. Writing into existing software reduces adoption friction, but it also leaves the system of record—and therefore the strategic customer relationship—with someone else. Kebra presently creates convenience inside another company’s monopoly.

The founders are technically credible for the product they have built. Luca Hadife worked on multimodal AI at Microsoft, while Mohamed Hosny taught computer architecture and signal processing at Berkeley and engineered data-center switches at Arista. Those facts explain the ability to combine voice, images, and computer-use agents; they do not establish the founding structure. The dossier does not confirm full-time status, prior work together, founder ownership, or whether all core engineering remains in-house. Their Berkeley overlap is not evidence of long alignment, so this gate remains unproved rather than failed.

The bull case is sharper than the score may suggest. Kebra could become the neutral operational layer across fragmented FSM systems, learning from the complete job rather than from whichever fragments one incumbent stores. Live usage at Quality Air and deployments in three states could be the beginning of proprietary context and integration switching costs. Yet the known exception for a genuine zero-to-one AI event does not apply: no independent accuracy, completion-rate, or labor-saving result distinguishes Kebra’s technology from the agents incumbents already advertise. The current pattern is the one I avoid—an application in a fashionable AI category facing several capable substitutes.

There is also no fund-return arithmetic. Public evidence supplies neither price nor revenue, gross margin, expansion per customer, or a bounded beachhead Kebra can dominate before moving outward; the $5.10 billion global FSM estimate describes the competitive category, not the company’s revenue engine. I pass. The verifiable flip condition is ten independent field-service companies retained for twelve months while Kebra documents that its accumulated Company Brain produces materially higher autonomous job-completion accuracy than each customer’s incumbent FSM tools. That would convert the story from overlay to proprietary learning system—from useful workflow to possible last mover.
