# Applied Kinetics -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The seven-week transformer is the interesting claim. Applied Kinetics says it can build and test equipment in seven weeks when the open market can take 65. Show me one order where that happened. Which approval threatened the schedule? What did Rohit or Adhik do about it? When did the customer accept the equipment? The [October 2026 launch](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/WJj-applied-kinetics-ai-native-manufacturing-for-data-center-and-energy-projects) supplies a capability claim, but no completed shipment tracing that sequence. I cannot yet distinguish founders who have overcome the bottleneck from founders who have identified it.

There are reasons to take the team seriously. Rohit worked on GPU telemetry at Crusoe, and Adhik built AI agents at IBM. They also built a hackathon project together before Applied Kinetics, so this is not a cofounder relationship with no shared execution. But those facts answer narrower questions than the one controlling my investment. They establish relevant technical exposure and some working history. They do not establish resourcefulness under a named constraint, a customer-driven change of direction, or a personal encounter with equipment procurement. I leave those judgments at their missing-evidence defaults.

The strongest argument for investing is the work Applied Kinetics proposes to absorb: approvals, testing, customs, freight, and service. A developer may care much more about getting an accepted transformer on site than about whether the coordination software is novel. Forty-plus partner plants could give two founders considerable reach. This is precisely the sort of ugly operational territory where a small company can discover something others overlook. The reported specification-review and production-allocation agents, together with the seven-week claim, earn limited technical credit as self-reported capabilities. They do not yet satisfy my recurring preference for a difficult substrate already assembled and demonstrated. Supplier access is not evidence that the founders can make those suppliers deliver.

Who urgently wants the crude version? Data center developers are a plausible niche, but the dossier names no buyer using it, ordering again, or paying. The manufacturing base’s **10+ GW** belongs to that base’s history; it is not Applied Kinetics’ delivery record. Project quotations could become a substantial business, but their value depends on margins, acceptance obligations, and repeat orders that are presently undisclosed. Separately licensable agents do not establish software demand either. Direct founder solicitation is sensible, and there is no evidence of the red flag where one large partnership substitutes for customer acquisition. Still, forty supplier relationships cannot answer the demand question.

I would pass today. This is an evidentiary pass, not a judgment that two founders should already have a long operating history. The usual exceptions would require an impressive working product, independently validated proof, or unmistakable customer pull; none is documented here strongly enough to replace the missing founder evidence. My one flip condition is a **customer-verified repeat-order case** documenting an initial seven-week build-and-test cycle, a specific delivery obstacle the founders personally overcame, and the customer’s subsequent paid order. That would connect the technical claim, founder behavior, and actual need. At present, Applied Kinetics has an attractive bottleneck and a proposed way through it. I would want to see the founders get through.
