# Derya -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the appealing picture is obvious: a hardware company describes what it needs, software selects suppliers and routes, prepares customs documents, and manages exceptions until delivery. Derya already touches much of that workflow. But the dossier does not explain the transitions from a two-person outsourced freight office to autonomous supply-chain infrastructure. The only bridge offered is a consensus forecast that agentic supply-chain software reaches $53 billion by 2030. That tells me what is fashionable now, not what Derya uniquely knows about what is coming.

The founders are the strongest part of this company. Oguzhan Karaca worked inside CEVA/CMA CGM, studied maritime and international-trade law, and previously built Ivy Consultants to six-figure ARR. Mert Turna built ship-operations software used on more than 30 vessels representing $200 million in fleet value. They have known each other for eight years, debated together, and already started a business together. This is not a pair that discovered logistics after seeing an AI market chart. Karaca understands the contractual machinery, Turna has shipped into the physical operating environment, and their skills are genuinely complementary.

The user behavior is meaningful even if it is still self-reported. Customers entrusted Derya with more than $3.2 million of freight opportunities in its first month, $80,000 became contracted revenue, and TVO signed for 20 containers. Containers are delightfully unforgiving artifacts: they cross borders, generate documents, incur storage charges, and expose weak operations quickly. A customer does not hand over 20 of them as casually as it joins a waitlist. Still, I cannot yet distinguish repeat product pull from founder-intensive service delivery because there are no cohorts, renewals, completed-shipment data, or gross margins.

Derya has found real rent in the system—hardware teams chasing brokers, quotes, components, customs paperwork, and manufacturers—but its claimed $1.3 trillion of inefficiency is too broad to reveal who captures that rent or which broken rule creates it. The flat monthly plan and absence of freight-rate markups are customer-friendly. They may also be economically dangerous: specialists execute variable work while Derya accepts operational and compliance exposure for a fixed fee. If every exception requires another human, the company has created a more transparent freight office, not an enabling layer beneath the industry.

The bull case stings because these founders may learn the hidden workflow faster than a software-only competitor precisely by doing the work themselves. Shipment histories and exception resolutions could become proprietary training material, while the initial revenue finances automation. But today that asset is hypothetical, Flexport and Forto already overlap with the service, and 5U AI is automating quoting, booking, tracking, and reconciliation inside existing forwarders. No structural barrier, controlled dataset, or scarce technical dependency is yet visible. My verifiable flip condition is straightforward: show that at least 70% of customers renew for six months while shipment volume grows at least threefold without proportional growth in human operating hours. That would prove Derya is converting operational tuition into software leverage. Until then, I am neutral rather than writing a personal check.
