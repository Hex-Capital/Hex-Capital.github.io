# Dock -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders are the asset; the current product is not yet the asset. Govind Kavaturi helped Branch grow from zero to $100 million in revenue, co-founded Thena, and has now built three companies alongside Mike Molinet. Molinet co-founded both Branch and Thena and operated across product, customer communication, international expansion, and go-to-market. That is costly, action-revealed knowledge of B2B coordination. It is adjacent to Dock’s problem, though the dossier does not show that either founder previously built, sold, or operated a multi-agent workspace specifically.

Dock has built a coherent control plane: shared documents and tables, separate agent credentials, workspace-scoped permissions, attributed history, REST/MCP access, and human approval gates. But run the copying test. Linear, Cursor, Lindy, Replit, Hex, and Relevance AI already possess products, distribution, or capital from which they can reproduce these features. The dossier identifies no proprietary model, scarce data, hardware, exclusive rights, or demonstrated workflow lock-in. Stored schemas and audit histories might eventually compound, but a possible future switching cost is not a present moat.

The strongest exception case is Notion: a strange, general-purpose workspace can precede an obvious category and observable distribution loop. Dock shares the broad artifact layer and a potentially early market. The material difference is that Dock enters after powerful incumbents have already assembled adjacent agent surfaces, while its public evidence shows one GitHub follower, one star on its leading visible repository, and no usage or retention signal demonstrating a product language users insist upon. Experienced founders can navigate a pivot; they cannot make copyable software durable by declaration.

The $5.3 billion enterprise-agentic-AI market projected for 2026, growing toward $24.5 billion by 2030, supplies room to maneuver. Yet Dock has not named a sharp initial buyer or shown how shared artifacts carry it causally into adjacent transactions. Its agency program and self-serve MCP/CLI onboarding distribute software, but they do not form a network: no cohort, referral, contribution, or liquidity evidence shows that one organization makes Dock better for the next. This is collaboration inside a tenant, not permissionless leverage across tenants.

Pricing at $19 and $49 per organization per month proves that value capture is intended, not that it has occurred. No payer, completed transaction, or recurring revenue is reported. Low prices may accelerate experimentation, but they also leave little room for the security work enterprise adoption requires: Dock lacks SOC 2 Type II and a third-party penetration test, while webhook secrets and browser-session tokens remain recoverably or plainly stored server-side. These are product-stage liabilities, not evidence of founder misconduct; the dossier reveals none of the integrity, emotional-dependence, or artificial-deadline red flags that would create a founder veto.

Within 36 months, I expect this team to pivot Dock from a horizontal multiplayer-agent workspace into a narrower AI customer-operations product and remain below $3 million ARR. The founders are capable enough to find demand, but the present system does not accumulate value while they sleep. I pass on this version. The verifiable flip condition is six months of retention from at least 20 paying organizations, accompanied by evidence that contributed workflows or agent histories measurably improve another user’s activation or output and cannot be exported to a copied interface without losing that advantage.
