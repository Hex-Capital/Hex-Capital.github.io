# Ailit -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The question I would ask Ailit’s founders is: what broke while building the first locomotive interface, and what did you do about it? The dossier does not answer that. It says the full-scale prototype is under construction, with deployment targeted for summer 2027. That is a schedule. I want the incident that tells me how these people behave when the schedule becomes impossible. There is no documented blocker, shipped workaround, or elapsed time here. I cannot infer those things from their degrees or their financing.

Emil Tagesson is the strongest reason to keep looking. His battery work at Koenigsegg and reported experience taking Candela’s electric ferry through certification and production are relevant to a machine that must work outside a laboratory. The proposed architecture also has a sensible starting point: use existing diesel-electric locomotives, supply electricity from a battery wagon, and retain diesel backup. But Ailit has not yet supplied a measured result from that system. Dayton-Phoenix already reports locomotive integration testing of its own modular battery tender. That makes Ailit’s integration problem look tractable, while making the basic architecture less distinctive. A competing machine’s test cannot establish that these founders have built theirs.

The strongest case for investing is that Tagesson knows how to cross the distance between an electric vehicle design and certified production, and Ailit is willing to own the unpleasant operational work around the vehicle. Batteries, charging sites, exchanges, and departure coordination are exactly the sort of burdens that can conceal an opportunity. Yet prior employment does not establish that the founders discovered this particular problem through their own work. Nor does retaining diesel backup demonstrate an evidence-driven product revision; the dossier gives no earlier hypothesis or observation that caused the change. There is relevant experience here, but no documented sequence of learning.

The commercial proposition makes the operational test unusually important. Ailit advertises energy priced 20% below diesel with no equipment purchase upfront. Someone still buys the equipment: Ailit. The company must pay for batteries and swapping infrastructure while keeping charged packs available when trains arrive. The first useful customer would therefore be a railroad with a specific route and enough repeated operation to expose both availability and cost. The dossier identifies no such customer, paid deployment, or repeat prototype use. At least SEK10M in previous financing and the separately reported $500K YC investment buy time to run that experiment. Trade coverage and conference appearances do not answer it.

I would pass with my own money today. Ailit fits my preference for founders tackling neglected operational difficulty, but it has not yet shown the concrete proof that makes unfamiliar industrial bets attractive. The disclosed hiring plan involves railroad and infrastructure relationships; it does not establish dependence on one indispensable partner, so I would not invent that red flag. Equally, none of the working-product, unmistakable-user-pull, or independently validated-proof exceptions is present to override the missing founder evidence. My one condition for reopening the decision is a dated customer operating record showing how the founders resolved a named deployment blocker and then completed repeated paid battery exchanges, with measured availability and fully loaded energy-delivery cost. That would turn several promises into something I could inspect.
