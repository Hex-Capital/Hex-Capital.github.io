# Bloomy -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Alex Southmayd taught seventh grade before building Bloomy, but Bloomy’s weakest signal is the absence of an ugly technical or regulatory swing: this is lived-experience mastery software, not a rocket hiding inside an education wrapper. Southmayd’s classroom, test-prep, and acquisition experience gives Bloomy an unusually authentic origin; the McKinsey-and-Stanford polish does not erase the years spent teaching children. The analytical signature is strong founder-problem contact paired with a category that already looks legible, funded, and crowded.

Niantic is the high rung because John Hanke lived location technology for a decade before Pokémon Go materialized; the biography and the product were one continuous sentence. Southmayd’s Teach For America classroom and Manhattan Prep instruction put Bloomy meaningfully near that rung: Bloomy’s prerequisite graph and 90% mastery gate address problems he personally encountered rather than a market discovered through his Stanford MBA. The divergence matters, though. Hanke had already shipped Keyhole and Google Earth at scale, while Bloomy’s dossier shows no comparable prior product or technical system built by Southmayd. Biz is evident, Tiz is not yet proven, and the McKinsey credential is a red flag I cannot simply perfume away.

Notion’s low-rung mechanism on frontier risk is pure-software replicability: a useful artifact can become enormous, but regulation and capital intensity do not protect the early product. Bloomy shares that exposure because Khanmigo, DreamBox, and Amira can reproduce overlapping tutoring and adaptive-learning features, while Bloomy has no structural barrier or exclusive dataset today. Child-data obligations and the promise not to let third-party AI providers train on identifiable student information add an unsexy compliance layer, but Bloomy remains much closer to classroom SaaS than to SpaceX hardware or Mindbloom’s clinical-regulatory gauntlet.

Khanmigo’s 2.0 million users reveal how Bloomy’s non-consensus score could decay over the next 18 months: every improvement in foundation models makes Socratic tutoring cheaper, while incumbents compound distribution through existing districts. Bloomy’s defense must therefore come from the 1,200-plus-skill prerequisite graph, mastery assessments with the tutor locked, and longitudinal misconception data—not from “AI-powered learning.” Bloomy is moderately weird in its refusal to let children advance before reaching 90% mastery, but the category itself is consensus. I see no evidence that sophisticated investors have rejected Bloomy for being aesthetically offensive or impossibly hard; this is a rebuttal, not a hard no.

Bloomy’s team size of one gives Southmayd de facto tiebreaker authority, which is cleaner than the HQ Trivia or GameCrush pattern of founders and investors fighting over product DNA. Bloomy would move to the top governance tier if Southmayd were explicitly named CEO with documented founder vesting and an operating protocol for the founding engineer receiving 1.5%–2.0% equity. Bloomy would fall several tiers if that hire became an equal co-founder without a clear decision structure. For now, one founder means one steering wheel, even if the corporate paperwork is invisible.

HQ Trivia showed that a product children—or adults—voluntarily return to can temporarily overpower other weaknesses. Bloomy has the beginnings of that tactile signal: more than 90% of surveyed pilot students requested continued access, 20% used Bloomy outside required school time, and teachers at Baystate Academy, Edna M. Scott Elementary, and Unbound Stockton can observe the learning loop directly. Bloomy is not a developer abstraction; a child can use it and a teacher can watch the result. Nearly twice projected NWEA MAP growth is intriguing, but Bloomy’s own correlational, limited-sample caveat keeps this well below HQ Trivia-level felt virality.

Three named school deployments lead me to predict that by July 2029 Bloomy will remain an independent, school-focused mastery platform with $3 million–$7 million in ARR, concentrated among charter schools, districts, and ESA-funded families—not a breakout general-purpose AI tutor and not a dead product. Bloomy’s founder-problem fit should sustain careful curriculum iteration and school trust, while Bloomy’s low frontier moat and Khanmigo’s installed base should cap explosive consumer distribution. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing: the bet is not on a chatbot, but on whether a former teacher can turn a mastery ritual into institutional habit.

Khanmigo’s 770,000 students reached through U.S. district partnerships make the strongest bear case sting: Bloomy could become a feature, its pilot lift could disappear under controlled measurement, and its interaction data may never become proprietary enough to matter. The DeepMind exception does not rescue Bloomy merely because Southmayd has Stanford and McKinsey credentials; Bloomy’s dossier does not yet show polymath technical compounding. The facts that win today are the 90% continuation request, 20% voluntary usage, three named schools, and a founder who actually taught the children Bloomy serves. I would invest a measured personal check, but I would flip to Pass if twelve-month cohort data showed no statistically credible NWEA advantage and fewer than two of the three named schools renewed.
