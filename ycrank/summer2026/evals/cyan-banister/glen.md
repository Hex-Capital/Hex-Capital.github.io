# Glen -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. An organization’s agents should inherit not only its files but why decisions were made, which approaches failed, and which recurring methods deserve to become executable skills. Glen supplies a plausible path: capture work from Slack, GitHub, tickets, calls, and agent sessions; assemble a decision history; inject the relevant pieces into later prompts; distill repetition into skills. That is more specific than “enterprise AI,” and the production product is a small leak from that future. But the dossier does not establish how these steps compound, what remains technically difficult, or why Glen—not an agent platform—will own the resulting memory.

Nikos Dritsakos is the strongest part of the bet. He worked inside Composio, operated as FliteHouse’s VP of Technology, and built and sold SalesBop for $500,000 after roughly eighteen months. That is real Rizz–Tizz–Biz evidence: he understands agent integrations, has shipped B2B software, and has converted a product into an exit. The solo-founder structure also gives me one accountable decision-maker rather than a governance puzzle. What is missing is evidence that organizational memory was a wound he pursued before Glen: no dated experiments, public technical artifacts, or recruiting proof show a long, independently derived obsession.

The system complaint is credible but incomplete. Decisions are fragmented across tools and separated from their rationale; Glen reconnects them. Yet who captures the rent today? What delay, duplicated labor, or failed decision costs a customer enough to switch? Whose agency is being returned rather than merely whose retrieval is improved? Without a measured cost or institutional gatekeeper, this remains a useful workflow repair, not yet a decoded broken rule.

Three named testimonials—from Amorphic Labs, Zaplar, and Composio—plus production access through wave-based onboarding show that the artifact exists. They do not show the behavior I need. There is no paid contract, renewal, usage cohort, corpus size, or example of a team changing how it works because Glen recalled something indispensable. The bullish case stings: Dritsakos could use his Composio relationships and 5,376-person LinkedIn audience to seed exactly the organization-specific histories that become hard to remove. Dust’s 3,000 organizations, 51,000 monthly active users, and 300,000 agents prove that shared agent context is becoming real. But those same figures also show that Glen is entering an occupied system before demonstrating unusual user pull.

The enabling-layer story is therefore aspiration, not control. Glen sits beneath workplace agents, but Glean, Dust, Zep, Mem0, and model providers can build against the same integrations and MCP interfaces. No proprietary corpus, retrieval advantage, or scarce technical constraint is documented. More seriously, the product’s value rises with the breadth of prompts, conversations, and observations it stores, while private channels and private mode create necessary holes in that context. Row-level isolation, access controls, encryption, source links, and a no-training policy are sensible protections, but passing customer content through OpenAI, Vercel, Neon, and Inngest keeps consent and custody near my hardest gate.

My base case is that within 36 months Glen either becomes a sub-$2 million ARR agent-context product acquired by Composio or narrows into an agent-skill and observability tool; the founder is credible enough to produce an outcome, but the current evidence does not identify a standalone bottleneck. I am neutral today. One verifiable condition flips me to invest: three named design partners renew paid annual contracts after at least 90 days while more than half of their eligible users or agents use Glen weekly. That would show both costly behavior and an organization-specific asset that competitors cannot reproduce merely by adding another connector.
