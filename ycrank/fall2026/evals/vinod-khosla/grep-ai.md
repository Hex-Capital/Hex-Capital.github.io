# Grep AI -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Grep AI claims 150,000 hours saved in six months. That is a useful starting quantity. What fraction of the underlying work disappeared, and can the mechanism repeat across customers? The dossier does not establish either. Financial compliance is an expensive expertise bottleneck, and eliminating manual investigation could expand access to financial services. But the company-wide savings claim spans product generations; it does not quantify the cost pool Grep’s current product can replace. I see evidence of useful automation, with the orders-of-magnitude consequence still unproven.

The technical idea deserves attention: convert repeated reasoning into executable procedures and reserve expensive inference for exceptions. If that works reliably, usage should make the product cheaper. [IG’s deployment](https://grep.ai/case-studies/ig) supplies concrete evidence of customer value: flagged-customer activation fell from two or three days to under ten minutes, automated screening increased from 10% to 30%, and reported review accuracy exceeded 99%. But elapsed activation time includes queues and handoffs. It does not establish a comparable improvement in computational efficiency or investigator labor. Nor does this legacy deployment isolate the benefit of automatic procedure generation. Against n8n, LangGraph, and general agents, Grep still lacks a controlled benchmark showing what its learning mechanism uniquely buys.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ response to reality. Asver and Ríos encountered the problem inside Brex, operated compliance agents through Parcha, and now name customers including Airwallex and Worldpay. Asver’s account of redesigning the harness after nearly $80,000 in monthly AI spending is evidence of changing the implementation when economics contradicted the plan. That matters more than prestigious résumés. My broader investment pattern includes execution-heavy financial infrastructure; scientific novelty is not an absolute requirement. The exception would be a team whose evolving wedge opens an enormous existing value pool. Grep has the domain experience and some evidence of adaptation, but the dossier does not establish that expansion mechanism or difficult complementary recruiting. The exception remains plausible rather than earned.

Months of shadow testing at IG, followed by governance approval and continued human oversight for higher-risk individuals, are meaningful risk removal. The team tested before granting autonomy. However, aggregate accuracy above 99% leaves the decisive question open: what happens to rare, costly exceptions when learned procedures replace reasoning? The dossier supplies no false-negative breakdown, explicit acceptance threshold, or experiment budget. Regulated deployment fits my willingness to tolerate difficult adoption cycles, but customer governance approval does not establish a permission barrier or a proprietary scientific learning loop. Likewise, calling this an ordinary model wrapper would overstate the evidence; the procedure-generation claim deserves a test. It has not yet earned technical-discontinuity credit.

I would pass with my own money today. The economic concern is specific: Grep sells $200 and $500 monthly subscriptions, while task-level inference cost, human-review burden, and current margins remain undisclosed. Historical loss-making runs do not prove permanent subsidy dependence, but the harness redesign does not prove competitive unit economics either. SAP’s integration of n8n creates additional pressure on any advantage based mainly on orchestration. **My flip condition is one independently auditable customer trial showing that Grep’s automatically generated procedures deliver at least 10x lower fully loaded cost per correctly resolved case than the customer’s existing process, on held-out exceptions, without increasing material compliance misses.** That single result would connect the claimed learning mechanism to reliable, unsubsidized expertise substitution.
