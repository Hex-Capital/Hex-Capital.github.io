# Boat -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Agent developers are becoming buyers of persistent computers, and Boat’s opportunity depends on whether managing those computers creates enough value to sustain attractive margins as compute prices fall. The underlying problem is concrete: long-running agents need browsers, development tools, and environments that survive between tasks. Boat supplies that infrastructure. The market thesis is credible, but the controlling variable remains only partially established: how much economic value Boat retains per recurring workload after paying for the infrastructure.

The published pricing makes adoption plausible. Boat quotes $0.036 per default VM-hour against $0.331 for E2B and Daytona—roughly a ninefold difference. Those are vendor-authored comparisons without demonstrated resource equivalence, so I would not underwrite a ninefold economic advantage. Google’s May 2026 description of agents requiring execution environments supports the demand shift. Neither fact establishes Boat’s margins. Dedicated AMD Ryzen hosts could support efficient delivery, but a cheap selling price becomes an investment advantage only when the cost structure supports it.

The strongest evidence is that customers are already paying. The founders reported more than 1,000 active paying users on October 24 and six-figure ARR during October. That is meaningful pull for this stage, discounted because both claims come from the company. I would reconcile the figures before treating either as a clean revenue baseline: 1,000 customers continuously paying the published $20 monthly minimum would imply at least $240,000 in annualized subscription billings. “Six-figure ARR” accommodates that number, but the definitions and timing matter. Boat has demonstrated willingness to pay; recurring expansion remains unmeasured.

OpenInspect’s October 23 integration is more strategically interesting than the Product Hunt ranking. Tunnel URLs, prebuilt images, and filesystem snapshots place Boat inside another product’s execution path. Better models could increase the amount of work passing through that integration, while persistent environments give customers something to preserve between runs. This is a real operational layer. However, Boat has not demonstrated that it controls enough workflow state or distribution to resist substitution. Google already offers provisioning and snapshots, and Modal has launched overlapping VM sandboxes.

That competitive structure is the strongest objection to writing a check. My pattern of investing across a technology discontinuity supports examining Boat, but this market is already crystallizing around well-funded infrastructure providers. Modal’s reported billion-dollar annualized run rate covers its broader platform; it does not establish comparable sandbox revenue. It does establish an incumbent with substantial distribution. The relevant red flag is commodity compute economics presented as a software opportunity. Boat has not proved that concern applies, but its pricing alone cannot resolve it. The rare research-team exception offers no separate underwriting basis here.

The founders provide a useful execution signal: Nougaret reports building cloud coding agents before extracting their infrastructure into this product. They encountered the workload themselves and shipped the underlying service. That earns credit for direct problem experience, without inferring exceptional motivation from prior employers. I would monitor Boat rather than invest today. One verifiable flip condition would be a six-month customer cohort showing at least 120% net revenue retention with positive contribution margin after compute, storage, and support. That would connect today’s paid demand to a business whose economics improve as customers do more work.
