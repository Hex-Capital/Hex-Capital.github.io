# Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The contested truth is precise: radio imaging can produce a millimeter-accurate, scale-native map of concealed structures from across a room. The claim is falsifiable—blind field tests across sheetrock, tile, marble, quartz, brick, and insulation should either reproduce 1 mm accuracy or destroy it. This is more substantive than a fashionable hardware slogan, but Applied Electrodynamics has published neither an independent benchmark nor a specification sheet. The secret remains an assertion by unusually credible inventors, not yet a discovered fact.

The team is the strongest evidence. Bilgehan Avser led antenna technology across four iPhone generations; Brian Huppi worked on iPhone multitouch and the first Nest thermostat; Paul Leutheuser shipped Apple hardware; George McLean worked across semiconductor imagers, retinal implants, and optical sensors. More importantly, the four founders report eight years of shared work and multiple shipped products. This is an assembled operating unit, not a networking-event company. Yet the dossier does not establish every founder’s full-time status—McLean still lists himself as self-employed—or disclose ownership and economic exposure. Strong foundations are visible; complete alignment is not.

The proposed construction beachhead is real but not yet monopolistic. Contractors suffer costly rework, and a scanner operating from across the room would be categorically different from moving a $199.95 Walabot against the wall. But Vayyar already commercializes RF imaging, Camero-Tech sells tactical through-wall systems, and Applied Electrodynamics has not shown that its algorithms, hardware, or cited 250-plus team patents belong to a defensible corporate position. Technical complexity is not proprietary technology. Until a blind test demonstrates the claimed precision under field conditions, this is a feature advantage surrounded by well-financed substitutes.

The strongest bull case is that conventional pre-seed evidence understates a veteran hardware team. These founders may already possess the tacit knowledge required to cross a threshold competitors missed; preorder registration, hundreds of Build Expo conversations, and outreach at BuiltWorlds could be the first signs of an overlooked construction wedge. That is also the relevant exception to my usual suspicion of coordinated hard technology: an independently validated unit can collapse several risks at once. But credentials cannot perform that collapse. Today validation, manufacturability, pricing, and customer acquisition remain separate unknowns.

The economics are weaker than the technology story. The cited global through-wall radar market was only $134 million in 2025, no unit price or gross margin is disclosed, and the company simultaneously seeks construction, industrial-inspection, and defense introductions. Those are not one distribution route; they are three different buying systems with different validation requirements. Conference conversations and a preorder form demonstrate interest, not a repeatable channel. Expanding into cargo screening or defense before dominating construction would turn a definite product into an indefinite portfolio of applications.

I would investigate, not invest. Applied Electrodynamics has the kind of long-aligned operator network I prefer, but it also exhibits the hard-technology red flag of requiring performance validation, manufacturing, and multiple sales systems to mature together. The verifiable flip condition is narrow: an independent blind trial showing approximately 1 mm accuracy from room distance across at least three common wall assemblies, followed by ten paid deployments with general contractors through one repeatable sales motion. That would convert the secret from testimony into truth and reveal whether the initial market can be owned.
