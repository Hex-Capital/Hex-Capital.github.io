# Prized -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, employees do not file engineering tickets for every operational edge case. They create disposable software against company data, while permissions, credentials, and auditability live beneath the generated application. That future is plausible. Prized’s present-day leak is employees already connecting Claude Code and Cursor directly to corporate systems because spreadsheets and engineering queues are insufficient. But the dossier does not map the intervening transitions—how administrators delegate safely, how generated tools survive organizational change, or why Prized becomes the control plane. I can see the destination more clearly than I can see this company owning the road.

The broken system is specific enough to matter. Ops, support, finance, and RevOps workers lack the authority to repair their own workflows, yet handing them production credentials creates an unacceptable security trade. Prized returns some agency through administrator-approved connectors, per-tool database roles, brokered secrets, outbound-host allowlists, and audit logs. Those are thoughtful operating details, not “Lovable for internal tools” decoration. Still, the dossier never quantifies the engineering wait, SaaS replacement cost, or administrative burden, and adoption continues to require both the frustrated employee and the security owner to say yes.

Hudson Griffith and Marinos Eliades have real building credibility. They worked together on fleet-management software at Gander before its acquisition; Griffith was its founding engineer and later worked at Suno. The 10 hand-delivered toolboxes—with target-specific software, QR codes, and several hundred fortune cookies—show hustle and a willingness to make a strange artifact rather than send another outbound email. What is missing is the first-believer story: no dated pre-company obsession with enterprise permissions, no customer wound they personally carried for years, and no evidence yet that they recruited a user into behavior the founders did not manufacture themselves.

The strongest bull case is that the infrastructure is already more serious than the traction suggests. Read-only integrations by default, administrator approval for risky migrations, and credentials kept outside both code and agent context directly address the production-data danger. A working product plus security-aware founders can precede revenue at pre-seed. The problem is that Superblocks already offers natural-language internal-app generation with centralized permissions, auditing, policy enforcement, private-cloud deployment, and more than 50 integrations. Prized currently controls a careful implementation, not a scarce bottleneck.

The privacy red flag is mitigated rather than ignored: Prized has an explicit limiting architecture around data access. Governance is less clear. Griffith is identified as CEO, but the dossier gives no ownership or contractual-tiebreaker information for the two-person founding team; I will not manufacture a 50/50 problem from silence, though I would verify it before signing. More important, no user has yet paid, repeatedly built tools, enabled write access, or entrusted a consequential workflow to the system. Ten delivered boxes are founder behavior, not costly user behavior.

My working forecast is that within 36 months Prized pivots from a broad non-engineer app builder into the governance and credential-brokering layer for agent-generated internal software, reaching roughly $1–3 million ARR—or disappears inside the crowded builder category. I am neutral today because the most differentiated thing is underneath the advertised product, while the least evidenced thing is whether anybody wants to inhabit it. One condition flips me to invest: a named customer renews a $100,000-plus annual contract after non-engineering employees have run production workflows through Prized for six months without engineers rebuilding those tools.
