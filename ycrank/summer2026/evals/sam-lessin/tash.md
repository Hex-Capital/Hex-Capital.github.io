# tash -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I first write down the deal: post-money unknown, competing-term-sheet count unknown, and no evidence this is genuinely the first scarce check. That is not neutral for me. The dossier also never says what the round buys beyond launching regulated portfolios. SEC qualification is pending, but tash has not converted that dependency into a dated experiment—how much capital, which first index, how many subscribed dollars, and what result would prove that investors want portfolio exposure rather than individual cards. I am being asked to finance a process, not yet a truth-revealing venture moment.

The strongest evidence is the prior exchange product: more than $22,000 of volume across 710 trades and 250-plus listed cards. Combined with over $1 million of cards and collectibles personally transacted by the founders, this is more than résumé fit. The team has operated inside card sourcing, pricing, and exchange behavior, while UBS, Houlihan Lokey, J.P. Morgan, and the broker-dealer relationships provide access to the financial side. But those receipts belong to a whole-card marketplace, not the proposed security. They prove that the founders can cause card transactions; they do not prove demand for a managed card index.

That distinction matters because the dark pool is still hypothetical. Proprietary sourcing relationships, performance data, and liquidity could compound, but none is documented today. Rally, Collectable, Alt, and Dibbs already occupy issuance, custody, valuation, and trading, with collectively substantial funding. Tash’s portfolio structure is different, but product geometry is not defensibility. Worse, its advertised CL50 history uses interpolated intermediate points while the final Card Ladder series remains unfinished. In an investment product, trusted measurement is part of the product, not marketing polish to repair later.

The bull case is real: four founders with years of card-market activity have already shipped an exchange, generated hundreds of transactions, and recruited Rialto Markets and North Capital Private Securities to cross the regulatory boundary. If qualification unlocks a diversified product that individual-asset platforms cannot offer, the old exchange activity could become the seed of proprietary sourcing and pricing data. That is the best argument against my pass. It does not reach the exceptional-evidence threshold, however: there is no current offering, subscribed capital, purchase conversion, revenue, fee schedule, gross margin, burn, or evidence that secondary liquidity survives after launch. Collectable’s halted secondary trading is a particularly ugly counterfactual.

Deleting the next financing makes the problem clearer. Tash must acquire inventory, maintain custody, support regulated vehicles, and create two-sided liquidity, yet the dossier supplies no controllable cash metric or path to break-even. Four co-founders are named without identifying who has final authority over product, regulation, or financing—exactly when the business needs singular decisions under pressure. My 36-month base case is that tash abandons regulated multi-card securities and returns to a conventional whole-card marketplace, brokerage, or portfolio-tracking product rather than reaching $1 million in annualized fee revenue.

I would flip on one verifiable result: a qualified first portfolio that attracts at least $500,000 from paying investors, produces repeat secondary trades for six months, and discloses positive contribution economics. That would show the regulated index—not the founders’ prior marketplace—is the asset customers actually purchase. Without it, I am investing in an elaborate wrapper around unproven demand at an undisclosed price. Pass.
