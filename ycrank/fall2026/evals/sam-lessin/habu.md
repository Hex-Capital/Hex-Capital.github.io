# HABU -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

HABU’s potentially interesting asset is the developer collecting the fee. Owning the workflow could let the company capture automation savings directly instead of selling another software subscription to somebody who captures them. But what am I paying for that possibility? The dossier supplies no post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or evidence that this is the first scarce check. YC, TipTop VC, and Copper Sky Capital appearing on the website establishes backing, not an auction or an attractive entry price. My price gate remains unresolved.

The founders give me a reason to take the operating model seriously. Bryan Tharalson has managed more than $80 million in development projects and was Mutiny’s first sales hire; those are relevant experiences for joining property execution with technology commercialization. Junhao Chen brings Meta AI operations and Navy logistics experience. This fits my pattern of backing people who translate between software and difficult physical operations. The limit is specific: the dossier does not document a software deployment or completed HABU project demonstrating that this team has successfully joined those systems. I give the operating experience credit without treating the claimed bridge as already built.

The strongest bull case is that HABU needs surprisingly little corporate capital. Investors supply project equity, HABU earns development fees, and internal automation expands the spread between fees and delivery costs. The website’s request for $5 million–$20 million equity checks concerns property projects; those amounts should not be mistaken for HABU’s startup financing requirement. A profitable Phoenix developer could be an excellent investment without becoming a global software monopoly. That possibility matters. But no disclosed fee schedule, delivery margin, burn, or signed mandate establishes that the business can support itself. The operating model permits capital efficiency; it does not yet demonstrate it.

What exact truth does my check reveal? HABU claims more than 90% task automation, five times more projects per employee, and 10%–15% lower project costs. Those claims have no supporting project-level results in the dossier. Faster desk work becomes an economic advantage only if it reduces fully loaded delivery costs or generates additional collectible fees despite permitting and construction constraints. TestFit already sells overlapping feasibility automation, so access to automation alone offers limited protection. HABU could develop a proprietary pool of investor relationships and project outcomes, but an inquiry form and 271 LinkedIn followers do not demonstrate customer action. Nor do anticipated hires identify a funded experiment with a deadline and a pass/fail result.

I pass at this evidence level. The favorable physical-operations pattern is real, while the AI performance claims remain unverified; there is no evidence of an auction, equal-founder deadlock, or financing dependency to justify stronger adverse conclusions. My exceptions require something concrete to overcome unresolved gates—paid conversion, demonstrated economics, or an explicit capital structure and use plan. HABU supplies none of those yet. One verifiable condition would reopen my underwriting: an executed, paid investor mandate whose contracted fees cover the fully loaded cost of delivery. That would establish a functioning base business beneath the automation option. Price would still determine whether I buy it.
