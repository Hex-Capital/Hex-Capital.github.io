# LATO -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: an investment firm does not commission a new commercial study for every deal. It maintains a living market model, continuously refreshed by expert conversations, portfolio-company data, and observed outcomes; partners test pricing, competition, and customer behavior before committing capital. LATO has one artifact leaking from that future—a functioning voice agent interviewed 30 recruiters—but the causal chain stops there. The dossier provides no measured forecast accuracy, reusable corpus, or evidence that simulations improve after reality arrives. Today it is an automated study; the six-year claim is an unverified simulation layer.

The present-day leak is expensive enough to matter. Traditional outreach takes weeks, studies cost more than $300,000, and the knowledge is discarded when the next engagement begins. Expert networks and consultants capture that rent while investors surrender time and analytical control. LATO returns some agency by sourcing experts, conducting multilingual interviews, and combining the results with fund-owned data. Blume Equity and FoodLabs being identified as customers makes this more than a diagram. This broken-rule analysis is the strongest part of the company.

The founders fit the workflow better than most teams entering fashionable financial AI. Tymek Staniszewski spent six years inside Verdane and should understand how diligence is purchased and used; Tien Chu brings eight years of software and machine-learning work plus published deep-network research. Their eleven-year relationship is reassuring, although the dossier does not disclose ownership or a contractual tiebreaker. What is missing is pre-company obsession. LATO moved from an Excel model checker in April 2026 to interviews and simulations by July. That may be excellent learning speed, but it may also mean the team is still searching for the largest story around a useful agent.

There is real product behavior, but not yet weird or costly user behavior. The product completed a 30-person recruiter study, research reportedly represents 70% of use cases, and the earlier tool found a claimed $67.9 million valuation error. I cannot tell whether either named customer paid, renewed, contributed proprietary data, or changed an investment decision because of a simulation. Those distinctions matter because AlphaSense already has more than 280,000 interviews, AI-led calls, and over $600 million in ARR. LATO cannot win merely by assembling the same workflow more elegantly.

The sharp bull case is that each fund’s private data and completed interviews compound into a market model that an incumbent transcript library cannot reproduce. That would turn founder-market fit and early customer access into a controlled data loop. But no corpus size, retention, simulation benchmark, or feedback mechanism is disclosed, while SOC 2 and ISO 27001 remain in progress and the expert-call MNPI process is undescribed. A system ingesting fund data and primary interviews must make consent, custody, and compliance part of the product architecture. Here they remain promises surrounding a thin orchestration layer.

My 36-month forecast is that LATO narrows into a private-equity diligence copilot with less than $5 million in ARR by August 2029, rather than becoming an independent market-simulation platform. I pass today. One result flips me: five named investment firms renewing annual paid contracts after using prospectively recorded LATO simulations whose predictions are later scored against actual market outcomes. That would demonstrate repeat behavior, validation, and the beginnings of a proprietary loop in one stroke.
