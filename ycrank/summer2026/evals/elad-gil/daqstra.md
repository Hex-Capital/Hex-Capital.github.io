# Daqstra -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: hardware teams now run increasingly complex, multi-vendor test environments, and Daqstra becomes large only if replacing manual integration with a shared control plane saves enough engineering time to make it the standard system for executing—not merely observing—physical tests. The controlling variable is expansion within each customer: whether one successful test-stand deployment propagates across additional stands, programs, and engineering teams. The signed energy pilot targeting more than $100,000 in annual savings makes that belief testable, but the dossier provides neither a deployment-expansion rate nor a savings threshold tied to pricing.

The product is materially deeper than an AI wrapper. Daqstra connects NI, Siemens, Modbus, OPC-UA, and custom-TCP systems, then retains version-controlled sequences, redlines, abort logic, and run context. Live static-fire campaigns show that this architecture survives contact with physical hardware. Better models could improve procedure generation or anomaly detection without absorbing the permissions, integrations, calibration history, and responsibility for safely commanding equipment. This owned operational loop is the strongest part of the company.

Demand evidence is promising but still company-reported. Active aerospace programs use the system daily, multiple rocket-engine stands have deployed it, and an industrial-energy customer signed a pilot. The March 2026 SEDS UCSD–Dispatch static fire is a verifiable implementation, although it does not establish commercial retention. For a Summer 2026 company this is meaningful product pull, but there is no disclosed revenue, renewal, paid expansion, or named commercial customer proving that Daqstra has crossed from useful engineering project to repeatable enterprise product.

The weak link is the market discontinuity. A $48.65 billion test-and-measurement market growing 6.4% annually establishes spending, not a new margin structure. The asserted move toward continuous, AI-ready testing is inferred from competitor activity, while Nominal has raised $182.5 million and Sift $67 million to pursue overlapping layers. Daqstra may have found a sharper control surface, but the evidence does not yet show that technology has suddenly lowered deployment costs, accelerated adoption, or expanded software margins. Supporting heterogeneous, safety-critical equipment could instead make every contract integration-heavy.

The founders have unusually direct domain experience: they built and tested liquid rocket engines together, co-authored a 2025 AIAA paper on distributed acquisition, and Anchit Kumar participated in more than ten Mojave campaigns. That explains why the product can command real hardware. It does not yet demonstrate second-time-founder learning or the costly commitment that distinguishes necessity from an expert-led project, so founder-market fit earns credit without overriding the market gate.

The bull case is that safety validation and protocol breadth create compounding workflow state before better-funded competitors reach the command layer. The bear case stings more: Nominal or Sift can add sequencing, while Daqstra accumulates bespoke validation work and service economics. None of the known exceptions applies yet—this is neither a newly forming frontier oligopoly nor documented reconstruction of physical-industry margins. I would monitor rather than invest. The verifiable flip condition is three commercial customers each expanding from one deployment to at least three test stands within twelve months, with disclosed recurring software revenue and gross margins above 70%.
