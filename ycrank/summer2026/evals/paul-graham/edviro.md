# Edviro -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The useful fact is not that buildings waste energy. Everyone in this market knows that. It is that Hursh Shah found roughly $360,000 of utility overbilling while working with a school, then turned that discovery into software operating at seven school sites. In three months Edviro reached $20,000 ARR. That is a real sequence: collide with an ugly problem, build something, and get institutions to run it. The dossier does not reveal the precise obstacle, workaround, and elapsed time for each deployment, so I cannot give the founders full resourcefulness credit. But they have done considerably more than produce an energy-management demo.

Schools form a good narrow well. They have constrained budgets, fragmented systems, and enough deferred facilities work for waste to hide in plain sight. Seven live sites, 29 described as expanding, and a construction-company measurement-and-verification pilot are more informative than the dozens of demonstrations. The claimed $400,000 in savings would be exceptional relative to $20,000 ARR, but it is still an aggregate company claim without named case studies or a published baseline. I believe the usage and revenue. I treat the savings magnitude as provisional.

There is also a working technical artifact rather than a diagram. Edviro ingests meters, bills, BMS data, sensors, and work orders; models normal operation; tests interventions; changes schedules or setpoints; and checks the result against meter data. Seven live deployments prove that some version of this loop functions. They do not yet prove that the “world model” predicts interventions reliably across different building systems. A per-building digital twin may become a useful proprietary substrate, but seven sites are too few to establish cross-customer learning or a durable moat.

The strongest bear case is that Edviro has built a feature bundle inside somebody else’s distribution channel. Siemens, GridPoint, 75F, Clockworks, and Trane already possess pieces of diagnosis, control, and optimization. Legacy integrations are slow, cybersecurity matters, and school procurement can turn “29 expanding” into a long queue of pilots. Dozens of demos alongside only $20,000 ARR may mean the founders are repeatedly explaining a product that buyers hesitate to adopt. If incumbent access to controls matters more than model quality, Edviro’s software-only advantage becomes a disadvantage.

But the founders’ behavior so far cuts against that outcome. They began with a specific billing error, entered the schlep-heavy work of connecting old building systems, offered free audits, and converted the effort into live sites and revenue. This also matches the more revealing pattern I care about: a difficult substrate and numerical proof appeared before a large financing round. The missing signal is route-changing evidence. There is no documented rejected hypothesis, failed deployment, or shipped revision, so I cannot yet tell whether Shah and Siripurapu are running backs or merely moving quickly along an unobstructed first route.

I would invest, but not because “energy world models” sounds important. I would invest because two founders have found a small group that appears to need a crude product now and have already pushed it through real institutional plumbing. My flip condition is concrete: if fewer than four of the seven current school sites renew or expand after twelve months despite meter-reconciled savings reports, I would reverse the decision. That result would show that the apparent pull was really subsidized auditing rather than a product customers keep.
