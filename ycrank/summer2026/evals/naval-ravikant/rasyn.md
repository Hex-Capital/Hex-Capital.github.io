# Rasyn -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The useful founder evidence is what the team has built, not the biographies. Daood Hashmi has direct computational-chemistry exposure at Caltech, and Rasyn launched a working product spanning 54 executable tools in August 2026. The preceding Larmor product was reportedly used by seven medicinal-chemistry teams, while Marcus involved an 87,000-row dataset from Aragen, IFM Therapeutics, and 1200 Pharma. These are signs of contact with the problem, but the supporting pages have disappeared, the evaluation remained in progress, and the dossier cannot attribute the work between Hashmi and Ayush Chauhan. This is credible specific knowledge in formation, not yet exceptional founder proof.

Marigold’s best mechanism is execution across heterogeneous infrastructure: 40 tools run on customer-connected hardware, 14 use hosted endpoints, and the agent checks intermediate results. That is more difficult than generating chemistry prose. But difficulty integrating software is not the same as durability. Rowan already offers more than 15 computational workflows, many Marigold tools are third-party or open source, and Rasyn has not demonstrated exclusive data, a proprietary model advantage, or workflow lock-in. If a well-funded competitor copied the interface and integrations, the dossier identifies no asset that would make Rasyn improve faster.

The proposed data flywheel does not yet resolve this. Customer scientific data is excluded from training without written consent, which is sensible for trust but prevents automatic accumulation of scarce data. Nor does one laboratory make the product more valuable to another: there is no contribution, referral, liquidity, or shared-workflow evidence. Rasyn currently sells software through labor-intensive integration and execution, not a permissionless chemistry network that compounds while the company sleeps.

The market is real, but the path through it is underspecified. The cited AI-in-drug-discovery category grows from $2.3 billion in 2025 to a projected $13.8 billion in 2033, yet that top-down figure does not establish which adjacent buyers Marigold can reach from its initial researcher workflow. The 30.3% retrosynthesis Top-1 mismatch rate, absence of GLP or GMP validation, and lack of Part 11 compliance also place a ceiling on immediate migration into regulated production. A general chemistry interface can describe a large ambition while still entering through a narrow, low-trust wedge.

The strongest bull case is that the 54-tool system is an early operating layer rather than an AI wrapper. Rasyn has exposed transparent pricing—$89 monthly for researchers, at least $747 monthly for a three-seat lab, and institution plans beginning at $2,000—and its stop-on-failed-check design addresses a real weakness of general coding agents. If the earlier seven-team usage becomes repeat paid usage, connected laboratories could generate unusually valuable validation patterns. Today, however, the dossier shows prices rather than paying customers, and the privacy architecture prevents those patterns from becoming proprietary by default.

My base case is that within 36 months Rasyn either pivots toward one narrow, auditable computational-chemistry workflow or remains below $1 million ARR; general orchestration alone will not sustain differentiation. I pass now. The verifiable flip condition is a demonstrated cross-laboratory compounding loop: at least ten independent paying labs renewing after six months while their opt-in validated runs measurably improve outcomes for subsequent labs. That would turn integration work into a durable asset rather than a catalog competitors can reproduce.
