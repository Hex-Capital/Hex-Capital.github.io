# Marker -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Marker has not disclosed the proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or whether this is genuinely the first scarce check. That is not administrative trivia; it prevents me from knowing whether I am buying a discarded belief or paying Demo Day prices for the obvious 2026 thesis that enterprises need help deploying agents. Financing silence receives 6/28. The market is already capital-heavy—Distyl AI has raised roughly $202 million, Sierra $1.67 billion, and Writer $326 million—so cheap entry matters more, not less.

The capital also has no named card to flip. “Embed, map workflows, and deploy agents” describes the business, not the experiment financed by this round. Marker supplies no use-of-funds plan, dated milestone, pass/fail metric, or explanation of why the work cannot be financed through customer retainers. A real venture moment might be proving that accumulated process knowledge reduces deployment labor across customers, but the dossier does not say the round is testing that proposition. I cannot underwrite an inferred experiment, so this remains at the 6/24 default.

The team is the strongest part of the case. Richard Berwick, Will Drevno, and David Pastewka spent 15 years building together, sold Drapr to Gap in 2021, and then operated from inside the buyer; Berwick subsequently founded Gap’s Office of AI. That is unusually complete outsider-insider evidence. They have shipped enterprise technology on one side and lived the internal transformation problem on the other. Marker is therefore not three people discovering consulting through an LLM wrapper. It is a repeat team rebuilding a system whose organizational failure modes they have actually encountered.

There is also real product evidence, although the commercial causality is obscured. An agent reportedly removed 80% of manual product-operations work at a Fortune 500 retailer, while another deployment cut onboarding from 120 to 60 days and manual QA by 95% at an eight-figure prop-tech company. Those results require live workflow access and customer action, earning meaningful credit for embedded workflow. But the customers are unnamed, and Marker reports neither paid conversion nor renewal, deployment count, contract value, or retention. The company-specific process knowledge may become a proprietary dark pool; today it is a plausible residue of consulting engagements, not yet a demonstrated asset that causes repeat purchases.

The bull case is that these founders can turn privileged access into software faster than outsiders because they understand both the enterprise buyer and the implementation work. Ando is the relevant exception: enterprise AI deserved underwriting once measured accuracy, pilot-to-paid conversion, and retention proved a functioning business beneath the model option. Marker’s material difference is that its impressive operational percentages remain anonymous and disconnected from purchasing or retention. Meanwhile, its broad cross-functional scope overlaps directly with Distyl AI, custom delivery may scale with people, and no gross margin, burn, runway, or revenue metric shows that the company survives without another round. Human escalation is sensible product design, but without labor economics it can also hide a consultancy inside SaaS language.

I expect that by August 2029 Marker is a services-heavy AI transformation firm producing $3 million–$8 million in annualized revenue, but still unable to show software-like margins independent of implementation headcount. That can be a good business; it is not yet a good personal angel bet at an unknown strike price. I am passing today. I would flip if Marker produced a named cohort of at least five enterprise deployments that converted into renewed annual contracts at positive contribution margin without proportional implementation hiring.
