# Open Actuation -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I want a roboticist to specify the movement she needs and receive the motor that makes it possible, without redesigning her machine around whatever happens to be in a catalog. Open Actuation’s four-week custom-motor offer points toward that world. But that future is my construction: the dossier does not establish the intervening steps from quick-turn motor shop to broadly accessible custom actuation. I cannot give the company credit for a roadmap I supplied.

The most compelling evidence is on the factory floor. Open Actuation describes machining, winding, magnet insertion, balancing, and testing in Los Angeles, supported by proprietary manufacturing software and tooling. Those are physical capabilities beneath drones and robotics, where a component’s geometry and performance constrain what someone else can build. This fits my pattern of finding enabling infrastructure underneath a more visible market. The limitation is specific: Atlas Motion also offers custom motors within weeks, and Westmag has a flexible domestic manufacturing platform. Owning equipment establishes substance; it does not establish a process advantage.

Why must the drone designer accept the wrong motor? Available geometries impose engineering compromises, while domestic sourcing restrictions narrow the alternatives further. Open Actuation could return design control to that engineer. That is a concrete mechanism, although the dossier does not quantify redesign costs, procurement delays, or the margin captured by existing suppliers. July 2026 reporting connects demand to FCC restrictions, but imported magnets and silicon steel remain dependencies. Domestic assembly helps customers navigate the constraint; it does not yet remove the underlying supply constraint.

Jon Facer has relevant operating tuition: Gecko Robotics and five years as 1Factory’s first employee put him close to robotics and manufacturing software. I take that seriously. I still cannot trace the motor obsession backward through independently documented experiments or earlier technical builds, and job openings do not establish successful recruiting. My pre-revenue investment in **CosmicBrain AI** is the relevant exception: both companies address physical robotics enablement, but that commitment rested on lifelong building evidence and signed industrial proofs of concept. Open Actuation’s employment history and early samples supply a weaker bridge from founder to destination. Missing revenue alone would not stop me.

The samples are nevertheless important. AgFunderNews reported customer feedback in July, and the company reports building motors for unnamed drone OEMs. Someone has apparently moved beyond a rendering into testing hardware. That earns product credit, but there is no documented repeat ordering, payment, or costly customer commitment. The $187 CubeMars listing establishes an available catalog product, not realized revenue or demand for proprietary motors. Resale could eventually finance harder manufacturing development—a structure I like—but nothing here establishes that cash flow. Likewise, thousands per month versus thousands per week needs reconciliation; neither capacity claim is a shipment record, and the discrepancy alone does not establish fabrication.

I pass on a personal check today, with genuine interest in the machinery. Open Actuation has the enabling layer I want to find, but less evidence connecting that layer to a distinctive future or unusually committed users. There is an identified CEO, and the dossier supplies no affirmative evidence of the governance or privacy violations that would make this an immediate rejection. My one verifiable condition for reopening the decision is a customer-confirmed production history showing three unrelated OEMs repeatedly buying distinct custom motors, delivered within the advertised four weeks at positive gross margin. That would tell me whether this factory is becoming indispensable to other people’s inventions.
