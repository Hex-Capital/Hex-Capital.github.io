# HyperProbe -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

HyperProbe has demonstrated that runtime state can collapse particular debugging incidents from days to one attempt and from a traffic spike to a same-hour fix. That is useful. It is not yet a quantified consequence-of-success case. The dossier does not identify the engineering cost pool, outage burden, or population of developers whose productivity changes by an order of magnitude. I see a faster workflow, not yet a 100x societal or economic consequence.

The technical mechanism is more interesting than another coding-agent interface. A read-only probe captures line-specific production variables without redeployment, exposes them through MCP, and reportedly adds under 1% overhead at 3,000 requests per second. But the reference test is not independently validated, and HyperProbe supplies no controlled benchmark against Lightrun, Multiplayer, or Dynatrace/Rookout. Two customer stories show that the mechanism can work; they do not prove a repeatable 5–10x discontinuity or a capability competitors cannot reproduce.

The founders are the strongest part of the option. Karan Raina and Shailendra Singh previously built HyperTest around SDKs and application traces, brought that product to more than 100 teams, and then redirected the same team toward production debugging. That is evidence of relevant technical context and willingness to revise the product. It is not yet evidence of risk-tuned magnet recruiting: the eight-person team has no disclosed security, runtime-systems, or enterprise-observability recruit explicitly owning the largest weaknesses.

The bull case stings. HyperProbe has arrived as coding-agent usage reaches 80%; CheQ Digital and Housing.com report concrete incident resolution; and ordinary investigations could eventually produce a proprietary corpus connecting runtime states, root causes, and fixes. If that loop becomes an agent-native production control plane, the narrow probe is merely the wedge. But today the corpus is inferred, the market has funded substitutes, and a standalone SDK must earn trust to capture sensitive local variables inside production systems. Default PII redaction, audit logs, expiry, approval gates, and private-VPC deployment mitigate that risk; they do not establish an advantage.

Capital should now attack one kill risk: whether enterprises will repeatedly authorize agent-operated probes and pay for them after a security review. HyperProbe has published prices of $79–$99 per service per month, yet provides no conversion, retention, gross-margin, or cost-comparison evidence. My base case is that within 36 months the company pivots toward broader agent observability and remediation while remaining below $3 million ARR, because the founders learn quickly but the current wedge lacks a demonstrated technical or economic moat.

I pass with my own money today. The option has limited downside, but its uncapped side is still asserted rather than measured. One condition flips the decision: a controlled deployment across at least ten enterprises showing a greater than 5x reduction in mean time to resolution versus a named incumbent, with repeat paid usage after security approval and no material production incident.
