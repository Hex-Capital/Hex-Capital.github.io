# Orgo -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Orgo could supply the computers on which persistent AI workers operate. That is potentially consequential infrastructure. But the economic prize depends on how much useful work those agents perform and how much of that value their desktop provider captures. The dossier establishes cloud desktops with persistence, isolation, and programmatic control. It does not quantify a labor-cost baseline, a population served, or an orders-of-magnitude reduction in the cost of completed work. I can imagine the enormous outcome. I cannot credit my imagination as company evidence.

The technical question is whether Orgo changes the economics of agent execution enough to become indispensable. Its comparison with Fly.io and DigitalOcean establishes that desktop primitives arrive already assembled. That saves developers work. Yet E2B already offers graphical desktops, Daytona supplies overlapping persistent environments across operating systems, and Cua makes relevant components available as open source. None of that proves Orgo cannot win. It does mean that persistence and mouse control alone do not establish a discontinuity. There is no comparative result on task completion, reliability, throughput, or cost that earns technical credit above my absent-evidence default.

The strongest objection to passing is already operating in production: Orgo reports more than 1,000 teams and thousands of desktops, identifies Peter Steinberger as its first paying customer, and publishes a testimonial describing agents inside three small businesses. This is substantially more informative than a demonstration video. Kinney brings software engineering experience; Vasilescu has implemented business agents through computeruse.agency; Wang supplies another technical founder. Their proximity to actual deployment problems could produce an infrastructure advantage before a more heavily financed competitor recognizes it. My execution-heavy platform investments and willingness to back small technical communities leave room for precisely that possibility. But the disclosed usage does not yet establish a proprietary learning loop, exceptional recruiting, or a material belief revised after evidence. Those founder qualities remain unproven rather than adverse.

I would spend the next dollars on whether a customer can run a useful worker economically and reliably. Orgo disclosed payment-webhook failures that froze paid accounts and MCP failures, followed by fixes. Repairing those failures matters, but it does not establish a deliberate sequence of experiments against the largest existential risk. The dossier supplies no ranked kill risk, test budget, numerical threshold, or stop condition. Likewise, subscriptions at $29, $99, and $399 show what customers are charged; they reveal nothing about compute utilization, support burden, inference costs, or contribution margin. I see no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence. I also see no demonstrated route to superior unsubsidized economics.

I pass with my own money today. The red flag is competitive dependence on packaging capabilities that several providers already supply; there is no evidence here of the separate red flags around exit fixation, harmful categories, or founders rejecting disconfirming facts. The known exception routes require a documented extraordinary instigator addressing a giant existing value pool, or a consequence compelling enough to override missing commercial proof. This dossier establishes neither sufficiently. **My flip condition is an independently reproducible production benchmark showing Orgo delivers comparable successful agent tasks at one-tenth Daytona’s fully loaded cost, with equal or better reliability.** That would turn a convenient deployment platform into evidence of a cost curve worth underwriting.
