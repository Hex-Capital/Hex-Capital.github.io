# Lyon -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Lyon’s candidate secret is that a financial institution’s own transaction history can support a reusable predictive model that displaces months of task-specific engineering. What would prove that wrong? A comparison showing that pretrained tabular models or conventional supervised methods deliver equivalent results at lower total cost. The reported **4× precision in identifying premium-card converters**, after training on **28 billion transactions**, makes this a proposition worth testing. But the comparison is against rules, the methodology is undisclosed, and no named peer is shown rejecting Lyon’s proposition. I give the result meaningful credit without treating it as an established secret.

Who owns the advantage once the model works? Lyon trains inside the customer’s VPC and produces customer-owned models. Those choices may help secure adoption, but they leave the proprietary transactions with the institution. The dossier establishes neither exclusive access nor a mechanism through which serving one customer improves Lyon’s position with the next. Fundamental pursues pretrained tabular intelligence; SAP now owns Prior Labs. Their technical equivalence remains unproved, so declaring Lyon a commodity would outrun the evidence. Declaring it a monopoly would do the same. Financial prediction is an application domain; the dossier has yet to identify a bounded market Lyon can dominate.

The founding structure remains an open question. Gabriel Noya’s NVIDIA and financial-sector affiliations and Markus Höhn’s mathematics and machine-learning background make the technical undertaking credible. They do not establish prior work together, full-time commitment, ownership, or control of essential engineering. Höhn’s continuing description as a Stanford student warrants clarification, not a finding that he is part-time. The discrepancy between YC’s founder listing and his own co-founder identification likewise requires verification. I apply the founding-structure default rather than invent either alignment or a red flag.

The strongest argument against passing is that Lyon has already crossed an unusually difficult boundary for a two-person company: obtaining access to an enterprise’s transaction history and training at substantial scale. Credit deployment is reportedly underway, and an insurer is engaged. Lyon also fits my less advertised interest in financial infrastructure and the machinery beneath AI applications. If one customer-specific representation can support conversion, credit, and fraud, the initial implementation could become the institution’s common predictive infrastructure. That would make subsequent workflows substantially more valuable than isolated consulting projects. The dossier, however, establishes the conversion claim; it does not yet establish that expansion mechanism.

My exceptions for competitive AI investments require a demonstrated technical discontinuity, while exceptions grounded in customer behavior require observable persistence. An unnamed customer’s reported improvement over rules does not yet meet either standard. There is no disclosed technical retraction, regulatory prohibition, or outsourced core team to trigger an affirmative red flag. The present limitation is evidence of value capture: no pricing or margin structure supports power-law arithmetic, and founder email plus a scheduling page establishes availability rather than a repeatable acquisition route. Missing revenue is ordinary at this stage; a plausible model for capturing the value remains necessary.

I pass on writing a check today, while keeping the technical investigation open. My verifiable flip condition is **a customer-confirmed, paid annual renewal for a shared Lyon model used across multiple production workflows, following a controlled comparison against pretrained tabular alternatives that includes training and deployment costs**. That single event would connect the promising technical claim to an economically durable customer relationship. Today, Lyon has evidence of a useful prediction system. Ownership of an enduring business advantage remains unproved.
