# screenpipe -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Louis Beaumont has shipped 358 releases since creating screenpipe in 2024, yet screenpipe sits squarely inside AI SaaS, the sleeve I trust least. That high-founder-conviction / low-category-fit pairing is screenpipe’s signature: a missionary-built distribution asset trapped inside a consensus software wrapper. “Software is no longer investible” is an overstatement, obviously, but the operational point is that exceptional execution cannot repair a bad entry price or a five-round SaaS regatta.

**Drop.io** is the high rung on founder conviction: years of stubborn execution against free file-sharing incumbents made the missionary temperament legible before the outcome. Beaumont’s 18,900-star repository, 1,800 forks, and 358 releases put screenpipe close to that rung; screenpipe was built before YC and active users grew 6× in two months. The divergence is that Drop.io’s conviction survived multiple strategic tacks, while screenpipe’s evidence remains concentrated in one product cycle and Beaumont’s French-intelligence and defense-AI history is largely self-reported.

The canonical “AI agent for an enterprise vertical” is the low-rung category failure: consensus exposure, commodity model leverage, and a standard SaaS cap table priced as though differentiation were already resolved. screenpipe carries nearly every dangerous label—Artificial Intelligence, SaaS, B2B, Enterprise—but screenpipe is not merely another prompt wrapper. Local-default capture, a Rust/TypeScript stack, MCP access, programmable pipes, and cross-platform support create a real product boundary; they move it above the low rung, but not into my crypto, creator, DTC, holdco, or Wizard-of-Oz sleeves.

The $25 Standard, $50-per-seat Pro, and $150-per-seat Enterprise plans give screenpipe a plausible capital-efficiency mechanism over the next 18 months. Every free installation can feed paid conversion without a corresponding sales hire, while the commercial-license change can monetize organizations already consuming screenpipe’s source. The opposite tack is visible too: six employees, a $130,000–$180,000 opening, and enterprise sales could turn screenpipe into founder-drainage before recurring revenue catches the payroll. Tens of thousands of dollars within weeks provide a downside cushion, but not yet default-alive evidence.

No public round price or financing terms exist for screenpipe, so there is no evidence—applying the absent-evidence default. The counterfactual is unusually clean: screenpipe below roughly a $12 million post-money valuation is a value-seed bet on an illegible open-source distribution asset; screenpipe at a $25–30 million YC AI valuation is simply a hot deck wearing a local-first jacket. The 250,000-user claim creates leverage, but without price or named investor passes, I assume screenpipe’s SAFE is consensus until shown otherwise.

**John Fish / Bookshelved** is the single useful parallel for owned distribution: the audience existed before institutional capital, allowing the asset to compound without rented acquisition. screenpipe has the software analogue—18,900 GitHub stars, 250,000 claimed users, and Beaumont’s approximately 9,000 LinkedIn followers—so screenpipe owns a meaningful community before the round. John Fish’s attachment lives in a named person and survives product changes; screenpipe’s attachment lives primarily in a repository, meaning forks, licensing friction, or a superior open alternative can weaken it faster.

The 250,000-user claim and tens of thousands of dollars earned within weeks support a falsifiable forecast: within 36 months, screenpipe reaches $8–15 million ARR and Microsoft acquires screenpipe for under $250 million to make Recall cross-platform and programmable. The mechanism is conversion, not magic—screenpipe turns open-source adoption into signed binaries, commercial licenses, and enterprise policies while Microsoft buys the distribution and Linux/macOS surface it lacks. That is a good outcome, but potentially a modest venture outcome if today’s SAFE already prices the destination.

Microsoft Recall is also the bear mechanism that can kill screenpipe: Microsoft bundles memory into Windows, Granola spends its $67.3 million on collaborative workflows, Otter uses $100 million ARR to cross-sell agents, and screenpipe absorbs the privacy liability of continuous screen and audio capture. Default PostHog telemetry, optional cloud processing, 5%–10% CPU usage, and 5–10 GB of monthly storage make that objection sting. screenpipe’s 6× active-user growth, 358 releases, and cross-platform local architecture are enough to reject the “dead wrapper” thesis, but screenpipe’s missing round terms keep me Neutral. I flip to Invest if the current financing closes at or below a verifiable $12 million post-money valuation.
