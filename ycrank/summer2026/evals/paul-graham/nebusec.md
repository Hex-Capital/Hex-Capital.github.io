# NebuSec -- Paul Graham Evaluation

NebuSec’s founders can demonstrably break software. I’m less certain how they behave when building a company becomes difficult. Published working exploits answer a harder question than a résumé does. But the dossier contains no specific commercial or technical blocker followed by a founder-built workaround and a measured elapsed time. I would want that story. Finding vulnerabilities is evidence of technical ability; it doesn’t automatically establish resourcefulness in selling, installing, or changing the product.

The technical evidence deserves substantial credit. Zou’s published CVE-2022-27666 exploit and the team’s CyberMeowfia releases are inspectable artifacts. Wu’s automated-repair work also connects directly to what NebuSec is building. This fits an unstated pattern I like: technical founders who produce difficult things before asking investors to believe them. The September benchmark—88% maximum coverage against 80% for Codex Security and 74% for Claude Security—is weaker evidence. It is vendor-reported and covers 76 known vulnerabilities. It does not establish that NebuSec finds important unknown bugs in an unfamiliar customer repository. The working exploits earn the technical score; the benchmark earns provisional credit.

The most useful commercial fact is that PingCAP confirms engaging NebuSec for an investigation completed by October 23. A real company handed the team a real problem and received findings. That is much more informative than 1.7K GitHub stars. It also suggests a sensible route into the market: investigate something urgent, learn where existing tools fail, and automate the repeated work. Researcher-led service can be an excellent way to discover the product. But one completed investigation does not show customers repeatedly pulling the scanning software into their release process. Published plans and fixed scan quotes establish a way to charge, not evidence that anyone has paid or returned.

The strongest case for investing now is that these researchers already possess the scarce ability, and the business can be discovered around it. Their prior security work makes this substantially more credible than an invented “AI for security” idea. Yet the dossier does not establish the founders’ own originating collision with this particular workflow, or a product change driven by a customer observation. Those remain missing evidence. Against Semgrep and Aikido, the decisive question is whether NebuSec’s research ability becomes software customers depend on without purchasing more researcher hours. Dynamic exploit verification being disabled by default makes that question particularly concrete: how much of the distinctive capability reaches the ordinary customer?

I would hold off on a personal check. The usual red flags—waiting for a major partner, delegating customer learning, or persisting with a disproven route—are not documented here. Neither is the rapid response to resistance that would satisfy my founder gate. I have allowed exceptional working proof to substitute for missing founder evidence, but NebuSec’s exploits validate its researchers more clearly than they validate its autonomous product. My flip condition is one documented customer sequence: a named engineering team encounters a deployment blocker, the founders ship a workaround within two weeks, and that customer subsequently pays for and repeatedly uses automated scans. That would connect the impressive technical work to the company I would be buying.
