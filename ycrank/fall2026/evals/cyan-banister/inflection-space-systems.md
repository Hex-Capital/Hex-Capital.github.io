# Inflection Space Systems -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I want a scientist with a new sensor to order a spacecraft without first becoming a spacecraft manufacturer. Inflection Space Systems offers a recognizable path toward that world: one Exponent platform, terrestrial components, robotic assembly, and a target of 10,000 spacecraft annually by 2031. The booked Q1 2028 launch puts a first milestone on the calendar. But the bridge still requires flight qualification, customers accepting a common payload envelope, and repeatable factory economics. I can picture the future; the dossier establishes only part of the route.

Why does every payload need so much bespoke engineering? Which differences matter in orbit, and which persist because that is how spacecraft have always been sold? Inflection’s fixed platform could return engineering time and purchasing control to payload developers. Its published price falls from $1.5 million for one spacecraft to $700,000 each for one hundred. That is an explicit offer to reward standardization. It does not establish the incumbent markup being removed, realized customer savings, or profitable production at the lower price. The broken system is identifiable; its economics remain partly hidden.

The founders make this considerably more interesting than a factory rendering. Alex Lisso has worked across spacecraft design, manufacturing, testing, and operations at Millennium, Rocket Lab, Planet, and Interlune. Matt Hanley automated satellite integration and testing at Loft and worked on Lunar Photon at Rocket Lab. Those are relevant encounters with the operating complexity Inflection proposes to compress. Founding engineer Jackson Smith adds experience from Sierra Space and Loft. I see domain competence and complementary talent. I do not yet see the dated, independently pursued experiments that would establish a pre-company obsession, or evidence that the founders have mastered the commercial machinery alongside the engineering.

Inflection’s strongest fit with my less obvious investing pattern is its position underneath other people’s products. Payload businesses need power, communications, flight software, and a reliable physical platform before their own invention can earn anything. The company reports building more than half its internal stack, including flight and ground software: actual development toward controlling that dependency. Yet Apex already pursues standardized, vertically integrated production and has raised more than $718 million. The question is whether choosing one platform creates enough manufacturing repetition to outweigh the missions excluded by its 100 kg payload and 1.5 kW orbit-average power limits. Internal ownership gives Inflection something to improve; it does not yet establish a scarce advantage.

The strongest argument against waiting is that flight hardware must be financed before customers can inspect orbital results. My **CosmicBrain AI** investment illustrates the relevant exception: pre-revenue physical infrastructure can deserve a check when a working prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept connect founder capability to outside commitment. Inflection shares the manufacturing ambition and relevant technical experience, but the dossier does not establish equivalent prototype validation or signed payload customers. A launch booking demonstrates the team’s commitment. It does not demonstrate a customer rearranging a mission around Exponent. Missing revenue is acceptable here; customer behavior remains at the absent-evidence default.

I would hold my personal check today. This is a **Neutral** evaluation with a specific attraction: Inflection could make access to spacecraft manufacturing less dependent on assembling an entire engineering organization. Nothing disclosed establishes a privacy violation, misleading leadership, or dismissive attitude toward operational complexity; founder control arrangements remain unknown. My flip condition is one independently confirmed repeat order from a payload customer after accepting its first Exponent spacecraft at the contracted price. That event would connect the enabling-layer thesis to someone choosing to depend on it twice.
