# Asim -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Asim has a working technical starting point, but I cannot yet underwrite the investment being offered. The reported $500,000 financing in September 2026 establishes that someone supplied capital; it tells me neither the proposed post-money valuation nor how many investors are competing for this check. YC membership does not establish an auction, and it does not establish scarcity. Both remain unknown. More consequentially, the dossier names two activities—robot safety assessments and a runtime intervention layer—without identifying which finite experiment this round buys.

Those activities have different economic engines. Testing can produce customer-funded work and access to failure cases. A runtime layer must earn permission to intervene inside a customer's robot, where an incorrect intervention can itself create problems. The attractive mechanism is that assessments uncover hazards, customers pay for that discovery, and repeated engagements generate data that improves intervention. Asim's withheld test scenarios support evaluation integrity, but the dossier does not establish exclusive data rights, repeat engagements, or purchases caused by that access. A private test set is a possible input to a dark pool. It does not yet establish one.

The founders give the opposing case real weight. Tilley's human–robot interaction work, Lafargue's Sony research experience, and Neau's computer-vision and social-robotics research put relevant expertise around the problem. More concretely, Lecourtois's portfolio records a first-prize external robot-safety watchdog at the June 2026 YC Paris–Unaite hackathon. This team has built something directly relevant. It also fits my recurring interest in translating between systems: learned robot behavior and physical operating constraints. What remains undocumented is operating experience on the buyer's side—deploying these systems under production requirements and getting someone to pay for the bridge. The prototype earns founder credit; it cannot also become customer validation.

The strongest bull case is that independent testing becomes the entry point to indispensable safety infrastructure. Robocurve's MIT-licensed harness makes basic evaluation tooling easier to obtain, but it could increase the importance of differentiated scenarios and trusted interpretation. Asim could earn that position. Yet its terms describe illustrative demonstrations and provide neither certification nor deployment assurance, so I cannot assume the product already clears a customer's safety requirements. I also cannot invoke the exceptions I have made for AI businesses with documented conversion and retention, or unusual financing structures supported by prior exits and explicit capital plans. Those override facts are absent here. There is no evidence of auction pressure or economics predicated on errors disappearing, either; those would be invented red flags.

Delete the next financing and the business is still unspecified: negotiated testing engagements are inferred, with no disclosed pricing, margins, burn, or funded route to break-even. That is an underwriting gap, not evidence that a four-person pre-seed company is operating badly. My 36-month base case is that Asim concentrates on project-based robot safety assessments while a broadly deployed runtime product remains unproven. I pass today. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: a signed, customer-funded assessment-to-runtime pilot agreement defining retained incident-data rights, a dated intervention-accuracy threshold, and a paid rollout upon success. That would connect the team's demonstrated technical ability to an owned commercial asset.
