# FlowManual -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have learned quickly, but they have not yet shown specific knowledge acquired inside construction. David Bae and Michael Lin began building on April 18, 2026 and interviewed hundreds of construction professionals within two months. That is real action, not résumé theater. Michael also worked on software associated with Dijie’s growth from $3K to $336K MRR. But neither founder has shipped or sold into a contractor’s back office, and interviews do not prove the tacit knowledge earned by reconciling bids, contracts, invoices, and change orders under deadline.

The product addresses an expensive problem. A missed clause can expose a contractor to $5K–$10K, while document review consumes hundreds of hours per month. Source-line annotations and audit logs sensibly keep a human in the loop. More importantly, the same comparison engine can move from bid-to-contract review into purchasing, invoice reconciliation, takeoffs, and change orders. That gives FlowManual room to maneuver inside a construction-software market estimated at $7.7B in 2025. The breadth is plausible because the workflows share documents, vendors, prices, and obligations—not merely because a market report supplies a large number.

The fatal question is what remains after a competitor copies the code. Today the answer is very little. FlowManual sends extracted text to Anthropic, while Document Crunch, Trunk Tools, LightTable, and Primepoint already provide overlapping document intelligence. Customer-specific vendor prices and bid patterns could become valuable, but they are encrypted, isolated by tenant, and not pooled without consent. This produces private memory for each contractor, not yet a network or proprietary model. The same privacy posture that helps enterprise procurement prevents the data from compounding across customers.

The strongest bull case is speed: two young founders built a broad working product, interviewed hundreds of users, raised $500K, and offer every feature through a no-card free tier. Notion also began as a strange, capital-efficient product before an observable distribution loop existed. The material difference is that FlowManual enters a crowded category through commodity models, with no documented repeat usage, paid deployment, or distinctive product language yet. A prototype can justify patience; it cannot substitute for evidence that users return.

There is also no permissionless loop. Each contractor’s uploads improve that contractor’s history, but the next contractor does not receive more utility because the first one joined. Distribution remains founder outreach, 20-minute demos, and requested introductions. Likewise, custom Pro pricing proves an intention to charge, not direct value capture: the dossier identifies no payer, completed transaction, recurring revenue, or unit economics. Free usage can reveal demand, but without retention it can also conceal indifference.

I pass. I see no integrity or artificial-scarcity red flag, so this is not a founder veto; it is a leverage veto. The company currently sells copyable software through proportional sales into a market where better-capitalized competitors already have distribution. I would reopen if FlowManual reaches $500K ARR from at least 20 contractors, with six-month retention and an opt-in cross-customer price or correction benchmark that measurably improves results as participation grows. That single milestone would prove monetization, durability, and the beginnings of a network rather than merely promising them.
