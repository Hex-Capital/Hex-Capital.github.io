# Nori -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

$1,288 buys Nori’s L2 into an $18.1 billion household-robot market projected to reach $90.9 billion by 2033. Nori is a large-pivot-room robotics wager trapped inside a capital-and-labor-heavy machine. The market is Nori’s highest dimension; permissionless leverage is its lowest. Software can replicate freely. Robots must still be manufactured, delivered, maintained, and sometimes remotely operated.

Uber is the high rung for pivot-room: black cars became UberX because the underlying transportation market contained many adjacent businesses. Nori shares that geometry. Nori can move from household chores into hospitality, elder assistance, food preparation, light manufacturing, or teleoperated commercial work without abandoning its manipulation stack. The divergence is load-bearing: Uber expanded by adding participants to software, while Nori must make a $1,288 machine reliably load dishes, make beds, and prepare food before any adjacency becomes available. A large market provides optionality, not execution.

Anchorage Digital’s charter-dependent distribution would normally fail my leverage screen; permission became a moat only because regulation was the scarce asset. Nori has the dependence without the moat. Nori’s downloadable skills and direct website pre-orders are code-native, but every new user still requires motors, vertical lifts, assembly, shipping, support, and possibly a human teleoperator inside the home. The unstated portfolio pattern cuts against Nori: Twitter, Notion, and Replit distribute at near-zero marginal cost, while Nori promises Made-in-San-Francisco hardware below $1,400 against 1X’s $20,000 NEO. Cheap atoms remain atoms.

Antonio Sitong Li gives Nori a credible Stack Overflow parallel on specific knowledge: a builder serving a problem he has already worked inside. Li researched VR-demonstrated goals for robot teams at Columbia, combines computer science with architecture, and previously shipped software that reached 2,000 users and 1.5 million impressions. Nori therefore clears the “ex-FAANG PM found a TAM” red flag. Nori does not yet reach the Stack Overflow rung because no public repository, deployed fleet, task-success data, or multi-year robotics artifact demonstrates that Li can turn research fluency into reliable household manipulation.

Nori’s shared-skills marketplace could turn hardware into a network over the next 18 months. Each successful user-authored task could reduce training costs for every later Nori owner; more robots create more demonstrations, more skills improve robot utility, and utility sells more robots. That is real compounding geometry. Today Nori discloses neither shipped units nor marketplace activity, so the loop is promotional rather than operational. A marketplace without participants is a menu without a restaurant.

Antonio Sitong Li’s record shows intelligence and energy, but Nori provides no public evidence about the four-person group’s history, co-founder relationships, prior-team treatment, or execution under adversity. For Nori to flip this dimension into the top tier, Li would need to ship the first production batch cleanly, publish uncomfortable reliability and privacy data, and demonstrate durable authority within the founding group. Integrity is not inferred from marketing. Nori receives the quiet-founder default, modestly improved by Li’s documented record of building and publishing.

Summer 2026 delivery promises, undisclosed order volume, and broad task claims produce a falsifiable forecast: by July 2029, Nori will have discontinued or deprioritized the general-purpose consumer SKU and pivoted into teleoperated light-commercial robots for hospitality or food service, generating $1 million to $3 million in annual recurring software and operations revenue. Nori’s large market gives it somewhere to pivot; Nori’s weak permissionless architecture forces it toward environments where utilization can repay deployment and support costs.

41,000 launch views, 575 likes, a capacity-filled first production batch, a sixfold price advantage over Weave’s $7,999 Isaac 1, and Li’s robot-team research form the strongest bull case. Nori could be an Anchorage-style exception: hardware dependence becomes a fleet-and-data moat, while transferable skills create the permissionless layer after deployment. The bull case stings because a working $1,288 bimanual robot would expand the market rather than merely compete inside it. The dossier still withholds the facts that would make that exception real—delivered-unit count, task-success rates, marketplace usage, safety testing, and teleoperation privacy controls. I remain Neutral and do not write the check today. One verifiable condition flips the verdict: 1,000 delivered robots with at least 30% of weekly tasks initiated through user-authored marketplace skills.
