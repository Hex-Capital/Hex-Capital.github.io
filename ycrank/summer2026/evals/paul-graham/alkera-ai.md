# Alkera AI -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important fact is missing: I cannot find a named obstacle the founders encountered and then overcame. Alkera AI has a polished answer to the general danger of data agents—column-level lineage, graph-based planning, permissions, and approval controls—but the dossier never says what broke for a real user, what the founders did next, or how quickly it changed the outcome. Four years of shared work at Yale reduces cofounder risk. It does not prove the team is relentlessly resourceful. On my controlling criterion, this is missing evidence, so I use the default rather than inventing a founder story from their résumés.

The technical evidence is much better. The team shipped an agent and recorded 83.28% Pass@1 on DataAgentBench, ahead of SCRIBE at 81.85% and Spacedock at 74.33%. That is a measured artifact built before enterprise permission, not a diagram or future milestone. But 54 queries across 12 datasets and four database systems are too narrow to prove reliability across production stacks, and the result is reported on Alkera’s own site. The unfinished Looker, Tableau, Power BI, Metabase, Sigma, and Hex integrations make the limitation concrete. This is strong intermediate evidence of fierce nerds, not yet top-band proof.

The empty place is the narrow well of users. Alkera has 542 LinkedIn followers and prices Plus at $50 per month and Pro at $250, but the dossier identifies no paying customer, repeat user, production deployment, or data team tolerating the crude product because it urgently needs the result. Followers are attention, and a pricing page is not revenue. Nor is there evidence that user behavior falsified an earlier route and caused a shipped revision. Both demand and running-back flexibility therefore remain at their missing-evidence defaults.

The bull case stings. Rick Gao worked on treasury models, Tony Li built a multi-agent Python framework, and Andrew Tran worked on data infrastructure at HRT and Ramp. They have known one another for four years. Their product attacks exactly the ugly parts generic coding agents neglect: lineage, destructive-action permissions, organizational knowledge, VPC deployment, auditability, and eventually compliance. Customer-specific lineage graphs could compound into a proprietary substrate. If early data teams are already using Alkera privately, this dossier may be omitting the most informative fact.

But resemblance to founder-market fit is not the same as a documented founder-originated need. The public explanation begins with a broad category problem, not a particular incident one founder experienced. Meanwhile PromptQL already offers shared context and deterministic permissions, Altimate sells SQL and dbt agents for $29 per seat, and better-capitalized incumbents can add metadata and controls. Alkera’s benchmark lead is only 1.43 percentage points over SCRIBE. Without user pull, that can become a feature race rather than a company.

I would pass today. The operational gate fails: the dossier supplies neither the required founder workaround nor demand strong enough to replace it through a known exception. My one flip condition is verifiable: show me three named data teams using Alkera weekly in production, plus one dated example where a failure or integration blocker caused the founders to ship a workaround within two weeks. That would establish both the user well and the behavior of the founders when the path closes.
