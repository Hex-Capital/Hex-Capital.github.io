# Yam -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Yam has evidence that people want to play. It does not yet give me a deal I can underwrite. Post-money valuation, competing term sheets, and whether my money would be genuinely scarce are all unknown. That earns my missing-evidence score, without assuming a YC auction. The capital plan is equally unspecified: I cannot identify the finite experiment this round buys or the deadline for learning its answer. Those are my two largest criteria. Three hours of daily engagement cannot answer either question.

The strongest case for Yam is the team’s ability to connect consumer behavior with creator tools. Shan Zhong reports having grown a previous fintech to 100K users and 2M ARR, with the currency unspecified; that is prior operating evidence, not Yam revenue. Yu Qin built TikTok AI creation tools and reports growing Effect House’s creator community to 800K. Combined with the founders’ own participation in fanfiction and dating sims, these are meaningful receipts on both sides of the proposed bridge. This fits my pattern of backing people who translate between ecosystems. The founder dimension is Yam’s strongest.

The product also makes the bull case uncomfortable to dismiss. Creators can generate characters, edit scenarios, and publish playable links; the catalog shows 1.8M plays for “JJK Love island AU” and 516.4K for “JJK Ashley in Tokyo.” Alongside company-reported playtime exceeding three hours per DAU, that supports actual consumption of creator output. But the counters do not establish unique players, cohort retention, purchases, or creator-driven acquisition. My creator-investing pattern requires trust to change economic behavior. Yam has demonstrated gameplay; it has not yet demonstrated an owned distribution advantage that converts into cash. Popularity attached to existing fictional characters also leaves open who owns the asset attracting that attention.

The economics could invert the engagement story. Yam charges $9.99 monthly for Plus and $19.99 for Plus+, alongside currency packs, but the dossier supplies no paying-customer count, realized revenue, inference expense, moderation cost, or burn. Three-hour sessions could support a subscription habit or consume its entire contribution margin. I cannot choose between those outcomes from a pricing page. Nor will I assume a future model release solves the problem. The relevant AI red flag is dependence on shared technology without demonstrated customer ownership or cash economics; Yam has not supplied enough evidence to resolve that exposure. Missing economics receive the default, rather than an invented loss estimate.

I pass at 37/100. I checked the exceptions: exceptional paid conversion and retention could justify relaxing my financing gates, but play counters do not establish them; demonstrated person-level commercial trust could support a different security, but the founders’ prior-employer audiences are not theirs to finance. The strongest opposing argument remains substantial: experienced consumer and creator-tool operators have shipped something people apparently spend hours using. That warrants attention, but it does not establish what my check purchases. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: three consecutive months of positive operating cash flow from retained paying cohorts playing original or licensed stories. That would show Yam can own and monetize the attraction while paying the full cost of delivering it.
