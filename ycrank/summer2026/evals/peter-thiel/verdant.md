# Verdant -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Verdant’s contested truth would have to be: local permitting can be accelerated more effectively by an AI-native, jurisdiction-specific workflow than by replacing municipal systems or merely helping applicants navigate them. What would prove this wrong? If existing vendors can add the same ordinance-grounded completeness checks without losing customers or rebuilding their platforms, Verdant is a feature, not a company. The dossier already names GovWell and Clariti offering overlapping AI review, while PermitFlow possesses 12 million permitting datapoints. Verdant does not identify what these companies believe incorrectly, nor report a technical or customer result that could falsify its own claim. This is a slogan adjacent to a real problem, not yet a secret.

The 200 interviews with local-government employees are serious discovery work. They establish pain: incomplete applications consume scarce staff time before substantive review begins. The product response is also coherent—jurisdiction-specific intake, document and drawing checks, staff review, hearings, and draft reports, with officials retaining approval authority. But discovering that permitting is painful differs from discovering a monopoly. No deployed jurisdiction, proprietary dataset, measured accuracy advantage, or switching behavior is disclosed. Customer-specific ordinance configurations may eventually accumulate into deployment knowledge, but resemblance to a moat is not a moat.

The strongest case for Verdant is distribution. Aidan Ng has worked across DOJ, USDA, Capitol Hill, Deloitte’s government practice, and commercial-real-estate underwriting; Jason Yi has built government HR and legislative-tracking software and conducted public-benefits automation research. They know the institutional vocabulary and have named the actual buyers: planners, building officials, community-development staff, and city managers reached through direct introductions and demonstrations. This could become a small, defensible government-software beachhead if deployments compound into proprietary implementation data. Yet there is no disclosed sales cycle, price, contract, pilot, conversion rate, or renewal. Government familiarity can open a door; it cannot make procurement repeatable.

The founding structure remains unproved rather than discredited. Both founders attended Georgetown and later connected around government infrastructure, but the dossier supplies no specific history of shipping together, no explicit full-time status, no ownership information, and no evidence about whether all core engineering is in-house. Their relevant backgrounds prevent a founder-market mismatch, but domain credentials cannot substitute for long alignment or economic exposure. No explicit founding-team red flag is visible; the evidence simply stops before the gate is cleared.

The economics are less forgiving. Verdant sells modular proprietary SaaS through private order forms, but publishes neither pricing nor gross margins, and reports no revenue. The cited global permit-management software market was only $307 million in 2025 and is projected to reach $567 million by 2032. Perhaps adjacent municipal workflows eventually enlarge the opportunity, but that expansion would move Verdant toward the integrated government operating-system territory already occupied by better-financed competitors. Narrow enough to enter can become too narrow to matter; broad enough to return a fund becomes direct competition.

The bull case stings because these founders may convert unusually deep discovery into paid municipal deployments before horizontal vendors focus on land use, and each configured jurisdiction could make the next implementation faster. None of the known exceptions overrides the present failure, however: Verdant has neither independently verified zero-to-one technology, persistent transactions despite institutional hostility, nor a deployed physical or customer-qualified system. My 36-month base case is that it remains below $1 million ARR and shifts toward implementation-heavy permit preparation or an applicant-side workflow. I would reverse that judgment upon one verifiable result: five paid jurisdictions renewing after twelve months while Verdant demonstrates at least a 50% reduction in incomplete submissions. Until then, the problem is real, the founders are plausible, and the monopoly is imaginary. Pass.
