# Axonnic -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

A camera can watch someone sew and still miss the important part: the effort before the fabric moves. That is the little crack in the present that makes Axonnic interesting to me. In 2032, I can imagine skilled workers teaching machines delicate tasks without becoming robotics programmers. Getting there requires capturing intent, translating it across different bodies, and training policies that operate without the human sleeve. Axonnic describes those intermediate steps. The missing connection is whether muscle signals actually make the autonomous robot better; accurately reconstructing a hand is not the same achievement.

The founders have relevant operating tuition. Maxim Williams brings HD-sEMG research and Voxblock experience spanning PCB development, supply chain, and fundraising. Arsh Patankar contributed software to Oxford’s OxRAM quadruped project. That combination gives me more confidence than two people discovering robotics through a market report. But the dossier does not establish a long, independently pursued obsession, a sequence of pre-company experiments, or their ability to recruit others into it. I credit the work described without inventing the biography between the lines.

There is something here I could touch. The company reports 4.58-degree average joint-angle error at 50 predictions per second after 78 minutes of training data, and it has publicly demonstrated a Proception hand integration. Those are concrete artifacts. Construction recordings suggest people have participated in collecting data, while the Italian manufacturing LOI and reported Perloro Packing pilot provide a route into an actual task. They do not establish repeated customer commitment or successful autonomous sewing. The showcase win and investor names add little to that question.

Axonnic’s strongest fit is its position beneath the visible robot. An uncovered hand can perform ordinary work while the sleeve captures information video misses; the resulting training inputs could serve sewing, construction, and other manipulation tasks. That fits my pattern of looking for concealed infrastructure beneath an attractive application. But the strongest objection bites directly into that thesis: Hemlock already offers overlapping EMG data collection, calibration may remain person-specific, and customer contracts may prevent reuse. Axonnic could become a useful custom recording shop whose next engagement starts almost from scratch. Its technical implementation is an asset; a compounding, controlled bottleneck remains unproven.

I also want to know whose control changes. The dossier explains an information deficit for robotics developers, but identifies no quantified institutional rent, artificial restriction, or mechanism returning ownership or income to the workers supplying demonstrations. I cannot award that agency dividend merely because the product records electricians and plumbers. Those recordings also make consent and data custody consequential. The dossier establishes neither a severe privacy violation nor an explicit limiting architecture. Williams is identified as CEO, so there is no affirmative leadership shell game; founder voting arrangements remain unknown.

I would stay close, but keep my personal checkbook closed today. My willingness to fund pre-revenue robotics applies here: missing revenue is not the obstacle, and the prototype plus industrial interest are meaningful. The unresolved issue is whether this particular hidden signal deserves to become infrastructure. One result would move me to Invest: an independently measured Perloro sewing trial showing at least a 30% reduction in autonomous task failures against an otherwise matched video-and-pose-only training baseline, using held-out demonstrators. Show me that the robot learned something its eyes could not see.
