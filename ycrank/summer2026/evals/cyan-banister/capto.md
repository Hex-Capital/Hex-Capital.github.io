# Capto -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I want a small contractor to discover a government need, demonstrate its qualifications, and compete without first hiring someone who knows which portal hides the opportunity. Capto could participate in that world. But opportunity discovery must become reliable, company records must become reusable evidence, and pursuit outcomes must improve subsequent decisions. The dossier establishes a product addressing the first steps; it does not establish that compounding loop. “Company Brain” is an appealing name. What does it learn that the next bidder cannot buy elsewhere?

The founders have done something more persuasive than name it: they went looking for the work. Sankaralingam built RFP-search automation for a contractor, and the August 7 UMD profile describes approximately 50 contractor-office visits. That is willingness to acquire inconvenient knowledge. The pair have built together since meeting in high-school English class; the CEO also reports a prior product reaching 40,000 users and being acquired, although the transaction is not independently detailed. I see builders learning commercial reality, rather than just collecting credentials. I do not yet see a long, independently documented obsession with procurement.

Why does finding a solicitation require this much effort? Capto identifies fragmented portals, spreadsheet pipelines, and lengthy requirements. Those impose a real labor cost, and returning that time could let smaller firms compete more effectively. But the dossier does not quantify the burden or identify a particular gatekeeper collecting a rent that Capto removes. Increasing proposal output can also mean spending more time bidding unsuccessfully. The valuable agency is choosing better opportunities, not merely producing more submissions.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that customers are already doing something. The CEO reports paid pilots; Snovation says Capto found an RFI its existing pipeline missed. Those are useful early signals. The reported tripling of proposal output deserves attention, although the cohort and measurement period are absent. Customers’ 100-plus prime awards and $250 million in obligated value establish their activity, not Capto’s revenue or contribution. Still, this fits my less glamorous pattern: a mundane operating problem can conceal a substantial business. Dismissing it because the product lacks spectacle would be a mistake.

What stops that argument from carrying my money is the connection between useful workflow and durable dependency. Source-linked extraction and company-document grounding are sensible mechanisms, but GovDash already overlaps discovery, capture, and proposals while extending into post-award management. Capto has not demonstrated control of a scarce input beneath those applications. My pre-revenue exception for CosmicBrain AI rested on a working prototype, a lifelong robotics record, and a specific manufacturing future; Capto’s paid pilots provide commercial evidence, but do not supply the equivalent future or enabling asset. Meanwhile, the Company Brain makes consent and document custody consequential. The dossier establishes neither a severe privacy violation nor a protective architecture. It names an accountable CEO and provides no evidence of a governance shell game. Those are diligence questions, not invented reasons to deduct points.

I pass today. Capto’s strongest evidence concerns founders willing to learn; its weakest concerns ownership of the layer that learning might create. That combination can produce a useful company without producing the particular future I want to underwrite. My one verifiable flip condition is a prospective customer audit showing that Capto’s permissioned pursuit history materially improves qualification accuracy over the same public notices and company documents processed without that history. That would turn an inferred switching-cost story into evidence that each pursuit makes the product harder to replace.
