# Epsilab -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Epsilab’s consequential possibility is making agents capable of completing real work over long horizons. Reliable training environments could turn expensive human expertise into reproducible machine capability. But the dossier does not establish how much work becomes automatable, whose costs collapse, or by what multiple. I can imagine a large consequence; I cannot credit my imagination as company evidence. I pass on the evidence presented.

The product has a sensible mechanism: convert operational workflows into versioned states, tasks, rewards, and verifiers, then collect trajectories and replay failures. The decisive question is whether training inside Epsilab produces competence outside it. An agent getting better at exploiting a verifier creates impressive training curves and little economic value. METR’s reported cheating failures make this a concrete concern, although they establish no failure by Epsilab. There is no disclosed benchmark against HUD, Prime Intellect, or a lab’s internal environments, and no completed experiment demonstrating transfer. Technical superiority and risk-removal discipline therefore remain unproven.

The strongest argument against passing is the team’s demonstrated ability to build and commercialize. Hu and Peng previously worked together at Shortbread, where Peng reports $500K revenue and 2M episodes sold. Olsen brings experience from Tower Research Capital and XY Sense; Wang previously built Copper Health. Four founders could combine engineering, research, and commercial execution around a difficult workflow. That deserves attention. But prior-company sales are not Epsilab demand, and varied biographies do not establish fast belief revision or recruiting against named technical weaknesses. Those specific founder attributes remain at the absent-evidence baseline.

Epsilab also touches a pattern I favor: scarce workflows converted into repeatable learning infrastructure. Failure replay could make each deployment improve the next environment. The missing link is evidence of that accumulated advantage—exclusive workflow rights, validated trajectories, or measurable improvement from proprietary data. Meanwhile, HUD offers an environment marketplace and Prime Intellect bundles environments with training and compute. Prime Intellect’s reported annualized revenue above $100M belongs to its integrated business; it cannot establish Epsilab’s addressable environment revenue. Epsilab discloses neither pricing nor production costs, so I cannot yet distinguish scalable licensing economics from recurring environment-construction labor. Its pre-seed lack of revenue is not an adverse signal.

There is no documented exit obsession, advertising model, or rejection of disconfirming evidence. I also would not label this a standard-model wrapper merely because its technical work is undisclosed. Nevertheless, the exceptions that justify investing before conventional proof require an extraordinary instigator, a quantified enormous consequence, or visible technical progress; this dossier establishes none sufficiently to override the missing gates. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently audited customer experiment showing **at least 5x more successful completions on held-out real workflows per total training-and-environment dollar than that customer’s internal simulator**, with verifier exploitation counted as failure. That result would connect capability, transfer, and economics. It would make this an option worth reconsidering with my own money.
