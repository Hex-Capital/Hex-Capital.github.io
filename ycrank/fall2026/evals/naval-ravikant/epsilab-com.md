# Epsilab -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Epsilab’s founders have built things people used. Hu’s talk2arxiv has 531 GitHub stars; Peng reports $500K in revenue and two million episodes sold at their previous venture. Those are actions, and they deserve more weight than university names. They establish some ability to ship and sell. They do not yet establish specific knowledge of long-horizon reinforcement learning, reward design, or selling environments to AI labs. I give the team partial credit for demonstrated execution, without treating an adjacent accomplishment as mastery of this problem.

The decisive question is what Epsilab owns after a competitor reproduces its environment-building software. Versioned states, rewards, verifiers, and failure replay describe a useful product. The dossier identifies no exclusive workflow rights, accumulated proprietary trajectories, or measured advantage from deployment. Real-life data could supply the scarce asset, but describing the source of data does not establish ownership of it. This is missing evidence of durability, rather than proof that the architecture is inherently commoditized.

There is a credible need underneath the product. METR’s May 2026 report documents benchmark saturation and cheating failures. But demand for better training does not automatically become demand for an independent environment supplier. HUD offers a builder marketplace; Prime Intellect bundles environments with compute and training. Its reported annualized revenue above $100 million establishes spending on the integrated stack, without isolating spending on environments. Epsilab names AI labs and agent developers, but the dossier does not establish the adjacent buying path that turns this wedge into a large standalone business.

The strongest objection to passing is that reliable long-horizon verification could become the bottleneck as agent capabilities improve. Epsilab is addressing that bottleneck directly, and its founders have enough demonstrated execution to make the possibility credible. That fits my pattern of funding infrastructure where scarce inputs constrain abundant software. Yet the other compounding mechanisms remain unproven: multiplayer agents are not a customer network effect, and converting workflows does not establish permissionless contributions or reusable value flowing between customers. My exceptions for products before public adoption require unusually convincing product or founder evidence; the dossier supplies no comparable environment artifact or technical result. It also supplies no evidence of dishonesty, artificial fundraising pressure, or founder instability. Those vetoes are not triggered.

I pass on the present evidence. Epsilab has no disclosed payer, pricing, or revenue, so monetization receives the missing-evidence default; its founders’ earlier sales stay in the founder assessment. The problem is worth solving, but I cannot yet identify the asset that compounds as it is solved. My one reopening condition is a customer-verified deployment showing that exclusively licensed workflow trajectories improve held-out agent success beyond training on public environments. That would turn the proposed data advantage into something I could underwrite with my own money.
