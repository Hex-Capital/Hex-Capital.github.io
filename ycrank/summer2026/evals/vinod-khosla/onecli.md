# OneCLI -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence case is underdeveloped. OneCLI addresses a real problem: autonomous agents acting through employee identities can expose credentials or execute unauthorized production actions at machine speed. But a $1.8 billion agent-security TAM growing 38.9% is a market forecast, not a quantified societal transformation. The dossier never estimates credential-loss costs, expert labor displaced, enterprises protected, or a mechanism producing a 10x–100x reduction in security expense. Today this is a useful control plane, not yet an option on rewriting a consequential cost curve.

The technical mechanism is better than the consequence argument. Per-employee sandboxes, wire-level credential injection, policy enforcement, audit logs, and human approval distinguish OneCLI from credentials stored with OpenClaw, Hermes, or a channel-bound Claude Tag. Yet there is no measured 5–10x benchmark against Aembit, Pomerium, or an incumbent IAM stack. Worse, proxy-unaware tools and raw sockets can bypass enforcement unless egress lockdown is enabled—and lockdown is off by default. A security gateway that can be bypassed by default has not demonstrated a discontinuity. The July host-enforcement patch shows responsiveness, but a repaired defect is not proof of an auditable security advantage.

The strongest case is the team. Jonathan Fishner built zero-trust access at Axis Security; Guy Ben-Aharon was Argon Security’s first engineer before its acquisition. They previously built ChartDB together to more than $10,000 MRR and roughly 22,000 GitHub stars, then changed OneCLI from a credential vault into a per-employee agent harness. That is documented execution and belief revision, not résumé theater. The current repository’s approximately 3,300 stars and NanoClaw’s use of OneCLI as its sole credential path suggest the open-source wedge could become a platform. This is the company’s best evidence.

But the traction cannot yet validate that platform thesis. The claimed 350,000 downloads combine the old vault and current harness, Product Hunt produced 143 points, and the “Trusted by” logos are not identified as paid deployments. No retention, current-product usage, or revenue is disclosed despite published prices of $149 and $499 per month. The exception for a tiny, adaptable team entering a giant existing value pool therefore does not clear: the adaptability is visible, but the dossier has neither established the giant value pool nor shown that OneCLI owns its enforcement point.

The largest kill risk is concrete: can the gateway remain mandatory when arbitrary agents use proxy-unaware tools, raw sockets, and changing protocols? OneCLI supplies no experiment, pass/fail threshold, cost, or stop condition for answering it. Meanwhile Aembit already brokers secretless credentials, Pomerium offers an open-source per-request gateway, and Cisco owns Astrix. Apache-2.0 distribution may win developers, but it also leaves the mechanism copyable. Pricing demonstrates packaging, not a bottom-up path to superior economics or durable gross margins.

I pass today. The one verifiable flip condition is an independent red-team report showing zero credential exposure or policy bypass across at least 100,000 adversarial agent requests—including raw-socket and proxy-unaware traffic—with enforcement enabled by default in production. If OneCLI proves that while converting several named enterprises into paid deployments, the technical gateway could become infrastructure rather than another agent-security implementation. Until then, exceptional founders are carrying a thesis whose consequence, benchmark, and risk-removal plan remain unproven.
