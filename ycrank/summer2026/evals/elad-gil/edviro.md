# Edviro -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: facilities teams can now connect fragmented building systems without replacing them, and the controlling variable is verified energy savings per deployment dollar after integration and support costs. Edviro has identified the causal loop—ingest operational data, detect waste, execute an intervention, and verify the result against meters—but it has not yet published the threshold that makes this repeatable software rather than bespoke energy consulting.

Demand is the strongest evidence. Edviro reached $20K ARR in three months, operates across seven school sites, describes 29 more as expanding, and reports $400K of customer savings. A construction-company measurement-and-verification pilot also suggests the product can travel beyond schools. These are unusually useful pre-seed signals, although they remain company-reported: there is no named customer case study, cohort retention, contract expansion, or independently verified savings methodology.

The product is more substantial than an anomaly-detection dashboard. It connects bills, meters, building-management systems, sensors, and work orders; it can change schedules or controls; and it checks outcomes against meter data. Better models should improve diagnosis and simulation while Edviro retains integrations, permissions, intervention history, and responsibility for the completed workflow. That is real architectural depth. The weakness is that Siemens, GridPoint, 75F, and Clockworks already occupy adjacent parts of the loop, while Trane’s acquisition of BrainBox AI shows incumbents can buy autonomous-control capability and distribute it through existing channels.

The missing piece is a genuine economic discontinuity. “The cost of building has collapsed” explains why two founders can assemble the software, but not why customers suddenly adopt it, why integrations become inexpensive, or why Edviro earns software margins. Free audits may conceal substantial deployment labor. If every building requires custom data normalization, controls work, and savings adjudication, the reported customer value can be large while Edviro’s own gross margin and deployment velocity remain constrained.

The bull case is sharper than a generic energy-software story: $400K of claimed savings against $20K ARR implies considerable pricing headroom, and expansion from seven to 36 sites could turn one district relationship into reusable distribution. Shah also encountered roughly $360K of school utility overbilling directly, while Siripurapu brings ML and infrastructure experience. But neither the portfolio pattern of entering a newly forming technology cluster nor the physical-world exception applies yet. There is no documented market reconstruction, rare frontier technology, or quantified margin shift that overrides the incumbent-channel and services risks.

My 36-month base case is $1M-$3M ARR concentrated in schools and construction measurement-and-verification, followed by either a sale to a building-controls incumbent or a ceiling caused by integration-heavy deployments. I would monitor rather than invest today. The single flip condition is a third-party-verifiable cohort of at least 25 paid sites showing over 70% gross margin after deployment labor, renewal or expansion after 12 months, and IPMVP-verified savings; that would demonstrate that the workflow compounds economically rather than merely producing valuable projects.
