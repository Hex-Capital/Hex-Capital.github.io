# Fabraix -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Ahmed Aly ran fraud systems processing more than $1 billion annually, while Ibrahim Abdu built production-debugging agents at Meta; Fabraix is a lived-adjacency security company whose strongest signal is founder proximity and whose weakest is command authority. **Crusoe** is the high rung: Chase Lochmiller could enter an oil field with social license because he was *of* the environment. Fabraix sits below Crusoe but above the pedigreed AI tourist—Aly has fought adversarial fraud and Abdu has built the agents Fabraix now attacks. The divergence matters: neither founder is shown personally owning the moment when a customer-facing agent failed in production. Fabraix has strong Tiz, credible Biz, and unobserved Rizz; the dossier establishes technical adjacency, not yet autobiographical inevitability.

**HQ Trivia** is the low rung because two talented founders without an unambiguous tiebreaker eventually turned product magic into organizational shrapnel. Fabraix names Aly and Abdu only as “Founder,” despite a four-year working relationship; Fabraix names no CEO, equity split, operating authority, or conflict protocol. That is not a pre-seed traction omission. It is my specific red flag: two people can share a codebase, but they cannot share the last word. The dossier’s silence puts Fabraix near the HQ Trivia rung, and no explosive consumer pull yet earns the Known Exception that temporarily excused HQ’s structure.

Lakera already sells automated red-teaming, Mindgard offers continuous testing, and Prompt Security sold to SentinelOne; over the next 18 months, Fabraix’s “magically weird” signal either compounds through proprietary attack trajectories or evaporates into a feature checklist. Fabraix has 10,000-plus jailbreaks and an adaptive self-play loop, but Fabraix also open-sources Playground inside a market crowded with Promptfoo, Garak, and PyRIT. Fifty thousand challenge attempts can become a learning flywheel, yet there is no disclosed exclusive corpus, remediation loop, or investor rejection on aesthetic grounds. Cybersecurity for AI agents is timely, useful, and increasingly consensus—not Crusoe’s gas flares stapled to Bitcoin in North Dakota.

Prompt Security’s reported $300 million acquisition shows that an AI-security wedge can become strategically valuable, but Fabraix would flip my frontier-and-regulation score only if the software crossed into a hard boundary: autonomous testing against production permissions, regulated audit evidence, or liability-bearing certification. Fabraix currently performs black-box attacks against chat, voice, browser, and coding agents, which is more consequential than an undifferentiated AI wrapper but remains software that established security platforms can bundle. A named bank accepting Fabraix output as compliance evidence—or Fabraix assuming contractual liability for missed exploits—would move the category from fashionable security tooling toward the ugly, regulated terrain I like.

**Niantic** was tactile because I could play Ingress and feel the location graph before reading a metric. Fabraix’s Playground supplies a weaker but real parallel: a person can launch an attack, observe an agent break, and see why the artifact matters; 50,000-plus attempts are more alive than a slide full of TAM. Fabraix still stops short of Niantic because 71 GitHub stars and Product Hunt rank #6 do not demonstrate repeated operator dependence. The physical-world test passes halfway: Fabraix can be used and observed, but its value remains mediated through a developer-security abstraction rather than a camera, ride, drone, or game appearing in ordinary life.

Fifty thousand Playground attempts and vulnerabilities found in agents at dozens of Fortune 500 companies lead me to a narrow 36-month forecast: **Fabraix will be acquired by Meta for its adaptive attack technology before Fabraix exceeds $5 million ARR**. Fabraix’s founder-market proximity should produce technically impressive exploits, while open-source replication, platform bundling, and unresolved CEO authority make an independent distribution machine less likely. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing: the likely asset is not a durable assessment business but a compact adversarial-research engine and two founders a platform buyer already understands.

The 78% AgentHarm success rate is the strongest case against that forecast. Fabraix could turn every assessment into proprietary attack-and-remediation data, convert the Fortune 500 vulnerability work into recurring contracts, and use Playground the way **HQ Trivia** used tactile product pull—the Known Exception where something people can feel outruns imperfect governance. That bull mechanism stings because Lakera and Prompt Security validate acquisition demand, while Fabraix may be technically ahead. Fabraix has not yet shown the evidence that wins the argument: the Fortune 500 organizations are not identified as paying customers, remediation outcomes are undisclosed, and no corpus advantage is measured. I would flip to Invest if Fabraix names one CEO with tiebreaker authority and verifies at least three recurring Fortune 500 customers whose post-remediation agents Fabraix retests.
