# Erinys -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the interesting version of Erinys is not “AI helps lawyers write faster.” It is a new firm-creation system: a plaintiff lawyer chooses a practice, receives cases and working capital, and operates through shared intake, staffing, billing, collections, and compliance infrastructure. That would return meaningful occupational control. But the dossier does not map the intervening transitions or show that Erinys has crossed even the first one. Eve’s 1,000 firms and 200,000 cases are a leak from that future, but they validate legal AI generally—not Erinys’s network.

The sharpest thing here is the rule, not the model. ABA Rule 5.4 restricts nonlawyer ownership and fee sharing; Arizona requires an Alternative Business Structure license. Erinys proposes to supply capital and operate firms precisely where those constraints determine who controls economics and clients. That could become a wonderfully irritating system to unwind. Yet the dossier never explains whether Erinys owns licensed entities, charges fixed service fees, or participates in case proceeds. Until that mechanism is explicit, the regulatory rent is simultaneously the opportunity and the object blocking the company.

The founders have built something real before: in April 2026, Sasha Zyuzin and Jason Guo demonstrated an investigation prototype querying 22 government databases. Their OSINT, offensive-security, and software-defined-radio work is more substantive than three people discovering legal AI after reading a market report. But the artifact proves obsession with investigative systems, not with launching plaintiff firms. By August, Erinys had expanded from a False Claims Act tool into software, staff, capital, compliance, and firm operations. That is an enormous increase in organizational surface area without a lawyer, bar admission, converted practice, or named operating partner in the dossier.

The bull case stings because full-stack operations could be much harder to copy than another legal interface. Each operated firm could expose bottlenecks, improve shared workflows, and generate proprietary matter data; Lawhive’s reported $35 million in 2025 revenue shows that AI-enabled legal service delivery can become a business, while Eve’s adoption shows firms will place AI inside live cases. If Erinys owns the operating loop, it may sit beneath the visible application wave. Today, however, that loop is company-stated. No member firm, case volume, revenue, or repeated lawyer behavior demonstrates control of it, and existing firms can buy Eve or Supio without surrendering their operations.

My harder gate is custody. A team moving from 22-database OSINT into privileged case material will encounter consent, confidentiality, supervision, and surveillance questions immediately. The dossier supplies no limiting architecture for data access or model use. Three co-founders are named, but no accountable chief executive or governance tiebreaker is identified. Neither fact proves misconduct or deadlock; both matter before I put in my own money because Erinys intends to control sensitive workflows and capital, not merely sell a tool.

My 36-month base case is that Erinys narrows into investigation and intake software sold to existing plaintiff firms rather than operating a durable multi-firm network. I am not ready to write the check, but this is a curious Neutral rather than a dismissal: the team found a genuine institutional seam and has relevant technical muscle. One milestone flips me—show me a named, independently licensed Erinys-launched firm that has processed 100 paid matters under a disclosed Rule 5.4-compliant structure. That would turn the network from a diagram into a working artifact.
