# Vorelios -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Vorelios has shown what its model can do. It has not yet shown what its founders do when something breaks. Waiting hours for a fluid simulation is a real obstacle for an engineer, but identifying that obstacle is different from documenting a founder overcoming it. I want the sequence: a customer needed something, the existing approach failed, and Daniel McNeil and Krish Kapadia built their way around the failure. The dossier contains no such sequence. That leaves my most important founder test unanswered, rather than failed.

The strongest argument for investing is that two people have already produced something technically concrete. Vorelios reports inference times of 1.1–31 milliseconds on an H100, with NASA Common Research Model errors of 6.37% for pressure and 3.81% for skin friction ([results](https://vorelios.com/results/)). Those are inspectable claims about a particular engineering task. This fits my unstated preference for numerical proof before a financing story. But these are company-measured comparisons against solver outputs. They deserve partial credit until someone can reproduce them and establish where they stop working. A small average error can still conceal the particular error that makes an engineer reject a design tool.

The commercial question is whether that speed changes what a particular engineer does every day. Vorelios has identified aerospace teams, but the dossier identifies no team repeatedly using the product, paying for it, or asking the founders to extend it. A contact form asking about simulation bottlenecks could begin that conversation; it does not establish the answer. Luminary already offers aircraft-wing models and a model-building platform. That makes a narrow, urgently useful workflow especially important. Vorelios could win by making one engineer dramatically more productive, but neither an advantage over research benchmarks nor the broader $14.2 billion CAE market establishes that mechanism. Pricing and revenue are undisclosed; I cannot infer economics from inference speed.

The founders’ backgrounds make their technical direction intelligible. McNeil studied physics, and Kapadia worked in Florida’s Trustworthy Engineered Autonomy Lab during 2025–2026. Those facts do not establish that either personally encountered this customer problem. Nor does the dossier show an observation that caused them to discard one product route and ship another. I therefore leave problem origin and adaptability at their missing-evidence defaults. Shared university affiliations also leave their working history unresolved. There is no affirmative evidence of the red flags I worry about—waiting for a partner, delegating the product before building it, or defending a disproven plan—but their absence cannot substitute for demonstrated resourcefulness.

The exception that makes this uncomfortable is **Bindwell**: young technical founders can deserve a check before conventional business evidence exists when measured results expose unusual ability. Vorelios shares that numerical technical ambition. The material difference is that Bindwell’s case included laboratory validation and direct exposure to the problem; Vorelios currently offers internally measured agreement with simulations. Its generated simulation corpus might become a difficult proprietary substrate, another pattern I like, but the dossier does not establish its coverage or exclusivity. The bull case is substantial enough to justify a meeting. It does not yet justify overriding the founder gate.

I would pass on the evidence available. This is a promising technical result with an unproven connection to founder behavior and user need. My verifiable flip condition is one documented customer deployment in which an unfamiliar aircraft geometry exposes a failure, the founders ship a correction within weeks, and the customer independently validates the result and continues using it for design decisions. That single episode would reveal far more than another benchmark table.
