# Locke -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Parth Badhwar has worked in the White House, Deloitte policy, political campaigns, and public-affairs software GTM, but Locke sells separately delivered outcomes through consultations and human lobbyists. Locke is a specific-knowledge founder wrapped in a non-compounding business architecture. The highest signal is founder-market fit; the lowest is network value. Locke may become a good firm. Good firms and great investments are different species.

**Stack Overflow** was the high rung for specific knowledge: developers built a developer product after living the problem, and the shipped artifact made their knowledge legible. Badhwar has similarly lived government affairs across the White House, the DNC, Deloitte, and Clipbook, so Locke sits well above the generic ex-FAANG-founder profile. Locke diverges where the analogy matters: no public repository, proprietary policy system, or technical artifact demonstrates that Badhwar has converted domain knowledge into code that others cannot reproduce. Specific knowledge is present. Its leveraged embodiment is not.

**Perplexity** lacked direct user-added network value, a low-rung weakness that would ordinarily limit asymmetric upside; it escaped through Aravind Srinivas’s extreme technical depth, permissionless product distribution, and pre-consensus AI timing. Locke has the same missing network but none of those documented overrides. One Locke client does not automatically improve another client’s monitoring, lobbying access, or proposal outcome, and Locke has disclosed no shared dataset that compounds across engagements. Without that mechanism, Locke remains many independent client relationships disguised as one platform.

Locke’s consultation CTA, human lobbyists, and two openings paying as much as $200,000 reveal the leverage architecture. Over 18 months, every new Locke account creates more briefs, stakeholder work, compliance exposure, and lobbying execution; absent automation evidence, revenue pulls labor behind it. Hiring policy staff before Locke has disclosed unprompted organic adoption is one of my explicit red flags. Code should replicate the founder’s judgment. At Locke, code currently appears to assist people whose time must still be sold.

Quorum serves more than 2,000 organizations, FiscalNote reports $84.1 million in ARR, and AI-related federal lobbying organizations grew from 158 to 451 in one year, so Locke has room to maneuver across monitoring, compliance, contracting, and stakeholder intelligence. The market score flips upward if Locke can remove the service layer and become the system of record connecting policy events, relationships, proposals, and contracting outcomes across industries. The dossier instead describes a full-service government-affairs firm with no public TAM and no documented proprietary dataset. Locke has adjacent markets, but pivots funded by bespoke retainers are not the same as pivots enabled by software leverage.

**Notion** earned the long-term-partner bet because Ivan Zhao’s obsession survived repeated rebuilds and appeared in the product before it appeared in the financing story. Badhwar’s policy career gives Locke energy and credible founder-product-market fit, but Locke provides no public co-founder history, prior venture outcome, or evidence about how Badhwar treats partners under pressure. Approximately 4,000 LinkedIn followers signal reach, not integrity. I apply the absent-evidence default: intelligence looks likely, energy is visible in the first partners and active hiring, and the ten-year relationship remains unproven.

Locke’s first few partners and growing pipeline lead to a falsifiable forecast: by July 2029, Locke will generate $1–3 million in annualized revenue primarily from human-delivered retainers, remain a technology-enabled lobbying firm rather than a network product, and look more like a Quorum acquisition target than an independent venture-scale platform. Locke can sell because Badhwar knows the buyer and the workflow. Locke will struggle to compound because relationships, regulatory accountability, and proposal execution remain attached to expensive humans.

Locke’s strongest bull case is Anchorage Digital: regulation can turn permission into a moat, and Locke could convert citation-backed briefs, stakeholder maps, and contracting outcomes into a proprietary government-intelligence graph while demand expands with AI lobbying. That case stings because Locke already has partners, direct policy experience, and a market in which trust matters more than self-service convenience. The dossier still says no defensibility signals, no verification process, no proprietary dataset, and no customer names, while Quorum, FiscalNote, Plural, Polintel, and Sporos already sell AI-enabled intelligence. I pass because the unstated pattern is backwards: labor distributes Locke, while code merely assists delivery. I would flip to Invest if Locke demonstrates that more than half of new customers arrive without relationship-led sales and that software-only revenue exceeds service revenue at greater than 70% gross margin.
