# Insurf -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Chang Lu is a Brown BS/MD student building inside a regulated healthcare category, while Insurf offers no evidence that Lu or Bryan Chung ever personally fought a prior-authorization denial. That pairing is Insurf’s signature: an unusually unsexy, regulation-shaped problem attached to founders whose relationship to it appears academic rather than lived. Insurf has Tiz—two IEEE publications, clinical modeling, a source-snapshot methodology—but I cannot yet see the autobiographical scar that usually makes me reach for my own money.

**Mindbloom** is the high rung because regulation, physician involvement, and social discomfort create a moat before software does. Insurf shares that mechanism: production PHI remains disabled until BAAs, vendor coverage, technical gates, and founder approval are complete, while physician-attested appeals preserve policy and outcome snapshots. Insurf diverges where it matters: Mindbloom’s patient experience was the product, whereas Insurf currently shows a synthetic or redacted demo and no issued SOC 2 report, production outcome series, or evidence that its compliance burden has become an advantage rather than a gate.

**The consensus YC AI-agent darling** fails when credentialed founders discover a giant market in a memo and wrap a model around someone else’s pain. That failure mechanism appears uncomfortably close here: Chung brings applied mathematics, computer science, physics, and two IEEE publications; Lu brings neuroscience, economics, and cancer-modeling research; neither founder is documented running hospital appeals or revenue-cycle operations. Insurf may have found an important problem, but Insurf has not shown that the problem found either founder first. No evidence of a lived prior-authorization origin means I apply the absent-evidence penalty.

Amperos has raised $20.2 million to automate portals, calls, collections, and appeals, while Ember, Cofactor AI, and Red Sky Health occupy adjacent pieces of the same workflow. Over the next 18 months, Insurf’s “magically weird” quality will compound only if every resolved claim makes the coverage-decision graph meaningfully harder to copy; otherwise, Insurf becomes another AI appeal drafter competing on integrations and sales execution. The 80.7% overturn rate gives Insurf a sharp economic wedge, but four funded competitors make the category neither lonely nor obviously underpriced.

Chang Lu is explicitly named CEO and Bryan Chung CTO, which is better than the shared-title ambiguity that poisoned HQ Trivia and GameCrush. Insurf would flip this governance score upward if Lu holds documented tiebreaker authority, founder vesting, and a conflict protocol covering clinical judgment, model risk, and whether Insurf may submit an appeal. Until Insurf documents that arrangement, two Brown co-founders with divided functional titles are an organizational suggestion, not proof of who wins the irreconcilable argument.

**Niantic** was observable magic: I could play Ingress, walk outside, and feel the location graph before the spreadsheet arrived. Insurf’s public live demo is more tangible than a buried B2B API, and Insurf’s physician-review and staff-controlled submission workflow gives a hospital user something real to operate. Synthetic or redacted materials still prevent me from watching one denied claim become a posted payment, so the demo is a window into the machine rather than the Flock camera bolted to the street.

Insurf reaches roughly **$1 million to $3 million in ARR by July 2029 but pivots from autonomous appeals toward payer-policy intelligence and physician-attested workflow infrastructure**. Insurf’s regulated evidence trail can become valuable, while the 36.3% ceiling reported for end-to-end healthcare-administration agents makes full automation a brittle promise. I expect the source snapshots and decision graph to survive; I do not expect the original automation framing to remain the center of gravity.

Insurf’s strongest bull case is the **DeepMind exception**: young, credentialed polymaths can compensate for an origin story that is not autobiographical when their disciplines compound into something others cannot build. Chung’s physics-and-ML work, Lu’s BS/MD path, the 80.7% appeal-overturn rate, and CMS rules effective January 1, 2026 could let Insurf accumulate the definitive payer-behavior graph before Amperos notices the deeper game. The case does not yet win because Insurf publishes no graph size, customer count, production outcomes, or proof that private services agreements generate repeat usage. I would flip to Invest if Insurf verifies three production provider customers and at least 10,000 resolved claims showing a greater than 20% lift in recovered revenue against existing workflows.
