# OpenHack -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Ananay Arora has shipped something worth examining. OpenHack published a reproduction of Papermark’s authentication bypass, reported March 1 and patched March 2, 2026. Its open-source scanner is inspectable. These are better evidence than an Apple job title or an ambitious product description. But the disclosure establishes a technical result. It does not establish how Arora behaves when a customer refuses to install, an environment cannot be reproduced, or his first approach fails. That missing sequence matters most to me.

The strongest case for OpenHack is that Arora knows the work he is automating. His cybersecurity research and bug-bounty history precede the company. Exploit verification also attacks a specific problem: someone must determine which scanner findings actually matter. A working scanner and a disclosed vulnerability fit my preference for technical founders who produce proof before asking permission. I give the technical artifact substantial credit, while recognizing that OpenHack’s own disclosure does not establish reliable verification across customer applications. Browser and sandbox verification remain beta, with Docker required for sandbox execution ([repository](https://github.com/openhackai/OpenHack)).

Who wants this badly enough to keep using it? Pocket AI, HydraDB and Docura Health are among the displayed logos, but the dossier establishes neither payment nor repeated use. The 430 stars and 65 forks show attention and some willingness to examine the software. They do not show security teams returning every week. The free entry plan—one initial full scan and five PR reviews monthly—gives OpenHack a way to discover that narrow well. Custom Enterprise pricing supplies a possible business model, rather than evidence that buyers have accepted it.

The objection to passing is substantial: a solo security researcher has already built the product and found a real vulnerability. Requiring mature revenue would miss precisely this kind of early founder. The working-prototype exception therefore deserves consideration. But the dossier does not add the unusual corrective action or unmistakable user pull needed to override my founder gate. OpenHack matches my pattern of concrete technical proof; it has not yet demonstrated the accompanying adoption. Nor does the dossier establish dependence on a large partner, premature sales delegation, or fixation on a ceremonial launch. Those red flags are unproven. The concern is narrower: Aikido already offers autonomous pentesting alongside broader security coverage, so OpenHack must discover a particular job users urgently prefer it for.

I would pass on the present evidence. Arora’s prior work establishes domain familiarity, but it does not document the personal problem that produced this product or a neglected operational burden he has overcome. There is also no documented evidence-driven route change; I would leave that criterion at its default rather than infer rigidity. One verifiable development could change my decision: a dated customer account showing Arora overcoming a blocked deployment with a shipped workaround within weeks, followed by that team repeatedly using and paying for verified findings. That would connect the impressive technical work to the founder behavior and urgent demand on which I would bet my own money.
