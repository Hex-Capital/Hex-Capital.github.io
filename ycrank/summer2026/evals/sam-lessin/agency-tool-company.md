# Agency Tool Company -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: no proposed post-money valuation, no competing-term-sheet count, and no explanation of what this round uniquely funds. Agency Tool Company may be a scarce first check, but the dossier does not prove it. More importantly, “build, deploy and manage real-world robots” is a product category, not a venture moment. The round needs to flip a specific card—perhaps converting Burro, Tempo Works, and Gather AI from private beta into paid fleets by a dated milestone—but no such capital plan, price, or pass/fail result is disclosed.

The founders are the real asset. Jack Morrison and Davis Foster built and deployed hundreds of autonomous mowers at Scythe, operated the company from 2018 through its 2026 acquisition by ASI, and are now productizing infrastructure they personally lacked. This is unusually strong outsider-insider evidence: they shipped the robots and endured the deployment system around them. Byte-level deltas, resumable transfers, device-specific artifacts, targeting, rollback, and audit history address concrete field failures rather than an invented software inconvenience.

The early workflow signal is credible but incomplete. Three beta partners across agriculture, construction, and logistics suggest that the pain travels beyond Scythe, and a claimed 20x improvement over Docker pull or SCP could materially change fleet operations. But these are company-reported benchmarks and private betas, not purchases, retained usage, or fleet expansion. The ROS launch, public waitlist, 388 LinkedIn followers, and founders’ reach are distribution inputs. They do not establish that Agency Tool Company owns access that reliably causes economic action.

The strongest bull case is that beta status understates the signal: repeat robotics founders with an exit have persuaded three real fleet operators to place deployment infrastructure inside operational systems where failures matter. That is exactly the sort of physical-world translation layer that can deepen into deployment history, approvals, integrations, and switching costs. If those partners are repeatedly shipping production updates through ATC, the workflow may already be more proprietary than the public dossier reveals.

The bear case still wins today. Balena, Mender, Formant, Foxglove, and Memfault already occupy adjacent control points and can add robot-specific transfer features; ATC itself supports standard OCI containers and arbitrary bundles, making migration easier in both directions. There is no pricing, revenue, gross margin, burn, runway, or route to break-even. There is also no disclosed decision structure between two long-standing co-founders, which matters when product scope expands from Deploy into hosted embedded-hardware Build. Without paid conversion, the 20x benchmark risks becoming metric theater around a feature that funded platforms can copy.

I pass at the current evidence level, despite exceptional founder-market fit. My one verifiable flip condition is a priced seed below a $10 million post-money whose proceeds are explicitly tied to converting all three named beta partners into retained paid deployments by a fixed date. That would simultaneously show scarce pricing, a finite truth-revealing experiment, and workflow-driven purchase behavior. Without it, I am underwriting accomplished founders inside a plausible market, but not yet a cheap, one-and-done experiment.
