# Olam Labs -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Olam Labs has demonstrated activity, not consequence. Its arena handles more than 1,000 daily matches, nearly 1 billion tokens per day, and 86,729 graded table-talk turns. Those numbers prove that two founders can build and operate a technically demanding system. They do not quantify which societal cost pool collapses if social-strategy evaluation works. The dossier identifies a $2.25 billion evaluation-services market, but no affected population, expert bottleneck, or 10x economic outcome. This is presently a tool market, not yet an option on world-changing infrastructure.

The technical idea is more substantive than another evaluation dashboard. Deterministic multiplayer environments, identical human and model action spaces, and linked game states and transcripts could produce a proprietary behavioral learning loop. Yet Olam has not benchmarked that mechanism against a named incumbent on prediction accuracy, evaluation cost, or ability to detect consequential failures. Most importantly, no published evidence connects Social Poker performance to production-agent behavior. A distinctive test is not a 5–10x discontinuity until it predicts something customers cannot measure another way.

The largest kill risk is game-to-deployment transfer. Olam is spending enough compute to process nearly 1 billion tokens daily while expanding a free arena, but the dossier provides no decisive transfer experiment, pass/fail threshold, budget, or stop condition. That reverses the risk-removal sequence: volume is increasing before benchmark validity is established. The cheapest useful experiment is not another million matches. It is a blinded comparison testing whether Olam’s measurements predict failures in real multi-agent workflows better than application traces or conventional evals.

The bull case is that the corpus itself becomes the platform. Thousands of users are already generating interactions, the environments are replayable, and Om Buddhdev’s AI Dungeon experience combines naturally with Shreshth Sharma’s reinforcement-learning and data-engineering background. Competitors with $68 million to $250 million raised may be optimized around pairwise voting, observability, or digital workflows, leaving social coordination undermeasured. If frontier labs conclude that negotiation and deception are gating deployment, Olam could own a scarce dataset before larger platforms recognize the category.

That case still collides with two red flags. Patronus AI is already building simulated digital environments, so games alone are not a structural barrier. More seriously, Olam discloses neither enterprise pricing nor the cost of producing a useful evaluation while giving away a compute-intensive product. Relevant founder backgrounds earn credibility, but the dossier shows no material belief revision, difficult complementary recruit, or explicit owner for transfer validity and inference economics. Friendship since middle school is cohesion; it is not yet risk-tuned recruiting.

I would pass with my own money today. The likely route is a pivot from a public social-game benchmark toward paid custom agent-training or workflow simulations, because that is where transfer validity and economics can be tested together. One result flips my decision: a blinded paid deployment study with a named frontier lab showing Olam’s scores predict real multi-agent failures at least twice as well as the lab’s incumbent evaluation method. Until then, this is an intriguing measurement instrument spending aggressively before proving that it measures the consequential variable.
