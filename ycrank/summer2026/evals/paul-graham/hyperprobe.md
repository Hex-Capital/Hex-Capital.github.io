# HyperProbe -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Shailendra Singh and Karan Raina have already built two startups together, which makes HyperProbe a repeat-founder pair entering a real schlep zone with surprisingly little public hacker trail. That pairing is HyperProbe’s signature. Singh and Raina have spent years building production-traffic testing infrastructure, yet neither founder has a verified public repository, open-source contribution, or Hacker News artifact. HyperProbe therefore looks stronger as an organization than as a hacker cult. I would invest, but I would not yet call HyperProbe the next Stripe.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for co-founder configuration: two brothers who had been building together long before they needed to manufacture startup chemistry. Singh and Raina share the load-bearing part of that pattern. They built Transporter.city together, founded HyperTest in 2019, and carried the same relationship into HyperProbe. HyperProbe diverges from Stripe because the Collisons’ complementary hacker abilities were visible in the artifacts themselves, while HyperProbe’s dossier shows an eight-person organization and founder résumés more clearly than it shows who personally wrote the difficult code. The pair is unusually durable; the division of founder labor is less legible.

**Loopt** (2005) is the low rung’s warning: force of personality can temporarily override a thin public technical artifact, but it cannot make weak product timing disappear. HyperProbe does not repeat the worst version of that failure because HyperProbe has shipped an SDK that places non-blocking probes in JVM and Node services, and Housing.com says it exposed a race condition within an hour. But the unstated hacker pattern remains weak. Karan Raina has a Georgia Tech computer-science MS and was a LimeTray core engineer, yet HyperProbe provides no verified GitHub trail, competitive-programming result, or pre-company hack. Credentials are not genealogy. The working production probe prevents a low score; the absence of visible fingerprints caps it.

HyperTest reached more than 100 product teams before Singh and Raina redirected their production-traffic experience into HyperProbe. Resourcefulness compounds over the next 18 months if HyperProbe turns every difficult deployment into another trusted introduction: personally clear security review, install the agent, reproduce the unlogged state, and hand the engineer a fix before Datadog’s dashboard has helped. CheQ Digital’s first-attempt synchronization diagnosis and Housing.com’s same-hour race-condition repair are tracks left by animals, though they are company-published tracks. HyperProbe has survived a prior startup, raised $1.5 million around the predecessor, and pivoted toward a sharper pain. What is missing is the cereal-box moment: no named rejection overturned, impossible customer won, or unscalable revenue stunt.

HyperTest’s production-traffic testing work gives HyperProbe an unusually plausible organic origin. Singh and Raina did not discover debugging through a market map; HyperProbe grew beside the system they had operated since 2019. The score would flip into the highest range if HyperProbe could show that Raina built the first probe to diagnose a failure HyperTest itself could not explain, then used that crude tool repeatedly before anyone asked to buy it. Without that specific founder-pain story, HyperProbe is adjacent to an organic idea rather than conclusively born from one. The distinction matters because genuine organic ideas usually arrive with an anecdote too concrete to omit.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the single useful parallel for HyperProbe’s schlep. Coinbase had to persuade customers that software touching money could be trusted; HyperProbe must persuade security teams that software inspecting live variables can be trusted inside production. HyperProbe already says probes are read-only, require approval, redact PII inside customer infrastructure, and disable themselves at 0.5% CPU impact. Those clauses are not website decoration. They describe the objections Singh and Raina will hear in every serious deployment. Lightrun, Dynatrace’s Rookout, and Datadog’s Ozcode prove the category exists, but HyperProbe’s willingness to live inside approval queues and incident reviews is the possible moat. This is schlep blindness in its native habitat.

Datadog acquired Ozcode in 2021, and my falsifiable prediction is that by July 2029 HyperProbe reaches $3 million to $7 million in ARR and is acquired by Datadog as its agent-native production-debugging layer. HyperProbe’s long-lived founder pair can grind through security-sensitive enterprise installations, while HyperProbe’s weak public hacker genealogy makes a durable standalone developer movement less likely than a technically useful acquisition. The mechanism is repeated production access: enough customer-specific debugging knowledge to become valuable, but not enough bottom-up tribal adoption to displace the observability platforms distributing adjacent tools.

Dynatrace’s Rookout acquisition and Datadog’s Ozcode acquisition supply the strongest bear case: HyperProbe may be building a feature that both likely buyers can already bundle, while Lightrun has raised $26 million to pursue continuous debugging independently. HyperProbe also has no published pricing, no defensibility signal, and only two current-product testimonials; the predecessor’s 100 product teams cannot be silently relabeled as HyperProbe traction. The Known Exception is Loopt: unusually forceful founders can outrun weak timing, and Singh and Raina’s repeated-company history gives that argument some bite. It does not yet beat incumbent distribution and the missing hacker trail. Twelve months of evidence that at least 20 engineering organizations use HyperProbe weekly and pay after security review would flip my verdict from Invest to Strong Invest.
