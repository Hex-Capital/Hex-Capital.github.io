# Ordo Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The best detail about Ordo Labs is that it starts with the files a laboratory already has. That gives the founders a way to put something useful in front of a scientist before integrating with the laboratory’s instruments. But a sensible product decision is not yet evidence of resourcefulness. I want to know which laboratory was blocked, what the founders changed, and how quickly it worked. The dossier describes the resulting product without that sequence. I cannot infer the founders’ response to resistance from the elegance of their proposed shortcut.

There is something built here. Ordo reports reductions exceeding 95% for a first QC pass and 82% for report completion and review. Source-linked values, human approval and electronic signatures make the product more substantial than a report generator ([Ordo website](https://ordolabs.ai/)). Those results deserve technical credit, discounted because they are company-reported. In this setting, speed and correctness are coupled: saving review time matters only if missed deviations do not create more work downstream. The validation package—specifications, traceability documents, qualification protocols and release notices—shows attention to the unpleasant work required to make the software usable ([validation documentation](https://ordolabs.ai/solutions/software-validation)). It does not establish detection accuracy.

The strongest argument for investing is that laboratories in both the US and Europe are already paying for pilots. Payment is a better answer to “who wants this?” than the hundred scientist interviews. A narrow reporting tool could enter through one painful task, become part of an approved workflow, and expand from there. That is a credible mechanism. Yet the dossier supplies neither a named laboratory returning to the product nor evidence that a pilot has become routine use. The paid pilots establish willingness to try; they do not yet establish dependence. Sapio’s existing AI-assisted compliance reporting makes that distinction consequential: Ordo needs to become useful enough that a laboratory chooses it despite an incumbent’s overlapping capabilities.

These founders have relevant ingredients. Tovatt combines more than ten years of software development with chemistry education; Hoang previously co-founded a pharmaceutical-authentication company. Their KTH connection supplies a real relationship origin, though not a record of surviving difficult work together ([Ordo About](https://ordolabs.ai/about)). None of this documents a founder personally encountering this reporting problem, or changing direction after a customer disproved an assumption. I leave those dimensions at their missing-evidence defaults. My preference for concrete technical proof supplies some support here; my recurring interest in programmable tools adopted by technical users supplies little additional support. There is no evidence of users building unexpected workflows on Ordo. Nor does the dossier establish dependence on a major partner, premature delegation, or persistence with a disproven plan.

I would pass on writing a personal check today. The working-product exceptions deserve consideration: functioning software and paid pilots are precisely the evidence that could overcome uncertainty about the founders. Here they are not strong enough to replace the missing demonstration of action under constraint. This could be a useful laboratory business, but I have not yet seen what makes these particular founders unusually hard to stop. One verifiable event would change my decision: a dated customer case showing a failed qualification requirement, the founders shipping a correction within weeks, and that laboratory subsequently using the corrected workflow repeatedly in paid production. That would connect the product to the people in the way this dossier currently cannot.
