# Preload -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Preload’s strongest asset is the founders’ experience measuring biological signals. Wang’s EMG and motion-tracking research and neural-hardware fabrication are directly relevant to the proposed product. Li and Pandiaraju add robotics and neuroengineering experience. These are credible places to acquire specific knowledge. But the evidence comes principally from founder profiles; the dossier does not identify a dated sensing result, deployed system, or customer outcome attributable to this team. I can underwrite relevant experience more readily than demonstrated mastery.

The technical opportunity is real. Video cannot tell a robot everything about contact. Touch2Robot’s September 2026 results support the usefulness of contact-aware feedback, giving Preload’s combination of egocentric video, tactile gloves, and EMG a concrete problem to solve. The strongest bull case is that these founders can extract information from human demonstrations that competing collection systems miss. If that information transfers across robot embodiments, Preload could own a valuable input to physical intelligence. That possibility deserves attention before revenue exists.

What survives a competitor copying the collection software? The dossier establishes a sensor combination, but no proprietary sensor, exclusive corpus, or demonstrated calibration advantage. Axonnic already collects EMG; Human Archive already collects multimodal wearable data. TACROSS’s October work also shows researchers addressing cross-sensor alignment. Hardware involvement fits my recent preference for physical bottlenecks, but the durable asset still needs to be identified. Preload has not demonstrated that its particular integration becomes harder to reproduce with use.

The founders currently solicit manipulation-policy, VLA, and world-model teams directly. Those are identifiable buyers sharing a data requirement. Surgery offers another possible application, but the dossier establishes interest rather than a demonstrated expansion path. The quoted $1.31 billion robot-training-data market includes simulation and teleoperation; I cannot assign all that spending to this collection stack. More importantly, no evidence shows one customer’s participation improving another customer’s results. Reusable, lawfully shareable datasets could create that mechanism. Bespoke collections could instead require fresh labor for every contract. Neither outcome is established.

Preload also resembles my pattern of turning individual knowledge into scalable output: instrument human skill once, then train machines from it repeatedly. The missing link is evidence that the output can actually be reused. The October 1 launch video’s 246,186 tracker-reported views demonstrate attention, not that reuse mechanism. Pricing, completed purchases, and revenue remain undisclosed, so direct value capture receives the missing-evidence default. I see no documented integrity problem or manufactured financing urgency; my hesitation concerns the business mechanism.

I would pass today. My exceptions for pre-revenue companies require another unusually strong signal: self-reinforcing usage, a demonstrated exceptional prototype, or founder actions that overwhelm uncertainty elsewhere. Preload’s relevant research experience makes the opposing case credible, but the dossier does not establish those overrides. I would reopen on one verifiable result: a paid repeat deployment showing that a dataset Preload retains contractual rights to reuse improves an independent customer’s held-out robot-task success over a video-plus-touch baseline. That would begin connecting the founders’ specific knowledge to an asset that compounds.
