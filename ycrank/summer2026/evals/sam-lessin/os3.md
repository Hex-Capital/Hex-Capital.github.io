# OS3 -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I would begin with the blank financing record: no proposed post-money valuation, no competing-term-sheet count, and no evidence that this is a genuinely scarce first check. That earns the low prior, not an assumption that YC standard terms are cheap. More importantly, OS3 has not named what the round buys as a finite experiment. “Build better robots and deploy them” is a company plan; it is not a venture moment with a deadline, pass/fail metric, and dramatic value change.

The strongest fact is that OS3 says robots are already deployed in hospitality. A mobile platform with two five-kilogram arms operating in a hotel is more informative than a simulation or TAM slide, and physical complexity is an unstated pattern I will tolerate when software reorganizes a neglected operating system. But the causal chain stops exactly where underwriting begins: no hotel name, unit count, paid status, task-success rate, intervention rate, uptime, or retention. The deployment therefore shows possible workflow access, not that OS3’s access causes a purchase.

The bull case stings. Rishabh Chanana brings robotics and model-training research; Chris Hailey shipped production software at Coinbase on a team associated with more than $19 billion in trading volume and previously built a project reported at $10K MRR. Their in-house loop—hardware generating proprietary task and failure data that changes both the models and the body—could become a real dark pool. If the hospitality deployments are paid and recurring, OS3 may already possess the kind of operating receipt that can override missing seed gates.

I cannot underwrite that exception from the dossier. Pudu already markets a mobile two-arm D7, while Apptronik and 1X have raised vastly more capital. Training first on internet and first-person video is not itself proprietary, and OS3 publishes no result showing that limited robot data closes the reliability gap. The company is exposed to the AI red flag: the model story may improve with industry-wide inputs while the expensive hardware, safety burden, and field failures remain OS3’s alone.

Deleting the next financing makes the problem sharper. There is no unit price, gross margin, manufacturing cost, burn, runway, labor-replacement ratio, or route to break-even; parts sourced across Korea, Taiwan, and China add capital and supply-chain risk. There is also no disclosed decision hierarchy between two founders who met at a hackathon, and neither founder has documented hotel-operating experience. My 36-month base case is that OS3 pivots from hotel kitchens toward warehouse or production-line deployments and remains below $1 million ARR because constrained environments produce better demonstrations but still fail to prove one-and-done economics.

I am passing at this evidence level. The one verifiable flip condition is a named hotel deployment in which at least five paid robots complete 90 consecutive days with disclosed intervention frequency and an operator-verified payback below 18 months. That single result would prove technical reliability, workflow ownership, customer purchase, and plausible cash economics strongly enough to revisit even imperfect round evidence.
