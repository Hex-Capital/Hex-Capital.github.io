# Micora -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Micora has a credible reason to exist. I cannot yet identify the deal I would buy. Post-money valuation, competing term sheets, and whether my money supplies a genuinely scarce check are all undisclosed. That earns my missing-evidence default, not an assumption that YC has created an auction. The other primary gate is equally unresolved: the dossier supplies no capital plan naming the experiment, deadline, and result this round purchases. “100 kWp installed daily” is a product claim; it becomes a venture moment when the financing pays to establish something specific about its commercial value.

The founders are the strongest evidence here. Akemoto shipped heat pumps at Normal Robotics; Choo designed drivetrains and handled manufacturing at Serve Robotics, then deployed solar/BESS at Tesla ([YC founder profiles](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/micora)). This is an unusually concrete bridge between robotics and energy installation. It fits my pattern of backing teams that can reorganize difficult physical operations because they understand both systems. I give that operating history substantial credit. I cannot also count it as proof of proprietary customer access or cheap capital.

The commercial mechanism worth testing is whether Micora removes enough installation labor to change which remote power projects make economic sense. BoxPower already sells prefabricated microgrids, so packaging generation and batteries together does not establish that advantage. Micora’s forklift-placeable modules could be a sensible intermediate product, and forklift placement can coexist with robotic deployment. But the October 21 LAKI collaboration concerns **planned** robotic deployment; neither that announcement nor the autonomous-installation claim establishes field labor savings ([LAKI coverage](https://news.videoland.com.tw/article/02faa288-c248-44af-b1ea-7ddbadbccb6f.html)). Falling battery prices help competing integrators too. The scarce asset would have to emerge from deployment execution or customer access.

The strongest argument against passing is that the team may already be crossing that gap. Micora reports deployments on Pacific islands and at manufacturing sites—exactly where fuel logistics and scarce installation labor could make speed valuable. Those claims deserve attention at pre-seed. They do not yet establish paid conversion, repeat orders, or an owned workflow that causes purchases. Likewise, the purchase and 12/24-month lease options establish how customers can transact, but disclose no margin, burn, or cash-recovery schedule ([Micora order page](https://www.micora.com/order)). Leasing raises a particularly consequential question: who finances the equipment before customer receipts repay it? The dossier does not answer that, so I assign the economics default rather than presume an uneconomic business.

I pass on this evidence. My exceptions to missing financing gates require operating receipts strong enough to override them: demonstrated conversion, cash economics, or proprietary access. Unnamed deployments do not yet do that. There is also no evidence of auction pressure, equal-founder deadlock, or financing-dependent survival; those red flags remain unestablished. My one verifiable condition for reopening underwriting is a customer-accepted field report demonstrating 100 kWp deployed in one day with positive contribution margin after equipment, delivery, installation, commissioning, and warranty provisions. That would connect Micora’s strongest asset—the founders’ operating experience—to an economic advantage. Price and the round’s finite purpose would still determine whether I write a check.
