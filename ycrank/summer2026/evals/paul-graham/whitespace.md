# Whitespace -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Alex Tung and Leon Yao have been friends for 12 years, lived and worked together, and both grew up around wholesale-distribution businesses. Whitespace is therefore a strong-founder-pair company with a weaker animal trail: Whitespace has unusually good founder configuration, but no cereal-box moment, no rejected-application prototype, and no disclosed unscalable revenue stunt. The pair looks built to endure a schlep. The dossier does not yet prove they will attack one like animals.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for founder configuration: two brothers with years of shared history, complementary abilities, and commerce software already in their bloodstream. Whitespace has the same load-bearing mechanism in Tung and Yao’s 12-year friendship, shared living and working history, and complementary commercial and technical coverage. Whitespace diverges because the Collisons had already built and sold Auctomatic, while Tung and Yao’s dossier does not identify a substantial artifact they built together before Whitespace. This is a strong pair, though not yet a proven pair.

**Kiko** (2005) exposed the low-rung failure mechanism on resourcefulness: shipping something and finding an exit did not itself prove the kind of determination that later made Kan and Shear worth re-funding for Twitch. Whitespace similarly cannot substitute motion for animal behavior. Yao launched an AI slide maker after 30-plus consultant interviews and Whitespace pivoted three months later, which shows speed, while Kingfisher Direct and Warwick show that Whitespace has crossed the line from demo to deployment. But Whitespace discloses no revenue, no story of winning an impossible customer, and no improvised act that explains how those two deployments happened. Animals leave tracks. These are footprints, but they are still faint.

Both founders grew up in wholesale-distribution families, Tung advised manufacturers and distributors at BCG, and Yao built AI products for logistics-distribution companies at InstaDeep. That makes Whitespace unusually organic. Over 18 months this should compound because Whitespace can recognize the ugly exceptions hidden inside purchase orders, replenishment rules, and customer emails without first learning the industry’s vocabulary from consultants. Each implementation can sharpen the founders’ sense of which workflows matter. The danger is that Whitespace’s earlier slide-maker beta suggests the founders may have reached the right problem by searching rather than by needing the product themselves. Family exposure is close to the Dropbox forgotten-USB-stick pattern, but it is not identical to personally being the user.

SAP, NetSuite, Epicor, Infor, Dynamics, Sage, QuickBooks, Gmail, and Outlook make Whitespace a schlep company almost by definition. Whitespace must normalize contradictory records, handle purchase-order mistakes, encode approval thresholds, and survive customers blaming the agent for errors originating in their ERP. I would cut this score sharply if Kingfisher Direct and Warwick merely received read-only dashboards assembled through standard APIs. Evidence that Whitespace is writing transactions into production systems, with customer-defined approvals and exception review, puts it near Coinbase’s compliance grind instead. The integration burden is not incidental execution risk. It is the part mainstream AI founders will try to avoid.

Leon Yao’s IROS reinforcement-learning paper, InstaDeep research work, prior CTO role serving more than 1,000 users, and private-beta slide maker put Whitespace above the credential-only hacker. Dropbox remains the relevant single-anchor parallel: Drew Houston’s demo made the technical ability visible before the company was legible. Whitespace has shipped current-product deployments across inventory planning, customer service, sales analysis, and order processing, but Whitespace has no public repository, Hacker News artifact, competitive-programming record, or nontrivial open-source trail. Yao looks like a real builder. The dossier does not yet show the hacker genealogy that made Houston or the Collisons immediately recognizable.

Kingfisher Direct and Warwick make my 36-month prediction concrete: Whitespace will reach $6–10 million in ARR by July 2029 by narrowing from an “AI operating system” into two or three high-value order-entry and inventory workflows for UK wholesalers, rather than winning as a horizontal replacement for every back-office function. Whitespace can get there because two-to-four-week implementations and a claimed three-month payback create a repeatable wedge, while the unpleasant ERP work filters out wrapper companies. The operating-system story is too broad. The schlep inside it is valuable.

Proton.ai’s $20 million financing, Distro’s quoting product, Stockline’s food-wholesale ERP, and Fresho’s A$17 million Series B form the strongest bear case: Whitespace may be a well-matched pair arriving late to a crowded category with a commoditized agent layer and an integration surface too wide for two people. Whitespace’s slide-maker pivot makes that objection sting because it raises the possibility that the founders are capable searchers rather than obsessed users. Two named customers and production transaction controls beat the pure-wrapper interpretation, while the founders’ family histories beat the tourist interpretation, but neither proves durable pull. I would flip to Pass if Whitespace cannot show at least five paying customers retained for 12 months, with one workflow deployed in under two weeks without founder-written integration code. Until then, I would invest, but I would be investing in the pair and their appetite for the schlep—not in the phrase “$60 trillion industry.”
