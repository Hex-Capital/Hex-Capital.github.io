# RealPact -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

CourseMe reached 4,000 monthly users, 1 million page hits, roughly 90% Dartmouth penetration, and an acquisition before Ranvir Deshmukh and Erik Peterson started RealPact. RealPact is therefore a young-operator bet whose highest signal is verified product distribution and whose lowest is structural protection: capable founders have entered an atoms-light market where incumbents already control workflows. RealPact may build a useful business. Usefulness without monopoly, however, is not power-law shape.

Anduril is the high rung for founder lineage: Palmer Luckey had shipped Oculus to a billion-dollar exit before attacking defense. RealPact shares the important mechanism—Deshmukh and Peterson built and distributed CourseMe together, then sold it—but RealPact diverges by several orders of magnitude. CourseMe’s 4,000 monthly users establish unusual execution for student founders, not experience operating a legally consequential national platform. RealPact sits above the anonymous current-batch founder profile, but well below the shipped-to-scale pedigree that made Anduril’s founder risk invert into conviction.

Asana is the low rung on atoms-heavy or regulated-sector protection: pure workflow software had no physical or regulatory barrier, and only Dustin Moskovitz’s Facebook pedigree justified the exception. The same failure mechanism appears in RealPact. RealPact handles contracts and fragmented government records, but legal consequence is not itself a moat; unfinished SOC 2 work is a cost of admission, not regulatory capture. RealPact owns no hardware, certification regime, exclusive public-record access, or government relationship that would prevent another software vendor from following.

SkySlope already serves more than 900,000 professionals with AI-assisted transaction management, compliance, forms, voice assistance, and back-office products. RealPact’s secret is narrower and somewhat real: retrieving city, county, and state records before populating contracts begins earlier in the workflow than ListedKit’s uploaded-document intake. Over 18 months, however, that secret compounds only if jurisdiction-specific integrations and correction histories become proprietary; otherwise RealPact is merely teaching SkySlope which feature to add. A secret must explain why the incumbent cannot respond. RealPact currently explains only why the incumbent has not responded.

Four Seasons Sotheby’s gives RealPact a credible firm-wide pilot, but not yet a pricing-power hypothesis. RealPact would move up a full tier if the pilot revealed that cross-jurisdiction record normalization produces an exclusive data asset, materially lower error rates, and multi-year brokerage lock-in. Without those facts, every new county integration raises RealPact’s maintenance burden while SkySlope’s installed base lowers its distribution cost. Monopoly is the condition of success; fragmented connectors can become a toll road, but they can also become an expensive feature library.

SpaceX is the single-anchor parallel for definite planning because reusable launch required named technical milestones that could not be replaced by generic customer learning. RealPact discloses paying New Hampshire brokerages, a Sotheby’s pilot, and a prior pivot from the $9.99-per-month SignPact product, but no two-to-five-year architectural commitment. RealPact’s observable sequence—e-signatures, then brokerage automation, then AI agents—looks more like indefinite iteration than a fixed plan to own a specific national data layer. The pivot may be intelligent; it is not yet definite optimism.

The $500,000 YC financing should carry RealPact through enough integrations to discover whether local operational density exists. My 36-month forecast is that RealPact reaches $1 million to $3 million in ARR and is acquired by SkySlope before July 2029, because Deshmukh and Peterson can likely ship and sell a valuable upstream-records module while RealPact lacks the moat to remain an independent monopoly. A tactical acquisition is a good founder outcome. It is not the kind of outcome for which I invest my own money at pre-seed.

Four Seasons Sotheby’s, paying New Hampshire brokerages, and the CourseMe acquisition form the strongest bull case: RealPact could resemble the Asana exception, where exceptional young operators outrun a weak structural thesis and turn workflow depth into durable switching costs. RealPact’s jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction burden could even become schlep-based protection as corrections accumulate. The objection stings, but SkySlope’s 900,000-professional distribution, overlapping voice and compliance products, and RealPact’s absence of exclusive data still win. A three-year exclusive national master agreement with Sotheby’s that makes RealPact the system of record across its brokerages would flip my verdict.
