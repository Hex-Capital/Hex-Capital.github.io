# Perceptron ML -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: a lawyer should be able to ask a firm’s accumulated memory a question, receive an answer tied to primary authority, and let software execute the surrounding workflow without surrendering privileged data. Perceptron ML has one small leak from that world—three reportedly deployed systems for timekeeping, monitoring, and research—but the dossier does not map the intervening transitions. It does not show how bespoke installations become a common operating layer, which technical constraint remains unsolved, or why this two-person team controls the path. A 17.3% market CAGR and rising legal-AI adoption describe today’s wave, not what comes next.

The present system is certainly irritating. Lawyers reconstruct time, monitor dockets manually, and risk sanctions or embarrassment when models invent authorities. But where is the rent? The dossier does not quantify write-offs, research costs, malpractice exposure, or procurement delays, nor identify whose autonomy changes. “We make law firms AI-native” may make firms more efficient, but it does not yet demonstrate that an artificial gatekeeper loses control or that an individual gains it. Built-to-order implementation could even reproduce the expensive bespoke-service economics it is supposed to repair.

Michael Marcotte has real technical tuition: Stanford mathematics and computer science, NVIDIA research, and production agents for hardware debugging and formal verification. Peyton Marcotte built NASA-supported astronaut exercise equipment and obtained institutional grants. Those are first-believer artifacts, not résumé decoration. The missing bridge is legal obsession. Neither brother has documented legal-practice experience, and cached July 2026 positioning described a cross-industry event engine before the August law-firm focus. That may be intelligent discovery, but it could also be a rapid move into a fashionable market. I cannot yet tell who has been living with this particular wound or who is ultimately accountable between the two brothers.

The three deployments are the strongest evidence here. A law firm allowing unfamiliar software inside its matters and environment is costlier behavior than joining a waitlist, and customer-controlled deployment, no shared training, and source-gated output address the consent and custody questions that would otherwise stop me immediately. But the case studies are anonymized, with no dates, payment, repeat usage, retention, or independent accuracy benchmark. The product claims every surfaced fact traces to checked primary sources; until that survives adversarial citation testing, the claimed artifact remains promising rather than proven.

The bull case stings: if three firms have already crossed procurement and confidentiality barriers, Perceptron may have found the unglamorous wedge that broad copilots miss. Reusable integrations and a verified legal-source pipeline could turn early custom work into durable infrastructure. Yet Harvey already supports more than 25,000 customer-created agents, while Legora and Eve bring capital, integrations, and legal distribution. Perceptron currently controls no demonstrated scarce layer beneath them; it looks more like an expert implementation shop sitting on the same model wave. My August 2029 base case is that it remains an implementation-heavy legal-AI business below $3 million ARR rather than becoming a standalone platform.

I pass with curiosity, not contempt. The privacy architecture clears one red-flag gate, but no known exception overrides the combination of a recent positioning change, unverified deployments, crowded consensus demand, and absent six-year causal map. One condition flips me: ten named firms renewing the same source-gated workflow after twelve months, with independently measured citation accuracy and materially declining deployment time. That would show both weirdly costly user commitment and the beginnings of a controlled enabling layer.
