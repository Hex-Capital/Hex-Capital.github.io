# Canos -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

A dog cannot tell you that something feels different three months before the next oncology appointment. By 2032, I can imagine the veterinarian comparing each blood draw against that animal’s molecular history and deciding whether to investigate sooner. Canos’s banked baseline is a small, concrete piece of that future. Getting there requires reliable measurements between visits, then evidence that those changes arrive early enough to alter care. Origin establishes the starting point; OncoTrack is pursuing the next step. That earns my attention, although the clinically useful warning system remains unproven.

The founders give me more confidence than the cancer-detection headline. Ming-shan Tsai and Bin Li previously worked together on veterinary AI pathology at ZYTCA after meeting in Oxford in 2024. Tsai brings animal-disease diagnostics experience and reports that ZYTCA’s pathology product reached 10,000-plus clinics; that distribution belongs to the previous company. Li’s whole-slide tumour-detection repository, with 466 stars, is a tangible research artifact preceding this product. There is relevant work here beyond degrees and impressive employers. I still cannot reconstruct a long, independently documented obsession or determine how much of the previous commercial reach these founders personally built.

Why does the owner have to wait between visits? The dossier identifies a measurement gap, but it does not uncover a license, gatekeeper, fee structure, or artificial restriction that Canos removes. Earlier information could give owners and veterinarians more control. It could also create an expensive question nobody knows how to answer. Origin costs $699 including the veterinary visit, and Canos correctly says a result cannot confirm or exclude cancer. Until a molecular alert leads to a useful decision, I cannot count that information as agency returned.

There is actual scientific substance underneath the AI label. Canos reports an integrated model combining cfDNA fragmentation, copy-number variation, and native methylation, evaluated across 341 dogs and 896 sequencing runs. The 303-dog diagnostic analysis reports 90.6% sensitivity at 95.1% specificity under nested cross-validation. Those are research results, not prospective performance for the marketed assay. They support some enabling-layer credit, but CanCan already sells sequencing-based canine blood tests, and Canos’s benchmark incorporates existing research datasets. A patent filing with undisclosed scope does not establish control of the bottleneck.

The strongest case for writing a small check now is that Origin could finance the collection of longitudinal samples, turning a purchasable test into infrastructure for detection and treatment monitoring. That fits my pattern of looking beneath a visible product for a harder technical destination. But the financing loop is still a hypothesis: samples are processing, while completed-test counts, realized revenue, repeat use, and exclusive rights to outcome-linked data remain undisclosed. Free OncoTrack trial participation also cannot establish willingness to pay. My pre-revenue exception remains relevant—revenue is unnecessary when founder history, a working prototype, and concrete customer commitments carry the bet—but Canos has only part of that combination documented. Nothing here establishes a severe privacy problem or founder-control conflict; consent, sample custody, and governance remain unanswered questions rather than adverse facts.

I would hold my personal check today. Canos looks more likely to earn its place through a narrow oncology-monitoring use case than through broad routine screening, because the team’s demonstrated strength is interpreting disease-related signals while the everyday clinical benefit still needs proving. My one flip condition is a prospective, blinded, multi-clinic OncoTrack study showing that alerts precede independently confirmed relapse and change veterinary management while meeting a prespecified false-alert threshold. Show me that the extra information changes what happens to the dog. That would make the future considerably less imaginary.
