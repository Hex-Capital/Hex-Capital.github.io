# Central Blue -- Hypergrowth Evaluation

**Action:** Watch  
**Evidence confidence:** Low

### Company case

Central Blue’s strongest path is to sell recurring eclipse power to multiple satellite operators from shared HELION nodes. Assuming strong product-market fit with the first operators, the same imaging and propulsion jobs could recur across markets. Central Blue describes a common architecture built around dedicated receiver panels and CORE software for planned delivery handoffs. That architecture could transfer across markets, with the extent of spacecraft-specific adaptation still unclear. (*The Idea*, *Key Risks*)

The memo documents a mission intake form that collects power, location, and timing requirements. It makes intake reusable, but the path to first delivery still appears to require mission-specific planning and receiver integration. Recurring eclipses and an installed receiver give operators a reason to buy power repeatedly. Mission contracts are an inferred business model, and the memo gives no pricing or billable units to show how retained customers could spend more. (*The Idea*, *Key Risks*)

The two-person team reported a nearly complete prototype and a planned ground propulsion demonstration with Wave Motion. The memo establishes neither realized revenue nor confirmed pre-revenue status, so initial traction takes the default. The earlier CORE software demonstrations were announced for a different product focus and do not establish commercial uptake for HELION. (*Market & Traction*, *Key Facts*)

### Countercase

Dedicated panels could restrict adoption to spacecraft that can accommodate them, especially where an alternative can use existing solar panels. Under a shared-node scenario, incremental contract revenue could cover attributable selling, receiver and integration costs, and delivery operations while spare capacity lasts. If new missions require additional nodes, the bridge must also cover the cost of adding that capacity. The memo gives neither contract prices nor representative deployment costs, so cost economics takes the default. The structural downside gate stays open because the evidence establishes an integration burden but does not establish economically infeasible per-buyer work or capacity requirements. (*The Idea*, *Defensibility*, *Key Risks*)

### Decisive diligence question

Can one HELION node deliver recurring paid power to a second independent operator using the same receiver design at positive incremental contribution? Paid delivery records and the second deployment’s actual integration, operating, and capacity costs would answer it. A successful deployment would warrant rescoring for Reach out. Evidence that each buyer requires bespoke integration and dedicated capacity that its contract cannot cover would change the action to Pass.
