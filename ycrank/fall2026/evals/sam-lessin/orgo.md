# Orgo -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Orgo has shipped something people use. What I cannot underwrite is the check: proposed post-money, competing term sheets, and remaining capital need are undisclosed. Named backers do not establish an auction; they also do not establish that my money is scarce. There is no disclosed round-funded experiment with a metric and deadline. Both financing dimensions receive their missing-evidence defaults. That is a statement about the available deal evidence, not a penalty for being pre-seed.

The founders have a credible bridge between building infrastructure and implementing business agents. Nick Vasilescu previously operated computeruse.agency; the team now ships persistent desktops with files, terminals, screen streaming, and programmatic control. Those are operating receipts beyond JPMorgan or UIUC credentials. The [reported thousands of production desktops](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/WBl-orgo-computers-for-agents) support technical execution, while the agency experience supplies access to the customer side. I give that combination substantial founder credit without inventing a prior scaled infrastructure business.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that Orgo could own the agency deployment workflow. Its founder identifies tutorials, podcasts, and agencies as acquisition channels; Peter Steinberger is identified as its first paying customer, and a homepage testimonial describes agents operating inside three small businesses. That is more informative than follower counts or GitHub stars. But the dossier does not connect the founder’s community to measured paid conversion, or demonstrate that customer templates cause retention. Orgo has evidence of customer action; its proprietary distribution advantage remains only partly established.

That distinction matters because persistent desktops are available elsewhere. E2B offers graphical sandboxes, and [Daytona’s October 20, 2026 financing announcement](https://www.daytona.io/dotfiles/daytona-raises-60m-series-b-to-build-the-agent-cloud) describes overlapping infrastructure backed by substantially more capital. Those competitors might educate the market while Orgo wins a narrow agency workflow. They might also compress the price of the underlying computer. Orgo fits my unstated preference for founders connecting operating ecosystems, but the durable asset would have to be the customer relationship and deployment configuration. Feature availability alone does not establish that asset.

The expensive-round exception deserves a serious hearing. **NanoCo** demonstrated that technical adoption and early enterprise demand can outweigh price discipline; Orgo’s claimed 1,000-plus teams and named paying customer put it on the beginning of that evidentiary path. The material difference is the strength of external validation: Orgo’s dossier supplies neither comparable acquisition interest nor enough commercial detail to establish an override. Its infrastructure also has utility independent of any one model, which weakens the generic AI-wrapper objection. Still, actual account-freezing and MCP failures make reliability costs relevant, and nothing establishes that those costs fit inside the [$29/$99/$399 plans](https://www.orgo.ai/about).

Delete the next financing. We know Orgo charges money; we do not know gross margin, burn, runway, or the cash contribution of a retained desktop. That earns the economics default, without assuming losses. I pass on the available evidence despite credible founders and a functioning product. My verifiable flip condition is a documented sub-$10 million post-money round that fully funds a dated cash-break-even plan supported by observed paid-desktop retention and contribution margins. That would connect the product people already use to a check whose price and purpose I can defend.
