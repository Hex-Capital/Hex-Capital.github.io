# Torus -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The first fact I want is missing. What broke, what did the founders do, and how quickly did it work? Marcus Lima previously built a capital-intensive direct-air-capture company, and Rahul Thayil previously built an AI risk-intelligence company, but résumés are not evidence of resourcefulness. The dossier names no obstacle Torus encountered, no founder-built workaround, and no resulting change in a customer or system. I have to leave the most important criterion at its missing-evidence default.

The product is more convincing than the founder story. Torus reportedly extracts parameters across engineering documents, maps their relationships, performs more than 50 kinds of consistency checks, and drafts traceable project communications for human approval ([Torus](https://www.usetorus.com)). This is functioning software, not a diagram, and deployments with major EPCs and Fortune 500 companies imply that it survives contact with real documents ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/torus)). But all the technical evidence is self-reported. There is no measured accuracy, false-positive rate, detected-error count, or independently verified avoided rework. It is a credible technical proof, not yet an unusually concrete proof spike.

There is a plausible narrow well: engineers managing data-center and energy-infrastructure projects where one inconsistent parameter can create an expensive rework order. Torus is serving and deploying with multiple large companies, and the founders personally solicit five or six live project documents for pilots. That is better than a waitlist and better than a distribution plan whose decisive verb is “partner.” But the customers are unnamed, and there is no payment, repeat usage, renewal, or evidence that engineers pull the crude product back into subsequent projects. “Fortune 500” is investor-legible language; it does not tell me who wants this now or how badly.

The origin is suggestive but unproved. Lima studied energy systems and built Heimdal, while Thayil built enterprise risk software, so these founders are unusually close to capital-project complexity. The wedge is also full of useful schleps: incompatible document formats, sensitive project data, isolated deployments, engineering liability, and SOC 2 work still in progress. Yet the dossier never shows either founder personally encountering the inconsistency problem before Torus. Nor does it show a rejected hypothesis or a product route changed on customer evidence. I will credit the relevant background and ugly work, but not invent an organic origin or running-back behavior.

The strongest bull case is that two repeat founders have already built a substantial product and inserted it into some of the hardest enterprise environments at pre-seed. The strongest bear case is more uncomfortable: Trunk Tools, Transcend, Augmenta, and SWAPP have disclosed over $147 million across overlapping workflows, while Torus has disclosed neither a proprietary corpus nor a measured performance advantage. A broad consistency layer can become a valuable system of record, but it can also remain a labor-intensive pilot attached to commodity models. The unstated pattern I look for—an inspectable pre-round proof spike—is absent. The cofounders’ shared operating history is also unknown. No known-exception override applies because there is neither independently validated proof nor unmistakable user pull strong enough to replace the missing founder evidence.

My 36-month forecast is that Torus narrows around data-center engineering-document QA and reaches roughly $2–6 million ARR, rather than becoming a horizontal operating layer for physical engineering. I would not write the check today because the operational founder gate is unmet, though this is closer to an invest than an ordinary enterprise-AI pitch. One fact would flip me: a named EPC publicly confirming a paid renewal after at least six months with quantified rework avoided.
