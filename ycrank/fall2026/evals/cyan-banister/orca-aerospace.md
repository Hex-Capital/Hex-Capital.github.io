# Orca Aerospace -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a spacecraft encountering trouble, changing its plan, and recovering before anyone on Earth gets another communication window. Orca Aerospace is attacking the delay between something happening and someone being able to act. Its separation of AI-selected objectives, deterministic planning, and command guardrails gives that future a concrete architecture. The Lyra integration study supplies a small present-day artifact: people are examining which compute boards and spacecraft buses could support it. That earns my attention, although a concept study does not establish how far Orca has traveled toward autonomous operations.

The founders are the strongest reason to keep looking. Jacob Ososke spent more than a decade in Lockheed Martin guidance, navigation, and control and founded its first AI-GNC team. Hakan Chunton brings trajectory research at MIT and GNC experience at Lockheed. These are people who encountered the problem before founding the company. Creating an internal technical team also matters more to me than collecting another credential: someone had to recruit belief. The dossier establishes substantial relevant experience, though it supplies less evidence of independently shipped artifacts or commercial command.

Why must a spacecraft wait for permission from the ground? Scheduled passes and constrained bandwidth impose a real operational dependency, and moving decisions onboard could return control to the vehicle while freeing operators from routine intervention. But the dossier identifies a physical constraint more clearly than an institutional rent. It does not quantify controller costs, missed mission value, or who captures the savings. I see a useful transfer of agency; I cannot yet tell how much economic value Orca can retain from making it happen.

The most tempting interpretation is that Orca becomes an enabling layer beneath several space businesses. Its advertised avionics integration and hardware-in-the-loop work show awareness of the hidden engineering. That fits my preference for infrastructure beneath the visible wave. Yet integration requirements are work to perform, not evidence of an asset already controlled. Lyra’s finding that model memory drives board requirements, and board power drives bus sizing, could make the software valuable—or leave every deployment dependent on substantial custom engineering. The dossier does not establish reusable integrations or accumulated validation that would resolve that tradeoff.

The strongest case for writing a check now is that waiting for orbital proof could mean waiting until this unusually relevant team has removed the most valuable uncertainty. My pre-revenue investment in **CosmicBrain AI** offers a meaningful exception: deep founder experience can justify financing physical autonomy before revenue. The material difference is that it had a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept. Orca’s concept study does not provide equivalent behavioral evidence. Neither 302 LinkedIn followers nor an engineering vacancy shows a customer entrusting a mission to this product. Meanwhile, Shield AI and Sedaro reported 189 approved commands executed in orbit in August 2026. That validates a neighboring approach; it establishes no flight record for Orca.

I land at **Neutral**. Orca Aerospace fits my interest in difficult physical enablement, and the dossier gives me no affirmative basis to accuse these founders of chasing a fashionable round or depending on one closed model supplier. But the founder evidence currently outruns the working-artifact evidence. Deterministic command approval also leaves open whether a sequence of individually permitted actions remains safe for the mission. My verifiable flip condition is a customer-funded orbital demonstration in which Orca completes an autonomous recovery sequence on customer avionics, within the agreed power budget and mission safety envelope. That would turn the proposed transfer of control into something a customer has actually trusted.
