# Elite -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: patients no longer navigate phone trees, software completes routine administrative transactions across practices, and human staff handle exceptions and care rather than data entry. Getting there requires reliable identity verification, explicit consent, clinically safe routing, durable EHR write-back, and a liability model practices will accept. Elite names the current technology wave—real-time LLM voice—and a $13.7 billion market growing toward $106.7 billion, but it does not map those transitions. The government application-review launch on July 29 followed by a healthcare HIPAA notice on August 22 looks less like an artifact leaking from that future than a capable team moving toward the hottest available vertical.

The present-day leak is real. Holds and unanswered calls impose queues on patients while practice employees manually manage scheduling, referrals, eligibility, refills, intake, and billing. Charging per resolved interaction is a smart alignment mechanism: the practice buys completion rather than conversational theater. But the dossier neither quantifies that operating rent nor identifies a gatekeeper capturing it, and the agency returned is mostly staff capacity rather than new economic or occupational control for an individual.

There is more product here than a slide. Elite offers a public voice demo, protocols for 18 specialties, and claimed write-back into more than 19 EHRs. Encryption, audit logs, US data storage, HIPAA compliance, and BAAs show respect for healthcare’s hidden machinery. None of those facts demonstrate costly user behavior, however. There is no named practice, repeat cohort, resolved-interaction volume, revenue, or evidence that patients trust the system with sensitive information. Because triage and refill actions can cause harm, compliance plumbing without a disclosed consent, custody, and clinical-validation architecture leaves my privacy and safety gate unresolved.

Raj brings GTM experience from Deel; Vihaan was founding engineer number three at an acquired company and generated more than 1,000 signups in 48 hours for a Perplexity campaign. That is credible commercial and technical range. What is missing is healthcare operating tuition or a dated pre-company obsession with this problem. The recent government-to-healthcare transition is adverse evidence against independently derived obsession. Raj is identified as CEO, so there is no visible leadership shell game, but the brothers’ ownership and contractual tiebreaker remain unknown rather than cleared.

The strongest bull case stings: a two-person pre-seed team claims unusually broad integration coverage, has complementary sales and engineering skills, prices against outcomes, and is attacking a miserable workflow in a rapidly expanding market. This fits my unglamorous workflow-repair pattern. My pre-revenue exception still does not apply because the dossier supplies neither signed proofs of concept nor a founder history explaining why these two had to build this. Assort, Hyro, and Syllable already cover substantially overlapping workflows with $81.2 million to more than $222 million raised, while Elite discloses no proprietary dataset, lower-stack bottleneck, or structural distribution advantage.

I pass. My 36-month base case is that Elite narrows into a nonclinical eligibility or revenue-cycle workflow and remains below $1 million ARR rather than becoming the general healthcare front office. One result would flip me: three named practices across at least two specialties each sustaining 10,000 resolved interactions per month for six months, with audited clinical escalations and documented reductions in administrative cost.
