# isoquant -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Isoquant’s strongest asset is knowledge acquired while operating its own product. The founders say they developed the inference optimizations for Agent FM, which reached 60+ paying users, then commercialized that infrastructure in September 2026. There is a deployed endpoint, a published optimization experiment, and a named customer reporting roughly twice-as-fast inference. Those artifacts matter more than the Meta and Snowflake titles. The founders encountered a cost, built a solution, and found another buyer for it.

The difficult question is what they accumulate after solving that problem. Isoquant reports 452 ms median time to first token against Together’s 890 ms, and its banking specialist achieved 91.8% accuracy against a 74.7% baseline. These are company-published results with different workloads and limited generalizability, but they establish concrete engineering work. They do not establish an exclusive asset. Another provider can tune kernels, train a specialist, and offer a compatible endpoint. I cannot identify what isoquant would still own after a competent competitor reproduced its implementation.

The strongest objection to withholding a check is the speed of adoption. The CEO reports 1.2 trillion tokens in one month and 1,000+ customers; a customer describes a meaningful performance improvement. If those workloads recur, two founders may have discovered a much better production system than their public materials reveal. My framework permits an exceptional builder to outrun an incomplete business model or an immature product. Isoquant has enough action behind it to deserve that consideration. But the reported volume does not distinguish paid demand from subsidized experimentation, and it supplies no evidence that one customer makes another customer’s inference better.

That distinction determines whether this becomes a compounding system. Self-service access fits my preference for tools that let individuals produce without organizational permission. The shift toward infrastructure also fits my recent allocation. Yet isoquant has not demonstrated the contributor mechanism behind a permissionless network: customers consume inference, and the company supplies it. Customer inputs require written consent for training. A shared proprietary learning loop is therefore a possibility requiring both rights and technical proof. It earns no moat credit today. Nothing in the dossier establishes an integrity problem or an artificial financing deadline, and reusing infrastructure from Agent FM gives the pivot an intelligible cause.

There is considerable room to maneuver. Serving open models can lead into workload-specific training and enterprise deployment using the same optimization capability. Together, Fireworks, and Baseten provide independent evidence of substantial spending across those adjacent services; the $21.1 billion market estimate alone would be insufficient. Value capture is less established. Isoquant publishes prepaid prices of $0.07 per million input tokens and $0.15 per million output tokens, but realized revenue and contribution margins are undisclosed. Cheap inference attracts consumption. Whether it produces retained gross profit remains unanswered.

I would keep my money today while staying close to the founders. This is a credible engineering team in a large market, with the strongest evidence concentrated in execution and the weakest in customer-created value. My one verifiable flip condition is a consented proprietary optimization system shown, on a fixed held-out workload, to deliver higher contribution margins as additional customers contribute training or evaluation data. That would connect adoption to an asset competitors cannot obtain simply by copying the code.
