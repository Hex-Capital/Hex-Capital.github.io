# Prescience, Inc. -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: employer health costs reached $26,993 per family in 2025, employers already buy through brokers and administrators, and Prescience becomes large only if it can produce repeatable, risk-adjusted savings per covered life that exceed its clinical, navigation, and acquisition costs. The savings-linked fee makes that controlling variable explicit. The dossier does not provide the necessary threshold, fee percentage, or completed claims-cycle evidence, so the causal thesis is identifiable but not yet validated.

Time Machine and Crystal are directionally more interesting than an AI benefits interface. Prescience proposes to route care, schedule appointments, answer questions, resolve bills, and automate plan administration and filings. A stronger model could improve this system because the company may retain permissions, claims history, workflow state, and responsibility for outcomes. The California administrator license and reported integrations across provider networks, HSAs, compliance, and plan administration support an owned-workflow interpretation rather than a thin wrapper.

The economic discontinuity is much less established. Premiums rising 6% is customer pain, not evidence that reinforcement learning changes the margin structure. Prescience supplies no clinical validation, sensitivity data, customer-level savings study, or cost-to-serve figures. Human-physician escalation may be necessary risk control, but it could also preserve service-heavy economics. Charging $0 in administrative fees intensifies the question: if savings are noisy, delayed, or difficult to attribute, the company may carry operating cost without capturing enough value.

The strongest bull case is that outcome pricing removes adoption friction while the integrated workflow generates compounding claims and navigation data. Even limited employer adoption would then create reusable distribution into adjacent benefits administration. But the current pull signal is weak: service to an unspecified number of employers is company-reported, the Time Machine waitlist has no participant count, and 51 Launch YC points say nothing about repeated use or willingness to pay. Angle Health and Collective Health already offer overlapping AI-enabled administration and navigation after raising $196.5 million and $714 million respectively, so this category is crystallizing enough that Prescience needs a sharper winner case than broad integration.

The founders are the best evidence. Rishab brings more than ten ML and health publications plus two company-reported acquisitions; Aditya adds an MD, biomedical engineering, and medical-AI experience. They are brothers who report building together for ten years. That is unusually coherent founder-market fit and credible prior-company learning. It does not qualify for a research-stage exception, however: the dossier contains no published Time Machine result, rare technical breakthrough, or capital plan tied to a specific validation milestone. Founder quality cannot substitute for the missing economic proof.

I would pass today despite substantial technical interest. My 36-month base case is that Prescience pivots from a full-stack employer health plan to care-navigation or claims-optimization software sold through brokers and TPAs, remaining below $5 million ARR, because that narrower product avoids underwriting the unproven savings mechanism. One result flips the decision: a named cohort covering at least 5,000 lives completes twelve months with risk-adjusted medical spending at least 8% below benchmark while Prescience retains at least 30% gross margin after clinical and navigation costs.
