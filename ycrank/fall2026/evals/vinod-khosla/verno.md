# Verno -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Verno’s consequential possibility is making industrial design changes propagate reliably across every drawing, equipment list and datasheet. That could remove an engineering bottleneck behind plant construction. But the dossier does not establish how much engineering labor, procurement error or project delay disappears. Native CAD editing is a mechanism; orders-of-magnitude consequence still needs a baseline. I cannot price an improbable breakthrough when the magnitude of success remains unspecified.

The product attacks a real weakness: a change in one document can silently invalidate another. Verno proposes native-file edits and downstream synchronization, with engineer acceptance before incorporation. That goes beyond searchable PDFs. Against Autodesk Plant 3D’s existing authoring and configurable validation, however, there is no measured improvement in engineering hours, missed inconsistencies or correctness. The March 2026 ChatP&ID research belongs to other researchers; its results cannot establish Verno’s technical advantage. Nor does using Amazon Bedrock establish that this is merely a wrapper. Both conclusions require evidence the dossier lacks.

The strongest argument for writing a check is the combination of relevant people and access to actual engineering work. Wesche brings aerospace and ML experience; Madan brings document extraction; Rohit Shinde adds more than a decade in petrochemicals and EPC consulting. That complementary team fits my preference for assembling disciplines around an expensive workflow. The July 2026 EPC partnership processed actual project drawings, and November disclosures report live customer deployments. These are meaningful pre-seed signals. Verno also fits the pattern of working inside installed industrial infrastructure. But deployment alone does not establish a proprietary learning loop, and the team record documents neither rapid belief revision nor unusually difficult recruiting wins.

The largest kill risk is whether reviewing Verno’s changes consumes the labor it promises to eliminate—or allows costly errors through. Engineer acceptance provides a control, but its economic effectiveness is unmeasured. The dossier gives no experiment budget, pass/fail threshold or decision branch tied to this uncertainty, so I cannot credit a risk-removal process. Equally, there is no evidence that the founders are recklessly scaling before testing it. Public pricing and delivery costs are absent. The relevant cost calculation includes model usage, connector maintenance, onboarding and engineer review per accepted change; software inference becoming cheaper does not settle that calculation.

My exceptions for an exceptional instigator addressing a giant existing value pool do not rescue the investment case yet. Relevant credentials and customer access are affirmative evidence, but neither quantifies that value pool or demonstrates a discontinuity. There is no disclosed exit fixation, permanent subsidy dependence or refusal to learn. This is a **Pass**, based on unproven fit rather than an active founder red flag. One verifiable flip condition would reopen the decision: an independently audited, paid EPC deployment showing at least 10x lower total engineering labor per completed change package than its existing Autodesk-based workflow, counting review and integration work, with no higher escaped-error rate and positive contribution margin. That would turn Verno’s promising workflow access into evidence of a cost curve worth underwriting.
