# Grip -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Jonas Gruetter’s reinforcement-learning work on a 12-ton excavator makes **Grip** a high-technical-risk, low-founder-defiance company: serious physical-AI execution applied to a thesis that still extrapolates directly from Gruetter’s ETH Zürich and Gravis Robotics history. **Grip** is not a commodity AI wrapper, and Gruetter’s published 70% boulder-removal result fits my unstated preference for founders with intellectual reputations. But credentials are not contrarianism. The dossier contains no documented moment when Gruetter contradicted robotics orthodoxy, abandoned a prior thesis, or argued that experts misunderstand wet-waste manipulation.

**Commonwealth Fusion** (2019) is the high rung for technical and capital risk because fusion required multiple falsifiable miracles before commercial proof. **Grip** shares the load-bearing mechanism: wet-waste sorting requires perception under contamination, multimodal grasping of irregular objects, and hardware that survives hostile operating conditions while learning from successful and failed grasps. Those are real miracles, not SaaS features. **Grip** diverges sharply because the dossier discloses no throughput, accuracy, uptime, dataset size, or capital-intensive deployment plan, while ZenRobotics, Recycleye, and EverestLabs have already commercialized adjacent robotic-sorting stacks. Technical uncertainty exists; evidence that consensus capital cannot underwrite it does not.

**Glean’s** ex-Google founder continuity is the low rung on founder defiance: the usual failure mechanism is that domain experts optimize the system they already understand. Glean overcame that red flag through Fortune 500 consequence and capital scale. **Grip** shows the same continuity without the override. Gruetter moved from reinforcement learning, sparse-LiDAR perception, irregular-object manipulation, and heavy machinery into reinforcement-learning manipulation of irregular waste. **Grip** may execute well, but the dossier supplies no controversial industry position and no evidence that Gruetter sees something ZenRobotics, Recycleye, or EverestLabs fundamentally cannot see.

**$962.3 million** of global waste-sorting-equipment revenue in 2024, reaching $1.43 billion by 2030, limits **Grip’s** consequence case as currently defined. Over the next 18 months, every deployed **Grip** unit could compound a proprietary grasp-failure dataset, improve contamination removal, and keep plastic out of compost and soil. But the economic mechanism that breaks is still unclear: **Grip** automates one difficult station inside an existing waste-processing regime rather than eliminating landfilling, replacing plastics, or collapsing manual waste sorting across a tens-of-billions labor pool. EU packaging rules may even reduce contaminated feedstock upstream. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself” requires a great consequence, not merely a stubborn workflow.

**Slash** (2024) earned its exception through a documented pivot that revealed founder learning rate. **Grip** has no equivalent evidence—no prior company sale, product pivot, rejected hypothesis, or public “we were wrong” moment—so I apply the absent-evidence default. Gruetter’s transition from a 12-ton excavator achieving 70% boulder-removal success to wet organic waste is an intelligent transfer of technical knowledge, but **Grip** has not shown whether Gruetter updates the market thesis as quickly as the manipulation policy.

**Grip** has published no pricing, manufacturing roadmap, or required capital to reach a commercial milestone. The capital score flips upward only if **Grip** demonstrates that contamination-tolerant end effectors, fleet data infrastructure, and facility retrofits require more than $50 million before repeatable deployment and more than $200 million to scale. The score flips downward if standard robotic arms plus commodity vision can serve the application for $5–10 million. On the disclosed evidence, **Grip** is hardware-intensive but not yet capital-hostile enough to make my check size an advantage.

**ZenRobotics** has sorted waste since 2007 and now sits inside Terex, which makes my 36-month forecast falsifiable: by July 2029, **Grip** will have pivoted from wet-organic plastic removal into a broader retrofit sorting cell for mixed waste and will remain below $2 million ARR. **Grip’s** strongest asset is transferable manipulation engineering, while its weakest is a defiant market thesis; capable robotics founders confronted by a narrow $1.43 billion equipment category tend to broaden toward the established demand rather than create a new regime.

**Jonas Gruetter’s** 70% field-tested excavator result creates the strongest bull case: wet organic waste may be the ignored wedge that looks small precisely because existing robots cannot handle it. **Grip** could follow the DoorDash exception—enter through an unglamorous operational bottleneck, accumulate proprietary facility data, and expand until the original market definition becomes irrelevant. That case stings because **Grip** combines custom hardware, learning from failed grasps, and direct operator outreach. It still loses today because there are no disclosed deployments, performance measurements, or customer commitments, while three funded competitors already own adjacent distribution. Three paid facilities demonstrating over 95% contaminant removal, production uptime, and at least $1 million ARR would flip my verdict to Invest.
