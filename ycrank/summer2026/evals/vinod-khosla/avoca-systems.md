# Avoca Systems -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Avoca has demonstrated a large operational improvement, not a large consequence-of-success case. More than 30% of radiology calls are abandoned, and deployments across networks operating over 150 clinics reportedly reduced abandonment by 90%. That matters to patients and operators. But the dossier quantifies neither the affected patient population nor the healthcare cost pool transformed. A $780 million scheduling-software market is a respectable software category; it is not evidence that an unlikely breakthrough rewrites healthcare economics.

The strongest evidence is product performance. Avoca reports 86% call resolution, 4.6/5 satisfaction, and 30 bookings converted in half a day, while writing directly into named RIS and EHR systems. These are real deployments, not a demo. But the comparison is against abandoned calls and manual operations—not against openDoctor, AbbaDox, or another named incumbent under controlled conditions. Voice, referral intake, communications, and clinic rules make a more complete product, but completeness is not a 5–10x technical discontinuity. Existing radiology platforms and well-funded general healthcare agents can assemble much of the same stack.

The bull case stings because this is not an inexperienced team chasing a hypothetical workflow. Max Shand was Afterpay’s first employee and previously sold Serenade; Campbell Mercer-Butcher has led engineering teams; Avoca already has recognizable customer logos, enterprise integrations, SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001, and HIPAA compliance. Execution-heavy workflow companies can compound from an operational wedge. Yet the dossier documents no consequential belief revision, no difficult risk-specific recruit, and no radiology expertise inside the team. Eight employees show recruiting capacity, not gene-pool engineering around clinical safety, U.S. infrastructure, and healthcare distribution.

The largest kill risks are identifiable but not being tested through disclosed pass/fail milestones. Avoca must prove that protocol-sensitive bookings remain safe, that Australia-only hosting can become a workable U.S. architecture, and that third-party integrations do not turn every deployment into services labor. Approved knowledge bases, human escalation, and monitoring mitigate the first risk, but there is no error threshold, experiment cost, timeline, or stop condition. Capital could easily fund sales and integrations while leaving the fundamental question—whether this scales as software—unanswered.

The economics are almost entirely missing. Enterprise contracts are inferred; revenue, pricing, gross margin, escalation rate, implementation cost, and retention are undisclosed. Human call handling supplies an obvious incumbent cost baseline, yet Avoca provides no bottom-up path to beating it after inference, escalation, support, and integration labor. My base case is a useful radiology vendor, not a transformative healthcare operating system. The red flag is not lack of traction; it is that integration breadth and workflow packaging may be mistaken for proprietary technical leverage.

I pass. One verifiable condition would flip me: a 12-month deployment across three U.S. radiology networks showing at least 5x lower fully loaded cost per correctly completed booking than openDoctor or the incumbent workflow, including human escalation and integration costs. That result would convert today’s attractive product metrics into evidence of a discontinuity and scalable economics.
