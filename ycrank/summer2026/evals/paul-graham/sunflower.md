# Sunflower -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when Koby Conrad first got stuck. The dossier does not name a blocker, a dated workaround, and the resulting change. Hiring a sobriety creator and obtaining 50,000–100,000 monthly link clicks may have been resourceful, but the causal sequence is absent. I cannot infer the founder test from growth alone.

What Sunflower has built is much harder to dismiss. It went from 200 monthly active users to 100,000 in under six months, reached 500,000 total users, and handled 7 million AI-sponsor messages. This is functioning software at real scale, not a diagram waiting for funding. The outcome claims remain weaker: the day-60 relapse figures are company surveys rather than a clinical trial. But users have already subjected the product to a more demanding test than a prototype demo.

The narrow well is unmistakable. Ten thousand people pay, 100,000 use the product monthly, and users have recorded 21.5 million sober days. Ratings of 4.9 on iOS and 4.7 on Android reinforce the same signal. These are people repeatedly bringing an urgent problem to the crude product. The 37,500 clinic requests then reveal where the app stops being enough.

Conrad encountered this problem personally, built Quirk before Sunflower, and later worked in health technology. The clinic is also genuine schlep: state SUD licenses, Medicaid approvals, county payer contracts, controlled health records, and physical-care operations. Pennsylvania and Michigan are limited coverage, but they prove the team has begun the ugly work. Sunflower is not merely attaching “AI” to a market selected from a spreadsheet.

The strongest objection is that the evidence may describe an excellent consumer-acquisition machine rather than a durable company. Conrad says apps can be cloned rapidly. One creator relationship supplies substantial traffic, competitors already combine digital recovery with clinical care, and $6.5 million has been raised at a $60 million post-money valuation. Meanwhile, the claimed clinical outcomes are self-reported and the 37,500 treatment requests are not yet 37,500 patients. The team could spend years turning cheap app demand into expensive regulated care while better-capitalized competitors distribute through employers and health plans.

But the pull is strong enough to override the missing founder-obstacle history. A clone can reproduce the interface; it cannot instantly reproduce 10,000 paying subscribers, millions of recovery interactions, two states’ clinical approvals, and a large queue requesting treatment. I would invest, though this is closer than the headline growth suggests. My flip condition is simple: if Sunflower’s paid subscribers have less than 40% retention at 90 days, I would pass, because the apparent narrow well would then be acquisition rather than need.
