# Native -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Native sits at a useful market transition: cheap generation lets small businesses buy outsourced marketing through software, but the controlling variable is whether automated idea selection creates enough incremental customer value to sustain the subscription. Generation and publishing are already available elsewhere. Norn must make better decisions. The dossier identifies that causal mechanism, but does not establish the performance threshold at which Native becomes materially more valuable than competing workflows.

The demand evidence is unusually strong for this stage. Native reports $2M ARR within six months and more than 2,200 paying customers across 20 countries. Those figures deserve substantially more weight than its social following. They remain company-reported, and ARR does not establish retention, but thousands of businesses paying for an unfinished category is meaningful product-market pull. At $2M ARR and 2,200 customers, the implied average is roughly $76 per customer per month, broadly consistent with the advertised $79 Pro plan. The revenue claim has internal economic coherence.

What remains unclear is how much economic improvement the technology creates. The $79 subscription offers an accessible entry point, and combining selection, generation, and publishing plausibly removes work. Native does not quantify labor displaced, delivery costs, or contribution margins. Briggs on Homes achieving 5.5% engagement against 2.9% for its own posts is encouraging, but engagement is upstream of customer revenue. The separate observational research selects retained accounts and acknowledges survivorship bias. Neither establishes that Norn causes an economic improvement customers will continue paying for.

Native does own more of the workflow than an output formatter. Brand context, approval and rejection feedback, scheduling, and publishing form a recurring operational loop. Better generation models should improve the assets moving through that loop. The strongest objection is that Blaze already describes a comparable strategy-to-publishing system, while Native depends on Ayrshare for publishing. The training mechanism is documented; an expensive-to-reproduce data advantage is not. Customers can opt out, and the dossier does not establish how much time or money a competitor would need to match the useful feedback. My pattern of investing across a technology transition supports examining this layer, but does not establish why this particular supplier captures durable value.

The founders provide a separate affirmative signal. They built Wepost together, reached reported NOK80,000 MRR, and wound it down before starting Native. That is actual operating experience with the problem, alongside content production, agency work, and machine learning. I give that substantially more credit than university or employer credentials. It does not resolve the market question. The research-stage exception also does not apply: this dossier supplies commercial execution evidence, rather than frontier research or scarce model-building capability that would justify bypassing the economic gate.

I would monitor Native. The bull case is substantial: paying demand has arrived early, the team has relevant prior learning, and the product controls actions and feedback rather than merely generating text. My hesitation is specifically whether predictive selection changes the economics enough to withstand comparable products. One verifiable result would flip me: a randomized customer cohort showing at least 20% higher six-month retained gross profit per account with Norn than with the same generation and publishing workflow without it, including inference and human-support costs. That would connect Native’s claimed differentiation to durable customer and company value.
