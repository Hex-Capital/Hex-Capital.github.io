# Elimentary -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Private-capital operations are becoming executable by agents, and fund managers now have a reason to buy automation beyond document search. For Elimentary, the controlling variable is the fully loaded cost of a correctly completed operation, including implementation and human review. The covenant case study reports four hours reduced to 15 minutes per portfolio company. That supports the mechanism. It does not yet establish how much customization must be repeated across funds, which determines whether this becomes a large software company.

The strongest evidence is the October 19 TresVista deployment: 500-plus filing checks previously required approximately 20 hours; the agent run took under 30 minutes, followed by another hour for sign-off. That implies roughly 92.5% less elapsed work time if the measurements are comparable. Human review is already included, which makes this considerably more useful than an inference-speed benchmark. These are company-reported results, and customer labor savings do not establish Elimentary’s gross margin, but the economic change is substantial enough to support a new purchasing decision.

The multi-year TresVista partnership and more than 25 reported production agents provide affirmative evidence of demand at this stage. More interestingly, an operations provider could become reusable distribution into additional fund workflows. That fits my pattern of backing companies whose first product opens a channel for subsequent products. The distinction matters: a partnership that repeatedly delivers customers is valuable distribution; a partnership that repeatedly requires bespoke engineering is a services pipeline. Elimentary has established deployment, while the repeatability of that channel remains unresolved.

The architecture gives me a reason to believe better models could strengthen the product. A semantic data layer, client-specific financial tools, and clause-level provenance provide operational context beyond generated answers. However, the benchmark’s 36 filing-contradicting statements show that prepared data does not eliminate correctness risk; without a denominator, I cannot infer an error rate. Analyst review remains part of delivery. Institutional knowledge also stays with clients, limiting any claim that accumulated customer data automatically becomes Elimentary’s moat.

The strongest objection is that the savings accrue to customers while implementation work and competition absorb the supplier’s economics. Hypercore already combines agents, loan software, and servicing oversight; Finley’s Concord combination adds verification and credit-administration operations. This market is crystallizing around companies that can bundle execution with infrastructure. My pattern of investing across a technology discontinuity supports the category, but it does not resolve Elimentary’s winner case. The relevant red flag is service-heavy delivery claiming software economics. It remains a risk rather than an established mismatch. Nor would a frontier-research or scarce-model-talent exception justify overlooking weak delivery economics here.

Sarda’s previous co-founding of Skit.ai provides genuine prior startup experience, and the team’s credit-product operating background is relevant. I give no credit for presumed desperation or an undisclosed exit, and the founders’ previous billion-dollar loan books are not Elimentary traction. I would make a personal investment because measured workflow savings, live deployments, and a credible distribution opening outweigh the remaining uncertainty. My single verifiable flip condition would be the next three production deployments showing no reduction in implementation-plus-review labor per completed operation versus the initial deployments. That would materially weaken the software scaling thesis.
