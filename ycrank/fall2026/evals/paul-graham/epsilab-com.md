# Epsilab -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Epsilab’s founders have built things before. What I cannot find is what happened when this particular thing became difficult. Hu and Peng previously built Shortbread, which Peng’s biography credits with $500K in revenue and 2M episodes sold. That deserves attention. But the dossier gives no named obstacle, founder-built workaround, or elapsed time for Epsilab. I cannot turn a previous business’s sales into evidence of how these founders handle resistance here.

The most useful technical evidence is Hu’s talk2arxiv project, an interface for chatting with research papers with 531 GitHub stars as of October 27, 2026. It is an inspectable artifact rather than a credential. It earns limited technical credit. Epsilab’s own description—versioned environments with states, tasks, rewards, and verifiers—is still a description. There is no demonstrated long-horizon task, reproducibility result, or test showing that an agent cannot collect rewards by exploiting the verifier. That last distinction matters enormously. An environment can run perfectly while teaching the wrong thing.

There is a plausible narrow well here: researchers whose agents fail on extended workflows that existing environments do not capture. But “AI labs” names an audience, not an urgent user. The dossier identifies no lab returning to an environment, requesting another task, or paying for continued access. YC backing and 46 LinkedIn followers establish none of those things. Commissioned development could be a sensible first business because doing the work manually would expose what researchers actually need. Licensing remains an inferred business model, however, and there is no evidence yet of which work someone buys repeatedly.

The strongest argument against passing is the combination of an existing founder relationship, a previously monetized product, and a technical founder who ships public software. That is substantially better than four people assembling around an attractive market. Epsilab also resembles a pattern I like: programmable infrastructure whose users can discover applications the founders did not anticipate. The missing part of that pattern is developers actually building on it. My exceptions allow a working prototype, independently validated proof, or unmistakable user pull to overcome uncertainty. None is documented for Epsilab’s environments. The earlier ventures also do not establish an evidence-driven change of route or a personal encounter with this problem.

I would not call the team passive, accuse it of chasing a trend, or assume it depends on a large partner. Those red flags are unproven. The concern is more specific: access to real workflows and reliable verification may be the whole business, yet neither advantage is established. HUD’s supplier marketplace and Prime Intellect’s integrated stack give researchers alternatives while Epsilab discovers its wedge. The opportunity may lie in the ugly work those platforms leave unfinished. I want evidence that these founders have encountered that work and begun solving it.

I would pass on the present evidence. The founder-resourcefulness gate remains unmet, and neither current technical proof nor current user pull supplies an override. One verifiable event would make me reconsider: a named lab renewing after the founders fix a documented verifier exploit, with a dated account of the workaround and evidence that the revised environment rewards successful task completion. That would connect founder behavior, technical validity, and an actual customer need.
