# Bryel -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Recent training advances let enterprises build specialized models from a few thousand examples; Bryel becomes a large company if those deployments can be repeated with substantially less expert labor per customer. That is the controlling variable. Foundry combines data creation, reinforcement-learning environments, and automated experiments, which gives the mechanism some substance. But embedding a training team could produce either reusable software or a succession of bespoke projects. The dossier establishes a plausible market opening without showing which economics Bryel has.

The strongest evidence is the reported economic improvement. YC’s October 2026 launch describes classification running 20× faster, search costing 5× less, and agents matching frontier performance at 5–8× lower cost. Those are potentially budget-moving differences. I discount them as company-originated claims without published methodology, and inference savings do not establish savings after training, implementation, and maintenance. Still, Bryel is addressing an actual cost and capability threshold. This is more interesting than adding another interface to a frontier API.

The strongest argument against passing is that Bryel has reportedly delivered customer models already. If those deployments deliver the advertised economics, a small team could use implementation work to discover a repeatable product before larger platforms recognize the segment. That fits the pattern of entering a technology discontinuity through its enabling infrastructure. But this market is already crystallizing: Fireworks offers training, serving, customer ownership, and continued improvement, with more than $1 billion in reported annualized revenue. Its revenue is evidence about the competitive environment, not Bryel’s demand. Delivered models earn Bryel some adoption credit; absent repeat usage, payment, or expansion evidence, they do not yet demonstrate product-market pull.

Foundry also has the beginnings of a durable technical loop: training environments, automated experiments, and continuous learning. Better underlying models could improve that system. The unresolved issue is whether Bryel remains responsible for an ongoing customer workflow after delivering the model. Customer ownership can help close a sale while leaving the vendor with little recurring leverage. No documented production integrations or retained feedback loop establish that leverage yet. The relevant red flag is service-heavy delivery being valued as software; the dossier leaves that risk unresolved rather than proving it.

Kevin Miao’s Berkeley research, Apple engineering experience, and September 2026 research coauthorship make the technical proposition credible. Frankie Li adds relevant AI-product experience. Neither background establishes costly founder commitment or prior startup learning, so that dimension receives its missing-evidence default. The frontier-research exception does not change my decision: the dossier does not establish a scarce frontier-model team with a differentiated research program and credible capital path. Nor does the competitive landscape show an abandoned category that would justify overlooking delivery economics.

I would pass on writing a personal check today, while keeping Bryel on a short monitoring list. The technology shift is stronger than the evidence that this particular company captures its value. My one flip condition is a verified cohort of three paying customers renewing after six months, with deployment records showing that the same Foundry pipeline delivers at least 3× lower total customer cost and positive contribution margins after Bryel’s engineering labor. That would connect the reported technical gains to a repeatable business.
