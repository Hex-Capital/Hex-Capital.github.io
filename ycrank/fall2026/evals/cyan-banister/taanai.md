# Taan.ai -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a filmmaker moving a scene boundary and having the score rearrange around it without losing the musical idea. Getting there requires reliable timing, fidelity that survives professional use, and revisions that preserve everything the creator wanted to keep. Taan.ai is attacking the first of those transitions. But that future is my extrapolation: the dossier establishes a present-day scoring tool, without evidence that creators are beginning to work in this new way. I would pass with my own money today.

The founders give me the strongest reason to keep looking. Adithya performed metal and built Aalap AI during December 2024–November 2025, reporting $1,000 MRR before Taan. That revenue belongs to the earlier venture, but the sequence matters: he was making music-generation products before this fundraising announcement. Devansh brings signal-processing research and ML engineering experience, and the two co-authored TimingBench. There is independently pursued technical interest here, beyond a pair of résumés. The evidence does not yet establish a long history of obsession or how they recruited their first believers.

Why does a creator have to repair the timing after asking a model for a musical event at a particular second? Taan identifies a specific failure that transfers editing labor back to the user. Its targeted timing and instrument controls could return some creative control, with entry pricing of $15 per month. That is useful system repair. The dossier does not identify a quantified toll, artificial restriction, or gatekeeper whose economics Taan overturns, so I cannot turn this workflow improvement into a larger liberation story.

The strongest argument against passing is unusually concrete: Taan reports 96.1% timing-instruction success against ElevenLabs v2’s 28.9%, with a beta announced June 11, 2026. If that difference survives real production, precision could become the concealed infrastructure beneath many creative applications—a pattern I actively seek. Public API and MCP access give developers a way to incorporate it. But the benchmark uses author-labelled validation data, independent human annotation remains unfinished, and Taan reports worse audio fidelity: 4.8 versus competitors’ 2.6–3.5, where lower is better. A cue arriving on time only matters if the creator keeps it. The dossier provides no repeated user behavior establishing that tradeoff, and ElevenLabs already offers video-to-music generation.

My pre-revenue exception applies when a working prototype, unusually deep founder history, and concrete customer commitments establish the path without revenue. Taan supplies meaningful pieces of that case, but not the complete mechanism. I see no documented governance or severe privacy violation; missing information does not establish either red flag. Nor does the dossier establish dependence on one closed model supplier. Still, patent applications with unverified company ownership and claimed licensed training do not yet demonstrate control of a scarce enabling asset. My flip condition is one documented cohort of three independent production teams renewing twice after choosing Taan over ElevenLabs in a blind workflow comparison. That would move me from pass to active diligence by showing that its unusual precision changes what people repeatedly do.
