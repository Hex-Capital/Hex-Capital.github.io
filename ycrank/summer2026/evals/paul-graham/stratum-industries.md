# Stratum Industries -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Raj Jagiasi averaged 220% of quota while Vihaan Jagiasi was founding engineer number three at an acquired startup, which makes Stratum Industries a strong brother-pair pointed at a problem neither brother appears to have personally suffered. That is Stratum Industries’s signature: unusually good founder configuration, weak organic origin. The pair may be animals, but the idea currently reads as something they discovered rather than something they had to build.

**Stripe** is the high rung for cofounder configuration: two brothers with complementary abilities, a long relationship, and the ability to build what they sold. Stratum Industries shares the important part of that mechanism because Raj can sell and Vihaan can ship, and brothers do not meet at a founder-matching event. But Stratum Industries diverges from Stripe where the analogy matters most: the Collisons had already wrestled with online commerce through Auctomatic, while neither Jagiasi brother has disclosed experience inside government adjudication or procurement. I score the configuration highly, not perfectly.

The polished AI-wrapper failure mode begins when founders select a large market and plan to hire their way into understanding it. Stratum Industries does not show the MBA version of that mistake, but the organic-idea weakness is real: Raj’s 220% quota attainment came from Deel, Vihaan’s engineering experience came from Cadastral, and neither biography contains a permit, benefits claim, patent application, or agency workflow. Stratum Industries may have found a genuine problem, but there is no lost-USB-stick story here. No evidence that either founder built the product for himself means applying the absent-evidence default.

Vihaan generated more than 1,000 Perplexity Pro sign-ups at Waterloo within 48 hours, while Raj ranked among the top five of over 1,000 Deel representatives. Those are animal tracks. Over the next 18 months, that combination could compound: Raj obtains one reluctant agency meeting, Vihaan manually converts its rubric into a working adjudication flow, and Stratum Industries uses the result to pry open the next agency. But Stratum Industries has disclosed no working deployment, revenue, or unscalable customer stunt. Resourcefulness transferred from prior jobs is promising; resourcefulness spent on the present problem is what earns the final ten points.

Millions of applications sitting for months or years put Stratum Industries in a genuine schlep zone: government procurement, sensitive data, consequential decisions, audit trails, and humans who cannot tolerate confident false answers. The score would fall a tier if Stratum Industries were merely selling a configurable agent layer through ordinary software channels. It would rise to the ceiling if Raj produced a named agency pilot in which Vihaan’s system processed real cases under human approval. Salesforce’s FedRAMP-authorized Government Cloud is evidence that the schlep is formidable, but also that Stratum Industries cannot pretend compliance is a feature to add later.

Vihaan’s founding-engineer role at Cadastral before its acquisition is the closest Stratum Industries comes to the **Dropbox** pattern: a technical founder who had already helped ship a non-trivial artifact before the current pitch. Stratum Industries also has a 2023 Devpost contribution credited to Vihaan, but no confirmed GitHub profile, open-source trail, or public demo. That is enough hacker genealogy to distinguish Stratum Industries from a sales-led wrapper, but not enough to show the taste Drew Houston displayed by putting the Dropbox demo on Hacker News before fundraising.

ClearOS already markets nearly the same reviewer-side adjudication wedge, so my 36-month prediction is that Stratum Industries narrows from “the U.S. government backlog” to one state or local permitting-and-licensing workflow and reaches $3 million to $7 million in annual recurring revenue by July 2029. Stratum Industries gets there through the high/low pairing: two brothers who can sell and build will discover the real product by doing agency work manually, but their lack of organic adjudication experience will force a narrower wedge than the present horizontal claim.

GovWell has more than 130 government customers across 34 states, Salesforce already possesses the authorizations agencies trust, and Stratum Industries has disclosed zero customers, pilots, or revenue. The strongest bear case is that the founders are talented tourists: government sales cycles consume Raj’s sales energy, compliance consumes Vihaan’s engineering time, and ClearOS wins the identical category before Stratum Industries learns why adjudicators resist automation. The precedent that prevents a pass is **Loopt**: exceptional founder force can outweigh weak initial timing and incomplete product understanding. Raj’s 220% quota record and Vihaan’s 1,000 sign-ups in 48 hours are specific evidence of force, but not yet an Airbnb cereal box. If Stratum Industries has no paid deployment with a named agency by July 2027, I would flip to Pass.
