# Maritime -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Maritime’s founders have earned the right to work on this problem. Hosting hundreds of research agents at MIT is a useful collision with reality: persistence, secrets, isolation, and debugging become operating problems rather than presentation slides. That hosting history is founder-reported, but the shipped platform and Maria Gorskikh’s Open Instinct project, with 416 GitHub stars, provide concrete artifacts. Their NANDA research matters because it connects to something they built. I give substantial credit for specific knowledge, without treating repository attention as customer adoption.

The harder question is what Maritime owns after someone reproduces the implementation. Persistent disks and snapshot-based sleep/wake solve real problems. They do not, by themselves, establish a durable advantage. Docker announced overlapping managed microVM infrastructure on September 24, 2026. Maritime’s accumulated files and integrations might make migration inconvenient, but the dossier establishes neither measured switching costs nor an exclusive operating advantage. Running infrastructure is not the same as owning a scarce infrastructure asset.

The strongest bull case is that the founders understand a workload that larger clouds will initially serve badly. Start with startups provisioning one persistent agent per customer; extend the same capability into personal agents and enterprise fleets. The cited enterprise-agent-infrastructure estimate—$550 million in 2026, growing to $4.45 billion by 2031—supports adjacent demand, although it does not establish Maritime’s reachable market. This also fits my preference for tools that let individuals turn knowledge into scalable output. An exceptional prototype can justify investing before a distribution loop exists. Here, however, the shipped product establishes execution more clearly than exceptional product behavior or capital efficiency.

Maritime has not yet demonstrated the compounding mechanism I want. One customer’s isolated computer does not demonstrably improve another customer’s computer. Fleet experience could eventually improve scheduling, but that remains a hypothesis. Product Hunt’s 253 followers and the reported website traffic do not establish referrals, retention, or customer-created utility. Self-service access removes permission from deployment; it does not automatically create a network effect.

The pricing is admirably legible: $20 monthly for 20 agents, $100 for 100, and $500 for 500, subject to concurrency limits. There is a defined buyer and a direct payment mechanism. There is no disclosed realized revenue or contribution margin. Snapshot sleep could make those prices economical, but the economics depend on actual activity and resource consumption. A subscription schedule demonstrates an offer; paying, retained workloads would demonstrate value capture.

I see no documented founder-integrity, artificial-deadline, or reassurance-seeking red flag. Indefinite retention of proxy-call contents, including after account deletion, is nevertheless a concrete trust concern for a service holding customer secrets; retained content should not be casually promoted into a proprietary-data thesis. My position is **Neutral**: strong founder relevance, an intelligible market, and insufficient evidence that value compounds inside Maritime. My verifiable flip condition is a production benchmark showing that a rights-cleared, proprietary fleet dataset produces a sustained reliability or cost advantage across independent customers that an otherwise equivalent implementation cannot reproduce. That would turn operating experience into an owned asset.
