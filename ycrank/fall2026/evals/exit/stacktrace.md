# Stacktrace -- Exit Evaluation

**Action:** Watch  
**Evidence confidence:** Low

### Company case

A strategic acquisition by an enterprise security platform is Stacktrace’s strongest private exit path. The memo documents Fleet’s October launch and describes a local sidecar that reads coding-agent session logs and uses hooks or gateways to intervene. Stacktrace says Fleet can enforce a single policy across multiple coding agents, and a founder reports unnamed design partners running local or unattended cloud agents. The memo provides no measured production benefit or realized revenue. (*The Idea*, *Market & Traction*, *Key Risks*, *Key Facts*)

A buyer could acquire Fleet to embed cross-agent oversight in its own platform and control the integrations and policy roadmap. Ownership could beat building or licensing if paid production deployments show that Fleet closes consequential gaps across multiple agent runtimes and that maintaining those integrations in-house would be costly. The launch and reported design partners make that milestone plausible. The reported $500,000 YC financing offers some support for getting there, while undisclosed burn and runway leave the cost and timing uncertain. The memo leaves IP ownership and transfer terms unverified. (*The Idea*, *Market & Traction*, *Key Risks*)

The memo reports the YC financing as a convertible note and supplies no terms for the check being evaluated. The illustrative case uses one $500,000 check at a $10 million post-money valuation, with 5% initial ownership diluted by 40% relative to 3% and no follow-on checks. A 5× gross cash return within eight years requires $2.5 million to the holder, or $83.3 million in distributable equity proceeds at 3%. The hurdle is $125 million at 2% and $62.5 million at 4%. (*Market & Traction*)

The sale prices below are scenario assumptions. For both sales, assume zero net debt and transaction fees, full cash payment at closing, and a simple pro rata waterfall with no preference adjustment. Enterprise value therefore equals distributable equity cash. Stock, escrow, earnouts, rollover, and compensation are each assumed to be $0. The memo provides no buyer economics or applicable deal terms to substantiate those prices or assumptions. (*Market & Traction*, *Key Facts*)

The strong sale clears the hurdle at 3% and yields only $2 million at 2%. In the prolonged-private scenario, growth plateaus, buyers stay away, public scale remains out of reach, and cash is reinvested. The investor retains the shares. The memo supplies no independently supported route to cash for this holder if a strategic sale fails. (*Market & Traction*, *Key Facts*)

### Countercase

Anthropic already offers managed permissions and hooks that cover some of Fleet’s oversight functions, and Bay deploys policy enforcement through existing security infrastructure. A platform buyer could build on native controls or license overlapping coverage, weakening the case for paying an acquisition premium. Fleet also depends on supported logs and enforcement points, which can limit the value of its cross-agent coverage. The memo leaves open whether those substitutes cover the workflows that matter to a buyer and whether they cap the price below the hurdle. That gap supports Watch, and the evidence falls short of the structural Pass override. (*The Idea*, *Defensibility*, *Market & Traction*, *Key Risks*)

### Decisive diligence question

Can an enterprise security platform quantify enough value from owning Fleet to support a price above the $83.3 million equity hurdle in the illustrative 3% case? A buyer’s build-versus-license analysis grounded in measured results from paid deployments across multiple agent runtimes would answer it. Evidence that ownership can justify that price would move the action to Reach out.
