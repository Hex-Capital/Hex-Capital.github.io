# GitCafe -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: coding agents are increasing repository activity and security failures, software teams may need an agent-native forge, and GitCafe becomes large only if that workflow change causes teams to move their primary repositories—not merely add another developer tool. The dossier never establishes that controlling variable explicitly or supplies a migration threshold. GitHub already has 180 million developers and 630 million repositories, while GitLab exceeds $1 billion in ARR. In this market, feature preference is noise unless it overcomes repository gravity.

The technical work is the strongest part of the company. GitCafe owns libgitz, the kiln runtime, a native `cafe:git` binding, the forge, and its CLI. Native stacked diffs and agent-skill installation make this more substantial than a browser skin over standard git. But architectural depth is not yet workflow ownership. GitHub Actions compatibility, GitLab CI support, and one-command migration are still planned; GitCafe therefore does not yet control the integrations, permissions, automation, and operational state that make a repository platform difficult to replace. A custom engine can be impressive while solving the wrong bottleneck.

The proposed discontinuity is plausible but weakly connected to adoption. Public commits rose 43% in 2025, and secret leakage in AI-assisted code reportedly runs at roughly twice the baseline. Those numbers support better agent-facing controls and push-time security, but they do not show that customers need a new system of record. GitHub, GitLab, or an independent security vendor could incorporate those features without asking a team to migrate its code, CI/CD, identity controls, and collaboration graph. The market shift currently expands the need for tooling more clearly than it expands the market for a new forge.

Engineers at eight named organizations are presented as users, which is meaningful pre-seed evidence that the product is real and can attract technical experimenters. It is not yet product-market pull: private repositories are free, pricing is unpublished, and the dossier provides no repeat usage, migrated production repositories, retention, or capacity constraint. The strongest bull case is that these users are the beginning of a developer-led transition and that vertical ownership lets GitCafe iterate faster than incumbents. That case stings because the two-person team has already built unusually deep infrastructure. It still does not qualify for a frontier-team exception: the dossier documents capable implementation, not scarce field-defining research or an institutional vacuum that neutralizes incumbent distribution.

Veronica’s experience as a founding engineer at Corgi and her Rust, TypeScript, and platform-binding work establish credible founder-market fit. They do not establish the costly commitment, prior founder outcome, or personal necessity that would justify overriding the market evidence. The company’s unstated-pattern fit is also incomplete: GitCafe is entering a mature, consolidated category rather than buying early coverage across an unsettled technical layer. The red flag is not that the product is thin—it is that substantial engineering may precede proof that the customer’s controlling constraint is the git implementation rather than migration, trust, integrations, and network effects.

I pass today. My base case is that within 36 months GitCafe remains below $1 million ARR and pivots from replacing GitHub to an agent-native stacked-diff, code-review, or repository-security product that plugs into incumbent forges. One verifiable condition flips the decision: at least 25 organizations move their primary private repositories to GitCafe, pay for the service, and retain usage above 70% after six months. That would show that agent-native workflow ownership can overcome repository gravity; without it, the custom stack is technology in search of a market-ending move.
