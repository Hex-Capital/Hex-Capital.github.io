# Obia -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Obia’s possible secret is that industrial tenants choose space through operational constraints that conventional property search handles poorly: permitted uses, site combinations, and throughput per dollar of rent. The October 23 logistics engagement makes this proposition concrete. It does not establish that other brokers reject it, or that Obia solves it substantially better. The falsification test is straightforward: give Obia and an experienced industrial broker the same requirements and compare verified, usable recommendations, research time, and completed leases. Until that comparison exists, I have a hypothesis worth testing, with no demonstrated contrarian truth.

The investment problem is value capture. Obia reports accumulating tenant requirements, but ordinary customer records do not automatically become proprietary technology or a network effect. Tandem already combines AI with office brokerage; Crexi supplies overlapping research automation to existing brokers. These are different competitive positions, but both constrain Obia’s claim to uniqueness. Industrial zoning could support a bounded beachhead. Representation across industrial, office, and other asset classes does not yet define one. The adverse evidence is existing product overlap; the missing evidence is a mechanism that lets Obia escape it.

The strongest bull case is that owning the transaction changes what the company can learn. Obia’s logistics work included site-combination analysis and throughput-per-rent comparisons—decisions with consequences beyond producing a faster property summary. Completed transactions could connect operational requirements to actual outcomes and improve subsequent recommendations. This fits my willingness to invest beyond celebrated technology categories, including ordinary commercial and financial workflows. But the dossier documents a research engagement, without a completed transaction or exclusive feedback rights. My known exceptions require a demonstrated technical breakthrough, persistent transaction demand, or a concrete deployment path that resolves the relevant risk. Nothing here yet overrides the competition concern.

The founding structure remains unknown. Samuel Cho’s Amazon and research background supports engineering capability; it does not establish shared working history, full-time commitment, ownership, or control. Jason Henkel’s customer-intake experience is relevant, while his precise founding role is unverified. The discrepancy between Obia Inc. branding and sole-proprietor terms deserves clarification before a check. It does not prove the part-time-founder, outsourced-engineering, or investor-dominated-capitalization red flags. I assign the founding dimension its missing-evidence default.

Obia has identified a real distribution route: online inquiries and its October 26–28 CoreNet exhibition put the team in contact with potential occupiers. Free tenant representation removes an upfront purchasing obstacle, although landlord-paid commissions still require successful transactions. That earns limited distribution credit. It supplies no commission rate, delivery margin, or repeatable acquisition economics from which to construct a power-law outcome. Brokers still conduct tours and negotiations, so faster research alone leaves the principal scaling question unresolved. The $292.6 billion brokerage-and-management proxy cannot answer it.

I pass with my own money. The concern is competitive economics, rather than Obia’s pre-seed lack of revenue. One verifiable result would change my decision to investigate: an independently audited cohort of completed industrial leases in one defined corridor demonstrating a substantial contribution-profit advantage per broker-hour over conventional brokerage, sustained by transaction data Obia has exclusive rights to retain. That would connect useful automation to an ownable business.
