# Mireye -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: AI agents can now query physical-world systems through MCP, data-center developers are an initial buyer, and Mireye becomes large only if its normalized, provenance-linked fields make recurring site decisions materially faster than incumbent GIS tools can. The dossier establishes the mechanism but not the controlling threshold. One customer reports sourcing off-market land 100 times faster, yet there is no query volume, retention, paid usage, or evidence that this speed advantage changes which vendor owns the workflow.

The technical shift is real but only partly captured. Remote MCP support arrived in May 2025, Mireye has integrated 85 sources, and its API returns typed fields with source URLs, timestamps, and confidence levels. That converts fragmented government portals into machine-readable inputs. But MCP also lowers integration costs for Mapbox, CARTO, and Fused. The protocol expands adoption for the category; it does not by itself expand Mireye’s margins or establish its value capture.

The strongest bull case is the combination of product velocity and founder execution. The catalog already exceeds 300 fields and is reportedly growing 20% weekly. First customers have built working land-sourcing agents, outbound to 50 prospects produced a 10% response rate, and Ansh Chokshi previously took Poker Pit to $250,000 ARR in four months before an acquisition. This is more informative than founder polish: it shows prior company-building learning and an ability to convert a narrow wedge into revenue.

The counterargument is that Mireye may be building a reusable physical-world data layer before the market has crystallized. Accepted field requests become available to subsequent callers, potentially lowering fulfillment cost while broadening the catalog. Better models should also improve query interpretation rather than erase the underlying source normalization and provenance work. That is the right direction for durable infrastructure.

The red flag is the unpriced moat. Mireye does not estimate what it would cost Mapbox or another well-funded competitor to reproduce the useful subset of its 85-source catalog, and its $19 and $99 plans imply limited present willingness to pay. The product currently supplies data to an agent rather than owning the resulting site-selection decision, permitting workflow, underwriting, or transaction. The attractive infrastructure pattern therefore remains a possibility, not demonstrated compounding.

None of the known exception cases supplies an override: this is neither scarce frontier research, a documented reconstruction of physical-market economics, nor irreplaceable technical talent whose asset value stands independently of workflow ownership. I would not invest yet. My flip condition is ten data-center developers each paying at least $25,000 annually, with six-month retention and repeat field requests showing that Mireye—not a customer’s surrounding application—has become the persistent system for site diligence.
