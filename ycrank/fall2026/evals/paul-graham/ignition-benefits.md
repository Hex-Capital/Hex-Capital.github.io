# Ignition Benefits -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Nick Taranto has encountered this problem from the right side of the desk. He managed payroll, a PEO, and benefits brokers while building Plated. That makes Ignition Benefits more interesting than an insurance company conceived by searching for industries to apply AI to. Benefits also contains precisely the work people avoid: carrier negotiations, enrollment, compliance, and employee questions. The origin and the schlep are real. What I cannot find is the particular obstacle Taranto encountered while building Ignition, what he did about it, and how quickly it yielded. Two previous exits make me interested in that conversation. They don't supply the missing episode.

The strongest argument for investing is that employers have already entrusted Ignition with something consequential. AWM reports saving $156,914 against its 2026 renewal quote while keeping Gusto for payroll. Light Labs reports $113,115 in first-year savings for 25 employees. These are named customers describing an actual purchasing outcome, which is much stronger than employers agreeing that insurance is expensive. Keeping the existing payroll system also makes adoption less disruptive. But these are company-published case studies. They establish useful brokerage work more clearly than repeated demand across renewals, and neither figure is Ignition's revenue.

The important question is what gets easier with the next customer. Ignition describes software supported by licensed experts, but the dossier contains no measured technical result showing that its software changes the amount of work required. Customer savings cannot answer that question: an unusually diligent broker might produce them too. Carrier-paid commissions provide an established way to collect money, although the commission economics remain undisclosed. My recurring preference for technical teams that have already built a difficult substrate or produced a numerical proof finds little support here. That is missing evidence, not evidence that the software fails.

Competition makes this distinction consequential. Alliant announced its acquisition of Nava in August 2026, and Nava already advertises renewal modeling, compliance tracking, and AI benefits answers. Ignition could still win by serving small employers much better. A crowded market doesn't prevent that. The opposing case to my pass is substantial: an experienced operator knows the buyer's problem, has delivered large reported savings, and has an acquisition channel that reportedly produces at least one qualified meeting daily. Partnerships are therefore not demonstrably the single dependency that would trigger my distribution red flag. Nor does a 12-person team establish premature delegation. But meetings are not customers, and service quality alone doesn't establish a rapidly improving software business.

I would pass at present. My exceptions allow an impressive working prototype or unmistakable customer pull to outweigh missing founder evidence; these case studies are encouraging, but don't yet establish either at that strength. There is also no documented evidence-driven change of route, which earns the missing-evidence default rather than a penalty for stubbornness. One verifiable result could change my decision: a documented renewal cohort showing that a founder-built workflow overcame a specific carrier or integration obstacle, customers renewed, and service hours per account fell while coverage was preserved. That would connect resourcefulness, software, and demand. Today those links remain unproved.
