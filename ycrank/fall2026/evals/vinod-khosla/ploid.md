# Ploid -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Ploid’s two-billion-person index measures coverage. It does not yet measure a consequential change in what expertise costs or who can access it. Reliable identity resolution could let agents perform research that currently requires expensive human verification. That is worth investigating. But the dossier supplies no labor-cost baseline, hours displaced, or demonstrated expansion in access. The adjacent $4 billion sales-intelligence market establishes an existing spending pool; it does not establish an orders-of-magnitude consequence. I pass on the evidence available.

The strongest reason I could be wrong is the product’s reported performance: 81% accuracy across 1,000 queries, against Exa’s 63% and Apollo’s 23%. This is substantially more informative than an agent-era slogan. Ploid has a working interface, developer distribution through API, CLI, and MCP, and a Monid integration announced September 4, 2026. Its claimed “thousands” of company users could conceal meaningful early adoption. Yet the benchmark is company-published and Gemini-graded, with insufficient detail here to establish representative queries or reliable identity ground truth. Against Exa, the reported error rate falls from 37% to 19%—roughly half, with considerable error remaining. That matters when an agent can propagate a mistaken identity into subsequent decisions.

The investment question is whether cross-site linking creates a defensible learning system or leaves Ploid assembling replaceable inputs. Its proprietary linking claim deserves technical diligence; the dossier does not justify dismissing it as an interface wrapper. But proprietary corrections compounding through usage remain an inference. Third parties supply some contact data, and Monid aggregates Ploid alongside competing vendors. That integration proves availability through a distribution channel, while also exposing substitution risk. My actual investing pattern includes execution-heavy businesses beyond scientific breakthroughs. The exception requires evidence of a wedge that can expand into a much larger system. Neither a consequential expansion path nor a demonstrated compounding advantage is established here.

Hernandez’s recommendation systems and embedding work at Wander give him relevant engineering context. Karic’s research background adds a different discipline. Neither affiliation nor friendship tells me how these founders revise beliefs or recruit someone to own a critical weakness; those remain unknown. Likewise, the dossier identifies accuracy and data dependency as risks but documents no founder-run experiment with a budget, numerical threshold, and stop-or-change decision. I would make dependable, economical identity resolution the first risk to attack. The missing evidence is about risk retirement, not team size, publicity, or fundraising history.

Ploid charges $200 for 1,000 credits and $600 for 4,000. Those are selling prices, not costs per correctly resolved person after retrieval, vendor fees, model inference, and verification. There is no basis to claim either attractive scale economics or permanent subsidy dependence. My one flip condition is an independently reproducible, held-out test showing Ploid delivers verified person reports at one-tenth Exa’s fully loaded cost at matched accuracy, including all purchased inputs and human corrections. That result would reopen my decision by demonstrating an economic discontinuity in the workflow. Today, the evidence supports a promising search product more strongly than it supports the asymmetric breakthrough I would buy with my own money.
