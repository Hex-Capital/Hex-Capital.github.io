# SpaceFlow Technologies, Inc. -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I have a $1 million raise, but no post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or evidence that this is the first scarce institutional check. Operators associated with Airbnb, Volvo Cars, Google, Encord, and Sequoia may be useful investors; they do not tell me whether I am buying a neglected belief or YC consensus. The capital plan is equally opaque. “Hire and grow” is not disclosed, but neither is one finite experiment with a metric and deadline. Those are my two primary gates, and SpaceFlow has not cleared either.

The strongest evidence is beneath the financing story. SpaceFlow has six-figure contracted annual revenue, hundreds of daily production users, and software touching $400 million of supplier spend. The spend figure alone is metric theater—it belongs to customers—but the signed national-restaurant contract, executive’s personal investment, and reported $120,000 annual savings connect an embedded workflow to an actual purchase. Customer-specific system mappings, approvals, and corrections also generate proprietary evals. That is a real dark pool, not 388 LinkedIn followers pretending to be distribution.

The founders have shipped enough to make the translation layer credible. Hakan Aksu previously generated $40,000 from ad automation and sold a tender-matching engine for five figures; the team then built an autonomous procurement system with a medical manufacturer before YC. That demonstrates technical execution and direct access to enterprise procurement workflows. It falls short of top-tier proof because nobody is documented as having owned procurement, ERP administration, or supply-chain operations from the buyer’s chair. They have crossed into the second ecosystem, but have not yet shown that they lived there.

The bear case stings because SAP already controls the installed system, identity graph, and procurement data that SpaceFlow must laboriously map. Zip, ORO Labs, Levelpath, and Arkestro collectively have raised hundreds of millions of dollars, while SpaceFlow must support customized tables, approval chains, air-gapped networks, and hostile supplier attachments. Its on-premises runtime may be defensible infrastructure, or it may be consulting disguised as software. No gross margin, burn, implementation cost, retention, or runway is disclosed, so six-figure contracted revenue cannot yet establish a business that survives without another round.

Still, this is not the generic AI-wrapper red flag. Models are interchangeable, agents run inside the customer’s network, identity passes through existing controls, and approvals remain auditable. The base business already produces contracted revenue and measurable customer savings; AI is attached to an operating procurement workflow rather than offered as magic. The unresolved red flags are capital allocation and organizational accountability: three co-founders are named, but the dossier does not identify who has final authority over product, hiring, and financing.

I am neutral at the current evidence price. My 36-month base case is that SpaceFlow reaches roughly $3 million–$7 million ARR as a deployment-heavy procurement automation vendor, but does not establish a broad enterprise-agent runtime because customization and incumbent distribution constrain expansion. One verifiable condition flips me to invest: disclose a sub-$10 million post-money round whose proceeds fund a dated experiment showing that a second customer can reach production within 30 days at greater than 70% gross margin, without founder-led implementation. That would simultaneously prove scarce pricing, a venture moment, and a path out of the capital factory.
