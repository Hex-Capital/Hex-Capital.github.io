# Risklytics -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Risklytics has helped customers buy something they previously could not obtain. That interests me more than “AI-native insurance.” But proposed post-money valuation, competing term sheets, and whether my money would be the first scarce check are all undisclosed. I cannot call this an auction because the founders attended Harvard and YC. I also cannot underwrite a cheap, non-consensus entry without a price. I would pass on the evidence available.

The useful mechanism is translating between physical-autonomy companies and insurers whose policy language does not cleanly describe their risks. The founders reported helping three companies obtain coverage after earlier declines in August 2026, spanning robotics rental, bridge-collision technology, and teleoperation. Those are actual placement outcomes, substantially more informative than almost 50 application users. Risklytics fits my preference for connecting mistrustful systems and tolerating physical-world complexity when it solves an operating problem. The unresolved question is who owns the access. Wholesaler and MGA partnerships enable placements; the dossier does not establish exclusive capacity or show that customers arrived because of proprietary relationships or data. The 736-filing exclusion tracker demonstrates useful work, but competitors can read the same filings.

The strongest argument against passing is that successful placements could create the very advantage currently missing. A broker who gets a previously rejected robot fleet insured can earn referrals, renewals, and increasingly useful knowledge of which underwriter will accept which exposure. Carrier-paid commissions of 10–20% offer a concrete business model, and licensed human review means the economics need not assume AI errors disappear. That is a credible base business. Yet premium volume, collected commissions, servicing costs, retention, and burn remain undisclosed. These three reported placements do not establish the retained paid demand or cash economics that would justify overriding my financing gates. Application automation also faces RiskCube and broader brokers; the defensible asset would have to accumulate in relationships and placement outcomes.

Gold and Risio have done more than collect credentials: they built the Ember catastrophe-modeling beta, shipped an application workflow, and Risio obtained producer licensing. That earns some credit for operating across software and insurance. It does not establish deep operating experience with deployed autonomous systems or a scaled brokerage. Their pivot from wildfire modeling may have brought them closer to an immediate purchasing constraint, but a completed pivot is not the experiment new capital will fund. There is no disclosed hypothesis, budget, deadline, or pass/fail milestone. I cannot award venture-moment credit for an experiment I could design on their behalf.

I can see Risklytics becoming a useful specialist brokerage without knowing whether its equity is attractive. For my own check, the distinction matters: commission income could support a durable business, while an expensive seed could still produce a poor investment. My single verifiable flip condition is a financing proposal below $10 million post-money that funds one dated test: demonstrate, within six months, that a defined cohort of previously declined physical-autonomy customers generates positive cash contribution after acquisition and servicing costs. That would connect price, the capital experiment, and an operating business. Today, that connection remains unproven.
