# Hesper AI -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Generative AI is making claim evidence easier to fabricate while giving insurers new tools to investigate it. Carriers, MGAs and TPAs now buy relief from investigation backlogs; the controlling variable is completed, defensible investigations per human reviewer-hour. Hesper AI’s move from unsuccessfully selling fraud scores to delivering investigation files identifies a meaningful distinction: another flag creates work, while a completed investigation can remove it. The dossier supports that mechanism, although it does not establish the quality threshold at which customers can consistently reduce review effort.

The reported economics make this worth underwriting. The founders claim approximately 80% lower handling costs overall and an 82% reduction in adjuster time at one MGA. Those are company-reported results, not independently verified unit economics, but the magnitude is substantial enough to survive some discounting. The important denominator includes human checking, external database licences and implementation. Hesper’s contract puts database access on the customer and requires qualified human approval for adverse decisions. If those costs consume the savings, faster document processing will not translate into a substantially better business.

Hesper AI has more demand evidence than a demonstration or an insurance-market forecast. The founders report 10,000-plus claims investigated and a production MGA deployment that identified $1.2 million in potential fraud over two weeks. That is meaningful operational exposure for a two-person company. It does not establish collected revenue, confirmed fraud recoveries or durable customer expansion. The first 200 claims free and 90-day platform-fee waiver also make activity an imperfect proxy for willingness to pay. I give the deployment credit without treating the reported $15 million in potential fraud as Hesper’s economic output or revenue.

The product’s architecture is directionally attractive: document inspection, external-record queries, timeline reconstruction and evidence-backed case files form a workflow that stronger models should improve. This fits my preference for software performing institutional work. But workflow breadth is ahead of demonstrated workflow ownership. The dossier documents no persistent integration advantage, proprietary evaluation loop or expensive-to-reproduce dataset. Restrictions on shared customer-data training further limit the obvious pooled-data argument. Customer-specific configurations could become switching costs; they have not earned that credit yet.

The founders have made a concrete commitment. Badu reports leaving his job five months earlier, and the team rebuilt after the initial product failed to sell. That is useful evidence of costly commitment and learning. Their Nanonets experience also makes document automation and enterprise distribution credible execution capabilities, although the $200,000 and $4 million ARR figures belong to their former employer. Neither establishes Hesper’s product-market fit or a prior founder outcome.

I would make a small personal investment. The strongest objection is that Hesper AI proves the workflow just as Shift and FRISS incorporate equivalent capabilities into established distribution. Shift’s Guidewire relationship makes that threat concrete. My portfolio pattern of backing integrated workflows supports Hesper; the associated red flag is assuming that integration automatically creates defensibility. Here, actual claim processing and potentially large labour savings justify the bet, while unproven retention and workflow ownership limit conviction. One result would flip me to pass: a customer-run, matched-file benchmark showing less than 50% all-in handling-cost savings at equivalent audited investigative quality. That is my underwriting threshold, not a result the dossier has already established.
