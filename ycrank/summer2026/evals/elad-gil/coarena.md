# CoArena -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: computer-use agents have become capable enough to attempt real workflows, AI labs now need failure data beyond fixed benchmarks, and CoArena becomes large only if its rate of acquiring unique, decision-relevant trajectories makes licensing cheaper and more useful than generating equivalent data internally. That last variable is measurable, but the dossier does not yet establish its threshold. The $60,000 earned within two weeks is encouraging evidence that someone will pay; it does not disclose the buyers, contract recurrence, trajectory volume, or whether revenue scales with corpus quality.

Demand is the strongest signal. Users doubled weekly during the first two weeks, 14 agents entered the leaderboard, and the associated hackathon listed 2,550 participants. At pre-seed, $60,000 of current-product revenue matters more than the modest Product Hunt response of 109 points. The open question is whether customers bought a recurring evaluation input or a launch-period experiment. CoArena should measure expansion from the first contracts, repeat task submission, and revenue per thousand useful trajectories—not registrations or battles.

The product also has more substance than a static leaderboard. Each comparison can generate a consented real-work task, two trajectories, observations, actions, reasoning, and a blind preference label. Fifty-seven published measures suggest the team understands that computer use is multi-dimensional. Better underlying agents should increase the value of difficult tasks and failure analysis rather than erase the product. Still, the dataset page shows neither task-category coverage nor annotation fields, and no integrations, customer feedback loop, or operational system of record is documented. Today this is a promising data-production mechanism, not yet an owned evaluation workflow.

The bear case is severe because this market is already crystallizing. LMArena has more than three million votes and $250 million raised; Patronus and Braintrust have raised $70 million and $121 million respectively. Those companies can move toward computer-use evaluation, while a well-funded lab can manufacture tasks or collect its own production traces. CoArena has identified no structural barrier. Its simultaneous operation of a competing computer-use agent creates neutrality concerns, and transmitting unmasked screenshots without SOC 2, ISO 27001, or an independent audit blocks the sensitive enterprise workflows that would produce the most valuable data. This is exactly where an asserted data moat must be translated into reproduction cost.

The founders partially offset that concern. Prateek Jannu dropped a Stanford graduate admission to build Coasty, and both founders previously operated a computer-use product reportedly serving thousands of active users and paying customers across four continents. Coasty’s reported 82.81% OSWorld result also gives them direct experience with the shortcomings they are attacking. That is costly commitment plus product learning, although it does not substitute for proving that CoArena’s corpus is difficult to reproduce.

I would invest, narrowly. My 36-month base case is that CoArena reaches $6 million to $12 million in ARR by becoming the independent real-work evaluation and trajectory supplier for computer-use labs, rather than remaining a public voting site. The verifiable flip condition is failure, within six months, to show repeat contracts from at least three unaffiliated labs alongside measurable growth in unique task categories and licensed trajectories. Without that evidence, the $60,000 looks episodic, the neutrality and security issues become binding, and I pass.
