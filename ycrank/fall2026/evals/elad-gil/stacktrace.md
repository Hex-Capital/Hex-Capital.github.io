# Stacktrace -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Unattended agents create a new buyer problem for engineering and security teams: supervision has to scale without adding a person for every running agent. Stacktrace becomes a large company if its findings reliably determine which agent actions an enterprise permits. That is the controlling variable. Reading transcripts is useful; becoming a prerequisite for production deployment creates a budget. The dossier documents detection and delivery mechanisms, but does not establish that customers depend on them to authorize unattended work.

The strongest evidence is architectural. Stacktrace processes transcripts locally, attributes tool calls to installed components, and sends findings into Fleet, Slack, or the running agent. Its inline gateway provides blocking. These are documented connections to an operational workflow, which gives the product substance beyond a security dashboard. The boundary matters, however: the endpoint sidecar cannot stop calls, dynamic steering is coming soon, and deterministic rules acknowledge misses and false positives. Better models could increase the volume of work requiring supervision, but Stacktrace has not demonstrated that its control becomes more effective as agent behavior becomes more complex.

The market opening is credible; the economic step change remains unmeasured. A demonstration of credential leakage in a remote support workflow establishes a concrete failure mode. It does not quantify supervision hours eliminated, losses prevented, or additional deployments enabled. The free tier—100 agents and 200 session-hours monthly—could build distribution, but annual custom Enterprise and Gateway pricing does not yet establish willingness to pay. Meanwhile, Noma advertises steering, masking, and kill switches, and Zenity raised $125 million in August 2026. This category is crystallizing around competing control platforms. Stacktrace needs a reason customers select its enforcement point, beyond the category growing.

The bull case is substantial enough to take seriously. The founders have operated infrastructure, security, and AI platforms; Kone’s software supply-chain responsibility at Harness is particularly relevant. A released CLI, documented integrations, and company-reported design partners running agents locally and in the cloud show execution against an actual deployment problem. Those partners earn some demand credit, although their identities, recurring usage, and commitments are undisclosed. Benedict’s previous company establishes prior founding experience, but the dossier gives no specific lessons or costly commitment that would support a stronger founder-motivation score.

My pattern of investing across infrastructure layers during a technology transition supports investigating Stacktrace. It does not establish that this particular layer captures durable value. Open-source collection and composition analysis may encourage adoption while leaving competitors able to reproduce that foundation. There is no documented proprietary-data advantage to underwrite. Nor does the exceptional-team override resolve the market question: these are experienced operators, but the dossier does not supply frontier research or a scarce technical breakthrough that warrants bypassing commercial evidence. There is also no affirmative evidence of status-driven fundraising or premature expansion; those red flags do not drive my decision.

I would pass today because neither market gate is established, despite a credible workflow foundation. One verifiable flip condition would change my view: three named enterprises renewing paid Gateway deployments after six months, with records showing that Stacktrace’s enforced policies are required for their unattended agents to remain in production. That would connect detection to deployment permission, deployment permission to recurring budget, and the initial installation to an owned control workflow.
