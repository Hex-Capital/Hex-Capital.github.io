# Derya -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Oguzhan Karaca worked inside CEVA/CMA CGM legal, while Mert Turna built ship-operations software used on more than 30 vessels representing $200 million of fleet value. Derya is a domain-native logistics bet with a governance-shaped hole: the founders have touched the machinery, but Derya has not disclosed who breaks the tie when eight years of friendship meets a disputed credit loss. I see Biz and Tiz. I cannot yet see whether Rizz belongs to one leader or is shared until it becomes dangerous.

**Anduril** is the high rung for lived origin: Palmer Luckey moved from building frontier hardware to building defense hardware because the artifact preceded the market thesis. Derya shares that mechanism more than most AI startups—Karaca encountered freight through CEVA/CMA CGM, and Turna previously built truck operations and deployed ship software across 30-plus vessels. Derya diverges because neither founder is documented as having run a freight-forwarding book, underwritten trade credit, or scaled an adjacent product; they are of the neighborhood, but I do not yet know whether they have slept on the loading dock.

**HQ Trivia** exposed the low-rung failure mechanism: two capable founders without an unambiguous tiebreaker can turn product success into organizational shrapnel. Derya names Karaca as CEO, which is better than a shared title, but Derya discloses no equity split, operating authority, vesting protocol, or deadlock agreement between high-school best friends. The known exception does not rescue them: HQ Trivia’s product was so tactile and addictive that I could feel the override in my hand, while a same-day freight quote is not explosive consumer virality. Until Karaca’s authority is explicit, “best friends for eight years” is warmth, not governance.

**$1.1 million from YC, 212, Umur Cubukcu, and angels** means Derya is not hiding undiscovered behind a North Dakota gas flare. Derya’s weirdness must compound operationally over the next 18 months: AI agents execute bookings, each shipment creates carrier-performance data, and financing binds customers more tightly than workflow software can. The unsexy part is real—customs, cargo liability, carrier qualification, and trade credit—but Flexport, Forto, Nuvocargo, and Zencargo already make digital forwarding legible. No sophisticated investor rejection or persistent cold pursuit is documented, so my unstated sourcing pattern is absent and the “AI-native” label risks becoming consensus clothing.

**Bookings above $20,000 qualify for Derya financing**, placing Derya closer to regulated financial infrastructure than to an AI-app wrapper. The score would flip into the top tier if Derya controlled underwriting data, disclosed a bankruptcy-remote funding facility, and proved that defaults or cargo claims were transferred rather than warehoused on its own balance sheet. Today Derya relies on partner licenses and publishes no underwriting criteria, loss history, or risk-transfer mechanism. That makes the ugly operational burden attractive, but not yet a compounding regulatory moat.

**Flock Safety** is the physical-world parallel: software mattered because cameras appeared in neighborhoods and produced an observable artifact. Derya directly quotes and ships freight rather than selling agents to forwarders, and Derya has signed TVO for 20 containers while reporting more than $3 million of managed transactions. A container arriving is wonderfully real. But a normal person cannot try Derya within 48 hours, and the dossier provides no shipment-level delivery time, repeat-booking rate, or customer testimony, so the artifact remains visible mainly to freight operators.

**$80,000 of contracted revenue in Derya’s first month** supports a specific forecast: by July 2029, Derya will reach $12 million–$20 million in annualized gross revenue as a trade-finance-enabled digital forwarder, while Karaca remains the sole CEO and Turna operates under a formally bounded technical mandate. Derya’s lived freight experience should win enough lanes to build a serious operating business; its crowded category and unresolved authority should keep Derya from becoming a magically weird infrastructure breakout during those 36 months. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing—the AI is the wrapper, and the wager is whether two old friends can turn containers, credit, and exceptions into institutional memory.

**Flexport, Forto, Nuvocargo, and Zencargo** make the bear mechanism sting: Derya could absorb customs liability and working-capital risk while incumbents copy the agents, compress forwarding margins, and retain superior carrier data. Derya’s $3.2 million opportunity figure is pipeline, the partner network is non-exclusive, and no proprietary IP prevents a polymath generalist from rebuilding the software layer. The evidence that wins—for now—is $80,000 already contracted, 20 signed containers, Karaca’s freight legal experience, and Turna’s software running on 30-plus vessels. I invest with one foot in love and one hand on the credit ledger; if Derya has no repeat customers or produces forwarding gross margins below 15% by January 2027, I flip to Pass.
