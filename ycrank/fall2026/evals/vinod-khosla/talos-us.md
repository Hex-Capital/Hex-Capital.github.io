# Talos -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

A transformer that takes years to replace is a consequential bottleneck. Preventing its failure could preserve electricity supply and scarce equipment simultaneously. That makes Talos worth examining. But the $3.11 billion transformer-monitoring market measures spending on monitoring, not the consequence of Talos succeeding. The dossier does not quantify additional transformer life, outages avoided, or capacity recovered. I see a potentially important intervention; I cannot yet price an extraordinary outcome.

The technical question is whether external measurements reveal actionable faults substantially better or cheaper than existing diagnostics. Talos has demonstrated something concrete: 304 magnetic readings mapped around a condensate pump. That earns limited credit for an implemented sensing method. It does not establish transformer diagnosis. Camlin already monitors dissolved gases, bushings, and partial discharge; Oktogrid already offers magnetic-adhesion sensing. Talos needs to show what its combination of magnetic fields, vibration, temperature, and humidity detects that these alternatives miss. Spatial localization becomes valuable when it changes a maintenance decision correctly.

The strongest bull case is the combination of retrofit hardware and complementary scientific skills. Kohli brings power-engineering and predictive-maintenance exposure from Constellation; Hwang researches physiological waveforms using over 100 TB of ICU data; Musku brings research experience including a first-author IEEE paper. This fits my less-public pattern of backing technology that works with installed infrastructure and turns scarce diagnostic expertise into a repeatable product. The objection to passing is serious: useful failure signatures may emerge from combining disciplines incumbents have kept separate. However, the dossier establishes neither that discovery nor a documented instance of belief revision or recruiting against a named weakness. Relevant backgrounds earn attention; those specific founder behaviors remain unproven.

Talos’s largest technical kill risk is whether its signals distinguish developing transformer faults from ordinary operating variation early enough to act. The pump demonstration does not resolve that question. There is no disclosed experiment with verified fault outcomes, a pass/fail threshold, cost, or stop decision, so milestone discipline receives the absent-evidence score. I would spend the next dollar on establishing diagnostic validity. My verifiable flip condition is an operator-validated transformer trial showing at least fivefold lower fully installed monitoring cost than a named incumbent, with matched or better fault detection, warning lead time, and false-alarm performance. That result would reopen my decision.

Economics could defeat even accurate sensing. Talos publishes no pricing, installation burden, servicing cost, or hardware replacement assumptions. External attachment offers a plausible route to inexpensive deployment, but plausibility is not a cost model. Likewise, equipment histories could become a proprietary learning loop only if they acquire verified failure labels. Neither missing revenue nor a three-person team troubles me at this stage. The missing evidence concerns what the product can accomplish and what delivering it costs.

I pass with my own money today. My known exceptions allow incomplete milestones or unconventional technology when demonstrated founder commitment and an enormous consequence justify buying the uncertainty; Talos has not established that override. I also find no evidence of exit-first behavior, permanent subsidy dependence, or founders ignoring contrary data, so a strong rejection would overstate the case. This is a credible technical team addressing an important physical bottleneck, with an initial demonstrator. The evidence does not yet establish the discontinuity that makes this my kind of bet.
