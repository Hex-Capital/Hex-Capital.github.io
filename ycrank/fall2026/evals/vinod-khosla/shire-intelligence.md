# Shire Intelligence -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Shire Intelligence claims 14% more guests served monthly, seven-minute faster table turns, and $40,000-plus average annual savings. Those are useful consequences for restaurant owners. They give me something more concrete than the cited $7.6 billion software market. But the measurements have no disclosed sample sizes, and the dossier supplies no baseline showing how much restaurant operating cost could ultimately disappear. I can see a better-run dining room. The case for an orders-of-magnitude change in the cost of delivering hospitality remains unproven.

The technical question is whether cameras can reliably close the loop between observing service and directing useful action. Recognizing an occupied table is one task; deciding that its guests need attention is substantially harder. Shire’s disclosed SAM3 and DINOv3 prototype establishes a plausible starting mechanism, but there is no measured accuracy, latency, or improvement against manual coordination or Eat App. Using available models is sensible engineering. It does not establish the technical discontinuity I need. The proposed footage-and-corrections learning loop remains an inference, with training rights and production feedback undocumented.

The strongest argument against passing is that these founders have actually worked inside the problem. Kuperman and Tabaku bring restaurant experience; Guduru brings engineering and classification research. Their reported hackathon win shows they built something. Existing CCTV and a $40 monthly entry price could make experimentation unusually inexpensive. Shire fits my unstated preference for applying AI to physical work through installed infrastructure, and my broader investment pattern leaves room for execution-driven businesses. However, the founders’ backgrounds do not establish fast belief revision or difficult, complementary recruiting. My known exceptions require a demonstrated instigator attacking an enormous value pool, or an extraordinary consequence that justifies buying the option early. Neither override is established here.

I would spend the next dollars testing whether Shire’s instructions save staff attention during a crowded service. False alerts could consume the very resource the product promises to release. The hackathon result does not answer that question, and the dossier contains no founder-defined kill test, numerical threshold, experiment budget, or stop condition. That earns the absent-evidence score for risk retirement. It does not justify an accusation that the team is spending irresponsibly. Likewise, commodity-model exposure raises my AI replication concern, but the available evidence does not establish that Shire competes solely through interface or implementation labor.

The economics also need careful attribution. Mathews Restaurant’s reported $65,000 payment-processing savings could validate a valuable payments offering, but it does not validate camera-driven coordination. The Intelligence subscription starts at $480 annually; inference, installation, integration, and support costs are undisclosed. A low customer price can encourage adoption while concealing expensive delivery. There is no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, and no bottom-up evidence of sustainable cost superiority.

I pass with my own money today. Shire Intelligence has credible operating context and an inexpensive route into a real workflow, but the evidence supports a useful restaurant product more strongly than a consequential technical breakthrough. One verifiable result would reopen my decision: an independently measured, 90-day deployment across ten restaurants showing at least a fivefold reduction in dining-room coordination cost at equal or better service quality, including inference, integration, and support. That would connect the product’s mechanism to a cost curve worth underwriting.
