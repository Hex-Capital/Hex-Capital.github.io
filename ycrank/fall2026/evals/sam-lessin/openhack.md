# OpenHack -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

OpenHack has demonstrated that it can find a real vulnerability. What am I paying to own that capability, and what does my check prove? The dossier supplies neither a proposed post-money valuation nor competing-term-sheet data. YC, Solo Founders and SF1 backing establishes neither scarce capital nor an auction. There is also no documented use of proceeds, deadline, or measurable experiment. Both financing dimensions get their missing-evidence defaults. I cannot substitute a technically interesting product for an underwritten deal.

Ananay Arora is the strongest reason to keep looking. His security research and bug-bounty record sit alongside shipped software, including the `apple-auth` library with 330 stars. OpenHack’s published discovery and reproduction of Papermark authentication bypass CVE-2026-36755—reported March 1 and patched March 2, 2026—adds a concrete operating receipt. This fits my unstated preference for people who translate between systems: he understands writing software and attacking its assumptions. That deserves substantial founder credit. It does not establish enterprise buying access or a repeatable sales process.

The strongest objection to passing is that exploit verification could turn security credibility into distribution. A reproducible exploit gives an engineering team a reason to act that another scanner alert does not. OpenHack already displays six customer/team logos, including HydraDB and Docura Health, and its repository has 430 stars and 65 forks. But payment, repeat usage and acquisition source are undisclosed. Those facts do not yet demonstrate that owned access causes purchases. The security-product exception in my framework requires unusually hard adoption and enterprise-demand evidence; the operating-AI exception requires measured conversion and retention. Neither override is established here.

The commercial vulnerability is that OpenHack makes its scanner available under an MIT license while customer-specific context remains a prospective moat. Aikido already combines code, cloud, runtime and autonomous pentesting; Semgrep and Terra attack adjacent workflows. A competitor need not reproduce Arora’s research reputation to bundle enough verification into an existing contract. That activates my concern about AI applications whose advantage can migrate to an incumbent, although the disclosed vulnerability makes dismissing this as an empty wrapper unfair. The $0 entry plan and negotiated enterprise usage rates reveal packaging, not contribution economics. With no margin, burn or runway data, the profitable-path dimension stays at its default; beta sandbox/browser verification raises a cost question without proving uneconomic operation.

I pass on the documented opportunity. OpenHack has credible technical authorship, but no demonstrated proprietary purchasing channel and no defined bargain for my capital. The strange-security exception offers no rescue either: the dossier supplies no person-level capital plan or contractual advantage from changing the investment container. My verifiable flip condition would be an offered sub-$10 million post-money round backed by a completed 90-day enterprise cohort test showing three named customers renewing at prices that cover inference and human verification costs, with the proceeds sufficient to reach a documented cash break-even milestone. That would connect the founder’s demonstrated capability to an asset I can price and a business that can survive without another financing.
