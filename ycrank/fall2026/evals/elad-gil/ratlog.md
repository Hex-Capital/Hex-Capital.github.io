# RATLOG -- Elad Gil Evaluation

RATLOG’s market turns on whether eliminating the turbopump reduces the cost of a reliable, reusable launch after accounting for everything the replacement architecture adds. Satellite operators are the buyers; delayed deployment is the pain. European launch diversification creates a possible opening, but the dossier does not establish a new cost or procurement threshold that RATLOG can already meet. Ninety percent fewer engine parts is a measurable engineering target. It is not yet a launch-margin calculation.

The strongest evidence is the company-reported engine hot-fire, achieved in four months for under $30,000. That is an unusually inexpensive way to begin testing a propulsion hypothesis. It supports the possibility of faster development and cheaper manufacturing. But the CEO also acknowledges increased bearing demands from rotating the engine. The economic question is whether complexity disappears or moves into bearings, structural requirements, maintenance, and qualification. Without flight-representative performance and durability data, I would give the prototype meaningful credit without extrapolating its cost to a 20-tonne orbital vehicle.

The $400 million in signed launch reservations is the strongest objection to passing. Combined with more than 70 customer discussions, it suggests the team has found buyers willing to engage before a launch vehicle exists. For a pre-seed hardware company, that matters. But unnamed counterparties and undisclosed payment and cancellation terms leave the strength of that commitment unresolved. These agreements could represent scarce future capacity that customers genuinely need, or inexpensive options on another provider. I would not treat the headline value as revenue or demonstrated product-market pull. SpaceX already operates reusable launches, while Stoke has raised $2.3 billion; RATLOG needs an economic advantage or a procurement channel those competitors cannot readily satisfy.

There is a credible case for backing this team before commercial validation. Weise and Kunhart have propulsion experience through Argonaut and Innuendo, Kelm brings avionics experience, and Driesnack has founding-team experience at Lio. The inexpensive hot-fire fits my pattern of funding technology that could change physical-industry economics. However, the research-stage exception requires exceptional technical evidence and a credible capital path; the dossier establishes neither at the required level. The physical-business exception similarly depends on demonstrated unit economics. A €30,000 government grant is useful prototype funding, but it does not establish financing through orbital deployment. There is no evidence of fundraising vanity or premature hiring; the unresolved issue is whether engine simplification changes the economics of the complete business.

RATLOG could eventually accumulate valuable manufacturing processes, test data, and recovery experience. Today those are prospective assets. The dossier does not document an operational feedback loop or proprietary launch state that earns an owned-workflow premium. Likewise, the founders’ relevant technical backgrounds establish competence, but not documented personal sacrifice or specific prior startup learning. I would keep those distinctions intact rather than letting one impressive engine test carry every dimension.

I would pass on a personal check today. My expectation is that by October 2029 RATLOG will have advanced its propulsion demonstrator but will not yet have completed a paid orbital launch. The single condition that would change my decision is an independently reviewed, flight-representative engine qualification campaign demonstrating that the turbopump-free architecture retains a material lifecycle cost advantage after bearing loads, maintenance, and reuse are included. That would connect the promising prototype to the market variable that matters.
