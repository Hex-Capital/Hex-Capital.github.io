# Ample Computer -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Ample Computer’s most useful founder evidence is an inspectable artifact: Ishan published the Hermes integration and reported a production protocol implementation on October 17, 2026. That is knowledge acquired by building in the actual problem. Ravi’s Shopify experience and earlier ventures provide context, but they do not establish expertise in operating application infrastructure. The team has shipped enough to earn attention; the dossier does not yet establish an exceptional insight into deployment reliability or economics.

The decisive question is what Ample owns after another hosting company reproduces its agent-to-deployment workflow. Isolated microVMs, databases, storage, logs, and an HTTPS endpoint make a useful product. The dossier identifies no proprietary model, exclusive data, or demonstrated switching cost that makes those capabilities compound into an advantage. Railway and Render already let agents operate infrastructure. That makes substitution a present competitive pressure. It does not prove Ample has no deeper asset, but I cannot finance an asset the evidence never identifies.

The strongest case against passing is that Ample could become the default infrastructure an agent selects before a human makes a purchasing decision. Its Anthropic-verified connector, added in October 2026, gives that possibility a concrete distribution surface. Agent-created applications that humans subsequently claim could turn deployment into customer acquisition. This fits my preference for tools that let individuals produce without an employer’s permission. But marketplace availability establishes access, and the six showcased applications establish examples. Neither demonstrates that one customer brings another customer or improves another customer’s deployment. Distribution through someone else’s platform is also subject to that platform’s choices.

There is room to maneuver. The initial user is building a web application with an agent; the same managed compute, database, and storage capabilities could serve the production application after its human owner takes responsibility. Vercel’s estimated $600 million annualized revenue indicates substantial existing hosting spend, without establishing how much Ample can capture. Usage billing and per-second metering also identify a direct payment mechanism. They earn modest credit, while undisclosed prices, payment status, and gross margins leave the economic advantage unresolved.

Ample’s working integrations make the exception worth considering: I can invest before a network exists when a distinctive product and unusually effective builders provide another basis for conviction. Here, the evidence establishes execution more clearly than distinctiveness. There is no documented integrity problem, artificial fundraising deadline, or dependence on investor reassurance; those vetoes do not apply. Nevertheless, infrastructure branding alone does not satisfy my preference for assets that survive cheap code. **I would pass with my own money today.** One verifiable result would reopen the decision: a deployment cohort showing that a customer-contributed corpus materially reduces failures for unrelated customers, with documented rights that prevent competing hosts from reproducing that corpus. That would turn Ample’s activity into an accumulating asset.
