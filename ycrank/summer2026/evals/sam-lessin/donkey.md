# Donkey -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Donkey’s strongest asset is its venture-shaped operating structure and its weakest is owned distribution: Benjamin Martindale has built a balance-sheet trading company that takes title, clears customs, and offers net-45 terms, while Donkey has only 78 LinkedIn followers and no founder audience. Software is no longer investible; Donkey is barely software, which is the compliment. Donkey is an illegible trade-finance and procurement asset wearing an “AI-native” YC costume, and the underwriting question is whether embedded margin can compound faster than inventory, transit, and buyer-default exposure consume capital.

Marina Mogilko / LinguaTrip is the high rung on capital structure because the underlying cash flow dictated a non-standard instrument rather than being squeezed into ordinary startup equity. Donkey shares that load-bearing mechanism: Donkey buys factory-direct, embeds its margin in a delivered-duty-paid price, takes title, and can finance qualified buyers for 45 days. Donkey diverges because Marina brought profitable operations and millions of subscribers, while Donkey discloses no current order volume, gross margin, loss rate, or working-capital cycle. I would want the financing to follow the asset—receivables facility, order-level special purpose vehicle, or revenue participation—not let an equity SAFE quietly fund every container.

The canonical $30 million AI-agent seed fails the founder-distribution test because paid enterprise selling substitutes for an audience that never existed. Donkey shows the same weakness in less fashionable clothing: Benjamin Martindale has no public X account, James Tan has 583 LinkedIn followers, and Donkey’s company page has 78. Donkey’s drive from Vermont to San Francisco to visit wholesale distributors is good Type 2 fun, but it is founder-led hustle rather than owned community. Eighteen months from now, every new importer may still require another drive, call, credit decision, and supplier investigation unless Donkey turns successful orders into referrals inside a tightly networked distributor niche.

Benjamin Martindale’s prior Prelude OS run—31 paying Chinese factory customers in 39 days and cash-flow positive—gives Donkey more asymmetric-conviction evidence than the polished “AI for supply chain” label suggests. The mechanism compounds if Donkey’s founders keep doing the ugly work: every settled shipment can add factory identity, defect history, landed-cost accuracy, and buyer-payment behavior to Donkey’s quoting loop. Martindale’s Mandarin, China-market advisory work, and literal cross-country customer search put Donkey well above an opportunity-tourist founder, although Donkey’s lack of public category writing keeps the missionary case short of proven.

No disclosed price, round size, tier-1 pass, or unusual financing term means Donkey cannot yet pass my value-seed price test. Donkey’s score would flip into the invest tier if the SAFE were below the Summer 2026 batch median and a receivables partner—not equity investors—funded net-45 exposure. At a hot “AI-native trade” valuation, Donkey becomes exactly the factory-model round I avoid: an operationally capital-intensive business sold at software multiples. At a boring trading-company price, Donkey’s customs complexity, China concentration, and first-sale documentation burden become the reasons there may be alpha.

Fin is the relevant single-anchor parallel for category fit: a manual service generated the operational data needed to discover what software should eventually automate. Donkey already coordinates inspectors across seven Chinese provinces, classifies factories versus forwarders, quotes within 72 hours, and inspects goods before final payment. That is genuine Wizard-of-Oz behavior in an unsexy execution market, one of my strongest unstated patterns. Donkey is not pre-consensus crypto or creator infrastructure, but it is a service-with-software trading operation that conventional SaaS investors may misread—and the customs and credit machinery is more interesting than the AI parser.

ImportYeti’s 70 million bills of lading and two million-plus users make my 36-month forecast falsifiable: by July 2029, Donkey will have shifted its center of gravity from supplier discovery to trade credit and managed procurement, producing $4 million–$8 million in annual gross profit rather than SaaS ARR. Donkey’s data-search layer is commoditized before it starts; Donkey can win only by underwriting the transaction, controlling inspection, and repeatedly financing reorder flows. If Donkey remains primarily a photo-to-factory search product, Donkey will be dead or absorbed into a larger sourcing operator.

Prelude OS is the strongest objection to my neutral verdict: 31 paying customers in 39 days, cash-flow positivity, Martindale’s Mandarin, Tan’s customs parser, and seven-province inspection coverage could mean Donkey has already found the unsexy execution machine that SaaS investors will dismiss. The known Ro exception also stings—capital intensity can become the moat when regulation and operational complexity exclude lightweight competitors. Donkey still lacks Ro’s demonstrated demand, and CBP first-sale requirements plus net-45 buyer exposure can turn every successful order into a larger financing liability. I would flip to Invest upon verifiable evidence of ten repeat importers completing at least three shipments each with positive contribution margin and credit losses below 1%.
