# CoArena -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is not the market size. It is what broke. The dossier shows two technically credible founders, a working arena, and remarkable early revenue, but it never names a serious obstacle followed by a founder-built workaround. Dropping a Stanford admission and launching software are evidence of commitment, not yet evidence of resourcefulness under resistance. On my most important test, CoArena therefore remains at the absent-evidence default.

The technical proof is much stronger. The team previously built a computer-use agent reporting 82.81% on OSWorld, then shipped a public system comparing 14 agents across 57 measures with matched sandboxes, paired trajectories, and blind human votes. This is functioning infrastructure built before institutional permission. The qualification is that the supposedly valuable corpus remains immature: the dataset page exposes neither task-category data nor collected annotation fields. They have proved the machine works, but not yet that it is accumulating the proprietary substrate the business claims to sell.

The $60,000 of current-product revenue within two weeks matters more than Product Hunt’s 109 points or the hackathon’s 2,550 listed participants. Someone paid for data or evaluation access while the arena itself was free, and users reportedly doubled in each of those two weeks. That is unusually strong pre-seed pull. But the dossier names no buyer, renewal, repeated workflow, or revenue composition. One contract could account for most of the result. The narrow well is visible—AI labs and agent developers needing current computer-use evaluations—but its depth is not yet measurable.

CoArena also looks like a plausible route change from building Coasty’s agent to measuring many agents. That could be good running-back behavior: the team preserved its computer-use expertise while moving toward infrastructure. But the dossier does not identify the failed hypothesis, customer observation, or technical constraint that caused the change. I cannot distinguish evidence-driven revision from the launch of a fashionable adjacent product, so this dimension stays at its default. The founders’ direct work on computer-use agents does provide some organic connection to the problem, while consent, trajectory collection, security, and human preference labeling supply genuine schlep. The origin story is still inferred rather than documented.

The hidden-pattern test is favorable but incomplete. CoArena has the concrete pre-round proof spike I often like: a shipped technical primitive plus immediate revenue. The contradiction is that its claimed compounding asset is not yet publicly demonstrated, while LMArena already has more than 3 million votes and $250 million raised. More seriously, Coasty both operates the benchmark and develops an excluded computer-use agent. Raw prompts and unmasked screenshots also pass through model providers without a third-party security audit. These are not cosmetic enterprise objections; they can prevent the very labs whose contracts would turn the arena into a dataset moat.

The known-exception test does not quite override the people-first gate. A working product and unmistakable pull can replace missing conventional credentials, but they cannot tell me how these founders behave when a lab rejects the product over neutrality or data exposure. My 36-month base case is that CoArena becomes an enterprise computer-use evaluation vendor with $2–5 million ARR, while the public arena functions mainly as distribution rather than the moat. I would not write the check yet. One verifiable event would flip me: a named lab raises a security or neutrality blocker, the founders ship a substantive fix within weeks, and that same lab signs or renews a paid contract.
