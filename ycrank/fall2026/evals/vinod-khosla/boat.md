# Boat -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Boat sells a default VM for $0.036 an hour against its quoted $0.331 for E2B and Daytona—a roughly ninefold price difference. If that difference survives comparable workloads and profitable operation, it could change which agents are economical to run continuously. That is worth investigating. But “billions of agents” does not quantify the consequence: the dossier supplies neither the affected spending baseline nor evidence that execution cost is the binding constraint on adoption. I can see a useful infrastructure business. I cannot yet calculate the extraordinary outcome I would be buying.

The product deserves more credit than a pricing slide. Separate Linux kernels, nested virtualization, snapshots, and dedicated AMD Ryzen hosts establish that the founders have built functioning infrastructure. OpenInspect’s October 23 integration supplies an external adoption signal. The unresolved technical question is whether Boat delivers substantially more useful computation per dollar at comparable isolation, reliability, and persistence. Its vendor comparison does not establish that. Google already offers provisioning and snapshots; Modal now offers VM sandboxes. Those competitors make the source of the price advantage decisive. Better engineering can compound. A lower margin can disappear.

The strongest argument against passing is unusually concrete for this stage: more than 1,000 active paying users and six-figure ARR, both founder-reported, from a tiny team. Nougaret also reports building cloud coding agents before extracting their infrastructure into the product. That is a credible route from firsthand pain to a platform others want. It fits my less-public pattern of backing small technical teams whose useful wedge can become infrastructure, including businesses without a scientific breakthrough. But the exceptional override requires evidence connecting that wedge to an enormous value pool and durable expansion. Modal’s reported billion-dollar run rate includes inference and training; I cannot assign that demand to Boat’s sandbox market.

The founders have demonstrated a change in product direction, which earns some credit for adaptability. Their shared INSA Lyon background and prior employers do not establish complementary recruiting against specific weaknesses. Likewise, paying users validate demand without proving disciplined risk removal. The dossier contains no ranked kill risk, budgeted experiment, numerical threshold, or stop condition. I would make sustainable delivered cost the first test. SOC 2 work matters for enterprise access, but completing it would not establish that the core economic advantage survives production workloads.

I pass at present. Boat’s published tariff is affirmative evidence about customer price; it supplies no bottom-up evidence about host utilization, depreciation, support, or margins. There is also no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, so I will not invent that adverse conclusion. Nor does this look like an AI interface wrapper or an exit-driven project. The missing link is durable, consequential economics. My single flip condition is a reproducible production study showing at least fivefold lower fully loaded cost per completed representative agent workload than E2B, with matched reliability and isolation and positive contribution margin. That would turn an attractive tariff into evidence of a discontinuity worth purchasing.
