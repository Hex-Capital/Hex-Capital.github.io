# LATO -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what broke. LATO moved from an Excel investing assistant in April 2026 to expert interviews and market simulations by July, but the dossier never identifies the obstacle, what the founders did under pressure, or how quickly their workaround changed a customer outcome. A change of product noun is not evidence of resourcefulness. Without the causal story, I cannot tell whether this was a running back changing direction or merely a team changing its description.

The founders nevertheless began from a real place. Tymek Staniszewski spent six years inside growth and private equity, where $300,000 studies, slow expert outreach, and disposable research are concrete annoyances rather than an invented “AI for investing” thesis. Tien Chu supplies eight years of software and machine-learning work, and the pair have known one another for more than 11 years. The ugly parts—expert sourcing, multilingual calls, fund-data integration, security, and MNPI controls—could form a good schlep-heavy wedge.

There is also a functioning artifact. LATO’s voice agent interviewed 30 recruiters, and its earlier Excel product reportedly found a $67.9 million valuation error plus eight other problems in a public LBO model. Those are better than diagrams. But they prove that the software can perform tasks, not that its market simulations predict behavior. LATO reports no accuracy benchmark, confidence interval, or forecast history. The technical proof is therefore meaningful but incomplete.

Blume Equity and FoodLabs are named customers, and research accounted for 70% of disclosed use cases. This is the strongest bull case: two complementary founders with a long relationship, direct buyer knowledge, named users, and working software before institutional permission. It matches the pattern of unusually concrete pre-round artifacts. But “customer” is underspecified. There is no price, revenue, repeat-study count, retention, or evidence that users pulled the crude product into an unexpected workflow. Thirty recruited interviewees demonstrate execution; they are not the paying niche.

The competition makes that omission decisive. AlphaSense already has more than 280,000 interviews, AI-led calls, detailed expert-compliance machinery, and over $600 million in ARR. LATO need not beat it by possessing more data, but it must show a behavior the incumbent cannot easily copy. The simulation claim might supply that difference, yet it is the least validated part of the product. SOC 2 and ISO 27001 remain in progress, while the public materials do not describe MNPI screening or post-call review. There is no dependence on a speculative partner, premature sales hiring, or newly assembled cofounder relationship, but enterprise trust is not optional in this market.

My base case is that within 36 months LATO narrows into an AI-run expert-call and diligence service for mid-market private-equity funds and reaches $2–5 million in ARR, rather than establishing a defensible market-simulation platform. No known exception overrides the missing founder-action evidence: the artifacts are real, but neither independent validation nor unmistakable user pull is strong enough to replace that gate. I would reconsider after one dated case showing a serious sourcing, compliance, or accuracy blocker that the founders themselves solved within weeks and converted into a repeat paid study. Until then, I would not write the check.
