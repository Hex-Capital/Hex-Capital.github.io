# EveryApp -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

EveryApp’s implied secret is that businesses will abandon proprietary subscriptions if open-source applications become easy enough to operate. What would prove it wrong? Customers might enthusiastically download the software while continuing to pay established vendors for reliability and support. The Cloudflare Gateway makes the proposition testable, but the dossier identifies neither a prevailing belief that named peers reject nor customer behavior establishing the opposing claim. A working architecture establishes feasibility. It does not yet establish the secret.

The investment problem is who captures the savings. EveryApp puts applications in the customer’s Cloudflare account, makes self-hosting free, and releases its code under MIT. Those choices can accelerate adoption while allowing another operator to sell the same functionality. Cloudron already combines packaged applications with centralized users; Coolify supports broader deployment environments and agent-driven management. EveryApp’s Cloudflare specialization is a concrete product distinction, but the dossier establishes no exclusive technology, dominant customer segment, or measured network effect. Making software easier to leave is an attractive customer proposition. Why does the resulting market belong to EveryApp?

The strongest objection to passing is OpenSEO. Ben Senescu has already built an application with 25.3K GitHub stars, a claimed audience of 3,000-plus entrepreneurs, and multiple successful Product Hunt launches. That is a credible starting point for recruiting users and developers into a shared application catalog. EveryApp’s October 26 launch also attracted 128,561 post views by the following day. A catalog could eventually make each additional application improve the usefulness of the others. But OpenSEO’s audience is evidence of an acquisition opportunity, not proof of EveryApp adoption or ecosystem retention. The current platform’s 351 stars do not close that gap.

Senescu’s operator background earns the company a hearing: a former Klaviyo senior engineer built the application whose infrastructure the platform now generalizes. That fits my preference for companies formed around firsthand operating problems. It does not establish the proprietary operator network that sometimes makes such origins unusually powerful. Nor does it resolve founding structure. YC identifies one founder while the reported team size is two; prior working relationships, ownership, and full-time commitments remain undocumented. I apply the stated default rather than invent a founding-team red flag.

The announced managed service starts at $10 monthly. At that base price, $100 million in annual recurring revenue requires approximately 833,333 paying accounts. Usage charges could materially improve that arithmetic, but the dossier provides no account expansion or gross-margin evidence. GitHub, coding-agent setup prompts, Discord, and OpenSEO cross-promotion offer a plausible inexpensive route to developers; conversion into paid managed accounts remains unestablished. The refusal to promise backward compatibility also creates a delivery burden for a product selling operational convenience.

I would pass with my own money. My exceptions to apparent competitive weakness require persistent transaction demand or an independently demonstrated technical discontinuity; neither is established here. The favorable outcome could still be a useful, profitable software business. The unresolved question is whether EveryApp can own an expanding market while giving customers and competitors the same underlying machinery. One verifiable flip condition would be a twelve-month managed-customer cohort showing that adopting additional catalog applications increases both retention and spending without proportionately increasing support costs. That would supply evidence that the catalog compounds value inside EveryApp.
