# ClaimKit -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Rachel Huang has done the work she is now automating. Preparing R&D claims at KPMG, then building ClaimKit into a product with a reported 80+ customers within six months, is evidence of specific knowledge acquired through action. Edexia’s named testimonial corroborates that the team can replace an accountant during a live engagement. Mahon supplies engineering experience; Huang supplies knowledge of what actually gets lodged. That combination earns substantial founder credit. The team’s historical $100M+ in credits does not establish ClaimKit’s transaction volume.

The investment turns on what remains after a competitor reproduces the software. Connected engineering and accounting workflows, together with retained claim histories, give ClaimKit some operational substance. But customer records are not automatically an exclusive asset, and historical documentation is not automatically switching cost. The dossier establishes neither a proprietary model nor a demonstrated improvement that compounds across deployments. Boast and Onshore already combine automation with tax expertise. ClaimKit must accumulate an advantage faster than competitors accumulate features.

The strongest objection to my hesitation is that these founders may already understand how to turn professional judgment into scalable output. Eighty customers is meaningful at this stage. Edexia reports faster completion at lower cost. Retiring the Grants module on April 22, 2026 could reflect disciplined concentration on the product customers actually want. Exceptional founders with a working product can justify investing before a network appears. But the dossier does not yet establish the capital efficiency that would support that exception. Specialist review remains part of delivery, and there is no observed mechanism through which one customer improves another customer’s product or acquisition.

ClaimKit does have a direct route to getting paid: an upfront software fee followed by a minimum-or-percentage service fee at lodgement. That is a concrete commercial mechanism, although undisclosed prices, collections, and review costs limit what it proves about value capture. The market case is less developed. The cited $1.45B estimate describes R&D tax-credit software; serving Australia and the US broadens geographic reach within that category. Competitors demonstrate that adjacent tax and finance products exist, but ClaimKit has not demonstrated the shared capability that would let it capture those budgets.

I see no documented integrity problem, artificial financing deadline, or dependence on investor reassurance. The product change alone does not establish repeated abandonment. My hesitation is structural: ClaimKit converts expert knowledge into useful automation, but has not shown that the resulting value compounds independently of additional expert work. Its adoption does not yet support waiving that concern on the strength of a self-reinforcing network. I am neutral today and would keep my money uncommitted. One verifiable result would change that: a matched second-year customer cohort showing materially lower specialist hours per completed claim because retained evidence mappings are reused, with comparable review outcomes and increased gross profit per claim.
