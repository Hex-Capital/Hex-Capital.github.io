# Silica -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a robot learning how firmly to hold something from demonstrations that record the force involved. Silica is reaching toward a useful piece of that world: joints that measure torque where the load actually meets the machine. But I have to supply the intervening story myself. The dossier does not establish how better measurements become transferable learning and then reliable work outside a laboratory. Its NVIDIA software-release reference suggests growing developer interest; it does not demonstrate those transitions. My daydream is a hypothesis, and I cannot count it as Silica’s evidence.

The strongest signal sits underneath the fashionable application layer. Silica is designing a physical component around an identifiable measurement problem: developers currently estimate contact forces from motor current and compensate in software. That fits my pattern of looking for concealed infrastructure beneath a visible wave. Yet HEBI and Sensodrive already sell torque-sensing joints. Silica’s output-side integration earns enabling-layer credit; affordable superiority remains unproven because prices are unpublished and specifications are design targets. I want to understand which troublesome engineering constraint this particular design actually removes.

Why do developers tolerate the workaround? What does it cost them in calibration, engineering hours, or failed tasks? Those answers would tell me whether Silica gives a small robotics team meaningful control over development. The dossier identifies the workaround but supplies no measured burden, incumbent rent, or demonstrated transfer of agency. I leave that criterion at its missing-evidence default. The $18.62 billion actuator-market estimate cannot answer a question about one developer’s bench.

Enrico Milletti’s materials and manufacturing work at Apple, experience as Artefact’s founding engineer, and engineering and industrial-design education make him credible enough for a serious technical conversation. They do not establish the pre-company obsession I underwrite: dated experiments, successive builds, or an independently pursued actuator problem. That evidence is missing, rather than evidence of opportunism. Likewise, opening preorders establishes a sales channel, but the dossier reports no completed orders, repeated testing, or customer integration. December 2026 prototypes and January 2027 production remain scheduled milestones.

The strongest argument against passing is that manufacturing knowledge can become valuable before customers can use the finished hardware. Silica’s founder has relevant process experience, and the component addresses a concrete sensing limitation. **CosmicBrain AI** is a useful exception here: both bets concern the physical machinery beneath robot intelligence, but that company supplied a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a documented lifelong robotics history. Those were the bridges across missing revenue. Silica’s dossier does not yet supply equivalent bridges. I see no documented governance or severe privacy violation, and no established dependence on one closed model supplier; those red flags do not drive this decision.

I pass on committing my own money today. Silica fits my infrastructure pattern, but the evidence does not yet distinguish its proposed component from available alternatives strongly enough to carry the bet. My verifiable flip condition is an independent robot developer publishing a repeat-use comparison showing that a delivered Q Series actuator materially reduces total integration cost at comparable sensing performance and durability versus a named commercial alternative. That would turn “affordable output-side sensing” into an observable reason to choose Silica.
