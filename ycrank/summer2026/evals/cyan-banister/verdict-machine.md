# Verdict Machine -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Banks hold tokenized assets as ordinary balance-sheet instruments, and every proposed transfer is checked against institution-specific policy before anyone signs it. Getting there requires regulatory permission, operating workflows that understand on-chain exposure, and controls reliable enough to block an irreversible transaction without freezing legitimate business. The SEC’s 2025 rescission of SAB 121, the OCC’s reaffirmation of bank crypto authority, and Verdict Machine’s August 2026 evaluations with large-bank digital-asset teams are credible leaks from that future. The dossier does not yet prove the final step: that an AI policy engine can earn authority over execution.

Why are institutions stitching together custody, wallet screening, runtime monitoring, and internal policy? Because regulations permit the activity while leaving the institution holding the risk. Verdict Machine attacks that fragmentation with one live model spanning inventory, posture, screening, and pre-execution enforcement. That is a real operating wound, but the rent remains blurry. The dossier does not quantify compliance labor, delayed transactions, losses, or which gatekeeper captures value today. It returns control to institutional risk teams, not yet to an individual whose time or occupational agency was artificially withheld.

The founders are the strongest evidence. Dan Danay and Isaac Grossman built keyTango together beginning in 2019; Danay later led Check Point’s Web3 security effort, Liran Kogan ran its blockchain-security product, and Grossman worked across defense cybersecurity, OTORIO, and SCRT Labs. This is not a fashionable category assembled after a regulatory headline. It is a recruited team with years of shared technical and product tuition, prior shipped infrastructure, and enough commercial fluency to begin POCs during Verdict Machine’s first weeks.

Those POCs matter, but “customers move billions weekly” describes the customers’ systems, not behavior elicited by Verdict Machine. There are no disclosed production transactions evaluated, policies generated, repeat usage cohorts, false-positive rates, contracts, or revenue. Pre-revenue is not my objection; I will waive revenue when a functioning artifact, signed POCs, and unusually deep founder obsession align. Here the founder history qualifies, while the public product proof stops at evaluations. I cannot tell whether users are trusting the machine or politely testing it.

The bull case stings: this exact team could convert its Check Point knowledge and bank access into the policy control plane, then compound proprietary institutional decisions and transaction outcomes into a dataset Blockaid and protocol-oriented competitors do not possess. But that is still future moat, not controlled infrastructure today. Blockaid already processes more than 180 million transactions monthly, Hexagate combines prevention with a decade of Chainalysis data across 75-plus chains, and Check Point itself launched an overlapping AI-assisted blockchain firewall under Danay. Verdict Machine currently resembles an unglamorous workflow repair—one of my favorite shapes—without yet owning the concealed enabling layer beneath it.

My base case is that Verdict Machine reaches $1–3 million ARR within 36 months as a policy module beside custody and monitoring platforms, rather than becoming the institution-wide control plane. I would not write the check today. The former-employer overlap needs a clean IP explanation, and a live model of institutional environments and policies needs explicit data-custody, consent, auditability, and human-override architecture. The functional titles identify an apparent accountable leader, but ownership and decision control remain undisclosed. One condition flips me: three named financial institutions running the product in production, with independently auditable transaction volume and false-positive performance, while Verdict Machine contractually retains a customer-permitted policy-outcome dataset unavailable to its larger competitors.
