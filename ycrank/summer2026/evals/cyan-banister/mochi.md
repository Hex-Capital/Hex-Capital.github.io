# Mochi -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: animation is no longer organized primarily around studios financing seasons. Thousands of tiny production teams release stories continuously, viewers become creators between episodes, and characters are tested with audiences before anyone commits years of labor. Mochi has a real leak from that future: 3,000 creators already make more than 500 episodes daily, while 100,000 users move between watching and constructing one-minute anime. The dossier does not yet explain the full path from this energetic feed to a durable new entertainment institution, but this is more than a fashionable generative-video story. People are already behaving as though animation can be made every day.

The behavior is the strongest evidence. Three hundred million organic views, 900,000 social followers, 100-plus series with daily releases, and roughly 1,900 combined mobile ratings show that Mochi has escaped the rendering-demo phase. Creators are spending time and creative reputation scene by scene; some are spending gems despite generation failures and costs near $5 for two or three short scenes. That friction is simultaneously useful and dangerous. Users care enough to suffer through an unreliable machine, but a machine that repeatedly eats paid currency can turn fascination into betrayal.

Robert Qiu has relevant operating tuition: a game reaching $200,000 in monthly revenue and lofi.town reaching 30,000 users are shipped consumer artifacts, not résumé decoration. Michael Ninh brings hundreds of video creatives, consumer-growth experience, and the slightly ridiculous but useful fact of having watched more than 400 anime series. Together they cover product monetization, distribution, and cultural fluency. What I cannot see is whether this obsession predates the current AI-video wave, how the founders came together, or whether Ninh’s anime knowledge has produced a proprietary creative grammar rather than simply informed positioning.

The bull case is a creator-viewer flywheel: social clips acquire viewers, viewers become creators, daily creation expands the catalogue, and viewing data identifies stories worth extending. The pricing surface—gem packs up to $99.99, $7.99 weekly VIP, and $19.99 weekly VIP+—gives Mochi several ways to monetize that loop. But prices are not revenue, and 300 million off-platform views can conceal weak in-app retention. The company also names no institutional rule or quantified rent it removes. It compresses production cycles and gives individuals creative agency, but it has not shown who previously captured the value or whether creators themselves can earn meaningful income.

Here is the part that stings: AniShort already combines vertical anime with AI creation, YouArt reports more than $120,000 in monthly revenue, and the dossier identifies neither proprietary technology nor switching costs. Mochi may be discovering a new ritual while building on tools every competing feed can rent. Its catalogue and behavioral data could become defensible, but today that is an inference, not a controlled enabling layer. Copyright and likeness obligations are pushed toward creators, while failed generations consuming gems create exactly the kind of trust problem that can prevent a marketplace from compounding.

I would make a small personal investment because 500 episodes created every day is strange, costly behavior arriving unusually early, and this team has previously converted consumer attention into real economics. I would not underwrite Mochi as animation infrastructure yet; I would underwrite it as a culturally specific network racing to become one. My verifiable flip condition is creator retention: if fewer than 30% of the creators active in one monthly cohort publish again in each of the following three months, I would withdraw the invest thesis regardless of aggregate views.
