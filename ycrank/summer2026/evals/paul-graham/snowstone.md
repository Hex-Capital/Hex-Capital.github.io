# Snowstone -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Snowstone has published a measurement of its software, but the dossier contains no comparable test of its founders. What happened when a customer refused access to the data? What did they build to get around it, and how long did that take? Those are the facts I would want before investing. Moreland and Baur’s history of building projects and entering hackathons together is encouraging. It reduces uncertainty about their ability to work together. It does not establish how they behave when something important breaks.

The strongest evidence is the private benchmark: Snowstone says its dollar estimates fall within 20% of ground truth in 66% of cases. Publishing a numerical result gives us something to examine. But the dataset, definition of ground truth, and performance on unseen companies are undisclosed. This earns partial technical credit. The practical question is whether an operating partner can cheaply distinguish a useful finding from a plausible mistake. Source-linked calculations could make that possible. If reviewing every finding requires reconstructing the analysis, the software may simply move the consultant’s work around.

There is a serious bull case here. Baur has actually worked on PE diligence and MedTech LBO models at Winterberg. The product addresses an ugly task involving fragmented records, entity matching, and sensitive-data access. Read-only permissions, isolated environments, and persistent reviewed matches suggest attention to that work. An operating partner who trusts the first diagnostic could bring Snowstone into another portfolio company. That could create repeat distribution from a small initial customer base. But the internship establishes domain exposure; it does not document the particular problem Baur encountered that caused him to build Snowstone. Nor does the reported $2B in reviewed transactions establish customer pull. The $50M represents potential opportunities, and “Payment Required” establishes neither collected revenue nor repeat purchases.

The product history also needs careful interpretation. Compendium appeared in July 2026, Cerenovus in August, and Snowstone followed. That establishes successive releases. Without the observation that caused each change, it supplies no evidence of learning from rejection. Against my unstated patterns, Snowstone has a numerical claim, but lacks independently validated proof or demonstrated developer appropriation of a new interface. Its accumulated matches could become valuable; their existence does not yet establish a difficult proprietary substrate. Celonis and Skan make it especially important to discover why one narrow buyer needs this particular product. The announced collaborations raise a distribution question, but do not prove dependence on partners. Likewise, the founders’ shared projects argue against assuming a team assembled merely for an application.

I would pass at 34. The decisive gap is the missing evidence of resourcefulness under a named constraint, which leaves my founder gate unmet. I checked the exceptions: the dossier establishes neither unmistakable user pull nor independently validated proof strong enough to override that gap. This is uncertainty about the central reason I would invest, rather than a judgment that a pre-seed company should already have substantial revenue. My flip condition is one documented customer episode: a named PE buyer encounters a data-access obstacle, the founders ship a workaround within weeks, and that buyer subsequently pays to use Snowstone on another portfolio company. That would connect founder behavior to a customer who wants the product back.
