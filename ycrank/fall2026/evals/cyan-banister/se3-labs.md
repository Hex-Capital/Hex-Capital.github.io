# SE3 Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, a robot developer should be able to send a policy somewhere and learn what happens when the gripper meets an awkward object in an unfamiliar workcell. SE3 Labs offers a small leak from that world: its October 2026 research tested Dream through a closed-source API, showing that physical evaluation can happen outside the model developer’s laboratory. I can work backward from independent testing to targeted failure collection to better deployed policies. The dossier establishes the first transition. It does not yet establish that the collected failures make the next policy better. That earns partial credit for the future, with the most valuable transition still unproven.

The machinery underneath this interests me. SE3 published 100 physical evaluation episodes across 10 tasks, with recordings, telemetry, task implementations, and scoring sheets. Someone has to reset the objects, synchronize the recordings, and decide what counts as success. Those details are where an apparently simple robotics service acquires operating knowledge. This fits my pattern of looking beneath a visible wave for its enabling layer. But Poke & Wiggle already offers physical evaluation on FR3 Duo stations, and academic infrastructure overlaps. SE3 demonstrates an operating capability; exclusive access to a scarce bottleneck remains unestablished.

Why must every robot team assemble its own testing operation? Shared workcells could return engineering time and give smaller developers access to equipment they cannot justify owning. That is an attractive possibility, but the dossier’s access problem is explicitly inferred. It identifies no measured equipment-access delay, testing bill, or gatekeeper collecting a particular rent. Contract-specific project fees describe how SE3 might charge; they do not establish whose economics have changed. I cannot give the company credit for releasing users from a constraint that has not yet been documented.

The distinction between running experiments and pulling customers into a new habit matters here. September’s 40-episode comparison—6 completed placements with code-as-policy versus 18 with agentic control—shows concrete experimental work. It does not show an outside team repeatedly spending money, shipping hardware, or changing its development schedule around SE3. Public revenue is unnecessary at this stage. A documented repeat prototype user could be enough. These reports establish a functioning artifact, while costly customer behavior remains missing.

The strongest argument against passing is that physical infrastructure deserves funding before customers know how to buy it. CosmicBrain AI provides a relevant exception: functioning physical-AI machinery justified investing before revenue, but lifelong founder obsession and signed industrial proofs of concept supported the leap. SE3’s founder identities and roles are unverified. Paige Xu’s reported operating background cannot substitute for evidence about whoever actually built and runs this system. That is missing biography, not evidence of opportunism or a governance shell game. Likewise, testing one closed model does not establish dependence on one supplier. Its written restrictions on reusing customer data are a useful privacy boundary, although they also prevent me from assuming an unrestricted shared-data advantage.

I pass today because SE3 Labs has demonstrated the testing station more convincingly than the people and customer learning loop that would make it compound. My flip condition is one documented customer engagement, led by an identified accountable founder, in which failure-directed collection improves that customer’s policy on a held-out physical retest and the customer purchases another cycle. That would connect the machinery to both measurable improvement and repeat behavior—the bridge this dossier currently leaves open.
