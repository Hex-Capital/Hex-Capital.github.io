# Robocurve -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

One completed physical-robot pilot makes **Robocurve** an atoms-adjacent evaluation company with no definite commercial plan. **Robocurve** sees a real secret: laboratories judging their own robots is not measurement but marketing. Yet the secret names an institutional conflict, not a specific incumbent whose architecture prevents independent evaluation. The highest signal is physical-world evaluation; the lowest is the absence of named two-to-five-year milestones. Robocurve has found a neglected problem before proving it can own the solution.

**SpaceX** made atoms-heavy infrastructure inseparable from the moat: factories, launch systems, and a definite reusability roadmap compounded into barriers no software fork could erase. **Robocurve** shares the load-bearing premise that physical reality matters—KitchenBench and DataCenterBench cannot be reduced to simulation—but Robocurve does not yet own a comparable physical stack. Inspect Robots is MIT-licensed, the repository already has 13 forks, and laboratories possess their own hardware and telemetry. Robocurve operates around atoms; it has not shown that it controls them.

**Quora’s** weak axis was an indefinite consumer roadmap: accumulate participation, then discover what the network becomes. The same failure mechanism appears in **Robocurve** more severely. Robocurve names general-purpose robotics by 2028, but gives no sequence of facilities, task distributions, certifications, customer classes, or architectural commitments. “Request an evaluation” is an inquiry path, not a plan. Definite optimism requires a destination; Robocurve currently has an interesting direction.

Frontier laboratories evaluating their own systems gives **Robocurve** a non-consensus opening, but the secret compounds only if Robocurve becomes the trusted third party whose results vendors cannot credibly reproduce themselves. Over the next 18 months, every independently repeated physical task could strengthen Robocurve’s longitudinal dataset and benchmark authority. The inverse is equally mechanical: MIT licensing, 13 existing forks, and vendor-owned telemetry let laboratories internalize Robocurve’s methods while withholding the results that would build Robocurve’s reputation. METR, Epoch AI, and Artificial Analysis prove that independent evaluation can matter; none is the named incumbent structurally unable to enter physical robotics.

Inspect Robots supporting more than 40 VLAs and 260 language models could become a distribution surface, but **Robocurve** has not yet converted breadth into pricing power. Robocurve would move into the investment tier if robot purchasers—or regulators—required its evaluations before deployment, creating certification depth, accumulated failure data, and switching costs. Today there is one pilot, 96 GitHub stars, no disclosed revenue, and no structural barrier. Monopoly is not having the most adapters. Monopoly is possessing something customers cannot obtain elsewhere at any tolerable price.

**Anduril** paired Palmer Luckey’s shipped-to-scale Oculus record with a precise understanding of defense procurement. **Robocurve** has relevant but earlier lineage: Jay Chooi worked at the UK AI Security Institute and contributed to Inspect Evals, while Aris Zhu built navigation and fleet-management systems at Amazon Robotics and Yondu Robotics. Robocurve therefore has credible founder-market fit, not the verified operator pedigree that overrides missing monopoly evidence. Intellectual proximity to the problem is useful; prior command of a scaled system is different.

One pilot and the absence of published pricing lead to a falsifiable forecast: by July 2029, **Robocurve** will have pivoted from an independent public benchmark into paid pre-deployment validation for warehouse and data-center robotics vendors, reaching roughly $1–3 million ARR without becoming the industry’s universal benchmark. Robocurve’s physical testing competence can sell as a service, while its weak definite plan and copyable framework prevent standard-setting power. Services revenue is plausible. Power-law ownership is not yet visible.

Forty-plus VLA integrations support the strongest opposing case: **Robocurve** could become the METR of physical robotics just before frontier laboratories target general-purpose robots in 2028. Robocurve’s founders span evaluation research and deployed robotics, and Quora shows that network effects can override an initially indefinite roadmap. The case still loses because one unpublished pilot does not establish neutrality, vendors can fork the MIT-licensed framework, and vendor-funded evaluations create an unresolved independence conflict. I would flip to Invest if three named frontier robotics laboratories paid Robocurve for recurring evaluations and at least one major buyer or regulator made Robocurve results a procurement requirement.
