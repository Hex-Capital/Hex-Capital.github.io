# Almanac -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the attractive version is clear: company memory maintains itself, employees stop hunting through six applications, and an agent can finish work because it understands why prior decisions were made. But Almanac has not mapped the road from today to that world. Public computer-use models are the present-day leak, yet that artifact belongs to OpenAI and Anthropic, not Almanac. The dossier names no intermediate unlocks beyond connecting accounts, building a wiki, and adding a browser.

The strongest signal is the team’s first-believer behavior. Kushagra Chitkara, Rohan Sharma, and Divit Sheth already shipped CodeAlmanac, attracting 791 GitHub stars and 71 forks before expanding from code knowledge into company knowledge. Their data-science, ML-research, and production-engineering backgrounds make this more than a fashionable landing page. But the transition also muddies the evidence: those developers chose the prior artifact, not the current $250-per-user assistant, and there is no dated account of the operating wound that forced the broader product into existence.

The current system is irritating, but Almanac has not yet decoded its economics. Fragmentation across Slack, Gmail, Calendar, Docs, and GitHub creates search time and stale decisions; it does not identify a specific rule, rent extractor, or artificial gatekeeper. The product may return time to employees, but the dossier neither quantifies that time nor shows whose authority changes. I see workflow convenience, not yet agency being wrested from an institution.

Here is the bull case that stings: a three-person technical team has built a source-linked wiki, Slack and iMessage access, and a dedicated browser for tools without APIs; a three-day trial can expose value quickly; and Glean’s more than $100 million ARR proves companies pay heavily for this problem. At $250 per user monthly, only a modest number of retained teams could create meaningful pre-seed revenue. Pre-revenue is not the issue. The missing piece is behavior: no current customer, repeat cohort, paid deployment, or consequential task completed through Almanac is disclosed.

The deeper problem is stack position. Glean already has 250 connectors, Dust supports more than 100 data sources and 300,000 deployed agents, and public computer-use APIs make Almanac’s managed browser reproducible. Its possible data flywheel is inferred, while its dependence on third-party models, APIs, and permissions is explicit. Connected-account authority to create, transmit, publish, or delete data also activates my consent-and-custody gate; confirmations and revocable connections help, but no limiting architecture is described. Three founders are identified, yet no accountable CEO or governance tiebreaker is.

My base case is that within 36 months Almanac narrows back toward developer or engineering-knowledge workflows rather than reaching $5 million ARR as a horizontal company assistant. I pass today. One verifiable condition flips me: show at least ten unrelated companies paying the published price, retained for 90 days, with employees repeatedly authorizing completed cross-tool actions—not merely asking search questions.
