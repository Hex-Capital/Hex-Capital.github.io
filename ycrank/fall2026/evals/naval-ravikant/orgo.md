# Orgo -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Orgo’s founders have earned knowledge by putting agents to work. Nick Vasilescu previously operated computeruse.agency; his desktop project has 531 GitHub stars. The team has shipped persistent computers with browsers, terminals, files, and programmatic control, and reports more than 1,000 teams using them. Those are artifacts and users, not résumé substitutions. I give the founders substantial credit for understanding the exact problem through action.

The investment question is what their customers accumulate that a competitor cannot reproduce. Orgo’s October 19 templates and triggers make deployment easier. Persistence makes migration inconvenient. Neither establishes a durable advantage without evidence that something exclusive compounds through usage. Daytona already offers overlapping persistent environments across multiple operating systems; Cua supplies open-source virtualization and desktop-control components. If another team copied Orgo’s interface and matched its reliability, the dossier identifies no asset that would keep customers from moving. That is affirmative competitive pressure on defensibility.

There is room to maneuver in the market. An agent developer buying a desktop and an agency deploying workers inside small businesses can use the same persistent execution environment. The testimonial describing agents operating across three businesses gives that adjacency some substance. E2B’s claimed eight-figure ARR, although reported through an employer posting, supplies directional evidence that buyers spend on agent execution. Orgo could expand from provisioning computers into operating fleets of business agents. But each additional customer currently adds another workload; there is no demonstrated mechanism by which that customer makes another customer’s agents more useful. The founder’s audience and community can distribute the product without creating a customer network.

The strongest objection to withholding my check is that distribution and operational experience may compound before their defensibility becomes publicly measurable. Orgo reports thousands of production desktops, identifies Peter Steinberger as its first paying customer, and charges $29, $99, or $399 monthly. Developers may happily pay to avoid assembling this infrastructure. This fits my preference for tools that turn individual knowledge into scalable output. I can also accept an early product before a distribution loop is visible when the prototype and founder evidence are exceptional. Here, however, overlapping alternatives already exist, and the dossier establishes neither unusual capital efficiency nor proprietary distribution. Posted prices and a named payer establish direct value capture; they do not establish contribution margins.

I see a credible business and remain neutral on the investment. The disclosed account freezes and MCP failures make dependable operation consequential, while the published fixes provide no basis for an integrity accusation. There is also no evidence of coercive fundraising or founder instability. The constraint is structural: customers can receive considerable value without Orgo acquiring lasting power over its economics. My flip condition is a measured customer-contributed template loop in which new contributions reduce subsequent customers’ activation time and sustain paid retention. That would show customer activity building an advantage beyond the code.
