# Decent -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The useful question about Decent is what the founders do when an insurer will not provide the data. Comparing plans can save customers money. Making a health plan improve throughout the year requires something harder: obtaining claims, finding an actionable problem, and getting someone to change the outcome. Decent explicitly excludes fully insured and PEO arrangements from its claims-report process. That puts a real obstacle at the center of the promise. The dossier does not show how the founders have overcome it. My largest criterion therefore stays at its missing-evidence default. Operating in a difficult industry does not itself demonstrate resourcefulness.

Nick Soman has a good reason to be here. He previously operated health plans and a PEO; this problem comes from work he has actually done. Licensing, carrier relationships, and plan administration supply plenty of the unpleasant work that can conceal an opportunity. Andrew Budker’s production AI experience makes the technical approach credible, and the founders report meeting while building Kindle roughly 16 years ago. That reduces the concern about a team assembled for an application, though knowing someone for years is not evidence of surviving a startup together. The September 29 announcement establishes a new company after the earlier Decent’s acquisition. It does not establish an evidence-driven product correction. I give the team substantial credit for problem exposure, but the route-changing criterion remains at its default.

The strongest argument for investing is that customers can already describe a concrete result. Serif Health reports $60,000 in annual savings; Locate reports more than $100,000 against its renewal proposal. Those are useful outcomes for identifiable employers, especially at this stage. The reported 67 companies “working with us” adds breadth, although it does not establish how many bought coverage, returned, or generated commissions. More importantly, both testimonials support the plan-comparison service. Neither establishes that continuous claims intervention caused the savings. Decent has evidence that customers value help buying insurance. Its more ambitious promise still needs its own evidence.

The claim that AI performs 89% of brokerage work suggests an operating system behind the service, but the unpublished measurement method limits the technical credit. My pattern of backing concrete proof before a round points toward inspecting that system, rather than accepting the percentage. A two-person brokerage with unusually low servicing costs could become valuable through ordinary commissions. But those commissions vary by provider, and bonuses can reward recommendations differently from customer savings. Nava already offers overlapping year-round support and bill audits. Decent must show that its founders have built a better way through the work, or competition can reduce it to another broker with similar software. Neither carrier approval nor absent public revenue is a red flag by itself; the unresolved issue is whether the distinctive workflow actually works.

I would not write a check yet. The exceptions for a compelling working product or unmistakable user pull deserve consideration: the named savings are the strongest reason this decision could be wrong. They do not yet replace the missing evidence of founder action under resistance, and there is no independently validated technical result to supply another override. One verifiable case would change my decision: a dated customer record showing initially unavailable claims data, a founder-built workaround shipped within weeks, and independently confirmed savings caused by the resulting intervention. That would connect the founders, the software, and the customer outcome. Today those are promising pieces with the decisive connection still missing.
