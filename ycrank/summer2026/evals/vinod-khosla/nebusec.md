# NebuSec -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

NebuSec could make scarce vulnerability-research expertise available continuously, before vulnerable code ships. That would matter. But the dossier does not establish how much expert labor disappears, how much defense costs fall, or how many additional systems become economically defensible. A $42.09 billion application-security market forecast establishes a spending category; it does not quantify the consequence of NebuSec succeeding. I can lose my entire check on an improbable breakthrough. First, I need evidence that the breakthrough changes something enormous.

The strongest product evidence is also the clearest limit on that argument. NebuSec reports 88% coverage on 76 known vulnerabilities, against 80% for Codex Security and 74% for Claude Security. Eight percentage points above Codex could be valuable, especially if those additional findings are exploitable. It does not establish a 5–10x discontinuity. The dossier supplies no comparable scan costs, researcher hours, or performance on unseen repositories. Dynamic exploit verification being disabled by default makes the distinction between finding candidates and reliably proving exploitability especially consequential. Semgrep and Aikido already address overlapping workflows; NebuSec needs a capability advantage that survives their next release.

The strongest objection to passing is that these founders can generate evidence other teams cannot. Their systems-security, program-analysis, automated-repair, and exploit-development backgrounds fit the actual work. Published exploits and team-reported Google rewards exceeding $400,000 give that expertise substance. PingCAP independently confirms an investigation completed by October 23, 2026. This resembles the pattern I favor: scarce technical work becoming a repeatable production platform. If NebuSec’s claimed 1,180-plus validated vulnerabilities become a proprietary learning loop, the researchers could build an advantage that compounds. But neither exclusive data rights nor improvement from accumulated cases is established. The engagement validates research capability; it does not yet validate autonomous delivery. The team’s record also supplies no documented belief revision or difficult recruit brought in to own a specific weakness.

The next dollar should resolve whether that research capability scales without researcher labor scaling alongside it. No disclosed milestone specifies that experiment, its budget, or a numerical stop condition, so I give the process its missing-evidence default. Fixed quotes and tiered plans likewise disclose packaging, not economics: compute, verification labor, remediation effort, and incumbent cost remain unknown. I see no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, a generic model wrapper, or spending aggressively ahead of unresolved technical risk. Those red flags are unproven. Their absence does not establish the cost curve.

I pass with my own money today. The exception case—an unusually committed technical team addressing a large existing need—deserves consideration, particularly given Tan’s departure from doctoral studies. It does not overcome the missing consequence calculation and modest demonstrated product delta. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently observed, blinded evaluation on previously unseen customer repositories showing at least 5x lower fully loaded cost per verified exploitable vulnerability than a named incumbent, at equal or better coverage, with human intervention counted. That result would connect NebuSec’s genuine research strength to an economic discontinuity worth underwriting.
