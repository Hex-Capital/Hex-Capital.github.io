# ORO AI -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

ORO AI’s founders have acquired relevant knowledge through action. Bansal worked on distributed-training evaluation and built on Bittensor at Crucible Labs; Schilbe worked on ECS and Cloud Map at AWS. Together they have shipped a functioning agent competition and responded to benchmark exploitation by introducing hidden-task races. That is stronger evidence than credentials alone. They have encountered the failure modes of the system they are selling.

The most interesting result is that outside contributors appear to produce something useful beyond their own competition scores. ORO reported over 345 competing agents in April 2026, and its June research reported Qwen3-4B improving from 18.0% to 42.7% on a guarded held-out partition. This fits my unstated preference for permissionless systems that aggregate useful work from outsiders. But the experiment establishes useful training material, not yet a self-reinforcing network. It does not show that another participant improves everyone else’s outcomes at declining cost. The $85,000 distributed to builders makes incentive dependence a live question; it does not establish permanent subsidy dependence.

The ownership question is harder. ORO reports 4,891,831 scored trajectories, but these are distinct agent-version/task pairs, not necessarily independent lessons. A competitor copying the MIT-licensed code would still need to recruit participants and operate changing environments. That creates work to reproduce. The dossier does not establish exclusive data rights, a proprietary model, or a participant relationship that becomes harder to displace with every deployment. I cannot turn an accumulated corpus into a durable asset merely by counting it.

There is a credible expansion path from shopping-agent evaluation into training data and custom environments for research teams. The published training experiment demonstrates that evaluation executions can serve a second purpose. Prime Intellect’s reported revenue above $100 million annualized supplies evidence of adjacent infrastructure spending, although it does not establish the size of ORO’s addressable slice. The website’s invitation to request data and environments identifies a possible commercial offering. No disclosed payer, price, or revenue establishes value capture, so monetization receives the absent-evidence default.

The strongest objection to my caution is that ORO has already crossed the boundary many supposed data networks never cross: contributors generated material that improved a model on a held-out test. Dismissing this as copyable software would miss the mechanism. My exceptions for working prototypes and emerging networks allow me to invest before revenue. Here, however, the research result has not yet established either durable ownership or an economically independent contribution loop. The reported 0.71 practice score versus 0.18 hidden-task score also shows why measuring competition activity alone would be dangerous.

I am **Neutral**. The founders and technical experiment earn serious attention; the compounding business remains unproved. The dossier supplies no affirmative integrity or financing-pressure red flag, and the crypto label itself is irrelevant to my decision. My one verifiable flip condition is an unaffiliated AI lab renewing a paid license for ORO trajectories after independently confirming held-out improvements it cannot reproduce from ORO’s public artifacts. That renewal would connect useful outsider work to differentiated value that a customer repeatedly buys.
