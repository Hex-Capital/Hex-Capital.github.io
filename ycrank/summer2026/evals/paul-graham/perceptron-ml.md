# Perceptron ML -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Michael Marcotte built production agents for hardware debugging and formal verification at NVIDIA, but neither Michael nor Peyton Marcotte has practiced law. That makes Perceptron ML a schlep-rich, non-organic startup: unusually willing to enter confidential customer infrastructure, yet apparently solving pain observed from outside rather than pain the founders suffered themselves. Perceptron ML’s analytical signature is strong appetite for unpleasant implementation paired with weak founder-problem fit. That pairing can produce a good business. It less often produces a black swan.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness: compliance was not incidental work but the reason saner founders stayed away. Perceptron ML shares that mechanism through customer-controlled deployments, matter-level integrations, no shared training, and verification against primary sources. Law firms are full of ancient systems, cautious partners, and documents no one wants uploaded to a shared model. But Perceptron ML diverges from Coinbase where the analogy matters most: Perceptron ML publishes no verification benchmark, SOC 2 certification, error rate, or named customer. The schlep is visible. Evidence that Perceptron ML has mastered it is not yet visible.

**Loopt** (2005) is the low rung for organic founder pain: product timing was weak, and Sam Altman’s force of personality—not evidence that he had built the thing to solve his own recurring problem—overrode it. Perceptron ML has no comparable override signal. Michael’s verification background explains why Perceptron ML can build source-gated systems, but not why Michael or Peyton needed a law-firm operating system themselves. No legal-practice background, identified legal adviser, or founder-as-user story appears in the dossier, so I apply the absent-evidence default. A technical capability looking for a lucrative workflow is not the same thing as Drew Houston repeatedly forgetting his USB stick.

The displayed 38.4 billable hours captured and 7.5 hours recovered show that Perceptron ML shipped something into a real workflow. Over 18 months, animal resourcefulness would compound if Michael and Peyton personally install Perceptron ML inside reluctant firms, reconcile broken calendars and docket systems, and turn every ugly exception into reusable software. The live Notyfi product also suggests they shipped before settling on the current idea. But Perceptron ML supplies no revenue, named customer, unscalable sales stunt, or account of overcoming rejection. Animals leave tracks; one unattributed case-study week is a footprint, not a trail.

Michael Marcotte’s Stanford mathematics and computer-science training, NVIDIA research, and formal-verification work give Perceptron ML more hacker substance than the usual legal-AI wrapper. Peyton Marcotte’s NASA-supported PMARC project adds evidence of building outside a classroom, although his public GitHub signal peaks at a one-star OrcaSlicer fork. Perceptron ML would move up a tier on hacker genealogy if the founders released a technically non-trivial verification artifact or published a reproducible benchmark showing that Perceptron ML catches legal hallucinations competitors miss. Credentials say they probably can build. Artifacts would show how they build.

**Stripe** (2010) is the useful single-anchor parallel for Perceptron ML’s founder configuration: two brothers can communicate with a bandwidth ordinary cofounders spend years acquiring. Michael explicitly says he is attending YC with his brother Peyton, so Perceptron ML avoids the solo-founder and startup-speed-dating red flags. But the Collisons had already hacked on commerce together through Auctomatic. Perceptron ML’s dossier establishes kinship, not years of building together or complementary ownership of technical and law-firm work. Brothers are a promising configuration, not automatic chemistry.

Harvey’s estimated $300 million ARR and Legora’s estimated $100 million ARR imply that Perceptron ML will not win by offering generic legal agents. By July 2029, I expect Perceptron ML to have narrowed to privately deployed timekeeping and docket-monitoring systems for mid-sized law firms, reached $2–6 million ARR, and remained services-heavy rather than becoming a broad platform. Perceptron ML’s schlep tolerance should win customers that resist Harvey’s unified platform, while weak organic founder pain and built-to-order deployments should slow the discovery of a repeatable product.

Harvey’s $1 billion-plus funding makes the strongest bear case: source grounding, firm knowledge, audit controls, and data isolation may become standard features before Perceptron ML escapes custom consulting. The opposing bull case stings too. **Airbnb** (2009) lacked an obvious market but survived because the founders’ cereal-box stunt revealed something more important than founder-market fit; Perceptron ML’s prior Notyfi product, Peyton’s NASA-backed project, and the 7.5 recovered hours could be early fragments of the same animal pattern. I do not give Perceptron ML the Airbnb exception because there is no equivalent stunt, named buyer, or proof that the founders can repeatedly force adoption. Two named law firms renewing Perceptron ML deployments above $100,000 annually would flip my verdict to Invest.
