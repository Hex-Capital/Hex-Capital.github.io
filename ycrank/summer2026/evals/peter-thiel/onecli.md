# OneCLI -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

OneCLI’s contested truth is precise: an enterprise agent should never possess credentials; authority should remain bound to an employee and be injected only when a gateway approves a specific request. The claim would be disproved if agents routinely bypass that gateway or if conventional identity products reproduce the architecture without adopting OneCLI’s workspace. Both possibilities are already visible. The shipped gateway, human approvals, and NanoClaw integration make this more than a slogan, but raw sockets can bypass enforcement when egress lockdown—disabled by default—is absent, and OneCLI has already patched a host-enforcement bypass. The secret is testable, but it has not yet survived its own strongest test.

The founders are the clearest reason to continue investigating. Jonathan Fishner built zero-trust access at Axis Security; Guy Ben-Aharon was Argon Security’s first engineer and worked at Aqua Security and Unit 8200. They have also built together since 2024, taking ChartDB past 22,000 GitHub stars and $10,000 MRR. YC and their current profiles identify both as active founders, and the core engineering appears internal. This is a company formed by security operators with a demonstrated working relationship, not two strangers assembled around an AI category. Founder ownership and economic exposure remain undisclosed, but none of the part-time, outsourced-core, or convenience-matched-team red flags appears.

Is the beachhead a monopoly or merely a feature boundary? Per-employee assistant agents are narrower than “agentic security,” and NanoClaw’s use of OneCLI as its sole credential path is a legitimate initial wedge. Yet one integration and roughly 3,300 shared-repository stars do not establish control. The code is Apache-2.0 licensed; Aembit already brokers credentials through policy; Pomerium offers an open-source per-request gateway; and Cisco owns Astrix. Installed policies and identity mappings could eventually create switching costs, but future inconvenience is not a present monopoly mechanism. OneCLI currently has differentiation without defensibility.

The strongest bull case is that the team already understands open-source distribution. The project reports more than 350,000 cumulative downloads, reached 110 Hacker News points, ranked seventh on Product Hunt with 143 points, and displays companies including Docker, Zoho, and Coralogix as trusted users. Prices of $149 and $499 per month create a clear conversion path from the free tier, while enterprise contracts could support a larger business. This also fits the less visible pattern of investing in infrastructure that makes adversarial systems legible: employee identity, approvals, and audit logs turn autonomous behavior into attributable action.

But the evidence changes when assigned to the current product. The downloads and repository history span a credential-vault product and the later team-harness pivot; the company discloses no paying customer, conversion rate, sales cycle, renewal, or gross margin. Logos are not contracts. Product Hunt attention is not enterprise distribution. The known-exception logic does not rescue this: there is neither persistent paid behavior under adversity nor an independently verified zero-to-one technical result capable of overriding weak monopoly evidence. My 36-month base case is that OneCLI abandons the full assistant shell, embeds the identity gateway into other agent platforms, and reaches $3–8 million ARR by August 2029—a credible security product, but not yet a demonstrated power-law company.

I would not invest today, but I would keep the company close. The team is stronger than the current market position, and that asymmetry often produces a useful pivot; it does not justify pretending the present gateway owns its category. One verifiable condition flips the decision: five named enterprises each paying at least $50,000 ARR for the current product and renewing after twelve months. That cohort would simultaneously prove a real buyer, durable use, and a distribution route capable of financing an expanding identity layer.
