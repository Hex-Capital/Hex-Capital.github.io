# Inkbox -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and an autonomous agent does not borrow its human’s inbox, phone number, credentials, or localhost tunnel. It has a durable address, receives verification codes, remembers who contacted it, and can be revoked without dismantling the person behind it. Inkbox has one small leak from that world: Vendo assigned identities to five agents spanning engineering, GTM, operations, and founder assistance. What is missing is the causal bridge showing how those endpoints become a trusted identity layer rather than a convenient bundle of Gmail, Twilio, and ngrok substitutes.

The lower-stack position is the exciting part. A mailbox, PSTN number, iMessage access, stable tunnel, scoped contacts, channel history, and zero-knowledge vault can become shared infrastructure for many agent categories. Sixteen public repositories, more than 20 external pull requests, and plugins for Codex, Claude Code, OpenClaw, OpenCode, and Hermes suggest that the team is building something developers can inhabit. But control remains incomplete: carriers govern 10DLC, Apple constrains iMessage, and Twilio already spans several of these surfaces. Inkbox owns the orchestration and emerging identity record, not yet a scarce underlying resource.

Ray Liao, Dima Vremenko, and Alex Wilcox have credible technical range. Ray built a virtual clinic after an MIT machine-learning PhD, Alex performed networking research and became Empallo’s founding engineer, and the team built Bluebox before Inkbox. That supports the ability to connect agents, networks, and communications. It does not yet show the pre-company obsession I look for: no dated trail of repeated identity experiments, no founder story explaining why this problem would not leave them alone, and no evidence yet that the team understands the commercial lever beyond publishing sensible $30 and $180–$200 monthly plans.

The strongest bull case is behavioral. Vendo did not merely test an inbox; it assigned five separate organizational roles to agents, while nearly 100 people joined the Discord and outsiders contributed more than 20 pull requests. That is stranger and more informative than a waitlist. Still, the dossier gives no deployment duration, paid conversion, message volume, retention, or evidence that losing Inkbox would hurt. The pre-revenue exception I made for CosmicBrain AI involved a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a founder’s lifelong robotics record. Inkbox shares the working-artifact mechanism but not those two independent proofs.

The present-day wound is also less structural than it first appears. Developers pay an integration tax by stitching together email, telephony, authentication, and ingress, but Inkbox has not quantified that cost, identified who captures an artificial rent, or shown whose occupational control is being restored. Worse, centralizing email, calls, texts, contacts, notes, and credentials activates my privacy gate. Zero-knowledge vault encryption, scoped access, allowlists, and provisional-account restrictions are thoughtful, but they do not yet explain custody and consent for the surrounding communications data. Convenience cannot be allowed to mature into surveillance infrastructure by accident.

I am neutral today. Inkbox fits my unstated pattern of concealed infrastructure beneath a fashionable application wave, but it also carries the corresponding red flags: component substitution, dependency on closed communications systems, and concentrated sensitive data. The one condition that flips me to Invest is three unaffiliated customers each operating at least 25 paid Inkbox identities for 90 consecutive days, with documented cross-channel usage and retention. That would establish that persistent identity—not discounted API bundling—is the product users reorganize around.
