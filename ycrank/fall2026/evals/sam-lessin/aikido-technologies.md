# Aikido Technologies -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Aikido Technologies has raised money, but I cannot price the next check. The $4 million seed in June 2024 and $1,721,999 subsequently sold establish financing history; neither establishes today’s post-money valuation, competing offers, or why capital is scarce. I would not infer an auction from the investor list. I also would not infer a bargain from difficult engineering. For my own money, the missing deal terms leave the first gate unresolved.

The potentially investable experiment is concrete: can putting compute on a floating wind platform produce reliable, economically competitive capacity? Aikido announced a Norway proof of concept for 2026, which gives that question somewhere to meet reality. But the dossier does not connect round proceeds to a funded test with measurable acceptance criteria. A projected PUE below 1.08 tests only part of the proposition. Wind variability, batteries, grid connections, diesel backup, and vessel servicing all enter the delivered economics. I need one experiment that resolves whether a customer will buy the resulting service at a price that covers those obligations.

The strongest argument against passing is that these founders have already moved beyond presentation engineering. ABS witnessed a quarter-scale upending demonstration and issued a statement of maturity in February 2025; Aikido also holds floating-wind patent US12571372B2. Kanner and Yu bring prior Principle Power experience, and Reeves brings turbine and helicopter engineering. This fits my willingness to fund neglected physical infrastructure: relevant operators have produced external technical evidence. It earns the team affirmative credit. However, that evidence validates the marine side of the bridge. The dossier does not establish comparable operating experience delivering datacenter capacity, and the demonstrated platform was non-operational.

Who owns the customer access? Commercial discussions around an identified UK site targeting 2028 operations are more specific than general AI demand, but they establish no signed purchase or retained usage. NVIDIA Inception membership establishes no conversion either. The patent protects floating-wind technology; it does not itself prove proprietary access to compute buyers. Meanwhile, Panthalassa’s $210 million of confirmed financing through May 2026 demonstrates a funded alternative, without proving either company’s economics. Delete Aikido’s next equity round and the dossier supplies no margin, burn, customer prepayment, or project-financing structure from which I can underwrite completion. Those are unknowns, not evidence that the business is uneconomic.

My exceptions to missing gates require unusually hard operating receipts or a financing structure that removes repeated fundraising dependence. Aikido’s engineering demonstration matters, but it does not substitute for those receipts. There is no evidence here of an auction, an equal-founder decision committee, or an AI application dependent on model scarcity; I am passing on an ununderwritten infrastructure investment, not assigning those red flags by association. One verifiable flip condition would be an executed pilot-financing package that discloses the equity price and fully funds delivery against a paying customer’s availability and cost acceptance thresholds. That would make the venture moment—and what my check actually buys—reviewable.
