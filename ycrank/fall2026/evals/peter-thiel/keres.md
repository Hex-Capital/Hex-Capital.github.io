# Keres -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Keres’s proposed secret is that a jet can destroy an incoming threat, survive the engagement, and return cheaply enough to make repeated interception economical. That is a concrete proposition worth testing. Repeated interceptions followed by expensive repairs would falsify the economic claim even if the aircraft landed successfully. But the dossier names no peer who rejects this proposition and reports no completed interception-and-recovery experiment. I give the specificity some credit; I cannot give the proposed result the credit of a demonstrated secret.

What market would that secret let Keres own? Recovery after a kill distinguishes Garmur’s proposal from an expendable explosive interceptor. Fortem’s net-capture aircraft also show that reusable interception already has competing implementations. The relevant beachhead must therefore specify which threats and operating conditions require Keres’s particular solution. “Drones or cruise missiles” does not establish that boundary. There is no demonstrated proprietary technology, exclusive operational dataset, or dominant position today. Equally, the dossier does not establish that Garmur is an interchangeable commodity. The monopoly mechanism remains unproved.

The strongest argument for proceeding is Christopher Palazzolo. More than four years spanning aircraft maintenance, avionics, flight testing, and XB-1 production at Boom, followed by operations leadership at H3X, put relevant practical experience inside this three-person company. Recoverability makes maintenance and turnaround central to the product, so his background matters. It does not establish that the founders have previously shipped together, are all full-time, retain meaningful ownership, or perform all essential engineering internally. Those founding conditions receive the missing-evidence default. The dossier establishes neither the operator-network formation pattern I favor nor an affirmative founding-team red flag.

Keres also addresses a real inventory and engagement-cost problem. DIU’s 2025 follow-on funding after counter-UAS demonstrations supports that demand context. It does not establish a procurement route for this company. The inferred aircraft-sales-and-maintenance model supplies no price, margin, service life, or expansion arithmetic; the broader $12.6 billion counter-UAS spending estimate cannot supply those missing economics. A public inquiry address identifies where buyers can contact Keres, but no named purchasing office, sales owner, integration partner, or procurement milestone establishes how Keres reaches them. The October 12, 2026 media profile supplies no customer validation.

The hardware coordination red flag deserves attention: destruction, survival, recovery, combat-system integration, safety approval, and procurement must connect. The dossier does not prove that every step requires simultaneous financing, but it documents no independently validated unit that breaks the dependency chain. I would consider an exception for a demonstrated technical discontinuity, persistent customer demand, or a concrete manufacturing-and-deployment path. None is documented here. Palazzolo’s experience makes that exception plausible enough to investigate; it does not activate it.

I pass on writing a check today. Keres has articulated a potentially important engineering secret, while ownership of the resulting market and access to its buyers remain conjectural. My single verifiable flip condition is a military-customer-witnessed trial in which the same Garmur destroys multiple representative targets across successive sorties, with recorded recovery, repair, and turnaround costs demonstrating an advantage over the customer’s current interceptor. That would justify reopening the investment decision.
