# ProvenMetal -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Will Carkner built circuit boards from childhood and later operated Lumindt’s PCB-testing pipeline, while Johnny Doyle shipped IoT building-management hardware. ProvenMetal is an authentic maker-founded industrial company whose strongest signal is lived contact with the problem and whose weakest signal is unresolved co-founder authority. ProvenMetal feels *of* the work rather than visiting a market discovered in a memo—but two longtime collaborators still have to prove who holds the knife when a seven-day delivery promise collides with quality, cost, or cash.

**Anduril** is the high rung: Palmer Luckey carried years of hardware-building identity into an ugly category conventional investors avoided. Doyle’s manufacturing background and Carkner’s PCB-testing work give ProvenMetal the same load-bearing mechanism—founders who already know what a failed board smells like before writing the company narrative. ProvenMetal diverges because neither founder has yet shipped an adjacent manufacturing operation at meaningful scale; five LOIs from more than 30 conversations validate the pain, but they do not establish Luckey-level earned authority over the production system. Biz and Tiz are visible. Rizz is still behind the curtain.

**HQ Trivia** is the low-rung warning: a tangible, magnetic product temporarily hid the cost of unresolved founder authority until conflict became part of the failure mechanism. ProvenMetal names Doyle as CEO and Carkner as CTO, which is materially better than shared titles, but ProvenMetal discloses no tiebreaker agreement, equity structure, vesting protocol, or conflict process between two founders who previously worked together at Syncra. The known exception does not rescue ProvenMetal here: a PCB-ordering service has no demonstrated HQ-style viral pull strong enough to purchase time from a governance fracture.

Five LOIs create a small flywheel for ProvenMetal’s magically weirdness: every repeated board revision can add supplier-performance history, inspection records, and manufacturing knowledge that make the managed network less interchangeable over the next 18 months. ProvenMetal fails to compound if those LOIs become one-off prototypes and MacroFab’s network of more than 100 North American factories simply matches the seven-day turn. Domestic PCB orchestration is pleasantly unsexy, but reshoring is already a popular narrative and the dossier identifies no sophisticated investors rejecting ProvenMetal on aesthetic or technical grounds. This is a rebuttal to consensus, not yet a rebellion against it.

MacroFab’s $82 million of financing exposes the counterfactual for ProvenMetal’s industrial-swing score. ProvenMetal would move into my highest band if it owned a critical fabrication or X-ray-inspection step, obtained defense-grade certification, or developed a process advantage that competitors could not rent from the same U.S. partners. ProvenMetal currently coordinates fabrication and assembly through vetted partners, making it a physical-world supply-chain bet without the frontier-capex commitment of a rocket, drone, or factory. The earlier benchtop X-ray concept hints at a harder company hiding inside the current service; reviving that proprietary inspection layer would change my score.

**Flock Safety** made the physical artifact itself a sourcing signal: a camera and even its Wi-Fi name could be observed before the spreadsheet arrived. ProvenMetal has a similarly tactile truth surface—customers submit Gerbers and BOMs, receive tested boards in seven days, and get production and inspection records—but ProvenMetal’s claimed 99.8% inspection confidence publishes neither methodology nor sample size. I can touch the board, which matters enormously to me; I cannot yet touch the proof behind the quality claim. The artifact is real even while the measurement remains theatrical.

The $98.9 billion global PCBA market gives ProvenMetal room to become a meaningful specialist without defeating JLCPCB everywhere. My 36-month forecast is that ProvenMetal reaches $6 million–$12 million in annualized revenue supplying U.S. drone, defense, and robotics developers, while one founder’s operating role is materially narrowed after delivery economics force an explicit CEO tiebreaker. ProvenMetal gets there because Carkner and Doyle have lived the hardware failure loop; the same two-founder ambiguity that lowers my governance score becomes unavoidable once partner capacity and seven-day commitments start competing.

JLCPCB’s 21 million annual orders make the bear case sting: ProvenMetal could be a thin-margin broker with no structural moat, an unverified 99.8% quality claim, five nonbinding LOIs, and a product already evolved away from its benchtop X-ray origin. ProvenMetal nevertheless has evidence the consensus-AI darling lacks—two founders with direct hardware histories, an orderable physical product, domestic traceability, and customer pain discovered across more than 30 industry conversations. I would invest my own money, sized for partner and governance risk. If fewer than three of ProvenMetal’s five LOIs become paying repeat customers by July 2027, the managed-network thesis has been falsified and I flip to Pass.
