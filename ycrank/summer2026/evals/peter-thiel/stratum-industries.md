# Stratum Industries -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

**Stratum Industries targets federal, state, and local adjudication backlogs, yet discloses no roadmap beyond requesting agency introductions.** Stratum Industries is a regulated-sector wager trapped inside an indefinite plan: the category resembles the neglected terrain that produced Palantir, while the operating posture resembles the iteration mode *Zero to One* warned against. Stratum Industries has chosen an unusually difficult customer, but difficulty is not yet a secret. The government moat exists only after deployment; before deployment, government is merely a slow market.

**Palantir turned government integration depth and institutional trust into a monopoly-grade barrier.** Stratum Industries shares the load-bearing mechanism: consequential decisions, sensitive records, agency-specific rules, and workflows that become difficult to displace once embedded. Stratum Industries diverges where the analogy matters most—Palantir built around intelligence customers Silicon Valley refused to serve, while Salesforce already offers FedRAMP-authorized public-sector agents, ClearOS advertises nearly identical adjudication workflows, and GovWell serves more than 130 municipalities and counties. Regulated customers can create a moat, but Stratum Industries has disclosed neither a customer nor a certification. I score the regulated-sector structure above the software default, not at the Palantir rung.

**The generic “AI for X” startup fails because iteration substitutes for a definite theory of the future.** That failure mechanism appears almost completely in Stratum Industries: no two-to-five-year milestones, no architectural commitment, no selected agency wedge, and no disclosed pilot. Stratum Industries describes agents that route work among models, request missing information, and generate cited summaries, but those are product functions rather than a plan. The request for introductions across federal, state, and local agencies makes the scope less definite, not more. A company cannot clear every government backlog before deciding which bureaucracy it understands.

**Salesforce, ClearOS, and GovWell compress Stratum Industries from both ends over the next 18 months.** Salesforce can distribute through existing Government Cloud installations; ClearOS can match the reviewer-side workflow; GovWell can compound integrations across its 130-plus government customers. Stratum Industries could accumulate agency rubrics, audited histories, and validated outcomes into switching costs, but only after winning deployments, and no deployments are disclosed. Until then, the model layer commoditizes faster than procurement compounds. Stratum Industries therefore has a hypothetical monopoly mechanism without a pricing-power hypothesis.

**Stripe would have flipped the secret score because it named PayPal’s precise blind spot: legacy processors could not treat developers as the primary customer.** For Stratum Industries to cross that threshold, it must show why Salesforce’s FedRAMP-authorized architecture or ClearOS’s adjudication product structurally cannot solve a specific category—perhaps patent examination, FDA review, or disability claims—and why that failure persists for five years. Stratum Industries currently offers the consensus proposition that pre-AI government software is slow. The backlog is real, but a visible problem is not a hidden truth. Naming Salesforce and ClearOS as competitors does not establish that either is constitutionally unable to win.

**Vihaan Jagiasi was founding engineer number three at Cadastral before its acquisition by Legora, while Raj Jagiasi averaged 220% of quota at Deel.** On founder lineage, Stratum Industries has a smaller version of the Anduril pattern: one founder has shipped inside an acquired startup, and the other has demonstrated exceptional enterprise distribution ability. Stratum Industries lacks Palmer Luckey’s billion-dollar hardware exit and both founders lack government procurement experience, but the engineering-sales pairing is substantive pre-seed evidence. The brothers’ credentials are the strongest argument that Stratum Industries might learn the institutional terrain faster than the dossier presently suggests.

**No customers, revenue, pilots, or processing volumes are disclosed as of July 2026.** My 36-month forecast is that Stratum Industries abandons the horizontal promise of clearing the U.S. government backlog, narrows to municipal permitting or licensing, and reaches between $1 million and $3 million ARR without establishing cross-agency monopoly power. Stratum Industries has enough sales and engineering capacity to win a narrow workflow, while the absent roadmap and direct overlap with ClearOS make broad adjudication implausible. The likely outcome is a useful vertical vendor, not the one ring of power-law distribution.

**GovWell’s $34.5 million funding and 130-plus government customers supply the strongest bull case for Stratum Industries: government modernization is real, procurement is opening, and a two-founder engineering-sales pair could enter before incumbents adapt.** The Known Exception is Asana—operator pedigree can override weak atoms and an initially crowded category—but Dustin Moskovitz had already shipped Facebook to global scale; Stratum Industries cannot claim an equivalent override from one acquired startup role and Deel quota attainment. The evidence that wins is direct: ClearOS already markets AI adjudication, Salesforce already has authorized infrastructure, and Stratum Industries names no incumbent architecture that cannot respond. I would flip to Invest if Stratum Industries produces one named agency deployment with repeat case volume and demonstrates a decision-accuracy or procurement advantage that ClearOS and Salesforce cannot replicate.
