# Glen -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Nikos Dritsakos has learned by doing. He operated inside Composio, built an AI sales-coaching company for roughly 18 months, and sold it for $500,000 in February 2025. That is meaningful evidence of specific knowledge in agent tooling and B2B workflows, although not yet proof that he has mastered organization-wide memory. The three named testimonials from Amorphic Labs, Zaplar, and Composio strengthen the founder signal: Glen is a production product being tested by identifiable users, not a résumé attached to a mockup.

The weakness appears when I copy the code. Glen captures activity from Slack, GitHub, tickets, calls, and agent sessions, then retrieves decisions and distills repeated work into skills. But Glean, Dust, Zep, Mem0, model providers, and agent platforms can all build against the same integrations and MCP interfaces. The dossier identifies no exclusive data, proprietary model, hardware, or demonstrated workflow lock-in. Organization-specific histories could compound, but a future switching cost is not a present asset. If the customer can export the corpus and a competitor can reproduce the retrieval layer, the accumulated context belongs economically to the customer, not Glen.

The market is real but the wedge is not yet expansive. Enterprise search was a $5.86 billion market in 2025, and Glean’s $300 million ARR proves that enterprises pay for better access to organizational knowledge. Yet Glen has not shown a causal path from its initial design partners into a broader transaction layer. It is entering an established category through a narrower feature set while Dust already reports 3,000 organizations, 51,000 monthly active users, and 300,000 deployed agents. A large parent market creates room to maneuver only when the startup owns a capability that travels across it.

The bull case is sharper than the current score: a solo repeat founder with 5,376 LinkedIn followers can sell directly, the product is already in production, and every captured decision or distilled skill could make future agents more capable. Glen might convert individual knowledge into leverage that compounds across the organization. But the dossier shows three testimonials, not repeated usage, retention, referrals, or evidence that one participant improves outcomes for another. Automatic aggregation by software is not yet a user-created network.

Economics remain similarly unproven. Stripe appears on the subprocessor list and the terms mention paid plans, but there is no public price, payer, revenue, or completed transaction. That is normal at pre-seed and not a fatal defect; it simply cannot substitute for direct value capture. I found no integrity, artificial-scarcity, or founder-morale red flag. The problem is structural rather than personal: Glen currently looks like capable execution in a crowded, rapidly copyable software layer.

I pass today. Within 36 months, my base case is that Glen pivots from broad organizational memory into a narrower developer-facing agent-context product and remains below $2 million ARR. One condition flips the decision: ten paying organizations retaining Glen for twelve months while their accumulated private skill graphs produce a measured improvement in agent task completion that disappears when Glen is removed. That would turn the proposed data moat and user leverage into an observed compounding asset.
