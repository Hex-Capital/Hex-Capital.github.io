# Jinko -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Jinko’s proposed post-money, competing-term-sheet count, and use of proceeds are undisclosed. I cannot underwrite the deal or identify the truth my money would reveal. That earns the missing-evidence defaults, without assuming YC means an auction. The actual investment question is whether Jinko can own the customer relationship between agents and travel suppliers when those suppliers already offer agent access themselves.

The founders give that question credibility. Gauthier, Texier, and Zhang built Mowgli together before its acquisition by Hopper; their experience spans Air France, consumer booking, European expansion, and engineering. This is unusually concrete evidence of people who have operated across the systems they now propose to join. Jinko fits my pattern of backing translation layers inside difficult physical businesses. The CEO/Co-CEO arrangement leaves final decision authority unclear, but titles alone do not establish a dysfunctional committee.

The strongest bull case is that a booking interface understates what customers need. Jinko supplies live pricing, payment, booking, and servicing across flights, hotels, and cars, and identifies itself as the contracting travel operator with liability coverage. An agent developer may happily outsource that operational mess. The founders’ experience makes this substantially more credible than an MCP demonstration. But Etraveli’s October 28, 2026 launch also includes payment, ticketing, and servicing. The competitive threat already reaches beyond interface replication. Jinko needs evidence that its particular operating layer wins and keeps the account.

That evidence is currently missing. Exa Connect provides a discovery channel; it does not establish a purchase. Coverage of 800 airlines and 2.6 million hotels describes accessible inventory, without demonstrating exclusive access or customer dependence. Neither the builder event nor founder audiences establish owned distribution that causes bookings. My concern is the AI red flag in a specific form: suppliers can expose the same underlying transactions directly, leaving Jinko responsible for expensive exceptions without controlling demand. The dossier does not establish that outcome, but it supplies no conversion or retention receipts sufficient to override it.

The economics make ownership of demand consequential. Jinko charges 1% per confirmed flight booking and $0.005 per excess search above a 1,500:1 search-to-book ratio. The excess-search charge acknowledges a real cost problem; it does not prove the fee covers that problem. There is no disclosed contribution margin after supplier, payment, and servicing costs, or seed-funded route to break-even. My known exceptions require exceptional observed conversion, retention, or a financing structure that makes repeated fundraising unnecessary. Jinko’s prior operating history supports the team, but does not establish those properties for this product.

I pass at 33/100. This is a credible team with an operationally substantive product, but the evidence is concentrated in founder capability while the deal, customer ownership, and cash engine remain unproven. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: a 90-day cohort showing agent customers repeatedly completing bookings at positive contribution margin after search, payment, and servicing costs. That would demonstrate that Jinko’s bridge earns money when customers actually cross it.
