# Cosmic Robotics -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that solar construction is large. It is that Emerick and Jones encountered a job where people repeatedly lift 90-pound panels in hostile terrain and built a 10,000-pound machine that has now handled tens of thousands of installations. The workaround is real. Cosmic-1A operates across mud, sand, ruts, and weather, placing panels within millimeters while crews fasten them. But the dossier does not identify one field failure, the founders’ response, and the time required to ship the correction. So the evidence shows resourcefulness in aggregate, not yet the unusually legible obstacle-and-recovery sequence I most trust.

The technical proof is much stronger. Cosmic reported 30–40-second placement cycles in 2025 and subsequently more than doubled traditional-crew labor productivity. Those are measured physical outcomes, not a simulation or a future milestone. The team also adapted the platform to install a cooling pipe in ten days. Emerick previously deployed autonomous construction equipment; Jones built propulsion and space systems and now leads a NASA robotics contract. This is what technical depth looks like when it becomes machinery rather than credentials.

Who wants it now? Utility-scale solar EPCs are a precise niche with an expensive job, and tens of thousands of installed panels indicate more than ceremonial testing. But Cosmic does not disclose a named EPC customer, revenue, repeat contract, fleet utilization, or retention. The $149,521 NASA SBIR and government prizes validate technical seriousness, not solar demand. Nor do the $4 million pre-seed round, Demo Day award, and 7,651 LinkedIn followers. Investors and judges do not have to keep the robot busy on a hot jobsite.

The strongest objection is that Cosmic may have proven a robot without proving a company. Gritt reports contracts covering 2.8 GW and can retrofit rented construction equipment, while Cosmic must manufacture, charge, transport, and service a custom 10,000-pound vehicle. Charge Robotics and Terabase are attacking broader portions of the same workflow with substantially more capital. If EPC adoption depends on hyperscaler introductions or a few large partners, the company acquires both hardware economics and partnership risk at once. Its field-data claim is promising, but no benchmark yet shows that Particle produces an advantage competitors cannot reproduce.

I still lean toward investing. The concrete proof spike matches the strongest pattern in unusually good hard-tech founders: build before permission, cross a numerical threshold, and collect real-world data before describing a platform. The founders also arrived through adjacent work—autonomous construction deployment, robotics research, and space hardware—then accepted the ugly burden of field maintenance and worker-assisted operation. That is a more credible origin than brainstorming “AI for construction.” The working system is strong enough to override the missing clean founder-obstacle anecdote, but not the missing customer economics.

My 36-month forecast is that by August 2029 Cosmic Robotics reaches $8–20 million in annualized deployment revenue primarily from solar EPC work, while adjacent data-center construction remains less than 20% of revenue. I would write a modest angel check, not a conviction-sized one. The verifiable flip condition is straightforward: if deployment records show that the tens of thousands of installations were mostly company-operated demonstrations without a repeat paying EPC customer, I would pass.
