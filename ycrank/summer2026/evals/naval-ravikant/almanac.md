# Almanac -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have acted inside the problem, but the strongest evidence belongs to the product they are leaving. CodeAlmanac accumulated 791 GitHub stars and 71 forks, showing that this team can ship a knowledge tool that developers voluntarily inspect and reuse. Their data-science, machine-learning, and production-software backgrounds support the mechanism. Yet the current product has expanded from a coding-agent wiki into an assistant spanning Slack, Gmail, Calendar, Docs, GitHub, and meeting notes. Specific knowledge transfers; adoption does not.

The decisive question is what remains after a competitor copies the code. Today, very little is visible. Almanac depends on connector-based retrieval and public computer-use capabilities that Glean, Dust, and other well-capitalized vendors can also deploy. A continuously maintained company wiki could eventually become proprietary context, but “could” is not an asset. There is no evidence of exclusive data, improving models, retention, migration costs, or a customer-created network. Glean already has 250 connectors and more than $100 million ARR; Dust reports 300,000 agents across more than 3,000 organizations. Almanac is entering their substrate, not creating a new one.

The bull case is real. A three-person team has shipped a public artifact, identified a painful coordination problem, and packaged an unusually broad assistant with source links, iMessage access, and a managed browser for tools without APIs. The $250-per-user monthly price also tests direct value rather than subsidized attention. If employees repeatedly correct the shared wiki and those corrections improve answers for everyone, the product could turn company context into a compounding asset. But the dossier shows neither paying customers nor that loop in operation. Pricing proves intent to capture value, not captured value.

The reported $15.36 billion knowledge-base market provides room, but it does not establish Almanac’s path through it. The initial buyer is unspecified, the three-day trial implies team-led adoption, and enterprise expansion remains inferred. Broad access to every company system can enlarge the market, but it also enlarges the trust surface: Almanac may create, transmit, publish, or delete connected-account data while acknowledging that actions can be inaccurate. Source links and confirmations mitigate the risk; they do not create distribution or durability.

The unstated patterns do not rescue this investment. Users are not yet contributing permissionless supply, improving matching, or distributing the product to one another. The known exception for a strange, capital-efficient prototype also fails because the current-product behavior has not been demonstrated; LinkedIn followers and prior-product GitHub stars are not repeat usage. I see no evidence of an integrity, morale, or artificial-deadline red flag in the founders. The pass comes from structure, not character.

My 36-month base case is that Almanac pivots back toward developer and repository knowledge, remaining below $3 million ARR, because that is where the team has demonstrated action-earned demand while the broad company-assistant layer remains copyable. I pass now. The single flip condition is verifiable: three cohorts of paying teams retaining for six months while their accumulated corrections measurably improve answer accuracy or switching costs. That would convert a software feature into a compounding company asset.
