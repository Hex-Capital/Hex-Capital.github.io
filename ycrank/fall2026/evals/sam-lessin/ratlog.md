# RATLOG -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

RATLOG built and hot-fired an engine in four months for under $30,000. That is the strongest reason to take the founders seriously. It establishes that this team can turn a small amount of money into hardware and information. My question is whether the next check buys another similarly bounded discovery. The proposed post-money valuation, competing offers, and round-funded experiment are undisclosed. I cannot underwrite the deal from the cost of the first prototype.

Eliminating the turbopump could create a legitimate venture moment: does alternative pressurization simplify the engine while preserving performance and reliability? But physically rotating the engine introduces bearing demands, which the CEO acknowledges. Ninety percent fewer parts does not establish lower lifetime operating cost. RATLOG targets flight-ready propulsion in 2027, yet the dossier supplies neither a qualification threshold nor a budget connecting this round to that result. A development date alone does not tell me what truth my money reveals.

The strongest objection to passing is substantial. These founders have relevant propulsion and avionics experience, an actual hot-fire, and $400 million of signed launch reservations. The reservations show prospective customers taking an action beyond a conversation; I give them limited credit for direct customer access. But payment and cancellation terms are undisclosed. I cannot turn that figure into cash, binding backlog, or proof that RATLOG owns durable distribution. The 70-plus customer discussions could help shape the vehicle; they do not establish that customers will finance its development.

RATLOG fits my unstated willingness to fund difficult physical infrastructure when a team can reorganize its economics. Weise and Kunhart’s Argonaut and Innuendo work, alongside Kelm’s electronics experience, give the engine result context beyond academic titles. The missing bridge is operational launch delivery: the dossier establishes neither a scaled launch operation nor secured infrastructure, with Oman and UAE sites still being explored. Existing reusable launches also mean customers must ultimately buy a measurable advantage over available alternatives.

Delete the next financing from the plan. What survives? The €30,000 grant and inexpensive prototype show resourcefulness, but the dossier provides no margin, burn, runway, or funded route to an operating business before the targeted 2029–2030 orbital launch. I apply the missing-economics default rather than assume insolvency. I also find no evidence of an auction or an equal-founder committee. My known exceptions do not rescue the underwriting: RATLOG has no documented paid conversion and retention, acquisition offer, or alternative security that captures a functioning cash-producing asset.

I pass at present. The technical result deserves attention; the investment still lacks a defined relationship between price, capital, and proof. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an executed customer-funded engine-qualification contract that covers a fully costed 2027 test against specified performance and endurance thresholds. That would show a buyer financing the next technical truth and could establish an engine business before RATLOG must finance an entire launch system.
