# Most Robotic -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important missing fact is not revenue. It is the first obstacle. Most Robotic tells us the founders studied computer science at MIT, researched robotics, and worked collectively at JPL, SpaceX, and AWS. It does not tell us what broke, what they tried, or what they managed to make work anyway. Those credentials make the team plausible. They do not establish resourcefulness. Even the roles and individual contributions are undisclosed, so I cannot determine whether either founder personally bent a system to produce an outcome.

“Research lab for physical AI” is a category, not yet a product. There is no demonstration, benchmark, dataset, robot embodiment, architecture, or deployment. This matters particularly because NVIDIA released open Cosmos 3 models in May 2026, while Skild AI reports an omni-bodied model and approximately $30 million of 2025 revenue, and FieldAI reports deployments across hundreds of industrial environments. The funded competitors are not the decisive objection; small teams often beat large ones. But they do it with a sharp technical proof the large organizations missed. Most Robotic has not disclosed one.

Nor is there a narrow well of users. The dossier names no task, customer segment, pilot, repeated prototype use, pricing, or revenue. The only distribution mechanism is an email address. Consequently, there is no evidence that users are pulling a crude product back, and no route revision can be scored because the founders have disclosed neither an initial route nor an observation that changed it. The absence is not itself evidence of rigidity. It is simply the default score for an untested company.

The strongest case for investing is the team rather than the current noun. Two middle-school friends with MIT computer-science training, robotics research, and exposure to JPL and SpaceX may have years of shared execution and may be hiding a difficult prototype while in stealth. This also resembles an unstated pattern I like: young technical founders entering an unfamiliar industrial field and converting research into numerical proof. But the numerical proof is the essential part of that pattern, and it is absent here. No known exception applies: there is no working product, independently validated result, immediate user conversion, or proprietary substrate strong enough to override the missing operating evidence.

The obvious red flags—press-led launch plans, dependence on a large partner, premature sales hiring, or newly assembled cofounders—are also absent. That keeps this from being a strong pass. But public secrecy cannot earn affirmative credit when I am deciding whether to invest my own money. My 36-month base case is that Most Robotic abandons the general research-lab framing for a narrower robotics developer tool or industrial automation task and remains below $1 million ARR while searching for repeatable deployment. I would pass today. One verifiable condition would flip the decision: an independently checkable case study showing a named deployment blocker, the founders’ shipped workaround, and repeated weekly use by three customers for eight weeks.
