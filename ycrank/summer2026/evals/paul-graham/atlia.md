# Atlia -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Eighty live properties make Atlia an operations-first schlep company with a strong founder pair but a thin public hacker trail. Atlia’s highest signal is the unpleasant work Edmond Niu and Shaan Yadav have accepted: cleaners, access, maintenance, guest screening, and city licensing. Its lowest signal is that their GitHub artifacts have zero stars and their technical biographies still read more like credentials than hacker folklore. Atlia looks less like two programmers selling software and more like two former property managers trying to turn a service business into software from the inside.

**Coinbase** is the high rung for schlep blindness: compliance was not incidental to Coinbase but the work competitors unconsciously avoided. Atlia shares that mechanism because San Diego requires an STRO license, Fort Lauderdale requires registration and inspection, and every one of Atlia’s 80 properties can generate a physical-world failure at midnight. The divergence is important. Coinbase converted regulatory work into infrastructure, while Atlia still relies on human operators and discloses no automation accuracy, retention, or property-level operating margin. Atlia is running toward the schlep, but it has not yet proved the schlep compounds.

**Loopt** is the low rung for hacker genealogy because immature infrastructure was overridden only by Sam Altman’s exceptional force of personality. Atlia cannot claim that exception yet. Niu worked on AI agents at Rilla and S&P Global, and Yadav did GPU work at Apple plus computer-architecture research, but Atlia’s named repositories have zero stars and neither founder has a visible Hacker News or open-source trail. Technical credentials mean they can probably build Atlia; they do not yet show the pre-company artifact that made Dropbox or Stripe obvious to hackers before it was obvious to investors.

Eighty managed properties could turn Atlia’s resourcefulness into a compounding advantage over the next 18 months. Every ugly exception—an absent cleaner, a broken lock, a suspicious guest—teaches Atlia what its software must remember and what its founders must redesign. But Atlia’s dossier contains no cereal-box moment, no rejected application rescued by a prototype, and no strange pre-funding stunt explaining how Niu and Yadav obtained those 80 properties. Atlia has shipped real operations, which is substantial, but animals leave more specific tracks than a deployment count.

Niu managed rentals for roughly a year, and Yadav managed short- and long-term rentals for about eighteen months before Atlia. That makes Atlia unusually organic: the founders encountered the operational mess personally, then built the property memory they wished existed. The score would fall a full tier if those experiences were merely passive ownership while contractors did the work, because Atlia would then be a market observation rather than a tool made for its own users. The disclosed history instead suggests Niu and Yadav knew the pain before naming the opportunity.

Niu and Yadav are longtime friends, former Duke roommates, and prior property managers. That puts Atlia near the **Reddit** pattern on co-founder configuration: people who knew one another before the startup and then built around a shared experience. Atlia lacks Reddit’s stronger hacker-community genealogy, but Atlia avoids the more dangerous red flags—a solo founder, a pair assembled for an application, or MBAs planning to hire engineers after funding. Two founders who have lived and worked together can survive more 2 a.m. maintenance calls than a professionally matched pair.

Atlia will manage 500–800 properties within 36 months and produce roughly $4–7 million in annual management-fee revenue, but Atlia will still employ substantial local operations rather than becoming a high-margin autonomous software company. The strong pair and genuine schlep tolerance should expand Atlia beyond 80 properties; the weak public hacker trail and still-human-supervised architecture make rapid software leverage less likely. Atlia can become a good property manager before it becomes a new kind of software company.

Evolve already serves more than 30,000 owners, Casago/Vacasa exceeds 40,000 properties, and Atlia charges the same 10% starting price while promising more local work. The bear mechanism is brutal: Atlia may automate the easy messages while retaining every expensive exception, leaving Atlia with worse economics than incumbents and none of their density. That objection stings more than commoditization by another AI wrapper. But Atlia already operates 80 properties, passes cleaning costs to guests, and passes repairs and supplies through at cost; those facts beat a merely theoretical operations critique. I would invest, but I would reverse the verdict if a mature market cohort fails to retain at least 85% of properties over twelve months or cannot produce positive contribution margin after local labor.
