# Grip -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: European waste operators face tighter contamination requirements, learning-based manipulation may now handle waste streams that defeat suction systems, and Grip becomes large only if each robotic cell removes contaminants more cheaply and reliably than manual labor. The dossier never quantifies that controlling variable. There is no pick rate, uptime, contaminant-removal rate, labor displacement, cell price, or payback period. Without a threshold such as sub-two-year payback at production-line uptime, the $3.29 billion market estimate and 19.6% CAGR are mostly noise.

The technology case is more interesting than the market proof. Grip combines custom gripper hardware with a learning loop built from successful and failed grasps, aimed initially at wet, irregular organic waste rather than the dry mixed recyclables targeted by many competitors. Jonas Gruetter has relevant field-manipulation experience: his reinforcement-learning excavation research operated a 12-ton excavator in real conditions. But 70% task success in that adjacent setting does not establish viable waste-line economics, and the company discloses no installed system, dataset size, or production performance.

The bull case is that organic-waste contamination is a sufficiently distinct manipulation problem to create a category opening. EU packaging rules began applying in August 2026, operators still rely on manual removal, and a dexterous gripper could reach objects that pneumatic systems miss. If deployments create a compounding corpus of material, facility, and failure data, model improvement could strengthen the hardware rather than commoditize it. That is the strongest part of Grip: a potentially useful action-and-feedback loop tied to physical work.

The opposing evidence is substantial. AMP, Glacier, Recycleye, and EverestLabs have each raised between $24.3 million and $195.85 million; several already disclose deployments, retrofit systems, analytics, or remote monitoring. EverestLabs reports training on more than five billion recyclable objects. Grip’s assertion that grasp data becomes a moat therefore needs a reproduction-cost analysis, not merely the word “data.” A better manipulation model could help Grip, but it could also help better-capitalized competitors with larger installed fleets.

There is no product-market pull yet: no customer, pilot, revenue, repeat usage, or named operator. That absence is normal at pre-seed, but it prevents the market and technology gates from reinforcing each other. Gruetter has strong domain adjacency, yet the dossier documents neither prior founder learning nor the costly personal commitment that would distinguish this from a technically credible research commercialization effort.

I would pass today. The physical-technology exception does not apply because Grip has not shown that its system reconstructs incumbent unit economics; it currently adds promising robotics to an unquantified operating model. My flip condition is one paid facility deployment demonstrating at least 90% target-contaminant removal, production-line uptime, and a customer-verified payback period below 24 months. That result would identify the controlling variable, validate demand, and show that the learning loop produces economic rather than merely technical progress.
