# Baud -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Baud’s GlobalFoundries 12 nm design is a frontier-hardware company whose highest signal is an audacious silicon swing and whose lowest is physical verifiability: Baud has built a machine that ordinary humans cannot yet touch. The 50M-parameter FPGA proof runs at 1,000+ tokens per second, but production silicon remains a year-end promise. I am a professional daydreamer, and multiplier-free training is my kind of dream; the question is whether Baud becomes a physical artifact or remains a beautiful simulation of one.

**SpaceX** made capital intensity the feature: rockets were difficult to finance, difficult to assess, and impossible to fake once they flew. Baud shares that load-bearing mechanism by co-designing the arithmetic representation, ASIC architecture, PyTorch compiler, and distributed stack instead of wrapping somebody else’s model. Baud diverges because SpaceX had hardware you could watch fail on a launchpad, while Baud has FPGA emulation and a GlobalFoundries-compatible design that has not taped out. The 12 nm choice makes Baud magically weird enough for my own money, but first silicon still has to turn the daydream into an object.

The consensus YC AI-agent darling fails because its abstraction can only be inspected through founder testimony; there is nothing to ride, scan, or photograph. That failure mechanism partially appears in Baud: a normal person cannot put Baud’s FPGA cluster in a backpack or run an existing model on it, and the requirement to retrain weights makes the artifact even less accessible. Baud is not vapor—the 50M-parameter demonstration on a U200 FPGA is observable—but Baud sits closer to a laboratory instrument than to **Flock Safety’s** roadside camera. Physical truth arrives only when production silicon runs a customer workload.

Sarang Zambare led ML for 100,000+ shipped Peloton Guide devices, while Eric Taylor completed four tape-outs across NVIDIA, NXP, Arteris, and Enfabrica. That founder mechanism compounds over the next 18 months: Zambare understands the indignities between a model and deployed edge hardware, and Taylor knows where ASIC schedules actually break. Baud therefore did not emerge from a TAM memo, although the dossier does not reveal the autobiographical moment when either founder first felt the arithmetic problem. Baud has the Tiz; tape-out pressure will reveal whether Zambare has the Biz and Rizz required to recruit design partners into a representation that discards their existing weights.

Cerebras raised $2.91B, Groq raised $3.9B, and Etched raised $1.1B, so AI accelerators are not an ignored category. Baud becomes truly non-consensus only if customers accept that the model representation—not merely the chip—must change; without that adoption, Baud is a small entrant in the hottest infrastructure trade on Earth. The tier flips upward if two named frontier-model developers reserve production capacity after training their own models on Baud’s FPGA service. Until then, Baud’s multiplier-free forward and backward passes are magically weird, while Baud’s round being underpriced is unproven.

**HQ Trivia** taught me that tactile product love can temporarily override bad founder structure, but it also taught me never to romanticize an unresolved tiebreaker. Baud names Zambare as CEO and Taylor as Chief Hardware Architect, which is better than shared titles, yet Baud discloses neither the founders’ working history nor authority, equity, vesting, or conflict protocol. The titles suggest a hierarchy; they do not prove one. With a tape-out deadline and two deeply specialized founders, ambiguity over who decides between model compatibility and silicon purity can become a very expensive argument.

Baud’s first production ASIC will tape out and, by July 2029, Baud will reach $3M–$8M in annualized revenue from fine-tuning and inference capacity rather than frontier pretraining. Baud’s high-hardware, low-verifiability signature produces that forecast: working silicon will attract narrow workloads first, while retraining requirements and the gap between a 50M-parameter proof and frontier models will keep full pretraining adoption slow. I expect Baud to find the sharp, ugly wedge before it earns the grand platform claim. I’m never playing the game I seem to be playing; the valuable product may be the representation and compiler hiding inside the chip company.

Cerebras’s reported $510M of 2025 revenue is the bear case with teeth: customers may prefer a funded, production-proven accelerator that runs conventional models, leaving Baud stranded between a clever arithmetic paper and an expensive tape-out. The unstated-pattern warning also stings—Baud’s founders have excellent credentials, but no disclosed lived origin, no public co-founder history, no named design partner, and no evidence that investors rejected Baud for being too strange. Baud still earns the check because four prior tape-outs, 100,000+ deployed Peloton devices, a working FPGA cluster, and an integrated compiler-to-silicon system are substance rather than pedigree theater. I flip to Pass if first silicon fails to demonstrate at least a 10× energy-efficiency advantage on a customer-owned model of one billion or more parameters within twelve months of tape-out.
