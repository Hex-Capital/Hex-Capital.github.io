# ClaimKit -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

What does ClaimKit know about R&D claims that Boast, Neo.Tax, and Onshore believe is false? The dossier establishes a useful workflow: connect engineering and accounting records, draft substantiation, and have specialists lodge claims. It does not establish a disputed proposition. Automating a consultant’s paperwork may create substantial customer value, but competitors already combine evidence automation with tax expertise. I give the secret its absent-evidence default; I score the demonstrated competitive overlap more harshly. These are separate judgments.

The distinction matters because ClaimKit’s strongest early evidence concerns selling the service. Eighty-plus reported customers within six months is meaningful at this stage. Edexia’s COO describes switching from accountants mid-project and completing the engagement faster and more cheaply. Huang’s Airwallex educational event supplies a concrete route to finance buyers. Together, these make the customer problem credible. They do not establish repeatable acquisition economics: the dossier does not connect the customer count to that event, disclose conversion rates, or report sales cycles. Meanwhile, Neo.Tax’s ONESOURCE distribution gives an existing competitor access through established tax-software relationships.

The strongest bull case is that Huang understands the work well enough to replace the traditional firm rather than merely sell it software. Her KPMG R&D experience, Mahon’s engineering background, and the tax specialists make that credible. Retiring the Grants module on April 22, 2026, also concentrates the business around one revenue engine. This fits my less-public pattern of backing finance and operational businesses; physical technology is not a prerequisite. But those credentials do not establish the founders’ shared working history, full-time status, or economic exposure. I retain the founding-structure default without inventing either alignment or a red flag.

ClaimKit charges an upfront software fee plus a minimum-or-percentage service fee at lodgement. That is a coherent business model, but the dossier supplies neither actual prices nor expert-review costs from which to calculate scalable profits. Historical claim records could eventually preserve value; today, their exclusivity and switching costs remain hypotheses. Australian and US coverage defines geography, not a demonstrated monopoly. The reported 100% claim success rate has no denominator or measurement period, and ClaimKit itself acknowledges that refunds remain reviewable. Neither that claim nor the team’s historical $100 million in credits establishes current-product economics.

I pass. The decisive issue is affirmative competitive overlap without an observable mechanism for retaining the value ClaimKit creates. I find no documented part-time-founder, ownership, or enforcement red flag; ordinary substantiation exposure does not justify inventing one. My exceptions for independently demonstrated technical breakthroughs or unusually revealing customer behavior do not rescue this case: the customer evidence validates demand, but does not remove the monopoly risk. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independent comparison showing a tenfold reduction in expert-review hours at comparable substantiation quality, attributable to proprietary evidence mappings unavailable to competitors, within a defined customer segment. That would establish something beyond a faster tax engagement.
