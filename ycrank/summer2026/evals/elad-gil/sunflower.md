# Sunflower -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: AI makes continuous behavioral support inexpensive, people with addiction already seek help through consumer apps, and the controlling variable is whether Sunflower can convert that engagement into recurring clinical revenue without acquiring each patient through traditional healthcare channels. The evidence is unusually strong for the first half of that proposition: 500,000 users, 100,000 MAU, 10,000 paying subscribers, and 37,500 treatment requests. What remains unproven is the conversion threshold. The dossier does not show how many eligible users begin treatment, remain in care, or generate reimbursed revenue.

Product-market pull is the clearest signal. Sunflower reached $1 million ARR in ten months, accumulated 21.5 million tracked sober days and seven million AI-sponsor messages, and maintains ratings of 4.9 on iOS and 4.7 on Android across more than 11,000 combined reviews. This is not a waitlist attached to a demo. Users are returning, recording sensitive behavior, paying, and asking for treatment. The company also appears to have found a repeatable consumer distribution wedge: one creator relationship reportedly produces 50,000–100,000 monthly link clicks.

The technology-driven economic change is less settled. A 24/7 AI sponsor can serve a user at far lower marginal cost than $125-per-hour therapy, while the clinic offers naltrexone for $89 monthly rather than the cited $350 quarterly. But those prices do not establish contribution margins, clinical labor requirements, reimbursement economics, or improved outcomes. The 2025 AI-care studies establish a category-level why-now; they do not validate Sunflower’s intervention. Its own 80% relapse-prevention figure comes from a company survey of day-60 respondents, a selection-biased cohort rather than a clinical endpoint.

The app-to-clinic loop is more durable than a standalone chatbot. Daily tracking creates longitudinal state, the product can escalate users into medication and human care, and licenses plus Medicaid approvals in Pennsylvania and Michigan introduce operational depth. Better models should improve check-ins and triage while Sunflower retains the patient relationship, permissions, treatment history, and care pathway. The bear case still stings: the founder says consumer apps can be cloned rapidly, Reframe and Monument overlap with the product, and Pelago has already raised $151 million around an employer and health-plan channel. Seven million messages are not a moat unless Sunflower can quantify what a competitor would spend, and what permissions it would lack, to reproduce their useful information.

Koby Conrad adds unusually relevant founder evidence. He has personal recovery experience, previously built the CBT app Quirk, led growth at Rupa Health, and already co-founded a YC company. This looks like accumulated learning around both the problem and distribution, not an employer credential retrofitted to a trend. The counterweight is capital discipline: Sunflower already has 15 employees and had raised $6.5 million at a reported $60 million post-money valuation by December 2025. The next dollars should scale a demonstrated app-to-care conversion channel, not fund broad clinical expansion before its unit economics are visible.

My 36-month base case is $20–30 million ARR, with most incremental revenue coming from medication, clinical care, or payer contracts rather than app subscriptions. I would invest because the demand signal is already top-quartile and the first product plausibly creates reusable distribution into a much larger treatment workflow. I would flip to pass if Pennsylvania and Michigan cohort data show that fewer than 2% of qualified treatment requesters begin paid clinic care within 90 days; that result would mean Sunflower has built a popular sobriety app, not the economically integrated treatment platform required to justify the operational complexity and valuation.
