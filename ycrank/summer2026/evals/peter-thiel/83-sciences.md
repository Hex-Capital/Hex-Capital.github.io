# 83 Sciences -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The contested truth is precise: a laboratory’s discarded and failed experiments contain more commercially useful information than the published literature, and structuring that history can produce materials discoveries faster than generation-first AI. The clean falsification test is whether Dalton can repeatedly propose conditions that outperform experienced researchers’ baselines on held-out experiments. 83 Sciences has crossed the boundary from slogan to experiment: it reports one novel material and synthesis conditions exceeding an anonymous battery lab’s capacity plateau, with a manuscript produced in under two months. But the result remains company-reported, the customer unnamed, and the paper unpublished. This is an intriguing secret, not yet a verified one.

The more important ambiguity is whether each partner’s private corpus creates a monopoly or merely a better consulting input. Discovery contracts channel unpublished notebooks, voice notes, instrument outputs, and failed runs through Dalton, so every engagement could deepen proprietary data. Yet no public terms establish exclusivity, cross-partner reuse, retention, or ownership of foreground IP. If each laboratory’s data must remain isolated, 83 Sciences accumulates implementations rather than a compounding corpus. Meanwhile, Citrine and MaterialsZone already organize industrial R&D data, while open MatterGen and OMat24 reduce the scarcity of the model layer. The company may create value without capturing it.

The founding structure is the strongest evidence. Ian Naccarella combines chemical engineering with battery-materials commercialization at Sila; Eric Riesel brings an MIT chemistry PhD and work in experimental analysis and generative chemical models; Yankang Yang previously led enterprise AI across a 30,000-person organization. Ian and Yankang worked at BCG, and the founders say they collaborated for years on superconductors and cooling textiles. This is not a team assembled around a fashionable YC application. The three-person company also keeps the core scientific and software functions inside the founding group, although the dossier does not establish founder ownership, compensation, or secondary liquidity.

The bull case is stronger than the current score: “dozens” of partnerships in progress, a completed battery result, and grants of up to $50,000 could seed a proprietary experimental-data network before better-capitalized competitors recognize that failed runs are the valuable asset. This also fits the recent movement toward the complete AI production stack rather than another standalone model. A verified, independently reproduced material breakthrough would be the sort of zero-to-one technical event that can override initially uncertain monopoly economics. Today, however, “in progress” is not deployed demand, an anonymous case study is not independent validation, and hiring a Head of Sales before disclosing one paying customer suggests that distribution remains an organizational intention.

The economics are still indefinite. The cited materials-informatics market reaches only $410.4 million by 2030, and the inferred model mixes bespoke discovery contracts with unspecified platform access. There is no price, gross margin, sales cycle, implementation cost, or evidence that one engagement lowers the cost of acquiring the next. In-person onboarding and subsidized grants may be rational for obtaining scarce datasets, but they can also conceal services-heavy customer economics. My 36-month base case is that 83 Sciences reaches roughly $1–3 million in annualized contract revenue but remains a laboratory-services business with Dalton as internal tooling, rather than establishing a scalable software monopoly.

I would investigate, not invest yet. The decisive flip condition is one named industrial partner publishing an independently reproduced Dalton-generated result while signing a paid, renewable contract that permits anonymized cross-partner learning. That single event would verify the secret, clarify data rights, and show that customer work compounds into proprietary technology. Without it, the company is caught between two bad identities: commodity AI software and non-scaling scientific consulting. Secret or slogan; accumulating monopoly or repeated project work.
