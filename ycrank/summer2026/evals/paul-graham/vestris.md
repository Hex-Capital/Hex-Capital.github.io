# Vestris -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when Vestris first broke. Valliani and Tang encountered an ugly process while working at the same title desk, but the dossier does not name a startup obstacle, what they did in response, or how quickly the situation changed. Building a product proves technical initiative. It does not by itself prove the more important thing: that these founders become unusually effective when their first plan fails. I cannot infer resourcefulness from a polished description of the result.

The origin is much stronger. Both founders processed real closing files before building Vestris, and their product attacks precisely the work they would have watched consume 8–10 hours: reading emails, comparing documents, visiting county portals, calculating prorations, and reconciling disclosures ([Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/vestris), August 2026). This is not an “AI for real estate” idea assembled from a market map. It grew out of direct contact with a narrow, unpleasant job. County-specific rules, human approvals, fraud exposure, and legal accuracy are all schleps, but here the schleps are the opportunity.

There is also a real artifact. A title shop is running Vestris alongside its existing process, and the company reports reducing processing time from 8–10 hours to one hour per file while doubling capacity ([Launch YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/SSz-vestris-al-native-title-processing-service), August 2026). That is substantially better than a diagram or waitlist. But it remains a company-reported result from one unnamed pilot, without an independently measured error rate or evidence that the system survives difficult exceptions. In title work, speed without accuracy is not merely incomplete; it can be negative value.

The pilot identifies a plausible narrow well: small and midsize title agencies whose processors repeatedly perform the same closing work. Yet the dossier gives no revenue, renewal, repeated-use count, referral, or second agency. Settling millions of dollars per month describes the customer’s transaction volume, not Vestris’s traction. The closest known exception would be a working prototype strong enough to override missing evidence elsewhere, but this proof has not reached that level. The human-approval loop is sensible; it also means the claimed capacity gain must be verified across enough live files to expose rare errors.

The strongest bull case is that reviewed outcomes become a jurisdiction-specific data substrate while the founders manually learn each county. That would match the pattern of technical teams assembling difficult infrastructure before competitors appreciate its value. The bear case stings more today: Qualia already owns ResWare, Flueid has underwriter-approved decisioning, and Vestris has disclosed neither ownership nor exclusivity for the decades of approved and rejected titles on which it says Schedule B was trained. Expanding beyond Washington, D.C., and Montgomery County could turn into slow configuration work, while incumbents inherit similar automation through their existing integrations and underwriting relationships.

My 36-month base case is $3–7 million ARR concentrated in fewer than 20 jurisdictions: a useful vertical workflow product, but not yet evidence of a national closing platform. I am neutral now because the founders have found an excellent schlep and shipped credible software, but have not shown what they do when either the software or the sales route resists them. I would flip to Invest if three independent title agencies each process at least 100 live files through Vestris and then renew as paying customers.
