# SE3 Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

SE3 Labs has put robots through 100 evaluation episodes. I want to know what happened when the founders themselves got stuck. The [October 30 report](https://se3labs.ai/blog/dream-eval-1/) supplies recordings, telemetry, and scoring across ten tasks. That is substantial evidence of building. But a robot failing a task is not evidence of a founder overcoming an obstacle. The dossier identifies neither the founders nor a specific blocker, workaround, and elapsed time. I would give the operating system considerable credit without inventing the founder story behind it.

The technical work is the strongest reason to take SE3 seriously. Its September study reports six completed placements with code-as-policy versus eighteen with agentic control, from twenty attempts each. Together with the downloadable October recordings, this establishes a functioning physical evaluation operation. It fits my unstated preference for teams that produce numerical proof before asking investors to believe a category. The boundary matters: these experiments demonstrate measurement and a control-method comparison. They do not yet demonstrate that SE3’s failure-directed data collection makes a customer’s policy better. The Dream report leaves fine-tuning and comparative retesting for subsequent work.

Who wants another evaluation badly enough to come back? Robot-policy developers are a plausible narrow audience, but the dossier names no customer who repeatedly uses or pays for SE3. The hundred episodes are technical output, not a hundred instances of demand. [Contract-specific fees](https://se3labs.ai/terms/) establish how the company could charge; they establish no realized revenue. This is missing evidence at pre-seed, not evidence that users rejected the product. But I cannot substitute the team’s research activity for a user pulling the product out of its hands.

The strongest case against passing is that operating the robot stations is precisely the ugly work that creates the opportunity. A three-person team already has physical tasks, Python documentation, and inspectable results. It could manually serve a few model developers and discover the indispensable workflow through those engagements. Yet Poke & Wiggle already offers overlapping physical evaluation, and SE3’s customer-data permissions constrain reuse. The valuable accumulation might therefore be operating knowledge rather than an exclusive training corpus. That can support a company, but neither repeat demand nor improving delivery economics is established here. Nor does the dossier document a founder’s personal collision with this problem or a product revision prompted by contrary evidence; those dimensions remain at their missing-evidence defaults.

I would pass on the present evidence. SE3 fails my founder-resourcefulness gate because the relevant history is absent. I checked the exceptions: a working prototype can override uncertain cofounder history, extraordinary user pull can override conventional economics, and validated technical results can outweigh concerns about founder background. SE3 has a working evaluation system, but the evidence does not establish an override strong enough to replace the missing founder prior. Equally, unknown cofounder history is not proof of a hastily assembled team, and nothing here establishes dependence on a large partner, ceremonial launch, or premature delegation. My one flip condition is a documented customer engagement in which the founders overcome a named deployment blocker within weeks and the customer buys a repeat evaluation after retesting demonstrates policy improvement. That would connect the apparatus to both resourcefulness and need.
