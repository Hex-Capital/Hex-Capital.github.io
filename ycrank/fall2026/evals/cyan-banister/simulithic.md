# Simulithic -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine software shipping continuously while synthetic users keep trying to break it. The interesting question is who gets to say, “This change is safe.” Simulithic’s October 2 verification-engineering post identifies a plausible transition: coding agents increase change volume, manually maintained tests become the constraint, and verification must discover journeys rather than wait for someone to specify them. That is a credible glimpse of what comes next. The remaining distance is substantial: discovering a journey, exercising its consequential steps, and reliably authorizing a release are three different accomplishments.

The little detail I keep coming back to is that the explorer never submits forms. That restriction protects customers, but checkout, account creation, and other state-changing actions are precisely where confidence becomes valuable. Repository mapping offers another route to discovering those paths; it does not establish that they work. Dedicated test accounts and staging show that the founders recognize operational complexity. Still, Simulithic must demonstrate that its safety boundaries leave enough useful coverage for engineers to surrender some of their checking. Otherwise, it adds another stream of findings to the person already responsible for the release.

Ryan Ning and Satyam Singh give me reasons to take that challenge seriously. They previously built GitWrap together and report reaching more than 100,000 developers; that belongs to their record of building together, not Simulithic’s customer count. Ning’s AI-agent research and Singh’s MLH 2026 Top 50 recognition add substance beyond employer names. Their reported encounters with verification problems at prior jobs also fit my preference for repairing an operating wound. What I cannot establish is a sustained, pre-company obsession with this particular problem. The strongest case against withholding a check is that this pair already knows how to build something developers adopt, and automatic discovery could eliminate the test-authoring chore entirely. That possibility deserves a product session.

The present economic mechanism is understandable: engineers spend time maintaining Playwright scripts and clicking through releases, and Simulithic proposes to return that time. There is no quantified maintenance burden or artificial gatekeeper here, so I would not inflate ordinary workflow relief into liberation from an institutional rent. Likewise, the October 15 claim of deployments at “several companies” is encouraging but supplies no repeat-use behavior. Plans allowing 2,000, 8,000, or 25,000 monthly sessions describe what customers can buy, not what they consume or pay. QA Wolf already advertises autonomous exploration and coverage mapping. Simulithic therefore needs evidence that discovery changes release behavior, rather than merely producing a more convenient test inventory.

My preference for unglamorous workflow repair is activated; my pattern of backing concealed enabling infrastructure is not yet established. Customer journey maps could become valuable, but the dossier demonstrates neither a scarce asset nor customer dependence on one. My exceptions for pre-revenue companies also do not resolve this: those require unusually strong founder obsession and technical validation to carry the missing commercial evidence. Here, those signals are promising rather than decisive. The CEO and CTO roles are explicit, and nothing establishes a governance or severe privacy violation; authenticated access nevertheless makes consent and data custody relevant before a check. I am neutral today. One verifiable result would change my decision: an independently confirmed eight-week customer deployment in which engineers repeatedly release without their former manual checks because Simulithic catches consequential, state-changing regressions safely. That would show the product has earned responsibility.
