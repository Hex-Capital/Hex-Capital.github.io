# Caladh -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A port lets authorized autonomous vehicles work while denying hostile ones access to its underwater infrastructure. Caladh’s buoys and tow-fish could belong in that world. But getting there requires selective denial that works in busy water, followed by deployment that leaves legitimate operations intact. Eclipse and Cloak describe the destination; the dossier supplies no demonstrated artifact showing those transitions have begun. I can imagine this future quite clearly. Imagination is my contribution, not evidence Caladh has earned.

Kristen Kita is the reason I keep reading. Her 2022 MIT–WHOI research demonstrated passive tracking of an underwater vehicle amid passing boats. That is a dated, pre-company encounter with the actual difficulty here: the ocean does not become quiet because your demonstration needs it to. Her Principal Engineer and Principal Investigator background at STR adds relevant institutional experience. This is substantially more informative than an impressive degree alone. It establishes technical commitment before Caladh, although recruiting ability and command of the commercial levers remain unshown.

The important gap lies between hearing a vehicle and making its mission fail. Caladh advertises denial of navigation, communications, and sensing, plus underwater concealment. The cited research demonstrates tracking. Those are different accomplishments. Sonardyne already advertises UUV detection at 1,200 metres and command-system integration; a buyer therefore has an existing detection supplier to extend. Caladh needs to demonstrate what its intervention accomplishes beyond that installed capability. The adjacent market’s estimated $1.98 billion size in 2026 does not answer that question or establish the economics of Caladh’s underwater segment.

My strongest objection to passing is that this is exactly where an early physical-systems investor should tolerate sparse commercial evidence. A founder has spent years inside a difficult technical domain, and a useful capability might emerge before procurement produces revenue. **CosmicBrain AI** is a relevant exception: both bets concern specialized physical enablement before substantial commercial adoption. The material difference is that its functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept connected founder depth to execution. Caladh’s briefing and pilot-request forms establish availability for a conversation, not completed customer behavior. Missing revenue would not stop me; the absent bridge from expertise to demonstrated denial does.

Caladh also resembles my pattern of backing concealed infrastructure beneath a visible wave: maritime autonomy needs protection. But a necessary function becomes an investable enabling layer only when the company controls something that delivers it. No controlled technical bottleneck or deployment-derived asset is established here. Nor does the dossier identify a particular fee, restriction, or institutional rent that Caladh removes for an individual operator. It addresses a serious security threat, which is a different mechanism. There is no documented governance or severe privacy red flag to justify an active-mismatch verdict; there is also no affirmative evidence that overrides these gaps.

I pass with my own money today. My one verifiable flip condition is a customer-observed sea trial showing Eclipse repeatedly denies a representative unmanned vehicle’s mission amid shipping traffic while authorized operations continue, with the protocol and results available for review. That would connect Kita’s demonstrated understanding of the water to the capability Caladh wants buyers to purchase. Until then, the strongest evidence belongs to the founder’s preparation.
