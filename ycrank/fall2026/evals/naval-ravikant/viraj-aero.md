# Viraj Aero -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Viraj Aero’s strongest asset is knowledge acquired in propulsion engineering and power development. Joseph Risson worked on propulsion at Ascendance and Beyond Aero; Mathilde Bouilloux developed six power projects totaling more than 120 MW. Those are complementary encounters with reality: making machinery work and getting infrastructure built. The reported full-scale aircraft-prototype integration gives their experience a present artifact. I give the founders substantial credit for specific knowledge, without treating the reported Safran relationship as proof of commercial acceptance.

Copy the software and the condenser still has to work. That makes Viraj materially more interesting to me than another application built on rented intelligence. But physical difficulty does not automatically create an exclusive asset. Steam injection has decades of prior use. The company reports a condenser patent, yet the dossier establishes neither its claim scope nor independently measured operating performance. The decisive question is whether competitors must reproduce years of accumulated engineering or can choose another water-recovery architecture. Today, there is evidence of hardware development and incomplete evidence of durability.

The retrofit offering creates a credible expansion path. Viraj can offer existing turbine-genset manufacturers a component before asking data-center developers to adopt an entirely new generating system. Water recovery and steam injection are the shared capability connecting that opportunity to the original aviation product. The IEA’s estimate that grid constraints could delay approximately 20% of planned data-center capacity supplies independent evidence of urgency. The broader $9.7 billion generator market supplies context, not Viraj’s addressable revenue. Aviation interest does not establish that this particular solution wins stationary-power procurement.

The strongest case for investing now is that these founders have already assembled the unusual combination needed to cross that boundary. Their €4.5 million financing, announced in August 2026, funds further engineering; €13 million of aviation LOIs indicates purchase interest. My willingness to back an unusual prototype before observable network effects or revenue is relevant here. But that exception requires unusually persuasive product evidence. GENA’s advertised 45% electrical efficiency and 2 ppm NOx remain development specifications. The unexplained 30% versus 40% power-uplift claims need comparable baselines; they are a diligence question, not evidence of dishonesty. Funding and LOIs cannot resolve the engineering uncertainty.

Viraj fits my recent pattern of investing around physical infrastructure bottlenecks. It does little for my preference for customers creating value for other customers: no contribution, referral, or shared operating-data loop is demonstrated. Nor is direct value capture established; equipment sales are inferred, and the aviation LOIs are nonbinding purchase interest rather than revenue. No documented integrity, coercive fundraising, or founder-behavior red flag supplies a veto. My hesitation concerns what the company has proved, rather than its stage.

I remain **Neutral**. Viraj could become a valuable turbine subsystem supplier before becoming a complete engine manufacturer, but I would not write a personal check on the current evidence. One verifiable result would change that decision: an independently monitored, paid retrofit deployment showing sustained net efficiency improvement after condenser and cooling loads, with measured water balance under representative hot-site conditions. That would connect the founders’ specific knowledge to a working economic advantage.
