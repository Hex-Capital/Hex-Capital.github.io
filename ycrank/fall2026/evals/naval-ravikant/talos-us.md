# Talos -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Kohli’s work on predictive maintenance at Constellation is the most useful fact in this dossier. He has encountered the problem inside an operator. Talos also shows something built: a condensate-pump demonstration mapping 304 magnetic readings. That combination earns founder credit beyond academic credentials. Hwang’s waveform research supplies relevant technical capability, but processing ICU data does not establish transformer diagnosis. I see an informed team with an artifact, without enough operating evidence to call their specific knowledge exceptional.

My decisive question is what Talos owns after another vendor reproduces its software and purchases comparable sensors. External vibration, magnetic-field, temperature, and humidity measurements could become a valuable diagnostic system. The dossier does not establish proprietary sensor performance, exclusive failure records, or a model advantage that compounds with deployment. Oktogrid already offers magnetic-adhesion sensing. Hardware gives Talos something physical to install; its existence alone does not establish a durable advantage.

The strongest case against passing is that this team could acquire the scarce asset through deployment. Kohli knows the operating context, the prototype shows spatial measurement, and transformer replacements taking years make earlier fault detection economically consequential. A small number of successful installations could produce unusually valuable learning. That fits my recent preference for physical infrastructure bottlenecks. But the bridge remains unverified: a pump scan does not demonstrate that external measurements reliably distinguish transformer faults with useful warning time. Talos has shown measurement; the investment depends on diagnosis.

The market is real. The cited estimate puts transformer monitoring at $3.11 billion in 2026, and established diagnostic vendors confirm existing buyers. Yet the dossier establishes no concrete expansion path into adjacent equipment markets. Nor does it show customers creating utility or distribution for one another. Failure histories could eventually improve a shared model, but neither access rights nor cross-customer improvement is demonstrated. Sensor sales and recurring monitoring are plausible economics; without a disclosed payer, price, or transaction, they remain hypotheses.

I pass at 34. There is no documented integrity problem or artificial financing pressure driving that decision. I considered the exceptions for an exceptional founder, a compelling early prototype, or a forming network that justifies postponing monetization. Talos has a credible beginning, but none is established strongly enough to override the missing compounding mechanism. My one verifiable reopening condition is a paid, utility-verified deployment showing that failure data Talos controls improves diagnosis at a second site without proportional custom engineering. That would connect the founders’ learning to an asset the company can own and sell repeatedly.
