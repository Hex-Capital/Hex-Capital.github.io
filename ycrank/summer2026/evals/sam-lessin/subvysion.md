# SubVysion -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

SubVysion’s first customer also became an investor after a founder cold call, making SubVysion a venture-shaped field-service regatta with no owned-distribution engine. That is the analytical signature: capital structure is SubVysion’s strongest dimension because project-based contracts can finance successive mapping tacks before software scale; founder-as-brand is the weakest because Maxwell Stone’s 1,129 LinkedIn followers and Wenson Tang’s 799 are résumés, not a community on the cap table. Software is no longer investible; a rover collecting paid field data might be—quick walk-back—but only if the service revenue keeps SubVysion default-alive while the data asset compounds.

Teamshares is the high rung for capital structure because operating cash flow, an unusual ownership model, and a hard-to-value real-world asset made the structure itself the opportunity. SubVysion shares the load-bearing mechanism: contracts on U.S. job sites, founder-led selling, and an inferred project-based model let SubVysion run a Wizard-of-Oz service before pretending autonomous mapping is finished. SubVysion diverges because no revenue, contract value, gross margin, equipment cost, or non-standard financing instrument is disclosed; an autonomous rover plus field crews and workflow liability could turn every new deployment into working-capital drainage. The regatta can work, but I cannot yet count its tacks.

Fin is the low rung for owned community because a deeply manual service without proprietary audience attachment remained operationally expensive and ultimately pivoted to workplace analytics. SubVysion shows the same failure mechanism in distribution: SubVysion’s first contract came from cold calling, neither founder has a demonstrated audience beyond roughly 1,900 combined LinkedIn followers, and no newsletter, channel, trade community, or organic contractor network appears in the dossier. The founder-as-brand red flag is active, although less fatal in construction than in consumer: SubVysion can sell through contractor relationships, but every relationship currently appears to be manufactured one call and one job site at a time.

Exodigo’s more than $200 million raised makes SubVysion’s missing round terms consequential over the next 18 months. There is no evidence on SubVysion’s valuation, round size, investor competition, tier-one passes, or instrument—applying the absent-evidence default—so I cannot call SubVysion mispriced merely because underground mapping is unsexy. If live deployments produce credible accuracy data, Exodigo’s capitalization can make SubVysion legible and heat the next round; if SubVysion accepts hot-YC pricing before that proof, the SAFE captures the risk while selling away the value-seed upside. Robinhood’s early-entry exception cannot apply when SubVysion’s entry price is unknown, and Pinterest’s network-sourcing exception has no operator-friend evidence here.

Maxwell Stone left a Roboflow role after building inference software deployed in more than 10,000 pharmacies, while Wenson Tang built a $500 LiDAR search-and-rescue drone; SubVysion therefore has more asymmetric conviction than a sanitized “AI for construction” deck. SubVysion also has the missionary signals of quitting, dropping out, cold-calling contractors, and working on live sites, but Stone and Tang only joined forces in January 2026 and the dossier contains no multi-year category obsession. The counterfactual that flips this dimension into the top tier is simple: show that either founder spent three years documenting subsurface-mapping failures, publishing field results, or repeatedly building for utility contractors before YC. Without that pre-pitch history, SubVysion looks unusually committed, not yet inevitably committed.

Sublime Security is the useful single-anchor parallel: an unsexy technical workflow where execution and customer pain matter more than pitch-deck glamour. SubVysion likewise integrates sensor capture, GPR processing, georeferenced reconstruction, quality control, and mobile AR rather than wrapping a foundation model, placing SubVysion near my Wizard-of-Oz and execution-machine sleeve. The unstated-pattern check is still mixed: SubVysion is neither crypto, DTC, prescription rail, creator infrastructure, nor a holdco, and the $1.45 billion mapping market is smaller than a canonical venture market. SubVysion earns sleeve credit for service-with-software and “rizz and tizz,” not because construction AR is already a category I am structurally committed to.

Contracts on U.S. job sites lead me to a falsifiable forecast: by July 2029, SubVysion will produce $2 million to $4 million in annualized revenue primarily as a tech-enabled subsurface-mapping service, not as a scaled autonomous-rover software platform. SubVysion’s mechanism is paid fieldwork funding better sensor models and a proprietary signature library while cold outbound replaces community distribution; that can build a sound business, but it compounds people, equipment, insurance, and deployment complexity alongside data. The likely outcome is a valuable specialist contractor with software leverage—Type 2 fun—not yet the default underground-data layer.

The estimated $30 billion in annual U.S. utility-strike damage is the strongest case that my forecast is too small: SubVysion has technically relevant founders, live contracts, an investor-customer, and an integrated workflow against a lethal problem. The Ro exception stings here—capital intensity, regulation, and operational schleps can become the moat—so SubVysion could use liability-grade validation and nationwide deployments to become infrastructure rather than a mapping contractor. SubVysion has not earned that exception yet: “100% visibility” is unsupported, insurance and accuracy standards are undisclosed, Exodigo already claims 100-plus customers and 86.1 million square feet mapped, and no proprietary dataset scale is reported. One verifiable condition would flip me to Invest: SubVysion reaching $1 million ARR at greater than 60% gross margin from repeat contractor customers.
