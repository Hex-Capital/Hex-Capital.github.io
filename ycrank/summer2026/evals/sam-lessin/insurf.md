# Insurf -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I begin with three blank cells: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and what this check specifically buys. Financing silence does not prove Insurf is being auctioned, but it also gives me no reason to believe I am purchasing cheap, non-consensus uncertainty. More importantly, the dossier never names the finite experiment funded by the round. “Build, integrate, and sell” is not a venture moment. A real version would be: process a defined number of cases for one specialty by a fixed date and prove that physician-attested appeals recover enough cash to change practice economics.

The strongest asset is the workflow record Inveto is already constructing. Insurf preserves payer-policy snapshots, submitted language, physician attestations, decisions, and posted-payment evidence. Selected specialty practices are piloting the product, so this is more than an academic model. If those records accumulate across resolved cases, the company could own a payer-specific outcome dataset that neither a foundation-model vendor nor a generic revenue-cycle system can easily reproduce. But “could accumulate” is doing the work: case volume, paid conversion, repeat usage, and outcomes are all undisclosed. LinkedIn reactions are not distribution, and a pilot without observed purchasing or retention is weak evidence that the workflow already causes economic action.

The bull case is unusually legible. Providers reportedly spend 13 staff-hours per week on roughly 40 prior-authorizations; CMS decision-time rules and the January 2027 FHIR mandate create a forced workflow transition; and Insurf joins authorization, denial appeals, attestation, submission, and payment reporting rather than selling another drafting box. Human review also contains the obvious AI failure mode. If the product reliably connects an appeal to cash collected, it can reduce progress to one controllable metric: net recovered dollars per case.

The problem is that Insurf has not yet shown that base business. There is no revenue, pricing, gross margin, burn, runway, or evidence that a software-plus-service model survives clinical review and exception handling without another round. Production PHI remains disabled pending BAAs and launch gates, SOC 2 is incomplete, and the browser workflow depends on CoverMyMeds and athenaOne. Meanwhile, Silna reports 50,000 patients served and Cohere processes more than 12 million authorization requests annually. The AI is therefore not the scarce asset; proprietary resolved-case data and profitable workflow ownership would be, and neither is proven yet.

The founders fit the two halves intellectually but have not shipped on both sides operationally. Chang Lu brings medical training and cancer-modeling research; Bryan Chung brings applied mathematics, computer science, and two IEEE publications. Those are credible inputs, not receipts from running a specialty practice, administering payer decisions, deploying healthcare software, or selling into hospital revenue cycles. The translation layer may be real, but today it is inferred from education rather than demonstrated access.

I am passing. The one verifiable condition that flips me is a published paid-pilot cohort showing at least 200 adjudicated cases for a named specialty, repeat use over six months, positive contribution margin, and a measurable increase in collected revenue. That single result would establish customer action, begin validating the data asset, and show that Insurf is a functioning cash-recovery business with an AI option—not an AI workflow waiting for its next financing.
