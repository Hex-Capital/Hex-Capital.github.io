# Open Actuation -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Open Actuation could remove a real bottleneck: robots and drones designed around whatever motor happens to be available. Purpose-built actuation could improve the machine rather than merely its procurement. But the dossier does not quantify how much energy, engineering expense, or machine cost disappears if this works. The $18.62 billion robotics-actuator market establishes a substantial component category. It does not establish an orders-of-magnitude consequence. I cannot purchase that consequence by assuming it.

The strongest bull case is that Open Actuation is building manufacturing infrastructure disguised as a component supplier. Proprietary software, winding equipment, machining, balancing, and testing could turn custom motor production into a repeatable process. Its reported builds for drone OEMs and July 2026 customer samples give that possibility more substance than a configurator alone. This fits my preference for scarce technical workflows becoming production platforms. The decisive uncertainty is whether each new geometry requires another round of expensive engineering labor. Four-week delivery does not answer it: Atlas Motion also advertises custom prototypes within weeks. There is no measured discontinuity against that alternative, and CubeMars resale supplies no evidence of one.

Jon Facer has relevant context from Gecko Robotics and five years at 1Factory as its first employee. I would take the meeting. Those experiences do not document how he revised a mistaken belief or recruited complementary technical owners around manufacturing risks. Likewise, samples undergoing customer feedback show contact with reality, but the dossier supplies no acceptance threshold, experiment cost, or decision tied to the result. The conflicting thousands-per-month and thousands-per-week capacity claims make actual production records especially useful. Capacity is not yield, utilization, or risk removed.

Domestic sourcing restrictions can win Open Actuation its first customers. They cannot establish globally competitive economics. Imported magnets and silicon steel remain inputs; progressive localization might improve resilience, but its cost is undisclosed. The $187 CubeMars storefront price is neither Open Actuation’s manufacturing cost nor its custom-motor selling price. I see no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, so I would not impose that red flag. I also see no bottom-up route to unsubsidized parity. The danger is a capable domestic supplier whose advantage depends on purchasing restrictions rather than a production process competitors cannot economically match.

I pass with my own money. My exceptions for execution-led businesses and unusually committed founders prevent me from demanding scientific novelty in every investment. Here, however, the dossier establishes neither a quantified transformative consequence nor founder behavior strong enough to override the missing technical and economic proof. One verifiable flip condition would reopen the decision: a matched OEM production trial across multiple custom geometries demonstrating at least 10x fewer engineering hours than the incumbent process, at comparable reliability and fully loaded delivered cost without a domestic-sourcing premium. That would test whether Open Actuation has built a manufacturing platform capable of escaping bespoke engineering economics.
