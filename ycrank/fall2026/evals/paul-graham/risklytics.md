# Risklytics -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Three companies had already been turned down for insurance. Risklytics says it helped them obtain coverage in the week before its August 26 launch. That is the most interesting fact here. A robotics rental business does not need an eloquent explanation of the insurance market. It needs a policy so it can deploy robots. The founders describe working through wholesalers and MGAs and reviewing wording around AI exclusions. These are concrete responses to obstacles. But the account remains founder-reported, without a customer-level record connecting each rejection to the change that secured coverage. I give the behavior substantial, provisional credit ([launch](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49451495), [interview](https://datainnovation.org/2026/07/5-qs-with-samuel-gold-founder-of-risklytics/)).

The team has also built something inspectable. Risklytics publishes an exclusion tracker covering 736 filings, 49 states, and 33 insurers, alongside its application software. Collecting and organizing that material is useful work. It establishes more than a diagram or a promise. It does not establish an unusual technical advantage: there is no measured result showing that the software finds coverage gaps or completes placements better than another competent broker. The tracker fits my pattern of founders assembling a useful substrate before claiming a platform, but its size alone does not demonstrate the depth of that substrate ([tracker](https://www.risklytics.ai/ai-exclusions/)).

Who wants this urgently is reasonably clear: companies deploying physical autonomy whose existing insurance may leave the central risk uncovered. How many want Risklytics repeatedly is unclear. Almost 50 application users are evidence that people will try the workflow, but applications are upstream of purchases. They do not establish bound policies, repeat business, or referrals. Carrier commissions of 10–20% give the company a straightforward way to earn money from solving the problem. They also make successful placement and renewal more informative than application traffic. Neither realized commissions nor renewal behavior is disclosed ([company LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/company/risklytics), [pricing](https://www.risklytics.ai/pricing/)).

Gold and Risio abandoned wildfire modeling during YC and moved into brokerage. That could be a good running-back decision, but the dossier does not identify the observation that falsified the first approach. I cannot score the existence of a pivot as evidence of learning. Their prior modeling work and Risio’s producer license establish relevant preparation; they do not establish that the idea arose from a problem the founders personally encountered. Being college roommates supplies some relationship history. Taking leave from Harvard supplies no evidence either way. Nor would I apply the teenage-founder red flag without evidence about their ages or behavior.

The strongest case for investing is that the founders are already doing the unpleasant work that reveals a valuable business. Three successful placements could matter much more than fifty software users. My exceptions allow unmistakable customer pull to outweigh an incomplete founder history, but the disclosed outcomes are not yet strong enough for that override. The partnership red flag also needs care: wholesalers are a route to supply, while YC and Harvard networks offer initial access to customers; neither proves repeatable demand. RiskCube already offers overlapping procurement and exclusion-review features. I would hold off on a check. My flip condition is five robotics customers independently confirming that they returned to Risklytics to insure additional deployments under policies explicitly covering their AI exposure. That would show the founders have found a narrow well worth digging.
