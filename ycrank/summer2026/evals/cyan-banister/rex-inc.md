# Rex -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Merlin Kafka spent four years building Sequence’s billing and finance-operations platform before founding Rex, while Lewis Blackwood led Sequence engineering. Rex is a lived-domain operator wrapped in its weakest form: consensus AI software that a normal human cannot touch. Rex scores highest because Kafka and Blackwood processed billions in receivables across more than 50 ERP integrations before choosing this problem; Rex scores lowest because 22,000 autonomous actions remain invisible back-office events, not a rocket, camera, ride, or game I can experience myself.

**Niantic** is the high rung for founder origin: John Hanke spent a decade inside mapping before Ingress and Pokémon Go made that accumulated obsession inevitable. Rex shares the compounding mechanism—Kafka and Blackwood worked together for four years at Sequence, serving hundreds of finance teams before Rex—but Rex diverges where the analogy matters. The dossier gives me résumés, Cambridge credentials, and adjacent operating experience; it does not give me Kafka’s physical moment of frustration, an environmental signal he did not know I was observing, or the persistence-after-rejection pattern hiding underneath Niantic and Flock Safety. Rex’s founders are *of the workflow*, but I cannot yet tell whether Rex was inevitable for them.

**DeepMind’s** low rung on physical verifiability is the abstraction problem: I could not install an intelligence laboratory or watch it operate in my street, and only Demis Hassabis’s extraordinary polymath compounding overrode that weakness. Rex exposes the same distance without the same exception. Synthesia can verify that Rex executed 22,000 actions and retrieved purchase orders worth more than $40 million, but I cannot personally ride Rex, scan Rex, or hand Rex to a friend within 48 hours. The audit trail is observable to a controller, which keeps Rex above pure API vapor, but no DeepMind-style polymath signal rescues the lack of a tactile artifact.

**Vercel’s first-place ranking among 2,500 accelerator applicants** makes Rex look selected, not neglected. Over the next 18 months, that consensus compounds against Rex: Fazeshift has raised $22 million, Monk has raised $29 million, and HighRadius launched more than 180 finance agents. Every overlapping agent improves the enterprise buyer’s negotiating leverage while eroding Rex’s claim that customer-specific procedures form a unique institutional-memory layer. Rex may be good, but “good AI agent for a $37 billion BPO market” is the game everyone appears to be playing; no sophisticated investor has visibly passed because Rex is aesthetically offensive, regulated beyond comfort, or magically weird.

**Merlin Kafka is the named CEO**, while Lewis Blackwood is the named CTO, so Rex clears the first governance screen that HQ Trivia eventually failed. Rex does not clear the second screen: the dossier contains no equity split, operating agreement, founder-vesting terms, or documented tiebreaker beyond titles. Four years together at Sequence is meaningful relational evidence, but familiarity is not authority. Rex would flip this dimension into the high tier if Kafka can show majority operating control and a written deadlock protocol; without that, I apply the governance discount because two Cambridge co-founders can still become a 50/50 partnership with nicer stationery.

**Rex’s privacy policy says SOC 2 Type II is still being pursued**, while the homepage describes it as “monitoring.” The single-anchor parallel is **Vigil Labs**: regulated financial workflows become interesting when compliance friction compounds into a moat that casual entrants refuse to cross. Rex sends customer communications and updates financial systems, so Rex inhabits an unsexy, consequential surface; approval thresholds, audit logs, and exception routing are real operating machinery. But SOC 2 ambiguity is procurement debt, not regulatory advantage, and software connecting ERP, CRM, inboxes, and portals is neither frontier hardware nor a capital-intensive swing.

**Synthesia went live in under three weeks** and used Rex across 30–40 customer portals, which is enough substance to forecast rather than daydream. Within 36 months, I expect Rex to reach roughly $15–25 million in ARR and be acquired by HighRadius rather than become the independent order-to-cash default. Rex’s lived-domain founders should execute well enough to win real enterprise revenue; Rex’s low touchability and low weirdness mean distribution will remain demo-led, integration-heavy, and vulnerable to bundling by the incumbent already selling 180 finance agents.

**More than $500 million in receivables managed** is the bear case against even that outcome: autonomous-action errors, changing portal rules, and services-heavy deployments could strand Rex below $5 million ARR while Fazeshift, Monk, and HighRadius compress pricing. Rex has unusually strong rebuttal evidence—Synthesia’s 22,000 completed actions, $40 million in retrieved purchase orders, and sub-three-week deployment make “PowerPoint agent” the wrong accusation—but none of my Known Exceptions fully applies. Rex lacks HQ Trivia’s tactile virality, HandUp’s autobiographical mission override, and DeepMind’s demonstrated polymath founder. I do not write my own-money check today. Verified net revenue retention above 130% across at least ten enterprise customers, without proportional managed-service headcount growth, would flip Rex from Neutral to Invest.
