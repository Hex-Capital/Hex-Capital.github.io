# Mosaic -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that Mosaic has built a multiplayer terminal. It is that the dossier never names what broke for these founders, what they tried first, or what they shipped in response. Dorsa Rohani’s distributed-inference work and Shubham Patil’s developer-interface experience make them plausible builders, but credentials are not resourcefulness. There is no dated obstacle-and-workaround sequence, so I cannot tell whether they are running backs or merely good programmers working in a fashionable category.

The technical evidence is stronger. By July 2026, outsiders had seen multiple humans share control of a terminal while Claude Code agents communicated and preserved context. Oflight also examined parallel Claude Code, Codex, and OpenCode sessions with Git-worktree isolation. This is functioning software built before permission, not a diagram. It is Mosaic’s best signal. But it is not yet a measured proof spike: there is no benchmark, security architecture, workload volume, or evidence that the product survives daily use by a real engineering team.

Who wants this now? The dossier cannot name one user. The Neuron coverage and Oflight analysis prove that the demonstration was legible, not that the problem was painful. A free Hobby tier and a $200-per-seat Team plan prove that Mosaic can publish a price, not that anyone will pay it. The product fits my pattern of programmable infrastructure that technical users might bend into unexpected workflows, but the revealing half of that pattern is missing: no repeated use, emergent behavior, payment, or referral has been observed.

The strongest bull case is that the founders have noticed the interface missing between increasingly capable coding agents. Rohani understands distributed systems; Patil has worked on developer collaboration; Claude Code reached a $1 billion revenue run rate by November 2025. A working multiplayer client could therefore precede the evidence of demand rather than lack it. But Conductor, Superset, and Pentagon can all converge on shared context and human control. Mosaic also inherits prompt-injection, credential, permission, and upstream-CLI risks without publishing the security machinery that could turn those schleps into a moat. At present, the difficult work is a risk Mosaic faces, not an advantage it has earned.

I see no active launch, partnership, or premature-sales red flag. I also see none of the unusually concrete pre-round proof spikes that characterize my recent checks. The separate Ocean branding adds route ambiguity without documenting an evidence-driven change, and the founders’ backgrounds suggest proximity to the problem without proving that either personally suffered it. My expectation is that within 36 months the company will have pivoted from multiplayer terminals toward an agent permissions or audit-control product, rather than producing more than $1 million ARR from the current Team offering.

I would pass today. The verifiable flip condition is simple: show five engineering teams using Mosaic every week for eight consecutive weeks, then show a common failure those teams exposed, the founders’ fix shipped within seven days, and at least three teams paying the published seat price afterward. That single sequence would establish both the narrow well and the founder behavior that the current dossier leaves blank.
