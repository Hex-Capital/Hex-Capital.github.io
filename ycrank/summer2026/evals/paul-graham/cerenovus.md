# Cerenovus -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Pilot customers were live by June 2026, but no founder is documented as having suffered the problem Cerenovus solves. That pairing is Cerenovus’s signature: a schlep-heavy enterprise product without an organic founder story. Cerenovus may have found difficult work, but the dossier does not show that the difficult work found Jonathan Waldorf, Oliver Moreland, or Lucas Baur first. Animals leave tracks. Organic ideas leave scars.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness: Brian Armstrong embraced custody, compliance, and banking work that made other hackers recoil. Cerenovus has a smaller version of the same mechanism. Connecting Slack, email, CRM, ERP, accounting systems, Google Drive, Notion, and Microsoft services means permissions, deletion rules, security reviews, and messy schemas. Cerenovus diverges from Coinbase because the dossier shows policy exposure, not evidence that the founders personally conquered it. Live pilots suggest Cerenovus has entered the swamp; they do not yet prove Cerenovus likes living there.

**Loopt** (2005) exposed the low-rung failure mechanism on organicity: a technically plausible product can outrun a problem people urgently experience, and only an exceptional founder can override that weakness. Cerenovus describes executives needing answers about restructurings, vendors, and broken handoffs, but none of Cerenovus’s three founders is documented as having run an enterprise organization or endured those failures firsthand. “Harvard students spotted inefficient corporate knowledge work” is a market observation. It is not Drew Houston forgetting his USB stick. No direct founder-pain evidence appears, so I apply the 10/25 absent-evidence default.

Compendium reached Product Hunt rank #16 with 123 points, launched a $39.99-per-seat plan, and had live pilots before YC Demo Day. Those are real artifacts, and Cerenovus gets credit for shipping instead of waiting to hire engineers. Resourcefulness compounds when founders convert each rejection into a stranger, faster route to users; over 18 months, Cerenovus could turn manual pilot installations into a repeatable map of how organizations actually break. But Cerenovus shows no cereal-box stunt, rescued rejection, named paying customer, or improvised revenue. Product Hunt activity and two outside engineering listings are evidence of motion, not yet the animal test.

Jonathan Waldorf worked on quantum algorithms at QuEra, while Oliver Moreland took Math 55 and computer-science coursework. Cerenovus therefore has more technical substance than the polished AI-wrapper founders I avoid, and Cerenovus plainly built a working product. The hacker score would flip upward if Waldorf or Moreland had an open-source graph engine, a difficult systems project, or a public demo that predated fundraising. No public repositories or hacker-community trails were found. Credentials say they can solve assigned hard problems; hacker genealogy requires evidence that they could not stop themselves from building one.

Three Harvard students started Cerenovus together in 2026. **Stripe** (2010) is the relevant single-anchor parallel because the Collisons had years of shared history and had already hacked commerce together through Auctomatic before Stripe existed. Cerenovus has the right number—three—and useful technical and commercial coverage, but Cerenovus has no documented two-year building relationship or prior shared artifact. A trio assembled at the same university is structurally better than a solo founder, though chemistry cannot be inferred from the word “Harvard.”

Glean already has more than $100 million in ARR, while Cerenovus charges $39.99 per seat and simultaneously presents itself as executive decision infrastructure and shared context for AI-native startups. By July 2029, I expect Cerenovus to have pivoted toward enterprise agent-context infrastructure, abandoning broad restructuring advice, while remaining below $3 million ARR. Cerenovus will discover that the durable work is not generating executive findings but maintaining permissions-aware context across constantly changing systems. The schlep survives; the original framing does not.

Glean’s overlap also creates the strongest bull case against that forecast: Cerenovus may be the **Airbnb** (2009) exception, where a reasonable investor mistakes an ugly young product for a weak founder signal. Live pilots, a shipped knowledge graph, and a five-person organization assembled while the founders were undergraduates could be the tracks the public dossier fails to capture. But Cerenovus lacks Airbnb’s decisive cereal-box fact: no named customer, revenue figure, repeat-use rate, founder-pain story, or unscalable deployment feat establishes unusual determination. I would flip to Invest if five named pilot customers each expanded into contracts worth at least $100,000 annually after twelve months; that would show Cerenovus turning the enterprise-data schlep into something people urgently want.
