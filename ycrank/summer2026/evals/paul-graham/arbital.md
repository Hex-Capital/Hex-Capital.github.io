# Arbital -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what broke. Arbital’s founders have impressive Aevo histories, but the dossier never names an obstacle at Arbital, what they built around it, or how quickly the result changed. Operating growth, partnerships, and sub-2-millisecond infrastructure elsewhere are relevant priors; they are not evidence that this team has already been relentlessly resourceful here. I therefore leave the most important criterion at its missing-evidence default.

The artifact is much more convincing than the founder story. Arbital has a functioning multi-venue engine with maker-only execution, inventory limits, loss thresholds, circuit breakers, and configurable strategies ([Arbital documentation](https://arbital-1.gitbook.io/docs/strategies)). It has routed $1.3 billion in seven months and is running at $377 million in monthly volume ([YC company page](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/arbital)). Those numbers turn the architecture into measured technical proof. This resembles the concrete pre-round proof spikes I actually respond to: difficult machinery was operating before anyone granted permission.

The user evidence is real but less complete. Some 2,600 traders generated that volume, which is far better than a waitlist or a ceremonial launch. But “traders” is still a category, not a demonstrated narrow well. The dossier provides no cohort retention, trading frequency, revenue, or separation between organic volume and exchange-incentive farming. In trading, volume can be rented cheaply with rebates and points. The decisive question is not whether $1.3 billion passed through the pipes, but whether the same traders return when the subsidy disappears.

The product transition makes this ambiguity more important. The public site presents a decentralized market-making engine, while the YC page promises a social, mobile-first terminal spanning perps, memes, and stocks. This could be a good running-back move. It could also be two products sharing one traction number. No dated user observation explains the change, so I give no adaptability credit beyond the default. The broad terminal also enters territory already occupied by Axiom, Fomo, Backpack, and Hyperliquid, while depending on third-party venues whose fees, incentives, and liquidity Arbital does not control.

The founders did encounter the underlying work before starting Arbital. All three worked at Aevo: Karon Pangestu led growth, Steven Mulyawan personally developed trader channels, and Vincent Owen Toniwan built backend and market-making infrastructure ([YC company page](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/arbital)). That gives them unusual access to the ugly parts—latency, inventory risk, integrations, incentives, and regulation. But the dossier does not show that Arbital began from a problem they personally needed solved; it may instead be a well-informed market opportunity. I give strong intermediate credit for the schlep, not full credit for an organic founder-originated need.

The strongest case for investing is that the working product and unmistakable usage qualify for an exception to the failed founder-behavior gate. The strongest case against it is that Arbital may be manufacturing apparent pull through incentive-dependent volume while changing from infrastructure into a crowded consumer terminal. My 36-month base case is that it abandons the broad social-mobile positioning and becomes a specialist cross-venue execution business producing $3–8 million in annual recurring revenue. I would not write the check yet. One result would flip me: show that a named cohort retained at least half its traders for three months after incentives were removed, and that their behavior—not positioning fashion—caused the shipped product transition.
