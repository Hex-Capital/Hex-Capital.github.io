# Tarifflo Inc. -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and customs work should look less like expensive interpretation performed entry by entry and more like a continuously updated control system: every shipment is classified, checked, and corrected before an importer loses money. Tarifflo has artifacts leaking from that future—an ABI filer code, a direct CBP connection, more than 1 million products audited, and 10 million linked shipment records—but the dossier never works backward through the transitions that make this system inevitable. It establishes a useful present-day product, not yet a six-year map.

The broken rule is wonderfully concrete. Importers pay consultants $250–500 per hour, buy per-entry or per-user software, and still make errors on an alleged one in eight entries. Tarifflo removes the upfront toll, audits the packet against HTS, Chapter 99, PGA, and CBP rules, then earns a contingency fee only when money returns. That transfers both information and negotiating power back to the importer. The model also creates a hard tradeoff: Tarifflo bears investigation costs while Post-Summary Corrections can remain open for 300 days. This is not “AI makes compliance easier.” It is a specific rent converted into a performance contract.

The Orly result is the evidence I keep circling: more than 10,000 items reviewed, petitions actually filed through Alba Wheels Up, and $1.3 million recovered. A customer surrendered a large, messy operating record and waited through a regulated correction process; that is costly behavior from a functioning product. The broader claim of 1 million products audited and reports that public companies were signing on by February 2025 strengthen the signal, although the dossier does not disclose repeat cohorts, Tarifflo’s resulting revenue, or whether customers return after the first refund hunt.

Bryce Judy has paid real operating tuition. He classified customs goods at KPMG, obtained a CBP broker license, studied information systems, and published an HTS-classification benchmarking paper before YC. That is much more persuasive than discovering tariffs when they became headline material. It does not yet establish a lifelong obsession or exceptional recruiting ability, but it shows technical, regulatory, and commercial fluency in the exact machinery Tarifflo must operate. YC listing Judy as the sole active founder also gives me one accountable leader; I do not see the governance shell game that would stop the investment.

The strongest bear case is that this becomes a clever refund consultancy wearing software clothing. Zonos already supplies CBP-selected classification infrastructure, KlearNow has customs connections across five countries, and Altana serves both enterprises and government agencies. Tarifflo’s direct connection is still in private beta, while the supposed learning moat—reviewed classifications and correction outcomes—is only inferred. The company also handles sensitive entry and supplier information without publicly establishing custody, consent, or retention boundaries. Ten million linked records sound like infrastructure only if Tarifflo owns the necessary rights and the outcomes make the next audit measurably better.

I would write a small pre-seed check because the present-day leak is unusually substantial: a licensed founder, live regulatory plumbing, completed filings, and a seven-figure customer recovery. This is an unglamorous operating repair with the possibility of becoming the compliance layer beneath import commerce, but I am underwriting that possibility—not pretending it is already proved. My verifiable flip condition is repeat behavior: if at least three unrelated importers do not complete a second recovery or filing cycle through Tarifflo within 12 months, I move to pass.
