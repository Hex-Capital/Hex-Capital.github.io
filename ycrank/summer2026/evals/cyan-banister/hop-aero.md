# Hop Aero -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A stranded unit does not request an aircraft, negotiate scarce lift capacity, and wait for a runway to become usable. It loads a critical component into a containerized vehicle, launches it on demand, and receives 250 kilograms on rough ground 750 kilometers away roughly fifteen minutes later. That future is wonderfully strange but does not require an unnamed scientific miracle. It requires reliable propulsion, controlled re-entry, precision landing, inexpensive vehicle production, permissive operating procedures, and FAA authorization. Hop Aero has two small leaks from that world—a tethered prototype flight and an engine-and-igniter hot-fire—but it has not yet crossed the decisive boundary into an untethered point-to-point flight.

The founders are the strongest part of this bet. Matija Milenovic previously founded a satellite-propulsion company and sold its IP, led more than twenty people building supersonic UAVs, and scaled Ukrainian loitering-munition production from roughly 10 to more than 100 units per month. Jacob Balaj has touched the actual machinery at the Marine Corps, SpaceX, Virgin Orbit, Masten, and SpinLaunch. This is not a pair of tourists who discovered defense logistics during a fashionable fundraising season. The biographies show technical tuition, recruiting capacity, and unusually relevant production experience. What remains undisclosed is their pre-Hop working history and governance arrangement; with two co-founders, I need to verify who has the contractual tiebreaker rather than infer it from CEO and CTO titles.

The broken system is less fully specified. Contested units plainly lose time when aircraft, roads, ships, pilots, or replacement parts are unavailable, and containerized launch could return operational control to commanders at the edge. But the dossier never quantifies the present rent: which failure costs how much, how frequently a 250-kilogram payload resolves it, and why existing missiles, drones, aircraft, or forward-positioned inventory lose economically. A fifteen-minute trip is a dramatic specification, not yet a demonstrated purchasing equation. The $1,249,909 Air Force Phase II SBIR award is meaningful institutional belief, but one development contract ending in October 2026 does not prove repeat mission demand.

The bear case stings because every difficult system is still ahead. Rook has not flown freely, commercial launch and re-entry require extensive authorization, and SpaceX, Rocket Lab, Inversion, and Outpost already possess capital, government relationships, or flight infrastructure. Hop Aero may be a capable propulsion team building an exquisite vehicle before establishing that customers need this payload, range, and response-time combination often enough to support its economics. The company also fits a crowded defense-and-space financing wave, while its controlled asset today is know-how expressed through a tether and a hot-fire—not yet repeatable flight data, certified operations, or a manufacturing advantage.

I still lean invest with my own money because this is physical infrastructure beneath the visible autonomy and contested-logistics wave, built by people who have already paid unusually relevant operating tuition. My 36-month expectation is that Hop Aero completes an untethered subscale point-to-point flight and accumulates $5 million to $20 million in U.S. defense development awards, while still having no recurring commercial delivery revenue. The verifiable condition that would flip me to pass is failure to complete a controlled untethered flight—including launch, transition, and landing—before the company needs another major financing round. Government enthusiasm cannot substitute indefinitely for the artifact.
