# Axiomat -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine an employee asking an agent a revenue question and acting on the answer without borrowing an analyst for the afternoon. Axiomat’s proposed logical business model could help make that ordinary. But why should I trust the model’s definition of revenue? Mathematical verification can establish that a query follows encoded rules; it cannot, by itself, establish that those rules describe the business correctly. The company’s documentation-to-model translation is where I would spend my afternoon. The dossier describes an architecture, but supplies no observed artifact showing that this transition toward delegated trust has begun.

The strongest reason to take Axiomat seriously is that its founders have worked near that boundary before. Ethan Kharitonov researched formal methods and AI verification at AWS; Jack Naimer’s prior work includes BioReason and robotic semantic safety; Elad Perlman built utility control software. Those are substantive experiences with systems where plausible output can be inadequate. Jack’s co-authored BioReason repository is a research artifact, although its 406 stars tell me nothing about demand for Axiomat. This is credible pre-company technical preparation. The record does not yet establish a long-running personal obsession, unusual recruiting ability, or command of the commercial levers.

I like the concealed-infrastructure possibility: several kinds of agents could need a dependable layer between generating a query and using its result. That fits my pattern of looking underneath the visible application. Axiomat also addresses mundane operating irritation, another attraction. But the present economics remain thinly specified. Employees manually checking answers lose time; the dossier does not establish the size of that burden, who controls the bottleneck, or whether verification actually lets those employees act independently. Nor is there evidence that Axiomat controls a scarce technical asset. AWS’s existing automated-reasoning service makes that distinction consequential, even though equivalent database-query coverage has not been established.

The bull case stings because this team might be unusually qualified to turn a difficult research capability into useful infrastructure before customers know how to request it. I have made pre-revenue exceptions: **CosmicBrain AI** paired technical founder conviction with a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept. The shared mechanism is enabling technology arriving before commercial maturity; the material difference is that Axiomat’s dossier supplies neither comparable product proof nor completed customer behavior. YC participation and a demo-booking page cannot substitute for someone repeatedly trusting the product with consequential work. Missing revenue is ordinary here. The missing interaction leaves my central hypothesis untested.

The “never hallucinates” promise deserves a precise boundary, particularly when a mistaken business definition could produce a perfectly valid proof. It is an unresolved claim, not evidence of fabrication. Likewise, leaving data in place does not establish consent or custody protections, but the dossier documents no severe privacy violation. Ethan is identified as CEO, and the Signiture corporate name is explained by the privacy policy; neither establishes the leadership shell game I watch for. These checks do not reveal a reason to reject the founders outright. They also do not supply the missing affirmative evidence.

I pass with my own money today. Axiomat has stronger evidence of relevant technical preparation than of a future already appearing in customer behavior. My flip condition is one independently referenceable, 90-day customer deployment showing employees repeatedly acting on verified answers, with an audited example of the system catching an incorrect business definition before it reaches an answer. That would test the exact place where this could become essential.
