# Rezora IO -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Better voice models let real estate agents and home services operators buy automated lead conversion; the controlling variable is whether Rezora IO generates enough incremental, consent-compliant bookings to justify a separate subscription. Answering calls is becoming easier to supply. Owning profitable customer acquisition could still produce a large business. The dossier establishes the connection between missed calls and booked appointments, but provides no measured conversion advantage or economic threshold showing why Rezora wins. I would pass with my own money today.

The market is already crystallizing. ServiceTitan offers native voice booking, Retell supplies configurable infrastructure, and Avoca spans the service-business front office. Rezora charges $289/month plus $0.20 per conversational minute, with a separate $589 home-services offer. Those prices establish a commercial product; they do not establish lower customer acquisition costs or attractive delivery margins. ElevenLabs’ October 2026 Realtime launch supports easier implementation across the category. Without comparative economics, I cannot tell whether that shift expands Rezora’s opportunity faster than it expands competing supply.

Rezora does have more workflow substance than a voice demonstration. Connecting phone lines and CRM sources, qualifying callers, handling objections, and booking appointments constitute useful actions. Better models should improve execution. But documented ownership stops short of durable customer state, evaluation loops, or difficult-to-replace integrations. Training on 60 sales books and thousands of conversations does not establish reproduction cost. The terms’ prohibition on training with customer recordings also means I cannot assume a proprietary learning loop develops automatically with usage.

The strongest argument against passing is that the founders may already understand distribution and customer needs better than the infrastructure providers. Matsay built calling automation for his own real estate prospecting. Richards previously built a service business with 80 clients across three towns. That is concrete personal need and operating experience. The claimed 2,400 users or teams and 1,000 booked meetings suggest something customers actually use. If those meetings recur for paying accounts, prepared conversations and fast setup could outweigh extensive technical differentiation for this buyer.

I give that evidence credit, but its definitions matter. Users and teams imply different adoption levels; the meeting count lacks a clear measurement period and customer denominator. The reported $40,000 in 40 days may belong to the predecessor agency, and the customer’s $65,000 in closed deals is customer revenue. Neither establishes current Rezora subscription revenue. Consent requirements also constrain the outbound prospecting wedge, while native platforms compete for inbound booking. The same workflow can therefore face restricted reach on one side and bundled substitution on the other.

My pattern of investing across a technology discontinuity supports taking voice automation seriously, but does not establish which application captures the value. The founders’ practical experience strengthens the case; it does not supply the scarce frontier research or technical asset that would justify overriding weak market evidence. The proprietary-training claim remains unproven rather than a moat. One result would reverse my pass: a verified six-month paid cohort retaining at least 90% of customers at positive contribution after voice delivery, onboarding, and acquisition costs. That would demonstrate that Rezora owns a recurring economic outcome customers will keep buying.
