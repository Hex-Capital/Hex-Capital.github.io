# Radley -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The post-money valuation is undisclosed, the competing-term-sheet count is unknown, and nothing establishes that this is a genuinely scarce first check. That is not evidence of an auction, but it is not evidence of cheap non-consensus entry either. With my own money, I do not fill in favorable financing terms because the underlying company looks sophisticated. The deal gate remains unresolved.

Radley’s proposed venture moment is legible but not yet specified. Acquiring a practice with $4.3 million in annualized revenue and replacing its clinical and operational stack with agents could test whether software ownership plus practice ownership produces a structurally better radiology business. But the dossier gives no acquisition consideration, closing date, use of round proceeds, or dated pass/fail metric. “Acquire and rebuild” can become one decisive experiment; today it can equally become an open-ended rollup requiring repeated capital.

The strongest case is the team. Maryam Jahed shipped healthcare products through Carbon Health’s expansion from 31 to more than 100 clinics, helped build a six-figure-revenue company, and worked through an FDA breakthrough designation. Seak Meng Lay built Carbon Health’s engineering and EHR infrastructure during the same expansion. Radley is joining clinical operations to software infrastructure, and these founders have operating receipts on both sides. The former software product’s $2.2 million of signed contracts and live deployment further shows that healthcare buyers did more than admire a prototype.

That prior conversion also makes Radley’s prospective dark pool credible: contracts, clinical workflow, facility relationships, and operating data could become proprietary once the practice is owned. But the acquisition is still pending, so Radley does not yet control the $4.3 million revenue base or the workflow data. Meanwhile, Rad AI reaches more than 40% of U.S. health systems, Sirona has 12 million annual studies under contract, and Radiology Partners already combines practice operations with AI across more than 25 million examinations. The translation-layer pattern fits; the owned advantage remains unproven.

The bull case stings because this may be exactly the wrong moment to demand mature metrics: two experienced healthcare operators have already sold and deployed a prior product, then recognized that owning the practice may capture more value than selling another software seat. But AI-native operations do not earn a known-exception override merely by sounding structurally clever. There is no reported model accuracy, customer retention, turnaround-time improvement, gross margin, burn, or clinical-governance result. The $4.3 million figure is acquired revenue, not demonstrated economics, and licensure, credentialing, and integration can turn a nominally profitable practice into a capital factory.

My 36-month base case is that Radley reaches $8 million–$15 million in annualized revenue through acquired or contracted practices but remains dependent on acquisition financing because software-like margins have not been demonstrated. I am neutral and would not write the check today. The one verifiable condition that flips me is two consecutive quarters of positive operating cash flow from the acquired practice after agent deployment, with no deterioration in clinical quality.
