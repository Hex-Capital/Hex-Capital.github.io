# Orca Aerospace -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Orca Aerospace could move spacecraft decisions from scheduled ground contacts to the vehicle itself. That changes which missions become possible when communications disappear. But how much existing expenditure disappears with them? The dossier’s $5.88 billion spacecraft-autonomy estimate includes hardware and services; it supplies neither an operator-cost baseline nor a quantified expansion in useful spacecraft capacity. I see a consequential problem. I cannot yet calculate the consequence of solving it.

The product has an intelligible technical mechanism: AI chooses objectives, a deterministic planner constructs actions, and guardrails authorize execution. Separating reasoning from execution addresses a real constraint in spacecraft control. It earns some technical credit. Yet safe individual commands can compose into an unsafe mission, and the dossier supplies no measured improvement against ground operations or competing autonomy systems. Shield AI and Sedaro reportedly executed 189 approved commands aboard a NOVI satellite in August 2026. Those are competitors’ results. They establish a competitive reference point, not Orca’s performance.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders. Jacob Ososke spent more than a decade in Lockheed Martin GNC and founded its first AI-GNC team; Hakan Chunton combines MIT aerospace and computing training with trajectory research. These people have worked inside the relevant constraints. Orca also fits my recurring preference for difficult physical systems and scarce technical workflows. The Lyra integration study gives that work an external counterpart. But domain competence does not establish fast belief revision or difficult complementary recruiting. Both founders share a Lockheed GNC background, and the engineering vacancy describes a recruit still to be won. I give neither a penalty for their small team nor invented credit for a demonstrated learning culture.

My first experimental dollar would attack mission-level safety under constrained compute. The Lyra work identifies the chain: model memory determines board requirements, and board power affects spacecraft bus sizing. The dossier does not describe a completed decisive experiment, numerical acceptance threshold, or spending sequence against that risk. Economics remain equally open. Platform-specific integration could consume the savings from fewer ground interventions; public pricing and integration costs are unavailable. There is no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, premature sales expansion, or the generic-wrapper red flag. There is also no demonstrated repeatable validation platform or proprietary learning loop yet.

I would pass with my own money. I can waive early milestones for an extraordinary quantified consequence, or accept a modest initial mechanism when a demonstrated instigator can reach a giant existing value pool. Orca’s evidence does not establish either exception. My single flip condition is a customer-witnessed hardware-in-the-loop acceptance trial showing a tenfold reduction in fully costed operator intervention per successfully resolved mission event, against that customer’s ground workflow, with zero mission-level constraint violations across a preregistered fault suite. Include flight compute and platform integration in the accounting. That result would connect Orca’s architecture to both technical discontinuity and economic leverage.
