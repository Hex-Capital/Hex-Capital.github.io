# Markov -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

150,000 Hugging Face downloads make **Markov** a missionary-execution asset trapped inside my least attractive sleeve: founder conviction is **Markov’s** highest dimension, while foundation-model-adjacent AI infrastructure is its lowest. AI data is uninvestible. That is deliberately overstated—the operational point is that Markov has already assembled 10,000-plus hours with two people, but the value currently accrues to model developers through a CC-BY-4.0 release rather than to Markov through a scarce, priced asset.

**Drop.io** is the high rung for asymmetric conviction: build the illegible primitive before consensus knows how to categorize it, then endure the turns of the crank. **Markov** shares that mechanism because Dev Mandal and Harish Ashok created 48,478 videos totaling roughly 12,300 hours before YC, while Dev previously worked at Sarvam AI and built doubt.ai to 700 users. **Markov** diverges from Drop.io because neither founder has published a durable, non-obvious argument for why GUI trajectories remain scarce once Scale AI, Turing, and software publishers respond; the corpus proves work, but not yet a missionary thesis competitors cannot copy.

**Fin’s** low-rung failure mechanism was that a compelling human-assisted automation wedge eventually became ordinary workplace analytics before the software economics resolved. That pattern appears in **Markov**: computer-use data is a fashionable input to Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google products, while Turing has already delivered more than 10,000 supervised GUI tasks. **Markov** has a Wizard-of-Oz flavor in collecting human activity, but Markov remains closer to generic AI supply infrastructure than to my crypto, creator, DTC, or structure-first sleeves. The 500 hours of synchronized gaming activity do not overcome that category problem.

10,000-plus recorded hours could make **Markov’s** capital structure compound over the next 18 months if each customer contract funds a reusable contributor network, task library, and privacy system. The opposite loop is equally plausible: **Markov** gives away the corpus, performs bespoke collection for each lab, and finances an increasingly labor-intensive service through successive SAFEs. With no disclosed revenue, pricing, contributor-rights policy, or paying customer, Markov sits between a bootstrappable data service and a founder-draining venture cap table; I would prefer contract-financed collection or revenue share over pretending every dataset vendor deserves five equity rounds.

Scale AI’s $15.9 billion of disclosed capital makes the counterfactual for **Markov’s** round dimension unusually concrete: Markov becomes interesting if the YC SAFE is priced below the batch median because tier-one funds classify GUI data as a low-margin service. **Markov** becomes an immediate pass if the same 150,000 downloads are being used to justify a hot computer-agent valuation without revenue. No round terms or investor passes are disclosed, so I apply the price-discipline default rather than inventing non-consensus alpha; one rejected offer from a named AI investor and a genuinely cheap cap would move this score an entire tier.

**John Fish / Bookshelved** is the useful single-anchor parallel for owned distribution because the person brought an audience that existed independently of the financed entity. Dev Mandal gives **Markov** a small version of that asset—approximately 5,000 X followers and 4,000 LinkedIn followers—but Markov’s meaningful distribution is 150,000 Hugging Face downloads, not attachment to Dev as a named person. **Markov** therefore owns a researcher channel, not yet a founder community: useful for launching benchmarks, weak for defending paid acquisition or customer retention.

150,000 downloads without disclosed revenue lead to a specific forecast: by July 2029, **Markov** will reach $2 million–$5 million ARR from custom GUI-task collection and evaluation, then be acquired by **Turing** for under $50 million rather than becoming a standalone training-data platform. **Markov’s** founders have demonstrated enough conviction to build the supply asset, but the open license, generalist competition, and weak structural moat make integration into Turing’s expert-data operation more probable than independent category ownership.

Computer Use Large’s 48,478 videos form the strongest opposing case: **Markov** may already possess the pre-consensus asset that looks commoditized until computer-use agents become a major software interface. The **Ro** exception stings here—expensive privacy review, contributor consent, and application-specific collection could become the regulatory and operational moat rather than founder drainage. The evidence still favors the smaller outcome because Markov released the flagship corpus under CC-BY-4.0 and discloses neither exclusive collection rights nor one paid contract. I would flip to Invest if **Markov** verifies at least $1 million ARR from three model developers, with repeat orders and exclusive rights to the newly collected trajectories.
