# Lyon -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The impressive fact is not that Lyon calls this a foundation model. It is that two people trained one on 28 billion transactions inside a fintech’s environment and produced four times the precision of the customer’s existing premium-card rules. That is a functioning artifact crossing a measured threshold, not a diagram or a promise to begin research after funding. The subsequent credit deployment and separate insurer engagement make the result harder to dismiss as a benchmark invented for a demo.

But the dossier skips the scene I most need to see. What blocked the founders? Did the fintech reject its first security architecture, refuse to export data, or demand an impossible deployment schedule? What did Lyon build next, and how many days did it take? SOC 2 Type 1, 35 monitored controls, and customer-VPC deployment show that the team has entered an ugly enterprise schlep. They do not show the founders turning a named obstacle into a shipped workaround. I cannot infer relentless resourcefulness from the fact that the finished system exists.

The customer evidence is promising but incomplete. A fintech serving tens of millions of active users let Lyon train on consequential data, obtained a 4x result, and is extending the system into credit. That is more informative than a waitlist or an LOI. Yet the customer is unnamed, revenue is undisclosed, and there is no evidence of payment, renewal, repeated production use, or users pulling Lyon into tasks the founders did not propose. The insurer supplies breadth, not repeatability. At present this looks like one technically successful enterprise engagement rather than a narrow well beginning to deepen.

The strongest bull case is that the proof itself should override the missing founder story. A Stanford EE/CS graduate with NVIDIA experience has shipped a difficult model, passed a meaningful security threshold, and gained access to data most pre-seed teams could not touch. The known exceptions do permit unusual technical proof to outweigh conventional founder concerns. But those exceptions combine the proof with direct problem exposure or unmistakable user pull. No banking or insurance operating history is documented here, the cofounder relationship is unclear, and there is no dated route change after contrary evidence. Lyon may contain fierce nerds; the dossier has not yet shown running backs.

The bear case is sharper than “enterprise sales are slow.” Customer-specific training prevents an evidenced pooled-data flywheel, while Taktile, Pecan AI, Feedzai, and H2O.ai already offer adjacent routes to the same budgets. Published tabular and transaction-model research makes the underlying technique progressively easier to reproduce. If each new institution requires a bespoke schema, VPC deployment, security review, and model-validation project, Lyon could become a talented consultancy whose benchmark improvements accrue primarily to customers. This contradicts the stronger unstated pattern: technical teams turning a concrete proof spike into a reusable substrate before competitors catch up.

I am neutral, not because Lyon lacks technical ambition, but because its best evidence answers “does it work?” while leaving “what do these founders do when it stops working?” unanswered. My flip condition is one dated customer case study showing a specific deployment or procurement blocker, the founder-built workaround shipped within weeks, and a paid expansion into at least two additional tasks. That would establish resourcefulness, pull, and reuse in a single verifiable sequence. Without it, I would not yet invest my own money.
