# Runtime.si -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a robot earning permission to enter a factory by passing an independent examination. Runtime.si could supply that examination. But first, simulated failures must predict physical failures, and robot operators must trust the results enough to make deployment decisions. The dossier establishes neither transition. Nomad’s action checks and task-success predicates make the destination imaginable; they do not yet establish that this future has begun. I would pass with my own money today.

The most promising detail is where the team has put its hands: between a policy proposing an action and a robot executing it. That fits my pattern of looking beneath the visible machine for the enabling layer. Yet Runtime.si’s October 15 research reports 152/525 successes without skills versus 283/525 for selected skill cohorts, explicitly using retrospective, in-sample comparisons. Those numbers cannot tell me whether its examination catches an unfamiliar physical failure. Artifact verification also remains pending. SignalFlag already combines hardware, replay, and simulation evaluations, while NVIDIA supplies open evaluation workflows. Runtime needs evidence that its particular tests reveal something customers cannot afford to miss.

The founders give me a reason to keep looking. Peng and Zhong have GRASP research connections; Ying brings manipulation and manufacturing experience; Zhong has published robotics-learning notes with 29 GitHub stars. That is substantive technical alignment. It does not establish the dated, pre-company obsession or first-believer recruiting behavior that makes me comfortable financing a long technical journey. The Lambda Robotics job descriptions introduce another uncertainty: did operating data-center robots expose the failure that became this product? That could be a powerful origin, but the dossier does not document it. Peng is identified as CEO; conflicting product descriptions alone do not establish a leadership shell game.

The strongest argument against my pass is that an evaluation company should exist before robots become dependable enough for broad deployment. Waiting for revenue could miss the opportunity entirely. My pre-revenue robotics exception, however, rested on a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a documented lifelong builder. Here, the announced 10,000-simulation collection is forthcoming, and the waitlist supplies no repeated customer behavior. I am not deducting points for absent revenue. I am withholding user-behavior credit because internal experiments do not show an outside team entrusting its deployment decisions to Runtime.si.

I also cannot yet identify whose economic control changes. Physical testing consumes time and equipment, but the dossier’s problem statement is inferred; it names no measured testing expense, gatekeeper rent, or customer delay that Runtime has removed. Nor does it establish an exclusive failure corpus or a feedback loop from deployed robots. The infrastructure resemblance is attractive, but resemblance cannot carry the check. My verifiable flip condition is one independent robotics customer repeatedly gating physical deployments on Runtime’s tests after a preregistered, held-out study shows those tests predict real-world failures. That would connect the future I can imagine to a behavior someone actually trusts.
