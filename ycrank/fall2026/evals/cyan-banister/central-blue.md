# Central Blue -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a spacecraft operator buying extra payload hours instead of designing every mission around its own worst-case power budget. Central Blue is building toward that world: collect sunlight elsewhere, transmit energy, and let another spacecraft do more work. But the intervening steps matter. Efficient delivery must become reliable across changing orbital geometry, receivers must survive the heat, and purchased power must justify the integration. The nearly completed Seattle prototype is a small indication of work toward that future; the advertised 300 kWh storage and 10 kW delivery remain targets. I can see the destination more clearly than the evidence that Central Blue can reach it.

The founders make me take that distance seriously. Brian Wu’s JPL visual-navigation work, robotics background and public DroneFormer project establish technical engagement before this company. Jeewoo Choi brings mechanical engineering and Boeing thermal-testing experience; their earlier planetary-robotics hackathon collaboration gives the partnership some history beyond a fundraising introduction. Those are complementary skills for a problem where software has to contend with heat and moving hardware. They do not yet establish sustained obsession with power transmission, or the ability to recruit the optical, spacecraft and commercial expertise this system requires. I see credible first builders, with first-believer recruitment still unproven.

Why must each satellite carry its own little power station? That is the useful systems question here. However, eclipses and line-of-sight interruptions are physical constraints, and the dossier identifies no quantified institutional rent or gatekeeper that Central Blue removes. Customers will still need onboard generation and batteries between deliveries. The economic test is therefore incremental useful payload output after receiver mass, thermal limits and service interruptions. Star Catcher’s existing-array approach makes Central Blue’s dedicated receiver an especially consequential tradeoff: integration could improve performance, but it also asks customers to commit spacecraft design decisions before service is established.

Central Blue does fit my pattern of looking beneath a visible wave for infrastructure it will require. Power is a genuine dependency for spacecraft operations, and CORE offers a potentially useful component while the hardware develops. Yet there is no demonstrated scarce asset under the team’s control, and no paying beachhead shown to finance the harder destination. The strongest argument for investing now is that waiting for revenue could mean missing the technical team early. My pre-revenue investment in **CosmicBrain AI** illustrates that exception, but its functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept supplied evidence missing here. A planned Wave Motion demonstration and a 2027 Blackwing software demonstration do not establish delivered power or costly customer behavior.

I would keep talking, but I would not write my personal check on this dossier. The earlier CORE positioning could represent sensible discovery; it does not establish a trend-driven pivot or a leadership shell game. Neither does naming a CEO and CTO settle contractual control. My hesitation comes from the unfinished link between promising builders and a customer accepting the hardware burden. One verifiable flip condition would change my decision: an independently witnessed end-to-end test using a prospective customer’s receiver configuration that delivers the customer’s specified usable power within its thermal limits and triggers a binding paid follow-on.
