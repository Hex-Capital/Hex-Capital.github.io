# Mass Magnetics -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The first question is what broke. Recovered magnets arrive with inconsistent chemistries, and aerospace buyers require lengthy qualification. Mass Magnetics says it answered these obstacles with machine-vision sorting, lot characterization, and rapid sintering. But the dossier never identifies a particular failed batch, rejected magnet, or qualification problem followed by a founder-built workaround and an observable result. The founders may be resourceful. There is simply no episode here that proves it.

The 60-second sintering claim is more interesting. Producing fully dense magnets that quickly would be a real technical artifact, especially when paired with recovered feedstock and certificates of analysis. But the compositions are described as illustrative, and there are no independent magnetic-property results, qualification reports, or customer tests. This is an unreproduced benchmark rather than the kind of pre-round proof spike that usually predicts an unusually formidable technical team. It earns credit, but not full credit.

The founders have chosen a good schlep. Eric Silver built lithium-sulfur cathode pilot lines; Matthew Groll worked on NASA’s ISS and Artemis systems and later at Draper. They have known each other since grade school. Their backgrounds plausibly connect materials processing to aerospace manufacturing, and the work involves scrap procurement, chemistry control, custom lots, traceability, and application qualification. But the dossier labels their collision with the problem as inferred. It does not show that either founder personally needed a magnet, failed to obtain one, and began building the missing supplier.

The bull case is sharp. China restricted specified rare-earth materials in April 2025, and applicable DFARS procurements face a January 1, 2027 covered-country restriction. A three-person team with relevant manufacturing experience may have found a faster process just as customers are forced to reconsider supply. But the nonavailability exception weakens automatic demand, while Noveon has raised $315 million and Vulcan $65 million. Mass Magnetics has preorders open, not disclosed preorders placed. Its 334 LinkedIn followers, Greentown membership, and launch-roundup coverage do not identify a user who has qualified, purchased, or repeatedly used the product.

None of my standard red flags is overt. The cofounders have a long relationship, the sales motion is direct quotation rather than dependence on a ceremonial launch or unnamed partner, and there is no plan to delegate early customer learning. But absence of a red flag is not evidence of pull. Nor does a known hard-tech exception rescue the case: those exceptions require independently measured proof strong enough to replace missing customer evidence. Here both proof and demand remain self-reported or undisclosed.

I am passing today. Mass Magnetics has selected an important, unpleasant problem and may possess a valuable process, but it fails the operational gate on both observable founder resourcefulness and validated technical or user pull. One event would flip the decision: a named aerospace or defense customer completing qualification and placing a repeat production order for magnets made with the 60-second process. That would turn the website’s logical claim into a working company.
