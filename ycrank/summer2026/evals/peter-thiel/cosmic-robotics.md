# Cosmic Robotics -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The contested truth is precise: unstructured solar jobsites can support autonomous heavy manipulation well enough to more than double installation labor productivity. The falsifier is equally clear—if Cosmic-1A cannot sustain that advantage across full projects after charging, maintenance, worker spotting, and panel fastening are counted, the secret disappears. Tens of thousands of installed panels and reported productivity above 2× make this more than a robotics slogan. But four funded competitors pursuing substantially the same work show that the belief is not lonely; the secret is technical, not contrarian.

Cosmic Robotics has chosen a real beachhead: utility-scale solar EPCs installing standardized panels in hostile terrain. Its field data across mud, sand, ruts, and weather could become proprietary infrastructure for physical AI. Yet data becomes a monopoly mechanism only if it produces a widening performance advantage. Gritt reportedly achieves the same panel-positioning task with rented equipment and off-the-shelf arms, while Charge Robotics and Terabase automate adjacent portions of the installation workflow. Today this is a differentiated machine inside an increasingly competitive category, not a last-mover position.

The strongest evidence is the founding structure. James Emerick deployed autonomous construction equipment at Built Robotics; Lewis Jones built propulsion and space systems across Relativity Space, SpaceX, and NASA. They prototyped together through Autodesk Research before selecting solar after visiting a farm. That is formation around an operating thesis, not two résumés assembled for YC. Their eight-person team has produced a 10,000-pound field robot, adapted it to cooling-pipe installation in ten days, and completed enough cycles to expose the product to physical reality. Full-time titles and in-house execution are evident, but founder ownership, compensation, and broader economic alignment remain undisclosed.

The missing element is not technology but economics. No revenue, price, gross margin, contract duration, fleet utilization, or service cost is public. A quote-based route to utility-scale EPCs identifies the buyer, and requests for hyperscaler and neocloud introductions identify an expansion channel, but neither establishes repeatable distribution. A custom vehicle requiring charging, precision sensing, manufacturing, and field maintenance can double crew productivity while still producing poor returns on capital. NASA’s $149,521 Phase I contract and DOE awards validate institutional interest, not a revenue engine capable of producing a power-law outcome.

The bear case is severe: Cosmic Robotics may be building excellent machinery in a market where value accrues to EPCs, equipment lessors, or the cheapest hardware integrator. Its expansion from solar panels to cooling pipes and lunar construction could become an indefinite collection of demonstrations rather than one dominant business. The known exception for coordination-heavy frontier hardware does not yet apply: Cosmic has field proof, but not paid production economics or a financed deployment path tied to a single urgent customer constraint. Its alignment with the expanding AI construction stack is promising, but an unstated portfolio pattern is not a substitute for monopoly.

I would investigate, not invest today. Within 36 months I expect Cosmic either to reach roughly $3–8 million of recurring or contracted annual revenue from solar EPC deployments or to retreat toward project-based robotics integration; the founders and field record make the former plausible, while the absent economics make neither outcome dominant. One verifiable condition flips me to invest: a multi-site EPC contract showing sustained end-to-end productivity above 2×, including fastening labor and downtime, with positive robot-level gross margin and repeat deployment without bespoke engineering. Until then: technical proof, commercial uncertainty; possible frontier, unproved monopoly.
