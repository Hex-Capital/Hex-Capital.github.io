# Cova -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Cova is licensed and live in Michigan inside a $107.07 billion U.S. home-care market, but Cova’s weakest property is the one I read first: leverage. This is a large-market, low-permissionless-leverage operator. AI handles intake, benefits, enrollment, insurance, and billing; humans still recruit caregivers, enter homes, deliver care, satisfy electronic-visit-verification rules, and collect from permissioned payers. Code assists the labor. It does not yet replace or distribute it.

Uber is the high rung for pivot-room: black cars became mass-market mobility because one broad market supported several products without changing the underlying network. Cova shares the large surface area—professional care, family caregivers, Medicaid, long-term-care insurance, and private pay give Cova several adjacent vectors inside a market projected to reach $176.30 billion by 2032. Cova diverges where the analogy matters: Uber’s next city reused a marketplace, while Cova’s next state requires new enrollment knowledge, payer relationships, compliance controls, and caregiver operations. The market is large; the expansion unit is still an agency.

Honor is the low rung on permissionless leverage: $325 million of equity and a network spanning more than 1,000 communities produced scale through capital, acquisitions, and care labor rather than code-native distribution. That failure mechanism appears inside Cova before scale—telephone support, caregiver training, clinical-service responsibility, payer compliance, and constrained Medicaid administration under the 80% direct-care compensation rule. Cova’s AI agents may lower overhead, but Cova still needs permission from Michigan, payers, and caregivers for every unit of production. “AI-native” describes the back office, not the business architecture.

Michigan allows eligible caregivers to enroll directly, so adding one caregiver to Cova does not automatically make Cova more valuable to every patient or caregiver already present. Over 18 months, intake and billing data could improve automation, but that is an internal learning curve, not a network effect; no case volume, proprietary dataset, referral loop, or measured cost advantage is public. Cova markets separately to caregivers, patients, and families, yet managed matching is not compounding liquidity when participants can bypass the agency. The Nth user must create value for the first user. Here, the Nth user mostly creates another case to administer.

Jordan Ibe brings VA medical-center exposure, while Ansh Tandon studied bioengineering and data science; both spent the prior year building AI for home-care agencies and hospitals before Cova became a provider. That is relevant immersion, but Cova has not shown the non-trainable specific knowledge that made a founder-product-market fit obvious: no public system deployed at scale, no meaningful open-source adoption, and Tandon’s visible GitHub project has two stars. Cova would move up a full tier if Ibe and Tandon could show that their agents processed thousands of real eligibility or billing cases with materially fewer denials than experienced agency staff. Specific knowledge becomes legible through artifacts.

Stack Overflow was built by developers who had spent years publicly thinking, writing, and shipping for developers; the founder signal existed before the financing story. Cova offers a thinner but positive parallel: Ibe and Tandon worked together for a year, recognized that selling software to agencies was insufficient, and accepted the harder burden of becoming licensed themselves. That reads as intelligence and energy. Cova provides no adverse integrity signal, but a clean prior venture, treatment of former colleagues, and ten-year operating history are also absent. At pre-seed, silence on integrity is neutral. Long-term games still require long-term evidence.

Cova’s prior year selling AI to agencies predicts its next move: by July 2029, Cova will have pivoted back from operating care directly to licensing enrollment and billing automation to other Midwest home-care agencies, reaching $1 million to $3 million in ARR. Cova can learn the workflow by owning it, but the $176.30 billion market rewards software geometry more than Michigan agency geometry. The provider is the laboratory. The software is the leverage.

Anchorage Digital is the strongest objection because permission can itself become a moat. Cova’s Michigan license, Home Help knowledge, EVV controls, and firsthand exposure to the Medicaid 80% compensation rule could let Cova automate a regulated system that outsiders merely model; the provider pivot may be the specific-knowledge acquisition strategy, not a retreat into labor. The objection fails today because Michigan permits direct caregiver enrollment, Cova discloses no case volume or cost advantage, and competitors Aidaly and Entyre already serve family caregivers. I would flip to Invest if Cova documents 1,000 active caregiver-patient relationships across at least three states while keeping administrative expense below 10% of reimbursement through its agents. Then permission becomes a moat and code becomes the scaling layer. Until then, I pass.
