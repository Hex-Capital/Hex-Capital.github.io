# Click -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Agents routinely pull professional, financial, mapping, travel, and social data without humans opening ten tabs. That future is plausible, but Click has not mapped the road to it. The dossier gives me MCP’s rise from a 2024 standard to more than 10,000 public servers by December 2025—the present wave—not the next system it unlocks. I see no intermediate transitions, dated future behavior, or mundane artifact proving Click will own that future rather than merely bundle today’s providers.

Aditya Asgaonkar is the strongest part of this company. His Ethereum Foundation research, consensus publications, and work on agent context engineering show real systems fluency acquired before Click. That is more than accelerator tourism. But the biography does not yet show a long-running obsession with information retrieval, a pre-company connector artifact users adopted, or evidence that this solo founder has recruited complementary commercial talent. The product’s movement from assistant frontend to operations automation to research connectors makes me wonder whether the obsession is agent infrastructure—or simply whichever agent surface currently has oxygen.

The irritation is real but shallowly specified. Users must visit LinkedIn, YouTube, Maps, financial databases, and travel sites separately; Click offers one MCP installation and charges $49 per month for 500 credits. But whose rent disappears? Which gatekeeper loses control? How much time or money returns to the user? Without those answers, convenience is being mistaken for a broken rule. The public traction—157 LinkedIn followers and NVIDIA Inception membership—does not show anyone repeatedly paying, rearranging work, or entrusting an important workflow to the product.

The bull case stings because the connector breadth could become a routing control plane. Exa, Crustdata, Google Maps, Reddit, X, YouTube, Apify, and financial-data services under one interface might generate valuable knowledge about which source answers which request. A protocol researcher could plausibly turn that operational exhaust into infrastructure. But the dossier labels that dataset only as inferred, while the current facts point the other way: no proprietary data or IP, no structural barrier, and dependency on providers that can change access or pricing. OpenAI already supplies native web search and remote MCP support. Click is presently sitting on top of the enabling layers, not controlling one beneath them.

The privacy architecture is also a gate, not paperwork. Tool arguments, responses, and results may be retained and transmitted to connector providers, while customers are told not to submit regulated or highly sensitive information. That limitation collides with the professional, financial, mapping, and travel queries that make the bundle useful. There is no 50/50 governance problem in a one-person company, but there is a different accountability risk: one founder, several product identities, and no evidence yet of a stable user truth anchoring the shifts.

I pass. My 36-month expectation is that Click pivots back toward operations automation or is discontinued as native agent platforms absorb generic connector routing. One fact would flip me: a verified twelve-month user cohort with at least 60% retention whose members repeatedly use three or more connectors. That would show the bundle creates durable, strange cross-source behavior rather than temporary MCP convenience—and would give me a real artifact from the future to follow.
