# Nebula Security -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Specific knowledge is not the PhD. It is the scar tissue. Nebula Security’s four founders have personally researched kernel fuzzing, browser exploitation, program analysis, and smart-contract security; spoken at Black Hat; reached DEF CON finals; earned more than $400,000 in bug bounties; and produced 98 public CVEs and eight Chrome zero-days. This is unusually dense evidence that the team has acted inside the exact problem. The founders are not borrowing a market narrative from AI security. They were finding vulnerabilities before the category became fashionable.

The distinction is between proving technical ability and owning a durable asset. Vega’s 1,393 validated findings show that the system can produce useful work, and exploit reproduction is a meaningful answer to model false positives. But the public CVEs and 1.3K-star exploit repository primarily demonstrate capability and reputation; they do not establish exclusive data, a proprietary model, or a feedback loop competitors cannot reproduce. Codex Security, Microsoft MDASH, RunSybil, XBOW, Depthfirst, and Hacktron are converging on adjacent workflows. If Vega’s code were copied tomorrow, Nebula would retain exceptional researchers and credibility. It has not yet shown that the product itself compounds independently of them.

The wedge has room to maneuver. Repository scanning can extend from ordinary application code into operating systems, browsers, infrastructure, agentic systems, Solidity, and EVM software, all sharing the capability to discover, validate, and remediate exploitable behavior. The $10.65 billion application-security market is large enough, and its reported 18.8% growth matters more than the narrower $550 million SAST category. The economic architecture is also sensible—metered access, per-repository enterprise plans, and fixed-price audits—but it remains architecture. No disclosed price, completed transaction, recurring revenue, or retention data proves direct value capture.

The public research could become permissionless distribution, but stars are not a network effect. The repository’s 1.3K stars and 364 forks show attention; the dossier does not show outsiders contributing findings, improving Vega’s models, or making the product more useful for the next customer. An enterprise private beta following a YC-company launch is similarly a distribution signal, not a customer-created value loop. Nebula may accumulate proprietary exploit-validation traces, but “could accumulate” is a future tense moat, not a present asset.

The strongest bull case is that founder-specific knowledge can override early commercial ambiguity: very few pre-seed teams can point to eight Chrome zero-days and $400,000 in bounty outcomes. That is enough to keep this from being a pass. But none of the exception mechanisms is established yet—no self-reinforcing network, no demonstrated capital efficiency around a strange new product language, and no evidence that a proprietary substrate is compounding. Conversely, the dossier shows no founder-integrity issue, manufactured financing deadline, dependence on reassurance, or refusal to update. The risk is structural, not behavioral.

I am neutral today. Nebula Security is a rare technical team inside a large market, but the present company looks more like highly leveraged expert labor than an autonomous asset that grows while the founders sleep. One condition would flip me to invest: a reproducible customer cohort showing that proprietary cross-repository validation data measurably improves Vega’s precision over a frozen baseline while at least 20 paying repositories retain the product for six months. That would prove both compounding advantage and direct value capture.
