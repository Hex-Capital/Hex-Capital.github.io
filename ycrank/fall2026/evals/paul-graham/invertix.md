# Invertix -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Invertix has apparently persuaded renewable-energy operators to buy something difficult to install. That interests me more than “energy superintelligence.” But the dossier skips the part I most want to see: an operator hit a particular obstacle, one of the founders built a workaround, and something changed within days. Embedded engineering could contain dozens of those stories. Without one, I cannot tell how Perrotta and Durmaz behave when their original plan stops working.

There is a real product signal here. Vireo reported more than **1.8 GW** managed through Invertix’s AI workers in May 2026. That deserves substantially more credit than an architecture diagram. It does not establish how accurately the software diagnoses faults or how much work it completes without human correction. The claimed physics-informed models have no disclosed comparative benchmarks. My pattern of backing technical teams with numerical proof only partly applies: capacity covered measures deployment reach; a measured reduction in missed faults would establish technical performance. The later-dated YC deployment update cannot close that gap.

The strongest argument for investing is the reported **“multiple six figures” in ARR**, reached within one month of financing. Renewable operators have an identifiable job: reconcile plant data, investigate faults, and get maintenance done. If they are paying to endure a roughly **60-day deployment**, the pain may be considerable. This is the narrow well I look for. But the revenue is company-reported, and the dossier supplies neither named paying customers nor renewal or repeat-use evidence. The customers’ combined **50 GW** portfolio is potential reach, not additional usage. The important question is how much work paying operators repeatedly entrust to Invertix.

Manual implementation could be precisely how the founders discover what to automate. I would not penalize it for being laborious. The danger is that each installation remains a separate engineering project while Delfos and Raptor Maps absorb the useful workflows into existing products. Customer-specific mappings could create switching costs, but customer-controlled data does not automatically produce a shared learning advantage. Invertix’s undisclosed pricing and delivery economics leave both outcomes plausible.

The founders worked together at Brightdale in early 2025 and became roommates before building the company. That is better evidence of a relationship than meeting for an accelerator application. Durmaz’s applied-AI work also supplies relevant technical context. Neither fact establishes a founder’s prior collision with renewable operations, however, and the dossier records no hypothesis rejected after customer feedback. Those are unanswered questions, not evidence of rigidity. TotalEnergies On participation likewise does not establish the partnership-dependence red flag; the reported existing revenue argues against treating program access as their entire distribution plan.

I would pass on this evidence. The working-product and real-world-pull exceptions deserve consideration: actual deployments and reported revenue make this a serious company. They do not yet supply unmistakable pull or independently validated proof strong enough to replace the missing founder test. My one verifiable flip condition is a customer-confirmed account of a deployment blocker that Perrotta or Durmaz personally overcame with a shipped change within weeks, followed by repeated operational use. That would reveal whether the implementation burden is where these founders learn fastest.
