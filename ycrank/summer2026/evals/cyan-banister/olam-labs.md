# Olam Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Om Buddhdev built Roblox games with roughly 6 million visits before becoming a staff engineer on AI agents at AI Dungeon, while Olam Labs now turns multiplayer games into behavioral laboratories. That makes Olam Labs a founder-lived gaming and agent-evaluation company whose strongest dimension is authentic problem proximity and whose weakest is the absence of physical, regulated, or capital-intensive machinery. The founders have been designing games together since middle school; they did not discover simulated social behavior in a market report. The artifact is real, but the moat still lives on a server.

Niantic is the high rung: John Hanke spent a decade inside maps and location systems before Ingress made that history playable. Om’s 6-million-visit Roblox work and Latitude experience give Olam Labs the same important shape—building games and agents before naming their intersection as a market. Olam Labs diverges because Niantic’s founder history compounded across a decade and multiple scaled products, while Shreshth Sharma’s reinforcement-learning work at RBC and Om’s record remain earlier and narrower. Still, Olam Labs is *of* this place, not visiting it. That matters more to me than the $8.20 billion TAM forecast.

GameCrush is the low rung’s failure mechanism: software spectacle without a hard technical, regulatory, or physical moat left the product vulnerable when governance and investors displaced its DNA. Olam Labs is also software-only today. Its 69,140 poker hands and 86,729 graded table-talk turns are interesting data, but no factory, license, sensor network, or frontier-hardware constraint prevents Patronus AI or General Intuition from entering. Olam Labs may be difficult intellectually; Olam Labs is not yet difficult industrially. Magically weird does not automatically mean structurally hard.

Patronus AI has raised $70 million, General Intuition $320 million, and yet Olam Labs is playing a stranger game than either: let humans and anonymous models bluff, negotiate, and expose themselves under equal rules. Over 18 months, that weirdness compounds only if each new environment produces longitudinal behavioral evidence that model labs cannot recreate cheaply. Olam Labs already has replayable reasoning traces, messages, actions, and ground-truth states; that is the seed of a data flywheel. The counterforce is consensus: “AI evaluation” is crowded, funded, and fashionable. Olam Labs is non-consensus in method, not category, so I would not assume the round is deeply underpriced.

Om Buddhdev is explicitly CEO while Shreshth Sharma is identified only as founder, which is better than the shared-title ambiguity I fear. Olam Labs would move into the top governance tier if Om held documented operating authority, founder vesting, and a written deadlock protocol. Without that evidence, Olam Labs still carries a subtle risk: two friends who have built together since middle school can mistake relational longevity for a tiebreaker. “We have always figured it out” is a rebuttal, not an answer. I need to know who decides when the friendship cannot.

HQ Trivia shows why a playable artifact can temporarily overpower governance anxiety: I could feel the product’s pull before the organization broke. Olam Labs offers a weaker but genuine version of that physical-world test. A person can enter the public Social Arena, play poker against models, and inspect what happened; 69,140 hands prove that Olam Labs has shipped more than a benchmark PDF. The Alpha arena displayed no ranked players at research time, however, so Olam Labs has observability without demonstrated human magnetism. I can touch the game, but I cannot yet feel a crowd gathering around it.

Olam Labs will, by July 2029, pivot from a public poker benchmark into contracted enterprise agent-safety evaluations and reach $2 million to $5 million in ARR, with social games serving primarily as data acquisition rather than the product customers buy. Olam Labs gets there because Om’s gaming experience makes environments engaging enough to generate traces, while Shreshth’s reinforcement-learning and data-pipeline experience makes those traces saleable. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing: the poker table is a sensor for deception, not the business.

Olam Labs’ strongest bear case stings: 69,140 hands may be a demo masquerading as a moat, LLM graders may certify their own artifacts, Pi harness sensitivity may scramble rankings, and Patronus AI can subsidize larger simulations from its $70 million war chest. Olam Labs also sits inside a consensus AI category, has no public revenue, and lacks the ugly regulatory or physical barrier I usually want. The bull mechanism still wins narrowly because the founders’ pre-company history and shipped dataset are unusually aligned for pre-seed, while a named CEO reduces—without eliminating—the GameCrush/HQ governance pattern. I would flip to Pass if Olam Labs cannot secure, within 18 months, a paid frontier-lab or enterprise contract worth at least $250,000 annually that validates these game rankings against deployed agent behavior.
