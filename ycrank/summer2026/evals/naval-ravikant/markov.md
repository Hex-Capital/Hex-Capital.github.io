# Markov -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders’ strongest credential is not IIT Madras or prior AI employment. It is the artifact: a two-person team produced 48,478 videos, roughly 12,300 hours of professional-software activity, and an AutoCAD environment retaining files, screenshots, model turns, and input events. That is specific knowledge exposed through action. The attribution is imperfect—the dossier does not show which founder built which system—but Markov has shipped directly into the problem it claims to understand.

The product has real distribution before conventional validation. More than 150,000 cumulative Hugging Face downloads, including 17,814 in the preceding month, show that researchers want the data. The bull case is that these public datasets become proprietary contracts: each lab supplies harder workflows, Markov turns them into environments and evaluators, and deployment compounds an unavailable corpus. A $3.2 billion training-data market growing toward $16.3 billion provides room to move from CAD, spreadsheets, and browsers into the broader evaluation infrastructure for computer-using agents.

But downloads are consumption, not a network. An added researcher does not yet contribute a trajectory, improve matching, or make Markov more useful to the next researcher. The permissionless distribution resembles one of my preferred patterns; the value-creation mechanism does not. At present, Markov performs the expensive collection work while users receive an 856 GB public asset. That is leverage for the user, not yet leverage for the company.

The defensibility is similarly split. The AutoCAD environment is harder to reproduce than a software wrapper, and building thousands of hours of synchronized recordings requires operational execution. Yet the flagship corpus is public under CC-BY-4.0, its recording-level chain of title is unclear, and research already shows that 312 human trajectories can be amplified with synthetic actions. Scale AI, Mercor, Turing, and Invisible possess buyer relationships and substantially greater resources. If a competitor copies the code, the public data comes with it; the proprietary feedback loop remains a hypothesis.

I will tolerate missing revenue when a prototype is unusually capital-efficient or user behavior is already self-reinforcing. Markov earns the first exception but not the second. There is no disclosed founder-conduct red flag, artificial deadline, or integrity violation, but the licensing ambiguity could become a structural veto for commercial buyers. Nor is there evidence of a payer, price, completed contract, or renewal. A “Talk to us” button proves intent to sell, not direct value capture.

I would not pass outright because the founders have built too much and distribution is too real. I would not invest yet because the company has produced a useful public resource without proving that it can retain the value it creates. My verifiable flip condition is three named AI laboratories renewing rights-cleared annual contracts totaling at least $1 million ARR for proprietary environments and trajectories. That would convert downloads into principals paying for an asset that compounds. Until then, Markov is a strong research project and a plausible data supplier, but not yet a durable network or proprietary substrate.
