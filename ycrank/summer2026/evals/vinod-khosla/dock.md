# Dock -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Dock does not quantify a consequence large enough to buy as an unlikely option. A $5.3 billion enterprise-agentic-AI market growing to $24.5 billion is a vendor forecast, not a societal cost pool. Gartner’s prediction that agents will enter 40% of enterprise applications establishes timing, but Dock never shows how shared documents and tables reduce labor, error rates, or operating costs by an order of magnitude. The stated problem is coordination friction. The promised outcome is better coordination. That is useful, but usefulness is not a 100x consequence.

The product is coherent: separate agent credentials, workspace-scoped permissions, real-time synchronization, REST/MCP access, attributed history, and human approval for sensitive actions. But the comparisons with Linear, Cursor, and Lindy are category distinctions, not technical benchmarks. Dock has measured no improvement in workflow completion time, human supervision, reliability, or cost. General-purpose artifacts may broaden the market, but they also place the company directly in the expansion path of well-capitalized platforms. With no proprietary learning loop or structural barrier identified, this currently looks like systems integration around standard agents.

The strongest bull case is the team. Govind Kavaturi joined Branch in its first year and participated in its growth from zero to $100 million in revenue; Mike Molinet co-founded Branch and served as COO; the pair then built Thena together. This is their third company together, and execution-heavy B2B software is one of my unstated exceptions to science-first investing. The closest precedent is Block: a small, adaptable team began without a 10x technical discontinuity. The material difference is that Block attacked a clearly enormous payments value pool, while Dock has not quantified the coordination spending it displaces or shown a wedge capable of becoming infrastructure. The founders’ history earns attention, but the dossier documents neither a belief revised after evidence nor a complementary technical recruit brought in to own a named risk.

The immediate kill risk is trust. A workspace intended to govern autonomous agents still stores webhook secrets and browser-session tokens recoverably or plainly, lacks a third-party penetration test and SOC 2 Type II attestation, and is not HIPAA-eligible. These are not administrative details; permissions are the product. SOC 2 targeted for the second half of 2026 is a roadmap item, not an experiment with a cost, pass threshold, and stop-or-pivot response. Conflicting invite-only and self-service messaging further obscures whether Dock is testing enterprise trust or lightweight adoption first.

The $19 and $49 monthly organization plans could become extremely efficient distribution, but there is no disclosed model connecting API actions, storage, support, and security costs to sustainable margins. Nor is there adoption evidence that overrides the technical concerns: the GitHub organization has one follower, its leading visible repository has one star, and Toolify reports no reviews. Those are not failures at pre-seed. They simply provide no evidence that users regard Dock as a system of record rather than a convenient interface that Linear, Replit, or another incumbent can absorb.

I pass. The downside is one check, but the upside remains an assertion rather than a measured discontinuity. I would reverse that decision if one independently observed 90-day deployment across ten paying organizations showed Dock reducing human handoff time per completed multi-agent workflow by at least 80% versus the same agents operating through existing documents and issue trackers, without a permission-boundary breach.
