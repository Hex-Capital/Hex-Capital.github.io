# Vestris -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first three numbers are missing: proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely the first scarce institutional check. A Launch YC score of 10 is not evidence of scarcity or an auction. It is noise. Without terms, I cannot tell whether Vestris is a cheap bet on an ignored operational problem or another AI thesis already bid into consensus. Under my framework, financing silence receives little credit.

There is a plausible venture moment, but Vestris has not named the capital-funded experiment. The product currently covers Washington, D.C., and Montgomery County; expansion requires learning jurisdiction-specific portals, calculations, and legal exceptions. A finite test could ask whether the system can enter a third jurisdiction while preserving human-approved accuracy and the reported reduction from 8–10 hours to one hour per file. The dossier, however, supplies no proceeds, deadline, pass/fail metric, or explanation of why capital—as opposed to customer revenue—must fund that test. I cannot award points for the experiment the founders might propose.

The strongest evidence is inside the existing title-shop workflow. One pilot customer settles millions of dollars of transactions monthly, reportedly saves about seven hours per file, and has doubled processing capacity. That is materially better than followers, integrations, or an AI demo: the software is acting on live documents, county portals, prorations, commitments, and closing disclosures. But it is still one unpaid-or-undisclosed pilot. Vestris has not shown that the workflow caused a purchase, renewal, referral, or retained paid usage. Its decades of approved and rejected title records could become a proprietary data pool, but ownership, exclusivity, dataset size, and rights to reuse the outcomes are all undisclosed.

The founders are the real reason not to dismiss this. Valliani and Tang both processed closing files before building the product; Valliani previously founded a software project and is named on three patents, while Tang shipped telemetry and communications systems for Michigan Formula SAE. They sit between two mistrustful systems—fragmented title operations and software agents—and have operated on both sides. That is the right translation-layer pattern. The uncomfortable counterpoint is that they are roommates, best friends, and equal co-founders with no disclosed accountable decision-maker. In a security-sensitive product that can affect legal title, “we decide together” is not an adequate operating model.

The AI economics remain unproven. Human approval is the correct safety architecture, but it may also leave Vestris with labor-heavy exception handling precisely where county variation and legal nuance are greatest. There is no revenue, price per file, gross margin, burn, runway, or route to break-even without another round. Qualia, Flueid, and Stavvy already control systems, integrations, or underwriting relationships that Vestris may otherwise need to rent. The known-exception bar is measured accuracy plus paid conversion and retention; a company-reported speed improvement at one pilot does not clear it.

I pass today. The product may be valuable, and the founder-workflow fit is unusually credible, but I would currently be buying unknown pricing, an unnamed capital experiment, and undemonstrated cash economics. One verifiable flip condition would be a signed sub-$10 million post-money first institutional round whose written 12-month milestone is ten paying agencies across three jurisdictions, retained for six months at positive contribution margin. That would establish both scarce pricing and the truth this check is meant to reveal.
