# Evergrove -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The impressive fact is not that Evergrove has built a voice agent. It is that two people have put one through 350,000 production calls involving IVRs, holds, transfers, retries, protected health information, and updates to claims systems. More than 500,000 calls are now scheduled annually for managed-care organizations and specialist physical-therapy networks. This is a working machine in an environment where demos usually die on contact with reality. The reported 40% increase in workflow completion and greater than 60% cost reduction make the technical proof unusually strong for pre-seed.

But I cannot yet see the founders clearly enough through the machine. The dossier names many obstacles Evergrove handles, but not one obstacle that blocked Sid Unnithan and Jacob Chia, what they personally built around it, how long that took, and what changed afterward. Perhaps the three-week integration process contains several such stories. They are simply not documented. For a people-first investor, production scale cannot prove every aspect of resourcefulness by itself.

The user well looks narrow and deep. Workers’ compensation care coordinators have an urgent, repetitive job conducted through antiquated phone, fax, and email systems. These users are not experimenting with a generic AI assistant; they are entrusting Evergrove with recurring production workflows. The call volume and completion improvement are stronger evidence than pilots or letters of intent, though the absence of customer names, revenue, retention, and expansion data keeps me from concluding that customers are pulling the product rather than merely processing calls through it.

The founders plausibly encountered this problem organically. Both worked together at Newfront, where Sid led AI products and Jacob built authentication, insurance-file infrastructure, and automated placements. They therefore knew the ugly part before choosing the fashionable part. HIPAA controls, BAAs, audit trails, system integrations, human escalation, and unreliable phone trees constitute a genuine schlep-heavy wedge. But the dossier describes founder-market fit retrospectively; it does not identify the particular failure at Newfront that caused them to start Evergrove.

The strongest bear case is that this is excellent implementation inside a feature market. Liberate already offers insurance-native voice agents, while CLARA, FurtherAI, and Gradient AI sell adjacent automation to overlapping buyers. Evergrove has disclosed neither proprietary-data scale nor structural switching costs. A demo-led enterprise motion centered partly on industry events could become slow conventional selling, and there is no evidence yet of the founders changing product direction when customers contradict them. Production calls are a concrete proof spike—one of the patterns I care about—but call volume without revenue or retention can conceal commoditization.

I am neutral today, despite evidence that points upward. The working product is strong enough to prevent a pass, but it does not replace the missing founder-action sequence required by my gate. One verifiable fact would flip me: show a dated deployment blocker, the workaround the founders shipped within days or weeks, and a resulting renewal or customer expansion. That would connect the impressive artifact to the people who made it happen.
