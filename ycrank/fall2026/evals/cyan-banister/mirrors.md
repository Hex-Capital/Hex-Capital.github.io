# Mirrors -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine an engineer teaching a fleet of agents one operational lesson without rewriting every agent’s instructions. Getting there requires reliable recognition of situations, safe delivery of corrections, and evidence that those corrections survive changes in models and tools. Mirrors has a concrete piece of that machinery: reviewed guidance delivered at the user-turn boundary. But the dossier establishes a present engineering workflow, not yet the chain connecting that workflow to a different future. I would pass with my own money today.

Why does every failed conversation become another exception in a global prompt? That is the useful question here. Mirrors lets engineers scope a correction to the situation where it belongs, preview matches, and activate it deliberately. The agency returned is specific: engineers can change one behavior without making that instruction everybody else’s problem. There is recurring labor to remove, although the dossier neither quantifies that burden nor identifies a gatekeeper collecting a rent. Custom pricing also leaves the economic value of a correction unestablished.

Aryan Singhal is the strongest reason to keep looking. Four high-school AI/ML papers associated with NeurIPS and EMNLP, research at UT Austin and UCSB, and a shipped GPU-optimization project are evidence of building before this particular company. That is more meaningful to me than the accelerator badge. The record supports technical curiosity and independent effort; it supplies less evidence of recruiting complementary people or understanding how customers turn this particular capability into a budget. The change from session replay to runtime guidance does not establish opportunism. It does make the continuity of the underlying obsession worth understanding.

The strongest argument against my pass is that the supposedly mundane repair could become essential infrastructure. Mirrors fits my pattern of looking beneath a visible application wave for the machinery it needs. Its documented shadow and off modes recognize that delivering the wrong lesson can be worse than delivering none. The SDK even distinguishes an accepted server decision from context actually being applied or an agent succeeding. Those distinctions suggest attention to operating complexity. But placement beneath an agent does not establish ownership of a scarce dependency. Papaya approaches correction through approved pull requests; Mem0 approaches context through persistent memory. Mirrors must establish why conditional guidance deserves an enduring, separate place in that stack.

The displayed **711,933 agent sessions** could conceal something much stronger than this dossier reveals. Engineers repeatedly trusting production conversations to an outside runtime would be meaningful behavior. Without a period, customer breakdown, or attribution to the current product, however, the counter cannot establish that behavior. My exceptions for pre-revenue companies remain available: a convincing prototype and unusually strong founder evidence can outweigh missing sales. Here, the documented implementation earns some credit, but neither repeated user commitment nor a difficult technical constraint has been demonstrated strongly enough to carry the bet.

I do not see evidence of the severe privacy problems, concealed leadership, or dependence on one closed model supplier that would make this an active rejection. Those gates are unresolved rather than failed; production conversation handling makes consent and custody consequential. My one verifiable flip condition is a documented customer renewal following a controlled production comparison showing that Mirrors prevents recurring failures across two agent frameworks without continual bespoke intervention from its founders. That would turn a useful correction mechanism into evidence of a portable dependency customers choose to keep.
