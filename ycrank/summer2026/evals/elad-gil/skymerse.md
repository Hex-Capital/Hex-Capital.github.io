# Skymerse -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: aviation operators can now apply domain-specific AI to fragmented operational data, but Skymerse becomes a large company only if it can safely eliminate a material share of the claimed three hours of planning work per flight—not merely deliver better NOTAM alerts.

The governing variable is therefore the percentage of dispatcher work Skymerse can automate without requiring parallel manual reconciliation. The LOT Polish Airlines deployment is relevant: network-wide monitoring, roughly 30-second processing, delivery in under three minutes, and 15–20 filtered alerts per day demonstrate operational utility. But the dossier never measures dispatcher minutes eliminated, and Notamify explicitly remains a non-authoritative source. The evidence establishes faster information processing, not yet trusted decision automation.

The strongest argument for investing is the pull that appeared before the broader story was polished. Notamify reports 3,800 registered users, thousands of daily users, three million processed NOTAMs, and a named airline deployment. This is unusually substantive pre-seed evidence. Damian Szumski also understands the workflow as a licensed dispatcher with experience at LOT, Lufthansa Systems, FLYR, and Flightradar24. The product transition from transcription to NOTAM intelligence and then flight operations could represent disciplined discovery rather than drift.

The architecture is more durable than a generic aviation chatbot. Skymerse structures affected runways, procedures, navigation aids, timing, and applicability, then proposes actions using schedules, weather, aircraft limits, MELs, manuals, and company procedures. Better models should improve this system. Yet the production proof is concentrated in monitoring, while the end-to-end operational layer remains demo-led. No documented permissions, feedback loop, or responsibility for final decisions shows that Skymerse owns the completed workflow today.

The economic discontinuity is the weak point. The cited flight-operations market is approximately $1.03 billion and growing 8.25% annually—real, but not being visibly reconstructed. Skymerse estimates three hours of planning per flight without quantifying realized labor savings, pricing power, or customer ROI. Meanwhile, Jeppesen ForeFlight launched Airflow with aviation data, customer data, certification knowledge, and contextual reasoning in July 2026. That is adverse evidence: the technical shift may improve the incumbent’s product as readily as it expands Skymerse’s market. Three million processed NOTAMs sound impressive, but the dossier does not estimate what it would cost Jeppesen or another funded competitor to reproduce the schema.

My 36-month base case is that Skymerse remains a useful sub-$5 million ARR aviation-operations product, with Notamify still carrying the business rather than a certified autopilot becoming the system of action. I would pass today but keep watching. The single flip condition is an audited paid deployment at a second airline showing that Skymerse eliminates at least 50% of per-flight planning time without parallel manual reconciliation. That would convert the current usage signal into evidence that the market’s controlling variable is actually moving.
