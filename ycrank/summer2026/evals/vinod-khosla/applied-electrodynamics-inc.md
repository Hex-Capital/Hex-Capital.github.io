# Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The attractive option is not a $134 million through-wall-radar market. It is eliminating a meaningful portion of the more than $20 billion allegedly lost each year to construction rework by making hidden infrastructure visible before crews cut, drill, or demolish. That is a real cost pool, but Applied Electrodynamics has not quantified how much WaveSight can remove, what population or infrastructure benefits, or how imaging becomes a 10–100x change rather than a better inspection tool. The consequence is potentially large; the dossier does not yet make it enormous.

The technical claim is much more interesting than the market estimate. A handheld system producing 1 mm point clouds from across a room would represent a material capability jump over Walabot’s $199.95 contact-based scanner with four-inch penetration. But this is a company assertion without an independent benchmark, specification sheet, field dataset, or disclosed test method. Radio penetration already exists at Vayyar and Camero-Tech. The investment question is therefore not whether radio sees through walls; it is whether this team has solved stand-off reconstruction across heterogeneous materials at construction-grade accuracy. Today there is no auditable evidence that it has.

The strongest bull case is the team. These are not generic AI founders assembling commodity components. Bilgehan Avser led antenna work across four iPhone generations; Brian Huppi worked on iPhone multitouch and the first Nest thermostat; Paul Leutheuser shipped product-design systems at Apple; George McLean worked on semiconductor imagers, retinal implants, and optical sensors. They have worked together for more than eight years and span RF, sensing, physics, mechanical design, and production hardware. That combination fits the pattern of scarce technical workflows becoming repeatable platforms. What is missing is equally specific: no documented belief revision, no risk-mapped recruiting, and no evidence that the claimed 250-plus patents belong to this company rather than former employers.

The next dollars should attack one kill risk: independently reproduce 1 mm localization from room distance across sheetrock, tile, marble, quartz, brick, and insulation, with concealed utilities whose positions are verified after scanning. The test needs predefined accuracy, false-positive, scan-time, and repeatability thresholds. Instead, the public milestones are preorder registration, conference attendance, and “hundreds of conversations.” Simultaneously seeking construction, industrial-inspection, cargo, and defense introductions is premature while each market requires different validation and the core performance claim remains unproven.

The economics are also blank. No unit price, bill of materials, manufacturing yield, deployment cost, or customer-payback model is public. A contractor may pay far more than Walabot’s price if one scan prevents a costly mistake, but that hypothesis is not a scale model. The product must beat incumbent inspection economics without depending on specialized operators or government procurement margins. Applied Electrodynamics has not yet shown that path.

My base case is a capable team producing a useful niche inspection instrument, not yet a company that rewrites construction economics. The exception case is credible because the founders have unusually relevant hardware depth and the underlying rework pool is large. But exceptional biographies cannot substitute for the cheapest decisive experiment. I pass today. One verifiable result flips me: an independent, blinded field trial showing repeatable millimeter-class stand-off localization across representative wall assemblies, accompanied by contractor purchase commitments demonstrating payback within several avoided rework events.
