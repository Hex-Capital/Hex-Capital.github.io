# Stoa -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Compute Exchange already aggregates procurement across more than 100 compute providers, while Ornn raised $33 million to turn compute into a commodity. Stoa is a frontier-hardware bazaar whose strongest feature is proximity to expensive, regulated physical infrastructure and whose weakest feature is being late to a suddenly fashionable trade. Stoa may look magically weird from outside the data center, but inside Summer 2026 it looks like several people discovered the same GPU-market thesis at once.

**Crusoe** put capital directly into oilfield machinery and made an ugly physical constraint—the gas flare—the heart of the business. Stoa shares the load-bearing mechanism: fragmented physical assets become more valuable when someone standardizes price discovery, counterparties, and settlement. Stoa diverges because Stoa does not take title, inspect GPUs, or assume counterparty disputes; Stoa is selling software around the rocket rather than building the rocket. That still places Stoa above ordinary AI-app SaaS on the frontier axis, but well below Crusoe’s operational and capital commitment.

**HQ Trivia** exposed what happens when visible heat outruns structural depth: tactile excitement can override discipline for a while, then the weak foundation collects its debt. Stoa has a different product, but Stoa shows the same danger on the non-consensus dimension: AI hardware is already crowded with Compute Exchange, Shadeform, and a freshly financed Ornn. No disclosed transaction volume, participant count, investor rejection, or unpopular technical wager makes Stoa underpriced. The idea is sensible; “sensible at the exact moment everyone agrees” is not magically weird.

Kaan Yigit developed quantitative systems on Uniper’s commodities desk, which is Stoa’s best founder-origin evidence. Over 18 months, that experience could compound into market design, dealer trust, and an instinct for where quotes become executable rather than theatrical. Stoa’s problem is that no founder is shown buying, financing, inspecting, or operating GPU fleets before Stoa: Berat Celik brings Cornell Tech and fintech software, while Eren Berke Saglam brings Dartmouth and Series 7 and 63 registrations. Those are useful instruments, but I cannot yet tell whether the founders are *of* the GPU market or visiting it with excellent résumés.

Berat Celik is explicitly Stoa’s CEO, so Stoa clears the first governance gate that many three-founder companies miss. The counterfactual is simple: Stoa would move into my invest tier if Berat held documented tiebreaker authority, founder vesting, and an operating agreement covering an irreconcilable dispute. No public co-founder history, equity split, or conflict protocol is available, so Stoa receives only modest credit for the title. Three accomplished co-founders can produce Biz, Tiz, and Rizz—or three steering wheels.

**Niantic** let me play Ingress and feel the product before Pokémon Go made the thesis obvious. Stoa offers a public RFQ interface covering A100, H100, H200, B200, B300, and GB200 NVL hardware, so Stoa has more physical-world verifiability than an abstract developer API. The parallel stops at the loading dock: access is institutional, Stoa does not inspect the hardware, and Stoa leaves conformity diligence with users. I can observe the marketplace, but I cannot yet watch a verified GPU move from seller to buyer and know Stoa made the transaction safer.

Stoa’s undisclosed RFQ volume and transaction count point to a specific 36-month outcome: by July 2029, Stoa will have pivoted from promising an independent liquid GPU exchange to selling GPU pricing data, collateral intelligence, and settlement software, producing $1 million–$3 million in ARR without becoming the primary physical marketplace. Stoa can collect valuable exhaust even if Stoa never creates the fire; the 0% fee for eligible data partners is already a clue that the dataset may matter more than transaction fees.

Stoa’s strongest bull case is the **DeepMind** exception: three cross-disciplinary founders—commodities quant development, computer science and economics, plus securities registrations—could be polymaths whose credential density masks genuine synthesis. Stoa’s public RFQ product and privacy-preserving data-partner program could seed a proprietary clearing-price network before reported revenue appears. The objection stings, but Compute Exchange’s 100-provider reach and Stoa’s absence of disclosed participants or completed trades currently win. One verifiable condition flips my verdict: Stoa clearing at least $25 million of annualized physical-hardware GMV with repeat institutional counterparties.
