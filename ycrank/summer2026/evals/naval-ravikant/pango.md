# Pango -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the agentic language; it is that Steve Rahimi has spent seven years inside e-commerce, completed reverse-logistics research in 2025, and helped put Pango into production with 30 brands. Lukasz Reszczynski previously built a patented configurator used across more than $1.5 billion in quotes. These are artifacts of action, not résumé adjectives. The founders have shipped configurable operational systems into closely related problems, and Pango’s October 2024 Shopify launch shows they began learning before YC.

The weakness appears when I copy the code. Pango still has 100-plus integrations, customer policies, and exception workflows across 40 countries, so replication is more difficult than cloning an interface. But integration breadth is accumulated labor, not automatically leverage. Narvar and parcelLab already sell autonomous post-purchase agents; Loop and AfterShip overlap on returns, tracking, fraud, and workflows. Pango has not shown that resolved exceptions create an exclusive model, that performance improves across merchants, or that switching costs compound faster than incumbents can match features.

The market offers room to maneuver, but the path is only partially proven. The narrow returns-software category was estimated at $1.799 billion in 2025 and $3.197 billion by 2032. Pango reaches beyond it into transportation management, warehouse execution, support, and checkout, all linked by the same policy-and-integration layer. That is a credible adjacency map. It is not yet evidence that one buyer will consolidate those systems around a five-person vendor, especially when broader competitors have raised between $64 million and at least $112 million.

The bull case is that the company is already unusually deployed for pre-seed: five named customers, 30 signed brands, five perfect Shopify reviews, and company-reported automation of 99% of shipping and returns work with a 20% operating-cost reduction. If those figures survive customer verification, Pango may become the operating layer rather than another returns application. The counterweight is economic: published per-order and negotiated-plan pricing identifies how value can be captured, but the dossier supplies no realized revenue, take rate, retention, or gross margin. Signed usage is evidence of demand; it is not yet evidence of a compounding business.

The unstated pattern I want—outsiders contributing supply, knowledge, or distribution that improves the system for everyone else—is absent. Each merchant currently appears to receive a private automation system assembled from its own stack and policies. No referral, liquidity, contribution, or cross-customer learning loop is demonstrated. Nor does a known exception rescue the missing loop: Pango is neither an unexplained new behavior spreading organically nor a strange pre-launch product whose exceptional craft makes conventional evidence irrelevant. There is no disclosed founder-integrity, reassurance, or artificial-deadline red flag, although the New York–Stockholm–Pango AB disclosure mismatch should be cleaned up before handling more sensitive merchant permissions.

I would not reject the founders; I would reject premature certainty about the substrate. My base case is a useful integration-heavy commerce SaaS company, potentially reaching $5–10 million ARR within 36 months, but without evidence yet that it becomes an independent, nonlinear outcome. I would invest only if Pango verifies one flip condition: cohorts showing that accumulated cross-merchant exception data produces materially better automation and net retention over time than merchant-specific rules alone. Then every deployment would create an asset; today, every deployment mainly creates another implementation.
