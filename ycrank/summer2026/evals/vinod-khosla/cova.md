# Cova -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence could be large, but Cova has not quantified it. Home care is a $162.35 billion U.S. market, enrollment takes weeks, and delayed approval leaves relatives unpaid. Yet the dossier does not establish how much of that spending is administrative waste, how many applicants abandon care, or whether automation changes caregiver availability by 2% or 100%. A large market is not itself a large consequence. I see a worthwhile service, not yet an option on a rewritten healthcare cost curve.

The strongest evidence is the founders’ behavior. Jordan Ibe and Ansh Tandon worked inside Palantir Healthcare, spent a year building AI systems for agencies and hospitals, and then converted their prior Omnition software into the operating system of a licensed Michigan provider. That is a substantive belief revision: instead of selling tools to incumbents, they became the incumbent and exposed themselves to reimbursement, compliance, and workflow failures. Their software-engineering, deployment, and bioengineering backgrounds fit the problem, although the dossier shows neither a difficult complementary recruit nor an example of revising the operating model after live-care evidence.

Owning the agency is clever because normal operations can produce exception-handling and payer-workflow data. But potential data is not a moat. Cova claims an end-to-end back office with no non-caregiver headcount while applicants still complete four state forms, photograph every page, and may require telephone support. There is no measured enrollment-time reduction, administrative-cost comparison, error rate, or automation benchmark against Aidaly, Honor, or a conventional agency. Until those numbers exist, this is implementation labor around available AI models, not a demonstrated 5–10x discontinuity.

Licensure and a live caregiver enrollment are real pre-seed milestones. They prove that the team can cross an initial regulatory boundary. They do not reveal the largest remaining kill risk or the experiment being funded to resolve it. The dossier gives no payer contract, reimbursement rate, claim-acceptance rate, contribution margin, or pass/fail threshold for agent reliability. With only $500,000 of reported financing, the right sequence is not geographic expansion or referral acquisition. It is proving that one Michigan cohort can enroll, bill, and remain compliant at radically lower administrative cost than a staffed agency.

The bull case is the known exception: an execution-heavy wedge can become infrastructure when a tiny team attacks a giant existing value pool and lets the route evolve. Cova’s licensed-provider strategy and founders’ willingness to abandon a pure software posture support that possibility. The material difference is that no platform expansion or radical operating leverage has been demonstrated. The broader pattern of successful technology-enabled marketplaces and service operators prevents me from dismissing Cova as “just an agency,” but the AI red flag remains: funded competitors can reproduce back-office automation unless usage creates a proprietary learning loop with measurable economic advantage.

I pass today. My verifiable flip condition is a Michigan cohort showing at least a 5x reduction in non-caregiver administrative cost per active case versus a conventional agency, while maintaining payer-compliant billing and positive contribution margin for six consecutive months. Without that proof, I expect Cova to remain a competent regional provider or revert toward agency software—not become a consequential new healthcare infrastructure layer.
