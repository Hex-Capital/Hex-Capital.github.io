# Dock -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years from now, companies may operate through constellations of specialized agents that share memory, negotiate permissions, and leave an inspectable record while humans intervene only at consequential moments. Dock has built a small leak from that future: shared documents and tables, separate agent credentials, workspace-scoped roles, MCP access, real-time synchronization, attribution, and human approval for sensitive actions. But the dossier skips the path between this product and that future. Gartner’s prediction that agents will enter 40% of enterprise applications describes today’s wave; it does not establish why a general-purpose workspace becomes the durable coordination layer rather than a feature inside Linear, Cursor, Replit, or Relevance AI.

The founders are the strongest part of the bet. Govind Kavaturi joined Branch in its first year and participated in its growth from zero to $100 million in revenue; Mike Molinet co-founded Branch and served as COO; both subsequently built Thena, and Dock is their third company together. That is real Biz and unusually strong evidence that they understand enterprise distribution, customer communication, and the hidden operations behind B2B software. It is not yet evidence of Tizz. The dossier contains no dated pre-Dock experiments, operating wound, unusual discovery artifact, or independently developed conviction showing that multi-agent coordination obsessed them before it became a fashionable category.

Dock identifies a concrete workflow irritation—outputs trapped in private agent chats with fragmented permissions and attribution—but not a broken rule or captured rent. Who loses money today? Who controls an artificial gate? Whose occupational agency is returned when Dock succeeds? The $19 Pro and $49 Scale plans are accessible, yet pricing is not evidence that an institution will surrender control or that users receive meaningful economic leverage. This currently looks like cleaner coordination, not the dismantling of a concealed system.

The bull case stings because the product is more substantial than its public footprint suggests. Six repositories, documented REST/MCP interfaces, agent-specific keys, replayable history, and approval gates could become neutral infrastructure precisely because individual application vendors prefer proprietary agent silos. Two repeat founders with enterprise operating experience may recognize this before public metrics appear. Pre-revenue alone does not defeat that case; a working artifact plus demanding institutional trials could justify an exception.

But Dock has not shown the costly behavior that would activate that exception. One GitHub follower, one star on its most visible repository, zero Toolify reviews, and no disclosed customers, revenue, cohorts, or repeat workflows reveal no human rearranging work around the product. More seriously, a workspace asking companies to entrust it with agent credentials and operational history still stores webhook secrets and browser-session tokens recoverably or plainly server-side, lacks a third-party penetration test, and is not yet SOC 2 Type II attested. Privacy and custody are not later-stage polish when custody is the product.

My 36-month base case is that these capable founders pivot Dock toward a narrower customer-operations workflow adjacent to their Branch and Thena experience, because domain-specific distribution will outrun an undifferentiated company-wide agent canvas. The red flags reinforce that forecast: two co-founders are identified without a stated decision tiebreaker, the homepage and documentation conflict on invite-only versus self-serve access, and the product controls no scarce data, model, or technical bottleneck competitors cannot reproduce. I pass today. One verifiable condition flips me: twenty organizations each running at least three agents through Dock weekly for eight consecutive weeks, with audited retention and no unresolved credential-custody findings.
