# GodHands -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders encountered knowledge loss at Graviton and built something to address it. That matters more than the IIT degrees. Bommakanti’s low-latency systems work and NeurIPS research establish technical ability; they do not establish knowledge of selling workflow software to support executives. GodHands has moved beyond describing the problem, but the evidence of exact-domain mastery remains modest: a desktop product and an anonymous customer account of documenting escalations within two weeks.

The market gives this team room to maneuver. Capturing a support escalation can lead into employee onboarding, operations playbooks, sales procedures, and eventually agent execution. The shared capability is observing work and preserving its context. Scribe’s company-reported $100 million ARR establishes that businesses already spend meaningful money on this family of problems. GodHands does not need an invented trillion-dollar TAM. It needs to earn ownership of a valuable part of an existing workflow.

My difficulty is what GodHands owns after the workflow has been captured. Customer-specific playbooks might become a durable asset, but the dossier establishes neither migration difficulty nor a proprietary learning mechanism. Scribe already offers workflow context and agents; Mimica learns from observed activity. Competitors overlapping the feature set does not prove GodHands is technically trivial. It does mean that “we watch work and generate instructions” cannot carry the investment argument. Nor does the dossier show that another customer makes the product more useful for existing customers. A collection of private deployments is not automatically a network.

The strongest argument against passing is that the valuable information lives in exceptions. A screenshot records what someone clicked; a useful operational memory captures why an experienced employee departed from the normal procedure. The two-week escalation testimonial is an early reason to investigate that distinction. GodHands fits my preference for turning individual knowledge into scalable output. If its observation system reliably extracts judgment that competing tools miss, the founders could build something much more consequential than documentation software. But that advantage is still a hypothesis. The testimonial demonstrates a useful result, without establishing differentiated judgment capture or compounding improvement.

I make exceptions for missing monetization when a network is visibly forming, and for missing distribution when an exceptional prototype and demonstrated capital efficiency justify the bet. Neither override is established here. Pricing and revenue are undisclosed, so I assign uncertainty rather than inventing weak economics. The shift from engineering memory in August 2026 to broader operations also does not establish repeated abandonment. There is no documented integrity veto. The discrepancy between execution marketing and read-only security language does, however, leave the product’s actual capability unresolved. Employee replacement anxiety adds friction to collecting the very observations on which the product depends.

I would pass with my own money. GodHands has a credible problem and capable builders, but the evidence currently supports useful software more strongly than an enduring asset. One verifiable condition would change my decision: a paid customer renewal supported by a controlled comparison showing that accumulated GodHands corrections produce materially better agent task completion than a fresh competing system given the same exported playbooks. That would demonstrate that operating the product creates something competitors cannot recover merely by copying its interface.
