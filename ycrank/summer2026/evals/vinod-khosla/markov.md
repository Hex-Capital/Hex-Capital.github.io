# Markov -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

150,000-plus Hugging Face downloads make **Markov** a consequence-rich, miracle-poor AI infrastructure bet. At maximum ambition, **Markov** supplies the experience layer that lets computer agents replace human operation of Salesforce, AutoCAD, Photoshop, Excel, and VS Code—the economic mechanism that breaks is wages paid for routine computer work. That is potentially part of the $15 trillion labor displacement I care about. Yet 10,000 hours of recordings do not constitute a technical breakthrough; they constitute an early data asset in a market where collection may become commodity. Markov’s analytical signature is high consequence magnitude paired with low consensus-repelling technical risk.

**OpenAI** (2019) is the high rung on consequence because better intelligence did not capture an existing market; it threatened to reorganize every knowledge-work market. **Markov** shares the load-bearing mechanism: computer-use training data can help models progress from answering questions to performing economically valuable work. But Markov diverges where OpenAI owned the model, the learning loop, and the path to general capability. Markov released 48,478 videos under CC-BY-4.0, cites a training-data TAM of only $16.3 billion by 2033, and discloses no revenue. Markov could enable a civilizational transition without capturing civilizational value.

A **HubSpot-style GPT-4 sales-call summarizer** fails my technical-risk test because any competent product group can reproduce the stack before the startup compounds an advantage. That low-rung mechanism appears uncomfortably often in **Markov**: no structural barrier is identified, Turing has already delivered more than 10,000 supervised GUI tasks, and Scale AI or Surge AI can finance comparable collection. Markov has perhaps one required miracle—turning rights-cleared human trajectories into a continuously improving proprietary environment—but the dossier documents recordings and annotations, not new learning science. This triggers my red flag against API- or service-deep businesses without a proprietary stack.

Dev Mandal’s Sarvam AI experience and IIT Madras aerospace background keep **Markov** from looking like career data-labeling experts extrapolating the past. Over the next 18 months, founder defiance compounds only if **Markov** proves a controversial thesis—for example, that authentic human trajectories outperform synthetic rollouts after models become competent—and then builds collection around the resulting failure modes. The dossier shows no public contradiction of field consensus, while Markov’s positioning follows the widely accepted 2024–2025 rise of computer-use agents. Youth is not contrarianism. Experts are often wrong, but merely arriving after Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google establish a category is not defying them.

Harish Ashok previously founded Zenith, while Dev Mandal built doubt.ai to 700 users and 5,000 resolved questions in four weeks. Those facts give **Markov** modest evidence of iteration speed, but the dossier records no pivot, discarded thesis, or explicit “we were wrong” moment. The score would flip upward if **Markov** showed that the founders used the 150,000 downloads to reject an open-dataset strategy, moved into proprietary RL environments, and won repeat deployment from a named frontier laboratory. Until then, prior projects demonstrate motion, not the learning-rate evidence that made the Slash pivot exceptional.

**Commonwealth Fusion** (2019) required a $115 million Series A because the commercial milestone could not be reached with ordinary venture arithmetic. **Markov** presents the opposite capital profile: two founders, freely distributed datasets, an inferred negotiated-contract model, and no named milestone requiring more than $50 million. Markov may need substantial contributor acquisition and privacy infrastructure, but those are scalable operating expenses rather than capital-hostile science. My money adds little advantage when Scale AI, Turing, or a well-funded laboratory can fund the same roadmap from operating budgets.

48,478 CC-BY-4.0 videos point to a specific 36-month forecast: **Markov** will pivot from publishing open GUI datasets into proprietary RL environments and managed data contracts, reaching roughly $5 million to $12 million in ARR by July 2029 rather than remaining a standalone open-data supplier. **Markov** will make that move because the consequence creates real customer demand while the absence of a technical miracle prevents durable capture at the raw-dataset layer. Make a great company and the money takes care of itself—but first Markov must move from distributing files to owning the learning loop.

**DoorDash** (2013) is the strongest objection to my caution: a capital-light YC company can turn operational execution and distribution into a consequential platform without scientific risk. My unstated pattern includes precisely these consensus-looking bets, and **Markov** already has 150,000 downloads, 10,000-plus recording hours, founder reach, and excellent timing. The bull mechanism is that open data becomes developer distribution, developer distribution attracts laboratories, and laboratory work generates proprietary tasks unavailable to Scale AI. The evidence that wins today is CC-BY substitution, Turing’s existing GUI capability, and Markov’s absence of a disclosed paying customer or exclusive contributor-rights system. A multi-year contract worth at least $5 million with a named frontier laboratory, coupled with exclusive workflow rights and a proprietary evaluation loop, would flip my verdict to Invest.
