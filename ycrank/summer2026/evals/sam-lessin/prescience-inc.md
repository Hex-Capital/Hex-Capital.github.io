# Prescience, Inc. -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I begin with three blanks: no proposed post-money valuation, no competing-term-sheet count, and no evidence that this is genuinely the first scarce check. I will not manufacture scarcity from the fact that Prescience is pre-seed. The company could be an unfashionable infrastructure bet or another auctioned YC AI company; the dossier does not tell me. That matters because healthcare’s $1.6446 trillion spending pool and “superintelligence” framing make consensus easy to sell and expensive to buy.

The second blank is what the check proves. Prescience bundles Time Machine’s care-trajectory modeling, Crystal’s navigation, plan administration, filings, and billing support. That is a company roadmap, not one venture moment. I cannot identify the dated experiment, pass/fail metric, or truth that would cause a discontinuous increase in value. “Serve more employers” is not enough. The round should test something like whether prospective care routing lowers a defined employer cohort’s claims expense by a specified percentage within one plan year; no such capital plan is documented.

The strongest evidence is the team. Rishab Jain has published more than ten ML and health papers and reportedly led two acquired companies; Aditya Jain brings an MD, biomedical engineering, and medical-AI research. Together they plausibly bridge model development and clinical reasoning, and the California administrator license shows they have done more institutional work than a generic AI wrapper. But neither founder has a documented operating receipt from underwriting, employer-benefits procurement, claims administration at scale, or nationwide regulatory execution. Medical intelligence and insurance operations are related systems, not the same system.

Prescience may already have the beginnings of a proprietary workflow: it reports serving U.S. employers, integrates with provider networks, HSAs, compliance, banking, and plan-administration systems, and can use de-identified health data to improve prediction models. The problem is causal proof. Customer count is undisclosed; no employer is named; there is no measured savings, retention, model accuracy, or paid conversion. Fifty-one Launch YC points and an open waitlist are distribution artifacts, not evidence that owned access caused a purchase. The success-fee model—no administrative charge, payment only from savings—is intellectually clean, but without the fee percentage, claims baseline, gross margin, or adjudication rules, it is not yet cash economics.

The bull case stings because the ingredients could compound quickly. Employer family premiums reached $26,993 in 2025, the founders have unusually relevant technical and clinical backgrounds, and performance pricing removes a major procurement objection. If even a few reported employers are live and producing verified savings, the claims-and-navigation loop could become the proprietary data asset while well-funded competitors remain burdened by conventional carrier or TPA economics. That would justify overriding my AI skepticism, but the known exception requires measured operation: accuracy, conversion, retention, and customer economics—not a description of the model.

I am passing today. Delete the next financing and Prescience has not shown the controllable cash metric that keeps it alive: savings generated, fees collected, contribution margin, burn, or runway. The product also carries clinical-validation, PHI-security, and state-by-state compliance risks while lacking a completed SOC 2 examination. My verifiable flip condition is one named employer cohort showing at least 10% annualized claims savings over six months, with the methodology independently auditable, a paid success fee collected, and positive contribution margin. That would convert Prescience from an ambitious collection of healthcare-AI claims into a functioning base business with a valuable option attached.
