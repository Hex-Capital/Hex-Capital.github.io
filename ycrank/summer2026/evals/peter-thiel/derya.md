# Derya -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Derya’s contested truth is that hardware companies will replace fragmented brokers and internal logistics staff with a neutral, AI-operated freight office sold for a flat monthly fee. The clean falsification test is whether customers repeatedly entrust it with shipments after the first month while the plan remains profitable without freight-rate markups. The first-month evidence—$80,000 in contracted revenue, more than $3.2 million in sourced freight opportunities, and TVO’s 20-container commitment—shows that customers will test the proposition. It does not yet show that the proposition is rejected by incumbents, technically difficult, or durable.

The founders are the strongest part of the company. Karaca worked in maritime law and at CEVA/CMA CGM; Turna built ship-operations software used on more than 30 vessels representing $200 million in fleet value. More importantly, they have known each other for eight years, competed together, and previously started a business together. This is a company assembled from a long relationship around an operating problem, not two strangers assembled around “agentic AI.” Public evidence does not establish their ownership, compensation, or Turna’s departure from UCLA, so economic and full-time alignment remain incomplete rather than adverse.

But what great company is no one starting? Derya has not yet answered this. Flexport and Forto already manage global digital freight, Nuvocargo specializes in cross-border freight, and 5U AI automates quoting, booking, tracking, and reconciliation for forwarders. Derya’s flat subscription and absence of freight markups may align incentives, but pricing architecture is not a monopoly mechanism. There is no proprietary technical result, exclusive supply, bounded segment with dominant share, or demonstrated data advantage. The company currently resembles an unusually capable service operator in a crowded market—the precise case where value creation can be real while value capture remains weak.

The bull case stings because the early transactions may be the beginning of the moat rather than evidence that none exists. Every completed shipment could generate carrier-performance histories, exception-resolution records, and customer mandates that make subsequent operations more automated and reliable. Founder-led introductions followed by workflow reviews have already produced contracted revenue and a substantial opportunity pipeline. If those records materially reduce labor per shipment, Derya could convert a service wedge into proprietary infrastructure. Yet this remains an inferred future loop, not an observed one; no retention, automation rate, gross margin, or exclusivity evidence establishes that the twentieth shipment is economically better than the first.

The revenue engine is consequently ambiguous. A flat recurring plan offers cleaner revenue than freight-rate arbitrage, but the company promises sourcing, booking, customs, insurance, storage, invoicing, and exception management while specialists execute the work. Without disclosed prices or gross margins, $80,000 contracted revenue could describe high-margin software or prepaid human operations. The broader positioning—from freight office to procurement, assembly, production, and distribution—also threatens to multiply operational burdens before one narrow market has been dominated. A $225.4 billion market cannot rescue unfavorable unit economics.

My base case is that within 36 months Derya pivots toward workflow software for existing freight forwarders and remains below $3 million ARR, because its founder quality and customer access are stronger than its present ability to capture value as a full-stack operator. The single condition that flips my decision is a twelve-month customer cohort demonstrating at least 70% gross margin while shipment volume per operations employee more than doubles through proprietary automation. That would turn today’s service activity into evidence of a compounding technical monopoly. Until then: strong founders, real demand, but commodity before monopoly. I pass.
