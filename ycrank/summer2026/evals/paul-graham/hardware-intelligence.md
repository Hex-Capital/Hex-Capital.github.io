# hardware intelligence -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when hardware intelligence first met resistance. Athreya Anand and Rishov Sarkar have the right raw materials: nearly a decade of shared history, agentic-AI experience at Google, chip exposure at AWS and Tesla, and direct work on AMD simulators and Siemens EDA compiler flows. But the dossier names no customer rejection, broken integration, or technical impasse followed by a fast founder-built workaround. A founder’s credentials are not a substitute for watching him run into a wall.

Wave is nevertheless real technical evidence. It reads multi-gigabyte VCD and FST dumps, traces signals, proposes fixes, reruns the simulator, and produces an auditable waveform diff. This is considerably better than a diagram or a plan to begin research after funding. The evidence remains founder demonstrations rather than an independently measured benchmark, however, and the missing FSDB integration excludes the established Verdi workflow where the hardest enterprise proof may occur.

The user evidence is weaker. Pilot onboarding opened in July 2026, and the founders personally conduct demonstrations and onboarding. That is the right motion, but a waitlist and an invitation to pilot do not show that a named verification team repeatedly used the crude product, tolerated its gaps, or changed its workflow around it. ChipAgents claims deployments at more than 120 semiconductor companies while Synopsys already offers AI failure analysis, signal tracing, and waveform comparison. In this market, “we can demonstrate it” and “engineers pull it back into tomorrow’s debugging session” are different facts.

The schlep is promising. On-prem deployment, customer-controlled model keys, simulator execution, proprietary file formats, auditability, and enterprise chip-design security are precisely the ugly constraints that can shelter a startup. Sarkar’s simulator and EDA history makes this more than a generic AI-for-semiconductors thesis. But the dossier does not connect Wave to a dated problem the founders personally suffered, nor does it show how they revised the product after encountering one. I therefore credit domain proximity and the burden they chose, not an origin story or running-back behavior that has not been demonstrated.

The strongest bull case is that this is a fierce technical pair entering a difficult market with working software before asking permission. They have also known each other long enough to remove the application-assembled-cofounder red flag, and founder-led onboarding avoids premature delegation. The unstated pattern that fits is the terminal-native programmable interface built by domain experts. The pattern that does not is the concrete pre-round proof spike: there is no benchmark, named customer, repeat usage, revenue, or proprietary substrate. No known exception supplies an override, because the technical demonstration has not yet become independently validated proof or unmistakable user pull.

I would pass today. The operational gate fails on resourcefulness, not because the founders seem unresourceful, but because the dossier provides no observed test. The incumbent overlap and absence of current moat make guessing especially dangerous. One event would flip me: a named semiconductor team documents that an FSDB or simulator-flow blocker stopped adoption, the founders shipped the workaround within weeks, and the same engineers then used Wave repeatedly on live verification work.
