# GrayPass -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most useful fact about GrayPass is that Mathur went to San Francisco and reportedly came back with a customer. Someone on this team is willing to sell the thing personally. But what happened when a customer said it would not work? The dossier contains no specific obstacle, founder-built workaround, and elapsed time. I cannot infer that sequence from a sale, Waterloo, or a research fellowship. That leaves my most important founder test unanswered.

The product has a sensible boundary: check control again when something consequential happens. A signed proof tied to a payment or permission change could make that check useful to application developers. But GrayPass describes behavioral continuity as experimental and unapproved for live enforcement. Its SDK, API, and models deserve provisional technical credit; the dossier supplies no measured detection performance, false-rejection rate, or latency. A system can correctly sign its decision while making the wrong decision about the person. That distinction matters enormously here. The disclosed compromised-endpoint limitation is an honest boundary, not itself a reason to pass.

The strongest argument for investing is that customers may already be teaching these founders how to close that gap. European pilots, a reported customer win, and the CEO’s claim of “five figures in revenue” are meaningful at this stage. At a starting price of $500 a month, GrayPass has at least described something a business can buy. The founders have also known each other since 2021 and built projects together. This is a stronger case than two strangers with a security presentation. Yet the revenue period is unspecified, the customers are unnamed, and payment for an evaluation does not establish repeated dependence on the product. I give demand more credit than the missing origin or pivot history, but it does not supply an override to the founder gate.

GrayPass could find its opening in the unpleasant work of instrumenting individual actions, obtaining consent, and making enforcement reliable. That is real schlep. There is no documented account of either founder encountering this problem in prior work, however, and computational neuroscience is adjacent expertise rather than proof of that origin. Nor is there a documented rejected hypothesis followed by a changed product. These are missing observations, not evidence of rigidity. Against my unstated patterns, the company has neither demonstrated developers building unexpected workflows on its primitive nor supplied a numerical technical proof spike. Existing products from TypingDNA and Twosense make those distinctions worth establishing.

I would pass today. The September coverage and October 22 announcement do not establish a launch-theater red flag because founder outbound and demonstrations are also documented. Likewise, I see no basis to accuse this young team of delegating away the hard work. The problem is insufficient affirmative evidence on my controlling tests. The working-prototype and validated-proof exceptions require stronger artifacts; the overwhelming-user-pull exception requires stronger behavior. One verifiable deployment could change my decision: a named customer documents a blocking integration problem, the founders ship its workaround within two weeks, and that customer then repeatedly uses paid, approved enforcement against agreed detection and false-rejection thresholds. That would connect founder action, technical proof, and actual need.
