# Qlo -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: generative AI can now turn unstructured broker submissions into executable underwriting inputs, commercial MGAs will buy without replacing their policy-administration systems, and Qlo becomes large if it can increase bindable-quote throughput while preserving rating parity. That is a measurable controlling variable. Distinguished Programs reports 3× primary-quote throughput, while Qlo’s paid pilot explicitly tests its output against the customer’s approved logic. The remaining gap is whether this result repeats across programs with different documents, raters, and exception patterns.

The technical shift is meaningful but not yet fully economic. Qlo does more than extract fields: it compiles macro-enabled Excel raters, cites source documents, applies program pricing logic, and generates quote-through-renewal artifacts. This can remove manual transfers among email, spreadsheets, raters, and policy systems without forcing a risky PAS migration. However, the dossier does not disclose implementation hours, exception rates, pricing, or gross margins. A two-week setup could represent efficient configuration or services work hidden inside software economics.

The strongest evidence is architectural. Better models should improve Qlo’s document interpretation while the company retains customer-approved rating logic, workflow state, traceability, and responsibility for completed artifacts. That is substantially more durable than an AI risk-summary layer. The bear case still stings: Federato, Sixfold, and Kalepa already overlap with ingestion, rating, and quote-and-bind, have raised between $16 million and more than $180 million, and could absorb Qlo’s differentiated Excel-rater execution. Qlo must turn the initial workflow into reusable deployment infrastructure before the category crystallizes around those larger platforms.

Product pull is unusually informative for this stage, although narrow. One enterprise customer uses Qlo for daily quoting, and a named customer reports a concrete throughput improvement rather than an LOI or registration count. The paid-pilot structure also asks customers to spend money and test parity. But there is only one publicly visible deployment, no dollar revenue, and no retention or expansion history. This proves that the workflow can matter; it does not yet prove repeatable distribution.

Ryan Hu brings approximately 2.5 years inside MGA, carrier, and broker workflows plus prior experience building PinchLabs to roughly $500,000 ARR and receiving acquisition term sheets. That is real second-time learning, not employer-brand inference. Liv Huang adds hands-on engineering experience, and the underwriting advisers deepen the team’s access to workflow detail. What remains undocumented is costly commitment or the kind of founder desperation that would distinguish this from a well-informed vertical-software project.

I would invest at pre-seed, but this is not yet a strong-invest outcome. My 36-month base case is $6 million to $12 million ARR from a focused set of MGA programs, with Qlo remaining an underwriting-execution layer rather than replacing the PAS. The verifiable flip condition is deployment repeatability: I would reverse to pass if, by August 2027, Qlo cannot show three paying MGAs using the product daily with independently measured quote-throughput gains and rating-parity results.
