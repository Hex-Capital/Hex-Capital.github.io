# Confetti -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Confetti wants to turn a sentence into a working physical object. The investment question is whether it can own the knowledge connecting those two things—and whether my check buys that discovery cheaply. Proposed post-money, competing term sheets, and financing history are undisclosed. I cannot call this an auction, but I cannot underwrite a scarce first check either. The dossier also names no capital-funded experiment with a deadline and a pass/fail result. Those are missing deal facts, not evidence against a two-person pre-seed team.

The strongest case is that Confetti could own the whole project outcome. Adam emphasizes engineering workflows; Flux emphasizes electronics design and manufacturing outputs. Confetti proposes design, component selection, printed-part ordering, and community remixing for beginners. If completed builds generate compatibility knowledge that makes subsequent projects work, the fulfillment burden could create proprietary information. This fits my interest in businesses that connect software to neglected physical operations. But successfully manufactured projects are the necessary receipts. An advertised workflow does not establish that the bridge works.

Gu and Yuan give that possibility some substance. They coauthored EngineLab, accepted at an ICML workshop; Gu has a public software project, Edgecart, and Yuan has Penn Electric Racing exposure. That earns modest credit beyond institutional affiliations. It does not establish that either founder has operated manufacturing, managed suppliers, or repeatedly delivered working hardware to customers. Their shared research history supports collaboration, while the CEO/CPO titles alone tell me little about final decision authority. I would not infer an equal-founder governance problem.

The dangerous version of Confetti is a conversational interface that inherits software competition and physical-world liabilities. Zoo already advertises geometry inspection and debugging; Flux includes sourcing and manufacturing outputs. Confetti therefore needs an asset those tools cannot simply reproduce. Community remixing could become that asset, but 58 LinkedIn followers establish neither owned distribution nor purchasing behavior. Published pricing is unverified, transaction fees are inferred, and there are no disclosed margins or burn figures. A wrong connector can consume the profit from several correct orders; the dossier does not establish how that cost is contained.

The bull case still matters: beginners may pay for a completed project precisely because professional tools leave them coordinating the pieces themselves. That could make Confetti a useful business even as generation becomes cheaper. My exceptions to missing financing evidence require demonstrated conversion, cash economics, proprietary access, or unusually strong operating receipts. None is documented here. Nor does the inferred Asteria transition establish failure or successful adaptation. I pass at present, using the stated missing-evidence defaults rather than treating absent revenue as adverse evidence. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: a 90-day cohort of 30 paying novice builders completing delivered projects at positive contribution margin after shipping, support, refunds, and remakes. That would begin to show whether Confetti owns a functioning business beneath the AI option.
