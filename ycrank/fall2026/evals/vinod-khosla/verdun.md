# Verdun -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Verdun identifies a consequential failure: nuisance alarms can leave a real threat waiting more than 20 minutes for human review. Removing that queue could make effective security more widely affordable. But the dossier does not quantify how much monitoring labor disappears, how many additional properties become economical to protect, or how security outcomes change. The $2.382 billion remote-monitoring market establishes an existing spending pool. It does not establish an orders-of-magnitude consequence. I cannot price an extraordinary upside from a category estimate alone.

The strongest argument for writing a check is the founders. Sonny Tai has operated in physical security; Tung Hoang built Actuate’s first gun-detection model. Their previous collaboration combines customer knowledge with relevant technical execution. Actuate’s reported 110,000 cameras across 15,000-plus sites makes that experience substantive, although those deployments belong to Actuate. Tai also discontinued a consumer project before returning to security. That demonstrates willingness to change direction, but the dossier does not establish what evidence changed his beliefs or show difficult complementary recruits joining Verdun. The team earns some credit here; it has not yet demonstrated the learning-and-recruiting pattern this criterion demands.

I do fund execution businesses when a narrow entry point can expand into consequential infrastructure. That is the strongest exception to a pass here: experienced operators could use monitoring contracts to accumulate footage, corrections, and ultimately an operational advantage. But Verdun’s proposed feedback loop is still a hypothesis. Its CV/VLM cascade and site context describe an implementation; they do not demonstrate a technical discontinuity. Hakimo already offers contextual verification and autonomous responses. Stealth Monitoring and Pro-Vigil provide named alternatives, but there is no comparative result. The red flag would be competing through standard models and implementation labor. The dossier does not prove that accusation, yet it supplies no measured capability that resolves it.

The next dollars should test whether Verdun can remove human review without removing the judgment that catches rare threats. Its evaluation hiring addresses night footage, compression, weather, and occlusion—appropriate problems—but a vacancy is not a completed risk-removal milestone. There is no disclosed decisive experiment, numerical threshold, cost, or stop condition. Nor does Tai’s explicit recognition of video-ingestion and inference costs establish viable economics. Cheap initial filters are sensible; without inference volumes, human escalation rates, prices, and fully loaded service costs, they remain an architectural mitigation rather than a scale model. Missing revenue at this stage does not drive my decision.

I pass with my own money. The documented founders make Verdun worth watching, but the exceptional-instigator override still needs a credible connection to exceptional consequence. Neither extraordinary founder commitment nor a transformative cost curve is established here. My single flip condition is an independently auditable, matched-site comparison against Hakimo showing at least 10x fewer operator minutes per camera-hour, no reduction in true-threat recall, and lower fully loaded delivery cost. That result would turn the same monitoring workflow into evidence of a different economic system.
