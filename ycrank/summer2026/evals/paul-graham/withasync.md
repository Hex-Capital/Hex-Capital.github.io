# Async -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Two named law-firm deployments make **Async** a schlep-heavy company without an organic founder story. Clifton Law and the Law Office of Joshua Lowenthal show that **Async** is already doing the unpleasant work of conflict checks, engagement letters, drafting, research, and email integration. But Ben Smith’s path from collectibles to nonprofit fundraising agents to Skope billing to legal agents does not show that he personally suffered any of these problems. **Async** looks like resourceful founders searching through real work, not founders pulled toward one problem they could not stop thinking about.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness: compliance was not incidental to the product but the reason softer founders stayed away. **Async** shares the important mechanism. Auditing workflows, encoding approval logic, connecting documents and CRMs, and maintaining agents inside law firms is exactly the sort of grubby work horizontal AI-platform founders hope customers will do themselves. But **Async** still falls short of Coinbase because the schlep is broad rather than proprietary. The same custom labor that wins Clifton Law may have to be repeated at every clinic and real-estate company, and outcome pricing could turn the schlep into low-margin consulting rather than accumulated leverage.

**Loopt** (2005) exposed the danger of a technology-led idea whose founder’s force of personality had to override weak evidence that the problem was organic. **Async** shows the same missing root without the known exception: the dossier connects neither Smith nor Connor Park personally to legal administration, healthcare operations, or real estate. The sequence from nonprofit fundraising agents to billing software to legal agents and then horizontal service businesses makes **Async** look discovered by iteration rather than grown from founder pain. Iteration can be good. But organic ideas usually become narrower and stranger as founders learn; **Async** has become broader.

Ben Smith dropped out of Michigan at 20, previously sold shoes, trading cards, and collectibles, and helped ship enough versions of Skope to earn 55 Hacker News points before **Async** reached two named law firms. That is evidence of motion, though not yet an animal moment. Over 18 months, resourcefulness compounds only if **Async** converts custom deployments into reusable machinery: one conflict-check integration should make the tenth materially faster. If every new customer requires another audit, another bespoke agreement, and founder maintenance, Smith’s selling ability compounds revenue while the operational burden compounds faster. **Async** has tracks, but no cereal boxes yet.

Connor Park studied computer science and shipped Skope, but his most visible GitHub repository has one star and **Async** has no documented open-source artifact, competitive-programming result, or hacker-community reputation predating the startup. The score would flip upward if Park released a technically surprising long-horizon agent runtime, showed reliable unattended execution across weeks, or produced an artifact hackers adopted independently of **Async** sales. Until then, **Async** has a technical founder by role and evidence of shipping, but not the hacker genealogy that made the Collisons legible before Stripe became obvious.

**Stripe** (2010) is the useful co-founder parallel because two founders covered both construction and persuasion while sharing a history deeper than a startup matching exercise. **Async** has the right shape in Smith as seller and Park as builder, and both attended the University of Michigan. But the dossier does not say whether Smith and Park built together for years, were roommates, or merely overlapped at Michigan. **Async** therefore earns more than a solo-founder default but not the presumption of the Collison brothers. A pair is not automatically a partnership.

Anthropic’s May 2026 small-business package makes my 36-month forecast specific: **Async** will abandon the horizontal “long horizon agents for small businesses” framing, concentrate on legal workflows, and reach roughly $1–3M ARR by July 2029. **Async** can get there because law firms will pay someone to own intake, conflict checks, engagement letters, and review rather than learn Gumloop. The low organic-founder score predicts continued searching; the high schlep score predicts that the search ends where customer-specific operational pain is greatest. That points to a narrow managed legal product, not a general agent platform.

Relevance AI’s $37M, Gumloop’s $70M, and Glean’s reported $300M ARR make the bear mechanism sting: platforms improve faster than **Async** can turn bespoke deployments into software, Anthropic absorbs the common workflows, and outcome pricing leaves **Async** holding model costs and integration labor until the founders tire of another pivot. The conflicting SOC 2 claims make that especially dangerous in legal work. But Clifton Law and Joshua Lowenthal already chose managed execution despite the existence of platforms, which suggests the unwanted work—not the model—is what customers buy from **Async**. Two paid follow-on workflow agreements from those named law firms, delivered at greater than 60% gross margin, would flip my verdict from Neutral to Invest.
