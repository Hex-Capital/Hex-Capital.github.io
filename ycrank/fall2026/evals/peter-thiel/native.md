# Native -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Native’s possible secret is that predicting which ideas a particular business should publish matters more than generating the content. That is a testable proposition: hold generation quality constant and see whether Norn’s rankings improve customer outcomes against ordinary brand-aware selection. Native has shipped a product around this distinction. But the dossier names no peer rejecting the proposition, and its observational research selects retained accounts and acknowledges survivorship bias. The reported 5.5% engagement for Briggs on Homes versus 2.9% for its own posts is suggestive; it does not isolate the predictive technology. There is an interesting hypothesis here, with limited evidence that it is a secret.

The more decisive problem is ownership. Who keeps the value if Native is right? Blaze already describes strategy, creation, publishing, and learning in one workflow. Native’s initial market—small businesses needing marketing—is broad, and the dossier identifies no bounded segment it can dominate. Collecting acceptance and performance data is a real operating mechanism, but exclusive access, cross-customer predictive improvement, and a durable technical advantage remain unestablished. Customers can also opt out of training. A feedback loop becomes a monopoly mechanism only when its accumulated learning makes the next competitor meaningfully worse.

The strongest argument against passing is unusually substantial for this stage: Native reports $2 million ARR within six months and more than 2,200 paying customers. Those figures deserve attention. At the published $79 monthly price, 2,200 subscriptions would produce approximately $2.09 million annualized revenue, broadly consistent with the claim, although that calculation does not verify the actual customer mix. Native has one intelligible subscription engine. Yet reaching roughly $1 billion annual revenue at that price requires more than one million subscriptions; retention, generation costs, and evidence for higher-value expansion are absent. Early revenue establishes commercial demand. It has not yet established power-law economics.

The founders provide the strongest independent reason to take the company seriously. Bekken, Gråsberg, and Hauga built Wepost together before Native, bringing content production, machine learning, and agency operations into the same founding group. This fits my preference for teams formed through actual work and for founders building from a problem they have operated inside. It also counters the red flag of convenient co-founder matching. The dossier does not establish present ownership, salaries, or every founder’s full-time status, so I cannot award complete founding alignment; equally, I find no evidence of part-time founders, outsourced core engineering, or investor-controlled economics.

Distribution is partly demonstrated and partly unexplained. Self-service onboarding gives small businesses a concrete purchasing route, while 40,000 posts published in one reported month establishes actual product delivery. But neither founder follower counts nor a pricing page explains repeatable acquisition. The dossier provides no channel conversion, acquisition cost, sales cycle, or implementation burden. Agency sales and an API waitlist could change the economics, but they remain prospective routes. Native’s existing customers make the bull case sting: their feedback might compound into the predictive advantage competitors lack. The evidence currently establishes collection, not compounding.

I would pass on this check. The crowded-product red flag is present, and the exceptions for independently demonstrated technical breakthroughs or extraordinary realized economics do not yet remove it. Neither institutional incubation nor participation in the broader AI stack supplies an alternative reason to own this particular company. Native could build a substantial business while remaining exposed to competitive pricing. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently audited, prospective comparison in a defined customer segment showing that Norn’s advantage over the strongest competing workflow increases materially as Native accumulates proprietary feedback. That would connect its commercial activity to an emerging monopoly mechanism.
