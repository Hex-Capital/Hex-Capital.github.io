# Keres -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Keres has identified the right economic problem: military operators cannot indefinitely exchange scarce, expensive interceptors for abundant drones. Its proposed opening is recovery after a successful engagement. The controlling variable is fully loaded cost per successful interception, including aircraft losses, inspection, repair, and turnaround. Reuse matters only if Garmur survives enough engagements to reduce that cost while maintaining defensive capacity. The dossier establishes the customer problem and a coherent mechanism, but supplies neither the required survival threshold nor measurements showing Garmur can reach it.

The strongest bull case is that procurement urgency makes this an unusually good time to prove that mechanism. DIU’s 2025 follow-on funding after counter-UAS flight demonstrations is concrete evidence that military buyers are supporting alternatives. Christopher Palazzolo’s more than four years across Boom’s aircraft maintenance, avionics, flight testing, and production operations are directly relevant to making recovery practical. Keres could turn maintenance knowledge into a product advantage: an interceptor designed around repeated sorties could have substantially different economics from an expendable weapon. That possibility deserves attention before revenue exists.

But the institutional opening and the technical achievement are separate facts. Garmur’s public description does not demonstrate the complete launch, interception, destruction, and recovery sequence. Nor does it quantify the economic improvement. Recovering an aircraft that needs extensive inspection or repair may save hardware without increasing magazine depth under sustained attack. The broader counter-UAS forecast of $12.6 billion in 2026 does not resolve this question; it includes other products and says little about the share a reusable jet can economically serve.

Keres also enters a market where distribution is already forming around competing suppliers. Fortem’s disclosed Army and DHS contracts, plus its September 2026 financing led by Lockheed Martin, establish buyer access that a successful prototype would still need to overcome. Keres’s October 12 media profile provides no corresponding evidence of customer pull. Its proposed interception-and-recovery loop could eventually accumulate valuable operating state, but the dossier documents no deployed integrations or accumulated mission data. I would assign no moat value to that future dataset without knowing its reproduction cost.

The team’s aircraft experience makes the physical-business exception plausible, but the exception requires demonstrated changes to unit economics. Relevant employment alone does not establish rare frontier capability, prior startup learning, or costly personal commitment. Likewise, the research-stage exception does not apply on the available record: there are no dated technical results and no documented capital path through the development bottleneck. These are missing facts rather than evidence of poor execution. They still leave Keres short of the market conviction required for my own check.

I would pass today. Keres has a specific economic thesis worth testing, but its product advantage remains a proposed result in a market with established supplier relationships. My single flip condition is an independently witnessed repeat-engagement trial showing Garmur destroying targets and returning to service at a lower fully loaded cost per successful interception than the buyer’s current alternative, at comparable defensive effectiveness. That would establish whether recovery actually changes the market’s economics.
