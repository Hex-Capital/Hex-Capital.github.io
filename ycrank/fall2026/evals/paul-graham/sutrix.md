# Sutrix -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The useful fact about Sutrix is that researchers paid the founders to do the work before they opened access to the software. The founder reports $150K+ in revenue over a few months; YC reports paying teams at 10+ institutions. Clinical research teams have an immediate job: turn scattered records into a dataset they can actually study. Payment is considerably more informative than agreement that this sounds useful. This is the strongest part of the application.

The founders also have a credible reason to have discovered this problem. Ritvik conducted clinical research at HSS, Emory, and Georgia Tech, including a 300-participant concussion study. The team built software inside research environments at HSS and NYU Langone. Reconciling records, preserving provenance, and getting institutional approval are precisely the unpleasant details that can conceal an opportunity. The brothers report four years of building together, which answers my concern about cofounders assembled for an application. Supratik’s dropout status adds nothing; his engineering experience and prior exit are useful background, but neither establishes what he has done when Sutrix encountered resistance.

There is a product here. The founders describe releasing software previously used to deliver their research work, and Sutrix documents reusable study definitions, source-linked fields, and reviewer decisions. That fits my preference for founders who build something before asking permission to become a software company. But the claimed 80% reduction in preparation time lacks independent measurement, and the FHIR connections are currently sandboxes. I give the functioning internal software meaningful credit without treating its documented features as proof of reliable deployment across hospital systems.

What I cannot reconstruct is the hardest obstacle these founders met and what they did next. File uploads might be a clever way around slow integration, but the dossier does not establish that sequence. Likewise, moving from delivering research work to offering software could represent excellent learning, but there is no rejected hypothesis or customer observation explaining the change. I will not invent either story. Resourcefulness and willingness to revise a route remain unproven here, rather than disproven.

The strongest objection is that Sutrix may have found a good research services business whose software chiefly makes its own staff more productive. Individually negotiated engagements and undivided service/software revenue leave that possibility open. Layer Health and Carta already combine automation with substantial clinical workflow knowledge. Sutrix wins a larger business only if each completed study makes subsequent studies easier to deliver. Reusable definitions suggest that mechanism, but the dossier does not measure it. The paid work is a concrete proof spike of the sort I like; it does not yet establish a reusable product that customers can operate without continuing founder labor.

I would keep talking, but would not write the check today. The exceptions I make for working products and unmistakable demand deserve consideration: Sutrix has actual payment and software built through delivery. Those facts make this substantially more interesting than an untested healthcare idea. They do not yet replace the missing evidence about founder behavior under constraint. There is no disclosed dependence on a grand launch or a major distribution partner; founder-led outreach is encouraging. My flip condition is one documented hospital deployment where a named blocker stalled a study and the founders shipped an institution-approved workaround within two weeks. That would show the behavior my investment decision depends on.
