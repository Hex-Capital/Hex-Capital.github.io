# Bear -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a small business asking whether it can afford another employee and getting an answer from books that are already current. Getting there requires reliable reconciliation, permissioned access for other software, and accountants reviewing exceptions rather than chasing documents. Bear’s proprietary ledger is a plausible starting point. But that future is my construction: the dossier establishes an accounting service, not yet an artifact demonstrating the transition to that larger world. I give the missing future-chain evidence its default, rather than treating my daydream as something the founders have proved.

Why does the founder have to carry documents between the bank, payroll system, and accountant in the first place? Each holds a piece of the business, and the customer supplies the connective tissue. Bear combines the ledger and the service under a fixed price, giving it a practical way to eliminate those handoffs. That returns time and potentially useful financial control to operators. The mechanism is concrete; the economics remain incomplete. There is no quantified delay, fee burden, or evidence showing who captures the savings when the work disappears.

The strongest argument against passing is that Bear has already persuaded businesses to entrust it with consequential work. Sami reports more than 10 paying customers, although the claim is undated; the July 2026 blog reports over $80 million processed for dozens of Finnish companies. That is transaction volume, not revenue, but accounting errors have consequences regardless of revenue. These customers are doing something more meaningful than joining a waitlist. Still, company-reported aggregate volume does not establish repeat usage by a dated cohort, so I keep the behavioral score within the persona’s evidence cap.

Sami and Riki have also built financial-data infrastructure before, including Riki’s petabyte-scale work at Token Terminal. Their shared operating history matters more to me than their degrees. Sami’s earlier Angomon project supplies another building artifact, though the acquisition is self-reported and the buyer unnamed. This is credible preparation, without the dated pre-company obsession or recruiting evidence that would make me stretch. My exceptions for companies without revenue rest on exceptional founder history and a working artifact tied to a specific future; Bear has customers, but the other parts of that exception are not established.

Owning the ledger is what keeps this interesting. It fits my pattern of finding infrastructure inside an ordinary service business: reviewed accounting work could help build something other products depend on. But ownership alone does not make an asset a bottleneck. Balance already combines agents and accountant signoff inside existing ledgers, while Skalar combines proprietary technology with professional delivery. Bear has not demonstrated that downstream products need its ledger or that its consent-based evaluation process creates a scarce advantage. A useful accounting firm could emerge without the infrastructure upside ever arriving.

I would pass on a personal check today. The disclosed read-only Google permissions, separated workspaces, encryption, and restricted reviewer access address my privacy concerns sufficiently to avoid assuming a severe violation. Likewise, four original founders becoming two listed active founders warrants clarification, but Sami is identified as CEO; that discrepancy does not prove a leadership shell game or equal-control deadlock. My decision turns on the missing next-world evidence. One verifiable result would change it: an independent developer selling a live product on Bear’s permissioned ledger that a named customer repeatedly uses to make and execute daily cash decisions. That would show the accounting service becoming an enabling layer.
