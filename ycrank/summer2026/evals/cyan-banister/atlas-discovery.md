# Atlas Discovery -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A biopsy taken before treatment tells a clinician which therapy a patient will respond to, and drug companies stop enrolling hundreds of people whose biology makes success improbable. That future is specific and valuable. Atlas Discovery’s present-day leak is its retrospective UNIFI result: 0.760 AUROC on 358 treated patients, with a modeled reduction of 458 participants at matched statistical power. But the bridge is incomplete. Atlas acknowledges that the predictor was trained and evaluated within the same trial and that cross-trial generalization is not presently usable for stratification. The artifact suggests the future; it does not yet traverse the distance to it.

The bull case stings. Nine of ten drugs fail in trials that can consume hundreds of millions of dollars and nearly fifteen years. Even a modest improvement in patient selection can create enormous economic value, and Atlas reports 0.7–0.9 retrospective AUROC across cancer and autoimmune studies. FDA and EMA principles are also beginning to define how AI-generated evidence may enter regulated development. A pre-revenue company can earn my check with technical proof instead of sales—but only when that proof survives contact with an institution. Atlas has no named biopharma pilot, signed hospital partnership, prospective validation, or repeat customer behavior. Its modeled participant savings remain a counterfactual, not something a clinical-development team has risked a trial on.

The founders are the strongest part of this company. Sanjukta Bhattacharya’s AI-biology PhD work and gene-expression research, Shaamil Karim’s experience at Cellarity and Asimov, and Christian Gensbigler’s cancer research precede the YC launch. All three coauthored the DCM and ExpressionVAE preprints, so this is not a recent category costume. They have produced real artifacts and assembled complementary biological and computational ability. What I cannot yet see is the Biz: who persuaded a hospital to release linked outcomes, who owns the pharmaceutical sale, and whether this team can convert research credibility into the long institutional process required for regulatory-grade evidence.

The broken system is legible but the agency transfer is not. Atlas quantifies the waste—trial failures, long timelines, and potentially hundreds of avoidable participants—but it does not identify an artificial rule or gatekeeper capturing that rent. Biopharma would receive better trial economics; patients might avoid ineffective treatment. Yet no current workflow shows a clinician, patient, or smaller drug developer gaining control previously withheld by an institution. This is optimization of an expensive system, not yet a demonstrated redistribution of agency.

The deeper problem is that the scarce enabling asset is not the model. It is linked pretreatment biology and treatment-outcome data. Atlas says those records remain in disconnected silos and is still requesting access, while Owkin already reports relationships across more than 800 hospitals. Atlas’s DCM code and weights are open, its repository has four stars, and no exclusive dataset or patent is disclosed. That makes the company an aspiring infrastructure layer sitting on someone else’s bottleneck. Because the desired inputs include molecular and clinical outcomes, the absence of an explicit consent, custody, and data-governance architecture also hits a real gate for me; “we need hospital partnerships” is not a privacy design.

My 36-month base case is that Atlas pivots toward trial-specific biomarker or subgroup-analysis services, remains below $1 million ARR, and does not establish a general cross-trial response model. I am Neutral and would not write the check today. One result flips me: a named biopharma partner prospectively pre-registers Atlas’s predictions on an unseen trial and reports clinically useful performance, with contractual access to the linked dataset and an auditable patient-consent architecture. That single event would prove the technical bridge, institutional demand, data moat, and privacy posture at once.
