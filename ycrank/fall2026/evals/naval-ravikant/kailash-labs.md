# Kailash Labs -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Sharma has earned some specific knowledge. Robot-learning research, precision-welding motion planning, and the May 14, 2026 Marlin release are actions with inspectable outputs. The 934 monthly model downloads establish that something shipped beyond a personal research folder. That is substantial founder evidence. Mete’s manipulation research would strengthen the team, but I cannot underwrite his involvement while his current role remains unresolved.

The investment turns on whether Kailash Labs can make the second factory installation benefit from the first. Its current offer bundles an arm, gripper, vision, and safety into a station with a seven-day pilot. That can create customer value. It creates compounding company value only if deployments accumulate something competitors cannot readily obtain: transferable policies, exclusive operating data, or reliably faster commissioning. None is demonstrated. Marlin’s Apache-2.0, Qwen-derived architecture establishes technical execution without establishing an exclusive asset. For the current robot business, durable advantage remains unproven.

There is room to maneuver across inspection, assembly, packing, and kitting. Perception and manipulation capabilities could connect those applications, although different tooling and reliability requirements could also make each a separate integration project. Formic’s recurring managed-automation offering supplies independent evidence that factories buy this kind of service. The broader $3.31 billion RaaS estimate does much less work: it includes demand Kailash may never serve. The opportunity is credible; the repeatable expansion mechanism is still a hypothesis.

The strongest bull case is that these founders can turn their research into a deployment engine before the public evidence catches up. Their move toward embodied systems fits my preference for solving physical bottlenecks, and the open model reflects a willingness to put useful work into outsiders’ hands. I can accept missing network effects when an exceptional prototype and capital efficiency justify the exception. Here, however, the released prototype demonstrates video-model capability, while the proposed business depends on factory performance. Marlin’s 610 likes and nine Spaces do not establish that one robot customer improves another customer’s product or acquisition. Nor does the apparent product change establish fickleness: there is no documented integrity violation, artificial financing pressure, or repeated abandonment that would justify a founder veto.

No customer capex could accelerate adoption, but it leaves unanswered who finances the equipment and bears redeployment costs. Undisclosed prices and customer results receive the missing-evidence prior; they do not prove bad economics. My concern is the mechanism: Kailash could become a capable automation integrator whose revenue grows alongside engineering labor and equipment commitments. My flip condition is one independently verified replication: a paying factory deployment whose learned policy transfers to a second customer’s comparable station with materially fewer engineering hours and positive contribution margin after equipment and support. That would show the beginnings of an asset that compounds. Today, I see enough earned knowledge to stay interested, but insufficient evidence to commit my own money.
