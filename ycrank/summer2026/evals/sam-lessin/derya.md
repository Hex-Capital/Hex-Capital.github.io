I would not begin with “agentic supply chain.” I would begin with the missing deal: Derya has raised $1.1 million from YC, 212, Umur Cubukcu, and angels, but the dossier gives no post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or evidence that this was a scarce first check. I cannot distinguish cheap convexity from a normal YC auction, so the primary gate remains unresolved rather than favorable.

The operating evidence is much better than the financing evidence. In its first month, Derya reported $80,000 of contracted revenue, more than $3.2 million of sourced freight opportunities, and a 20-container commitment from TVO. This is not follower theater; a customer crossed from interest into a contracted logistics workflow. The strongest bull case is that Derya has found a translation layer between hardware companies and the fragmented brokers, carriers, manufacturers, customs processes, and documents required to move physical goods.

The founders make that mechanism credible. Oguzhan Karaca has worked inside CEVA/CMA CGM, specialized in maritime and trade law, and previously built Ivy Consultants to six-figure ARR. Mert Turna built ship-operations software used on more than 30 vessels representing $200 million of fleet value. Together they have shipped on the operational and software sides of the system being joined. That is Derya’s best evidence and the part competitors cannot reproduce merely by adding an agent interface.

The problem is that the round has no named venture moment. “Source, book, clear, track, store, and distribute” is a broad operating mandate, not a finite hypothesis with a metric and deadline. Derya does not say whether this $1.1 million proves autonomous exception handling, repeatable acquisition in one hardware vertical, or profitable management of a defined shipment volume. Without that card, the capital risks financing an expanding outsourced logistics department rather than revealing one dramatic truth.

The economics decide whether the apparent dark pool becomes an asset or a trap. Customers pay a flat monthly fee, Derya adds no freight-rate markup, and specialists still perform services across customs, storage, insurance, invoicing, and exceptions. The $80,000 contract figure is meaningful, but there is no gross margin, workload-per-account, burn, retention, or realized cash data. If human exception costs rise with shipment complexity, revenue scales alongside labor and compliance exposure; deleting the next round from the model then exposes a services business wearing software language.

The strongest objection is that first-month conversion may already justify an exception to those missing gates. The closest precedent is Ando: both businesses sell operational improvement into enterprises, but Ando’s exception rested on greater than 90% forecast accuracy, complete pilot-to-paid conversion, and complete retention. Derya currently has one named container order and contracted revenue, not repeat conversion or retained unit economics. The exception becomes real if Derya verifies at least 80% gross retention after twelve months and a contribution margin above 50% while handling materially more shipments per operations employee.

Competition compounds the unresolved economics. Flexport and Forto already coordinate digital freight, while 5U AI automates quoting, booking, tracking, and reconciliation inside incumbent forwarders. Derya’s no-markup outsourced model is distinct, but no proprietary carrier access, exclusive customer channel, shipment dataset, or structural barrier is yet documented. The agentic-AI framing is therefore a red flag only insofar as it substitutes for operating leverage; the actual investable asset would be the exception-resolution workflow and customer mandates created through repeated execution.

My 36-month forecast is that Derya reaches $4 million–$8 million of ARR as a service-heavy managed logistics operator, but has not demonstrated software-like contribution margins or a one-and-done path to profitability. I am neutral today: the founders and customer action are unusually credible, but I would not invest my own money without knowing the price and seeing the first cohort prove retained, labor-efficient economics.
