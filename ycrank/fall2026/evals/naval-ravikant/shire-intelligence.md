# Shire Intelligence -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Four years working in restaurants matters more to me than the founders’ university names. Alex Tabaku and Cameron Kuperman have encountered the coordination problem themselves, worked together, and helped turn that experience into a camera-driven prototype. That is specific knowledge acquired through action. The founder-reported NexHacks win supplies evidence of building; it does not establish reliable operation during a crowded dinner service. Shire Intelligence has earned founder credit before it has earned technology credit.

The decisive question is what Shire keeps when somebody copies the implementation. Its disclosed prototype uses SAM3 segmentation and DINOv3 classification. Those components make the initial capability accessible to competitors too. Existing CCTV reduces installation friction, but Shire does not own the cameras. Restaurant footage could become a scarce training asset; the dossier establishes neither exclusive rights nor a working learning loop. This is an affirmative reproducibility concern, beyond simply missing moat information. PreciTaste already has adjacent vision and staff-guidance capabilities from which to expand.

The strongest argument against passing is that restaurant operators may care very little about this distinction. Shire reports seven-minute faster table turns and 14% more monthly guests served. If those results hold, founders who understand service could earn adoption while competitors debate architecture. Mathews Restaurant’s reported $65,000 payment-processing savings also suggests a customer relationship extending beyond a demonstration. But processing savings do not validate camera inference, and the operating claims lack sample sizes. My exceptions for unusual prototypes and unusually capable founders require evidence strong enough to carry the missing structural advantage. This dossier does not yet supply it.

There is room to maneuver. Dining-room coordination can lead into reservations, POS workflows, and payments for the same restaurant buyer; Eat App’s reported $12 million ARR demonstrates adjacent spending. I give that path credit without treating the entire $7.6 billion restaurant-software category as Shire’s addressable market. At $40 per month, the entry product offers direct monetization. Posted pricing establishes an offer, however, while completed subscriptions, processing revenue, and contribution margins remain undisclosed. Customer savings are value created; they are not automatically value captured.

Shire Intelligence currently automates work inside individual restaurants. The dossier shows no mechanism through which another restaurant measurably improves an existing customer’s experience or acquisition. Reseller options are a potential sales channel, without observed distribution evidence. My preference for systems where participants contribute useful supply is therefore unmet. Nor does using cameras establish the proprietary physical substrate I increasingly seek. There is no documented integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-instability red flag; the pass rests on the economic structure, not an invented character concern.

I would pass with my own money today. The team has more demonstrated operating context than demonstrated compounding advantage. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: a controlled production test showing that footage Shire contractually controls from additional restaurants improves accuracy at existing restaurants without restaurant-specific engineering. That would turn deployment experience into an accumulating asset. Today, that mechanism remains a hypothesis.
