# Shiraz AI -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what broke. Shiraz AI says robots are operating at customer sites, which is real evidence of shipping, but the dossier never names a failed deployment, a factory constraint, or what the founders built in response. Navid Aghasadeghi commercialized Spot at Boston Dynamics; Mojtaba Mozaffar led manipulation and policy-learning work at Amazon Robotics. Those credentials explain why they could build this. They do not yet show how they behave when a customer’s line defeats the model. Resourcefulness cannot be inferred from résumés.

The technical artifact is much more convincing. A system that watches one video, adapts without retraining, and then performs the task on supplied hardware is an ambitious product, not an AI wrapper. Robots operating at customer sites put Shiraz beyond diagrams and future research. But the decisive measurements are absent: task-success rate, cycle time, intervention frequency, exception recovery, and safety performance. The claim is therefore a working proof with a large uncertainty discount, not a measured threshold.

There may be a narrow well here. Contract manufacturers change production lines weekly, have labor shortages, and cannot justify rigid automation for short runs. Shiraz takes responsibility for the hardware, model, and on-site installation, which is precisely the ugly work that could teach the company what the scalable product should become. Yet “first customers” are unnamed, and site operation does not reveal whether robots remain in production, whether customers pay, or whether workers repeatedly teach them new tasks. This could be customer pull, or it could still be an extended demonstration.

The strongest bull case is that the public record understates an unusually strong pre-seed company. Two robotics PhDs with experience spanning Spot commercialization, industrial manipulation, perception, and policy learning have already crossed the laboratory-to-factory boundary. Aghasadeghi also reports a prior product reaching one million users. If the deployed robots are handling changing production tasks repeatedly, the founders have combined technical depth with the manufacturing schlep that better-funded model companies may avoid.

But Shiraz is not competing only against research projects. Skild AI advertises the same crucial mechanism—learning an unseen task from one human video without fine-tuning—and has raised more than $2 billion. Standard Bots supplies integrated industrial arms, while Micropsi works with existing fleets. Shiraz has disclosed no proprietary dataset, reliability advantage, switching cost, or evidence that customer deployments are accumulating into a superior system. The red flag is not competition itself. It is that the company’s proposed differentiation currently rests on a claim competitors also make, while the evidence that would distinguish it remains private.

I would not write the check yet. The team and working system keep this well above an ordinary pass, but the people-first gate remains unresolved: there is no documented obstacle-and-workaround sequence, and neither measured technical proof nor repeat customer pull is strong enough to override that absence. One condition would flip me: a named factory reporting at least 90 days of repeated production use, together with a documented deployment failure that the founders corrected within weeks and a before-and-after success-rate measurement.
