# Synapse Semiconductor -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A drone, robot, or satellite should not drag every pixel through an interface, memory, and GPU merely to decide what matters; the sensing surface should discard noise and perform useful inference before the data travels. Synapse Semiconductor has a real leak from that future: fabricated, measured pixels and a peer-reviewed CMOS-compatible MoS₂/graphene device performing 4-bit sensing and in-pixel convolution. But the path still contains several unnamed miracles. The public evidence does not establish wafer-scale yield, useful model capacity, target accuracy, or the claim that CNNs and VLMs run on a complete chip. This is a credible future chain, not yet a traversable one.

I keep asking why the current camera stack has its shape. Synapse identifies the components—sensor, MIPI or PCIe interconnect, memory, GPU—and names power and latency as the rent. It does not quantify that rent in watts, milliseconds, battery endurance, thermal load, or bill of materials. That omission matters because robotics engineers routinely accept architectural ugliness when it buys accuracy, programmability, and mature manufacturing. Without a system benchmark, I cannot tell whether RETINA removes the binding constraint or merely relocates complexity into a harder substrate. The dossier also does not show whose occupational or economic control is returned.

The founder evidence is much better. Tania Roy spent years working on two-dimensional materials, optoelectronics, neuromorphic devices, and reliability before this became a company. Sanjeev Chauhan joined her laboratory as an undergraduate, continued through his master’s degree, and helped carry the research toward commercialization. Their first-believer act is not a fashionable AI deck; it is a measured physical artifact. The missing piece is commercial command. There is no public evidence of semiconductor hires, customer discovery deep enough to change the design, or a production and capital plan. The professor-student origin also makes two questions non-negotiable: who is ultimately accountable, and does Synapse hold an exclusive, durable right to the Duke-developed IP? The dossier answers neither, although it does not establish an actual governance violation.

This is exactly the concealed infrastructure beneath a visible wave that attracts me. Robotics, drones, satellites, automotive systems, and defense products could all benefit if sensing and computation genuinely collapse into one transistor. Synapse controls technical know-how demonstrated in working pixels, not merely an interface to somebody else’s model. The bear case stings, however: SynSense already sells an integrated sensing-and-processing chip, while Sony and TSMC are jointly developing physical-AI image sensors. If Synapse owns neither exclusive IP nor reproducible process and yield knowledge, those companies turn its scientific insight into a feature before it becomes an independent business.

The relevant exception is **CosmicBrain AI**: both companies pursued physical-AI infrastructure before revenue and used functioning hardware to make the future legible. The material difference is that CosmicBrain had signed industrial proofs of concept and a founder whose robotics obsession had produced hundreds of builds; Synapse shows a working pixel, not a working customer system, and discloses no LOI or repeat prototype use. Fifty-four attendees at a founder event are distribution interest, not costly user behavior. The exception therefore keeps me curious, but it does not justify pretending the commercialization gap has already closed.

I am neutral and not writing my own-money check yet. The drone and defense tags make my privacy gate relevant, although a compute-in-pixel architecture could reduce raw-image movement rather than enlarge surveillance; the dossier describes no data-custody product architecture either way. My single flip condition is a paid design-in from one named robotics, drone, or space customer after its packaged RETINA prototype demonstrates a material power-or-latency advantage at the customer’s required accuracy. That one artifact would show the device survives system integration, the claimed rent is real, and a user will spend money and engineering time to adopt it.
