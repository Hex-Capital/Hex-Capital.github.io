# Decawork -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: every employee may direct a small fleet of agents, while IT manages those agents as identities with owners, permissions, histories, and retirement dates. Decawork sees a plausible piece of that world. Its separate credentials, approval-gated tool calls, attributed logs, failure alerts, and retirement workflows form a coherent control plane. The leak from the future is less convincing: the cited 88% incident rate is an industry survey, while Decawork discloses no current agent fleet behaving in a way that proves this operating model has arrived early. The destination is visible; the causal road remains partly narrated.

The founders have paid relevant operating tuition. Sarthak Aggarwal worked on AI systems at NVIDIA reportedly deployed at OpenAI and Meta, then enterprise AI at Ema. Aman Raj built Barclays compliance tooling used by more than 240 teams and previously helped products reach 52,000 families and 100,000 monthly active users. More importantly, their prior autonomous-IT product reportedly ran across enterprises in India and the United States. That is stronger than résumé adjacency, but the pivot matters: it demonstrates contact with enterprise IT while leaving unanswered whether agent governance was the wound they independently obsessed over or simply the hotter product discovered in 2026.

The broken mechanism is real but incompletely priced. Employee-built agents force IT into a bad binary: accept invisible access to company systems or block useful automation. Decawork returns deployment control through scoped identities and action-level approvals. Yet the dossier never quantifies the resulting delay, security cost, or labor burden, nor identifies which incumbent captures an artificial rent. This may be necessary infrastructure, but “IT needs visibility” is not yet the same economic excavation as showing why the queue exists and who profits from preserving it.

The hardest problem is behavioral proof. SOC 2 status by July 20, 2026 and a detailed security architecture suggest a working product, but there are no named customers, current deployments, revenue figures, repeat operators, or sensitive actions successfully governed by the new control plane. Prior-product deployments cannot be reassigned to this product. The pre-revenue exception does not rescue Decawork: that exception requires a functioning artifact plus concrete institutional commitments and unusually deep pre-company obsession. Here, neither current-product usage nor signed proofs of concept is disclosed.

The bull case stings because Decawork sits underneath the visible agent wave and the founders understand enterprise deployment. If identity, launch, monitoring, remediation, and retirement become one operational job, Okta’s registry may be only a component rather than the product. But Okta shipped overlapping agent registration and credential governance in April 2026, while Astrix, Noma, WitnessAI, and Zenity have each raised more than $55 million. Decawork currently controls a gateway, not a scarce asset; routing every tool call through it also creates a concentrated availability and policy risk. The inferred future dataset cannot count as a moat before the data exists.

I would not write the check today. There is no demonstrated privacy failure, closed-model dependence, or trivialization of incumbent complexity, but the two-founder governance structure is undisclosed and the reported team-size discrepancy—two on YC versus seven on LinkedIn—deserves resolution. My flip condition is verifiable: show three named enterprises running at least 100 production agents each through Decawork for 90 days, with repeat sensitive-action approvals and measurable reductions in deployment or incident-response time. That would turn this from a timely security surface into evidence that the future has actually started leaking.
