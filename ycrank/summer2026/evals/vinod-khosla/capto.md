# Capto -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Tripling proposal output is useful. The consequential question is whether Capto makes government purchasing substantially cheaper or better, or simply helps contractors submit more bids. The dossier establishes a contractor workflow problem; it does not quantify procurement costs eliminated, new suppliers enabled, or improved government outcomes. Customers’ reported $250 million in obligated contract value measures their portfolios, not Capto’s economic impact. I can identify the beneficiary today—the contractor’s capture team—but cannot yet underwrite an orders-of-magnitude consequence.

The founders provide the strongest reason to reconsider. Ananth Sankaralingam built RFP-search automation for a contractor; the team moved from browser-extension creation into government contracting and visited approximately 50 contracting offices. Those are observed actions, more informative than their LinkedIn, Amazon, and Plaid credentials. Paid pilots and Snovation’s report of finding an otherwise missed RFI make the bull case uncomfortable to dismiss: these founders could discover a much larger business through direct customer learning. Their partnerships add procurement knowledge, although partnerships do not establish that complementary leaders have joined to own specific risks. Founder adaptability earns meaningful credit; exceptional recruiting remains unproven.

Capto’s technical evidence is weaker. Company-document grounding and source-linked requirements are sensible mechanisms, and the reported threefold increase in proposal output offers some evidence of utility. But the claim supplies no measurement method, quality adjustment, or comparison against a named competing product. GovDash already combines discovery, capture, proposals, and post-award management. Capto must demonstrate why its company knowledge produces a capability competitors cannot reproduce. The red flag I test is competition through familiar AI features without a proprietary learning advantage. The dossier does not establish that Capto merely wraps an API, but it also does not demonstrate a technical discontinuity or an exclusive learning loop.

I would spend the next dollar testing whether Capto improves pursuit selection, rather than assuming more proposals mean better decisions. Its claimed PWin backtesting against millions of award outcomes does not establish prospective accuracy. Paid pilots demonstrate willingness to experiment, but the dossier provides no ranked kill risk, decisive threshold, experiment budget, or stop condition. That receives the missing-evidence default, not a judgment that the founders lack discipline. Economics receive the same treatment: undisclosed pricing and no inference, human-review, or support-cost model leave the cost advantage unestablished. There is no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence.

I pass at 32. My broader investment pattern includes execution-driven businesses; scientific novelty is not an absolute requirement. The strongest exception would be unusually adaptive founders using a narrow workflow to enter a giant existing value pool. Capto has evidence for adaptability, but the dossier has not established that broader mechanism or its consequence. The other exceptions—overwhelming societal impact or visible exponential technical progress—also lack supporting evidence here. **My flip condition is a prospective, independently auditable deployment across named paying contractors showing at least a tenfold reduction in total capture labor per qualified, compliant submission, with missed requirements no worse than the existing workflow.** That would establish a cost discontinuity worth investigating. Today, I see credible customer discovery and useful automation, with the transformative investment case still unproven.
