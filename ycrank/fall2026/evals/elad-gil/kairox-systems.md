# Kairox Systems -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Kairox Systems becomes interesting if aerial transit makes underwater missions materially cheaper to complete: defense buyers get subsea access without the launch infrastructure and logistics of separate vehicles. The controlling variable is **cost per successfully completed mission at the buyer’s required reliability**. Combining flight and swimming could improve that number, but it could also add weight, maintenance, and failure modes. The dossier establishes the operational problem; it supplies neither a purchasing threshold nor evidence that Tempest crosses it. I would pass on investing today.

The strongest evidence is the beginnings of an integrated vehicle. Kairox reports an assembled prototype, pressure-vessel architecture, and modular payloads, with its first prototype built using a $14,000 grant. Those are concrete development signals. Owning the physical system could let better autonomy improve mission execution while Kairox retains responsibility for the vehicle and payload integration. That earns some architectural credit. TRL 4, however, does not establish reliable air–water transitions under operational conditions, and the dossier provides no range, endurance, depth, or speed specifications. GPS-denied autonomy remains a company claim rather than demonstrated workflow ownership.

The market question is sharper because SubUAS already demonstrated an air–water vehicle and had more than $4 million in Navy Phase III contracts reported by 2020. That establishes a procurement precedent, although those awards belong to a competitor. Kairox needs to explain which mission its fixed-wing configuration improves enough to displace an existing approach. NATO’s January 2025 Baltic Sentry initiative supplies a relevant operational setting, but no quantified change in Kairox’s costs, margins, or adoption. I would assign no market-expansion premium to the publisher’s $3 billion estimate, particularly given its inconsistent growth arithmetic. Reported Marine Corps engagement is useful customer access; without documented trials, repeat use, or commitments, it does not yet demonstrate product-market pull.

The bull case deserves attention. Three founders with complementary mechanical, electrical, aerospace, and controls backgrounds have produced hardware on a small initial grant and reached military users. Jorge Cavazos’s control-law and hardware-qualification work is particularly relevant to the execution problem. A customer could reveal that rapid aerial deployment solves a sufficiently expensive operational constraint to make the combined vehicle compelling. This shares Anduril’s mechanism of using an integrated defense product to change how a mission is delivered; the material difference is that Kairox has not documented the corresponding economic improvement or strategic vacuum. My willingness to fund physical technology depends on that distinction. The dossier also does not establish the frontier research and financing path needed for a research-stage override. Relevant employment alone cannot establish founder desperation or second-time learning.

Kairox’s immediate capital task appears to be proving a mission, rather than scaling established demand. Nothing here establishes vanity fundraising, excessive hiring, or commodity margins; those would be unsupported accusations. Equally, neither a hardware configuration nor future operating data establishes a defensible business by itself. My one verifiable flip condition is **a paid military acceptance trial demonstrating a repeatable end-to-end Tempest mission at a lower fully burdened cost than the buyer’s current method, while meeting that buyer’s specified reliability threshold**. That would connect the engineering to the variable that makes this market work.
