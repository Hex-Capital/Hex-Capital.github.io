# Rasyn -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequential version of Rasyn would collapse weeks of fragmented computational chemistry into hours of reliable, autonomous work. The dossier does not quantify that consequence. A $2.3 billion AI-drug-discovery market growing to $13.8 billion is a market forecast, not a resource equation: no chemist-hours eliminated, experiment costs avoided, discovery cycles compressed, or patients reached. I cannot price a 10%-likely breakthrough when the dossier has not defined what becomes 100x better.

Marigold is currently orchestration, not a demonstrated technical discontinuity. It connects 54 executable tools, with 40 running on customer hardware and 14 on hosted endpoints, reads literature and files, and checks intermediate results. That is useful engineering. It is not a measured 5–10x result against Rowan, existing computational workflows, or manual chemist practice. The harder evidence is adverse: many integrated tools are third-party or open source, Rowan already offers more than 15 workflows, and Rasyn’s own retrosynthesis work reported a 30.3% Top-1 exact-match failure rate. Requiring experimental verification is responsible, but responsibility is not reliability.

The strongest bull case is real. Marigold launched in August 2026; a previous product reportedly reached seven medicinal-chemistry teams; another effort assembled an 87,000-row dataset involving Aragen, IFM Therapeutics, and 1200 Pharma. Daood Hashmi has direct computational-chemistry exposure at Caltech. The rapid transition from Larmor and Marcus to Marigold could represent the tactical flexibility I want. It could also mean the team discovered that a broad agent is the commercially useful wedge. But the pages are gone, the empirical evaluation remained in progress, and the dossier documents neither what evidence caused the transition nor whether those users followed into the new product.

The largest kill risk is trustworthy chemical reasoning, not tool count or distribution. Rasyn says Marigold checks between steps and stops when results fail, but supplies no measured end-to-end error rate, comparison protocol, decisive threshold, experiment budget, or stop condition. GLP/GMP validation and Part 11 compliance are absent, so regulated pharmaceutical workflows remain constrained. Capital should first purchase a blinded benchmark on representative multi-step tasks, with expert-reviewed correctness and time-to-answer measured against working chemists—not another twenty integrations.

The economics are more promising than the technology proof. Pricing of $89 per researcher, at least $747 monthly for a three-seat lab, and $2,000 monthly for institutions could undercut even a small fraction of chemist labor. Yet compute is rationed through units, inference costs are undisclosed, and customer data cannot improve the models without written opt-in. This may become an attractive vertical-software business while failing to become general intelligence for chemistry. The exception for a modest initial product requires either an extraordinary instigator or a wedge into a clearly quantified giant value pool; Rasyn has not yet established either.

I pass today. The likely 36-month outcome is a pivot toward one narrower computational-chemistry workflow and $1–3 million ARR, rather than a general chemistry platform. My flip condition is verifiable: show a blinded study of at least 100 real, multi-step chemistry tasks where Marigold cuts expert labor or cycle time by at least 5x while achieving at least 95% expert-validated correctness, then demonstrate repeat paid use by ten laboratories. That would convert integration labor into evidence of a discontinuity.
