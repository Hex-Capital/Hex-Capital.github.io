# River -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

River could make the computer execute knowledge work instead of making people shuttle context between applications. That is a consequential possibility. But the dossier establishes context-transfer friction, not how much expert labor disappears when River works. The $10.32 billion productivity-tools estimate measures a software category; it does not quantify the societal cost River can remove. I cannot price an extraordinary outcome from a market label.

The disclosed product integrates documents, spreadsheets, presentations, local files, and a signed-in browser. That could be useful. Its stated differentiation, however, is workspace organization and access, while Superhuman Docs already combines editing, AI, and external connections. River supplies no measured advantage against that alternative in completed-task throughput, accuracy, or human intervention. External models do not prevent application-level invention. But integration alone does not establish one. The red flag here is competing through an interface while suppliers and adjacent applications can reproduce the underlying capabilities.

The strongest objection to passing is that two founders have already put a substantial product into users’ hands. River claims 100,000+ creators; its desktop launch reportedly generated 130,000+ agents and 200,000+ files across 50,000+ users. These are activity signals, although they do not establish repeat usage. Supple brings Roboflow engineering experience; Joseff brings research exposure and experience on Progressly’s founding team. Their backgrounds are complementary. My execution-heavy software pattern matters here, and the **Block** exception is relevant: an ordinary initial tool can become the entry point into a much larger platform. River could own where work gets executed. The material difference is that its reported activity does not yet demonstrate ownership of an enduring workflow or an expanding economic relationship.

Those accomplishments also do not establish fast belief revision or recruiting against a named weakness. Ten months building together demonstrates commitment, but the dossier supplies no observed change of belief or difficult complementary recruit. Likewise, launching agents and files is different from retiring the largest kill risk. River’s critical uncertainty is whether cross-application execution saves substantial labor after users review and repair its mistakes. Edit review and authorization controls acknowledge that problem; they do not measure its resolution. No costed experiment, numerical threshold, or stop condition is disclosed.

The $14, $36, and $100 monthly plans, usage markup, and separately billed overages provide a mechanism for charging for consumption. They do not establish cost per successfully completed job against human work or competing software. Included AI credits are not disclosed supplier costs. I see neither proven unsubsidized replacement economics nor evidence of permanent subsidy dependence. The exception for an enormous quantified mission or demonstrated exponential technical progress also lacks support in this dossier.

I pass with my own money. River has evidence of building and distribution, but its investment case still depends on converting convenient access into a defensible collapse in work cost. My single flip condition is an independently auditable, 30-day customer study showing at least 5x lower fully loaded cost per accepted cross-application deliverable than Superhuman Docs plus human labor, including model charges and all review and correction time. That result would turn the central claim into an investable mechanism.
