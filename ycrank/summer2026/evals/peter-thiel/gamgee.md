# Gamgee -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Gamgee’s proposed secret is precise: a tumour-specific mRNA vaccine designed from each dog’s mutations can produce clinically meaningful cancer regression quickly enough to become a repeatable veterinary treatment. The obvious falsifier is a prospective cohort in which the bespoke vaccines fail to produce durable responses distinguishable from prior therapy. Rosie’s approximately 75% tumour reduction makes this more than an AI-and-biotech slogan, but it does not yet prove the secret: she had already received surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted drugs, retained cancer afterward, and UNSW called the project citizen science rather than a clinical trial.

The narrow market is real—dogs with cancer treated through veterinary oncologists—but Gamgee has not shown it can own it. Its mutation-specific mRNA construct differs technically from Torigen’s whole-tumour-cell vaccine, ELIAS’s vaccine-plus-T-cell treatment, and FidoCure’s sequencing-directed drugs. Difference is not monopoly. The claimed canine-specific models rest publicly on one documented case, while universities, genomic laboratories, and three funded competitors can assemble similar workflows. A future response dataset may compound; today it is a hypothetical moat attached to an observable commodity risk.

Paul Conyngham brings 17 years of machine-learning experience and coordinated scientists at UNSW and the University of Queensland to deliver a working treatment. That is meaningful operating evidence. But the foundational evidence is incomplete: no public co-founder history, ownership data, or proof that the two-person core team previously shipped together. Rinat Sadykov’s move from Meta suggests technical capacity, not long alignment. Missing evidence is not a red flag, but it cannot establish that this is a durable founder-company identity.

The economics are the deeper problem. Global pet-cancer therapeutics sales were only $400.9 million in 2024 and are forecast to reach $898.6 million by 2034. Gamgee discloses neither price nor revenue, and its $2,000 treatment cost and 24-hour turnaround are development targets rather than demonstrated economics; the current workflow takes approximately four weeks. Even dominating this stated market may not create a power-law outcome. A human-oncology expansion could change the arithmetic, but the dossier supplies no clinical, regulatory, or manufacturing bridge from dogs to humans.

There is nevertheless a coherent distribution route. Veterinarians can refer cases through a dedicated portal, an Australian trial is recruiting, and owners can apply globally. Founder reach and extensive press may generate initial demand. But there is no referral conversion, sales-cycle, enrolment, or contribution-margin evidence, while country-specific permissions for an investigational veterinary mRNA product add implementation friction. A portal is an intake mechanism; it is not yet a distribution machine.

The strongest bull case is that Rosie represents an unusually early natural-secret experiment: a two-person team assembled university sequencing, neoantigen selection, RNA manufacturing, and veterinary delivery before institutional scale, then observed substantial tumour shrinkage. That could justify investigating despite weak present economics, especially if canine treatment becomes a rapid learning system for human oncology. It does not justify my check today because the result remains clinically confounded, the learning loop contains one public case, and the plan still requires efficacy, regulation, manufacturing, and veterinarian adoption to work together. I would reverse this pass if a prospectively registered trial of at least 20 dogs showed durable objective responses attributable to Gamgee’s vaccine.
