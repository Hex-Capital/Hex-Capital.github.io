# Studio -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important missing sentence is what broke. Studio has five enterprise clients, but the dossier never names an obstacle the founders encountered, what they built around it, or how quickly the situation changed. Results do not retroactively prove resourcefulness. This is missing evidence, not adverse evidence, so the founder-workaround criterion stays at its default.

The strongest fact is the artifact. Two founders have built models spanning 200 million consumers, 1,400 sources, and more than 300 cohorts per simulated society. Tests with Danone and Mila reportedly matched live KPIs with greater than 95% accuracy. That is substantially more than a diagram or research proposal. But “95% accurate” is almost uninterpretable without sample sizes, baselines, error distributions, forecast horizons, and decision types. Studio resembles my unstated pattern of technical teams producing a concrete proof spike before raising, but the spike remains company-reported rather than independently inspectable.

Five enterprise clients are also better evidence than a waitlist. Danone and Mila identify a real user niche: large consumer businesses making consequential product, pricing, and messaging decisions. What remains unclear is whether these companies repeatedly use Studio, pay for it, or change decisions because of it. A successful design-partner test can still be a consulting project disguised as a product. With no disclosed revenue, renewals, or longitudinal usage, there is not yet enough pull to distinguish a narrow well from five interesting pilots.

The founders are unusually well matched to the mechanism. Nikita Mullangi researched decision-making simulations, while Cameron Malloy built agent-based financial-contagion and society models. Customer-data ingestion, privacy controls, calibration, and enterprise procurement also constitute real schlep. But relevant research is not the same as encountering an urgent need in one’s own work, and the dossier gives no evidence that Studio’s route changed after a forecast failed or a customer used the product unexpectedly. Those dimensions therefore receive partial origin credit and default route-change credit.

The bull case stings: a two-person team has assembled a large behavioral substrate, reached five enterprises, and tested against real outcomes while heavily financed competitors attack adjacent versions of the problem. I also see none of the obvious red flags—no hired sales force, ceremonial launch strategy, or dependence on one promised distribution partner. But no known exception clears the founder gate here. The exceptions require unusual energy made observable through rapid correction, unmistakable user behavior, or independently validated proof. Studio has not yet shown those publicly. I would pass today. One verifiable flip condition would change that: document a specific enterprise deployment where a data or accuracy blocker appeared, the founders shipped a workaround within weeks, and the same customer then paid to use the recalibrated product for three additional decisions with preregistered error measurements.
