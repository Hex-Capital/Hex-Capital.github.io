# Canos -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Canos’s strongest asset is knowledge acquired before this company existed. Ming-shan Tsai and Bin Li previously built veterinary AI pathology together at ZYTCA; Tsai’s YC biography attributes distribution into 10,000-plus clinics to that earlier product. Li’s tumour-detection research has a public artifact in dsmil-wsi. These are actions in the relevant domain. They earn substantial founder credit. The earlier company’s clinic reach does not establish distribution or defensibility for Canos.

The investment turns on what remains after a competitor reproduces the analysis. Canos reports a research benchmark covering 341 dogs and 896 sequencing runs, with 90.6% sensitivity at 95.1% specificity in a 303-dog diagnostic analysis. That is meaningful technical work. But nested cross-validation across research datasets does not establish prospective performance for the marketed assay, exclusive training data, or a model competitors cannot reproduce. CanCan already sells sequencing-based canine blood tests. A patent filing with undisclosed scope cannot resolve that competitive question.

The strongest case for investing is that these founders can turn each banked baseline and subsequent treatment outcome into knowledge unavailable elsewhere. Canos addresses a physical measurement problem, which fits my interest in bottlenecks beyond software implementation. Longitudinal monitoring could produce a valuable feedback system. Today, however, exclusive rights to those samples and outcomes remain unverified. Nor does the dossier show that one customer improves another customer’s results or brings the next customer. A collection network supplies access; a network effect requires demonstrated benefit between participants.

There is room to move within the market. Origin establishes a baseline; OncoTrack targets dogs already undergoing treatment. Both use molecular measurements, giving the team a concrete route between baseline testing and repeated oncology monitoring. The independently estimated $985.24 million U.S. veterinary oncology diagnostics market establishes adjacent spending, although it includes tests Canos may never replace. I give credit for that connected expansion path. The promise of cats adds no evidence yet.

At $699 including the veterinary visit, Origin names a payer and a price. Samples are processing, but completed paid tests, repeat purchases, and contribution margins are undisclosed; OncoTrack’s accepted trial participants receive free testing. Direct monetization is designed into the product. Its economics remain unproved. My willingness to fund exceptional founders before revenue or a user loop matters here, but that exception does not establish the missing durable asset. There is no documented integrity breach, artificial financing deadline, or founder instability to trigger a veto.

I would keep Canos close and withhold a check today. The founders could build a useful specialist diagnostics business; the evidence does not yet show how that business compounds beyond additional laboratory work. One verifiable result would change my decision: a prospective study showing that a model trained on Canos’s exclusively controlled longitudinal outcomes materially outperforms its research-data baseline on new dogs. That would connect the founders’ specific knowledge to an asset that strengthens with deployment.
