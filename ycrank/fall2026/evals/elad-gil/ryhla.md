# Ryhla -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Power demand is making grid access a development constraint; Ryhla sells developers an earlier decision about where to build, and its controlling variable is the cost of finding a genuinely connectable site. Screening must preserve viable projects while eliminating expensive failures. The reported 91% utility approval rate among model-approved 2025 applications bears directly on that proposition. But without the sample size, baseline approval rate, or fraction of sites accepted, I cannot distinguish a useful underwriting engine from a conservative filter that rejects most opportunities.

The strongest economic evidence is the reported elimination of eight of ten battery projects within days, avoiding approximately $160,000 in fees. That is a concrete reason to buy before submitting applications. It also fits my pattern of backing software around physical-industry transitions: Ryhla could capture value without financing batteries or constructing grid infrastructure. However, avoided customer expense does not establish Ryhla’s margins. The dossier provides neither pricing nor the engineering hours required per assessment, and faster rejection does not yet demonstrate faster connection.

There is some product pull. An unnamed Midwest solar developer has deployed the product, which is more informative than a waitlist or an introduction request. I would give that deployment credit independently of the approval and savings claims. There is no evidence of repeat purchases, expansion, or payment, so it establishes customer engagement rather than product-market fit. The missing revenue history is ordinary at pre-seed; it simply leaves the strength of demand unresolved.

The more consequential gap is what Ryhla owns after making its recommendation. The product description includes managing interconnection, but the dossier does not document application integrations, utility permissions, persistent project state, or an operating feedback loop. Better models could improve screening while developers still buy the surrounding workflow elsewhere. Paces already offers pathway reports and power-flow studies, and Enverus acquired Pearl Street in March 2025. Ryhla therefore triggers my proprietary-data red flag: dataset scope, access rights, and reproduction cost remain unspecified. Competition establishes an existing category; it does not establish Ryhla’s distribution advantage or strategic acquisition value.

The strongest objection to passing today is that the founders may already have crossed the hard technical threshold. Both reportedly researched grid models at Stanford and initially deployed them with utilities; the developer deployment suggests that work has reached a commercial setting. A reliable distribution-grid prediction engine could become the entry product for a much larger interconnection business. But the research-stage exception requires documented frontier work and a credible capital path, neither established here. The physical-market exception also remains incomplete because supplier unit economics are undisclosed. Their education and long relationship support technical plausibility, but provide no evidence of prior startup learning or costly personal commitment; that founder dimension stays at its default.

I would not write a personal check on this dossier. Ryhla has demonstrated a potentially valuable screening wedge, with insufficient evidence that it becomes a repeatable business controlling interconnection. My flip condition is one independently referenceable, paid repeat deployment across multiple utilities that tracks every screened site through utility disposition and documents positive gross margin after engineering labor. That would connect prediction quality, customer demand, and value capture in the same observable operating record.
