# tash -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The maximum consequence here is easier access to a $13.51 billion collectibles market growing 7.9% annually. That is a convenience and portfolio-packaging opportunity, not a 100x change in a societal cost pool, resource bottleneck, or scarce expert service. Even complete success leaves the world trading the same cards; tash changes the wrapper around ownership. The downside may be one check, but the upside is not consequential enough to compensate for the regulatory, liquidity, and trust risks.

The proposed index is different from Rally’s and Collectable’s single-asset securities, but difference is not discontinuity. tash provides no measured reduction in research cost, pricing error, spreads, custody expense, or liquidation time against those platforms or Alt. There is no proprietary technical architecture or learning loop today. Competitors have raised between $8.11 million and $106 million, while the inferred future moat—transaction data, sourcing relationships, and liquidity—only appears after tash wins the market it is trying to enter.

The strongest bull case is the team’s lived behavior. The founders have bought and sold more than $1 million of collectibles, and their prior exchange produced $22,000-plus in volume across more than 710 trades and 250 listed cards. Moving from that exchange to a managed index may reflect useful tactical flexibility. Their finance and data backgrounds also fit the mechanics of creating regulated vehicles. But the dossier does not show what evidence caused the pivot, which belief changed, or whom they recruited to own securities regulation and market structure. Four friends with overlapping Berkeley and finance backgrounds are not yet risk-tuned gene-pool engineering.

The largest kill risks are already visible: SEC qualification is pending, Collectable halted secondary trading in 2024, and tash’s advertised CL50 history includes interpolated points while the final daily-series chart remains unfinished. Those facts should dictate the experiments. Instead, the public evidence shows a waitlist, named broker-dealer partners, and stale terms describing the prior exchange. There is no pass/fail liquidity threshold, regulatory milestone schedule, test budget, or stop condition. Capital could easily fund the polished issuance of an illiquid security before proving that buyers and sellers will remain on both sides of the market.

The economic model is equally unproven. No issuance, management, sourcing, or trading fee schedule is public, so there is no bottom-up demonstration that custody, compliance, authentication, portfolio administration, and secondary trading can be supported without punitive fees or wide spreads. More damagingly, interpolated performance history creates a trust problem in a product whose entire value depends on trustworthy pricing. This is not merely missing pre-seed traction; it cuts directly against the credibility mechanism.

I would flip only if one SEC-qualified tash portfolio sustains at least $5 million of arm’s-length secondary volume over twelve months with median bid-ask spreads below 5% and fully reproducible, non-interpolated index reporting. That single result would test regulation, liquidity, economics, and trust together. Until then, tash is a familiar financial wrapper around a speculative asset class, not a technical option on a radically better world. I pass.
