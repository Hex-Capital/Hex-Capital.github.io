# Floracene -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: more orthopedic procedures have migrated from hospitals into physician-owned surgery centers, and those centers choose implants with the same price visibility they expect for every other operating expense. That future is plausible. Floracene’s leak from it is CMS adding 271 procedures to the ASC list in 2026 while implants already consume roughly 40–60% of orthopedic and spine procedure costs. But the dossier does not yet map the intervening steps—clinical-equivalence standards, surgeon switching, contract normalization, and integration into procedure planning—so several unnamed miracles still sit between a useful comparison screen and a new purchasing system.

The broken economics are much sharper than the future story. Surgeons select familiar brands or accept device-representative recommendations; manufacturers benefit from opacity; independent centers bear the cost. Floracene proposes to put at least three clinically equivalent implants and the center’s own contract prices beside one another. The claimed 90%+ interchangeability rate and $7 billion of annual ASC implant spending make this more than generic “healthcare is expensive” language. If physician-owners can retain the savings, the product returns purchasing agency to the person carrying the facility economics.

Kaye and Kupferman have paid meaningful operating tuition. They worked together on Palantir’s hospitals team; Kaye was a founding engineer at health-data company Metriport, while Kupferman later handled product at Garner Health. That is credible fluency in hospital operations, interoperability, claims, and contracting. It is not yet evidence of pre-company obsession: there is no dated implant experiment, independently built artifact, surgeon-side operating wound, or proof that they recruited others into this specific belief before the category became attractive.

The strongest bull case is that this is exactly the sort of ugly workflow where experienced healthcare operators can discover value before public metrics exist. CMS is pushing more procedures into ASCs, physician ownership aligns the buyer with savings, and two founders who have already navigated hospital data systems may be unusually capable of turning scattered contracts into a usable product. I make pre-revenue exceptions when a functioning artifact, institutional commitment, or exceptional lifelong technical record replaces revenue. Floracene’s dossier supplies none of those substitutes. YC participation and a two-person team do not show that a surgeon has switched implants, that a center has saved money, or even that the equivalence engine works.

The concealed-infrastructure pattern is still hypothetical. A normalized graph joining procedures, devices, clinical equivalence, contract terms, and purchasing outcomes could become valuable, but Floracene has disclosed no controlled dataset or integration. Curvo already claims coverage of 2.3 million supplies and approximately $110 billion in weekly clinical spending, while Libertas, SurgiSmartIQ, and SupplyLasso occupy adjacent workflows. More troublingly, the central 90%+ equivalence assertion comes from surgeon conversations without a published validation method. Manufacturer-contract custody is sensitive but not yet a surveillance red flag; founder control and a contractual tiebreaker are simply undisclosed. Those gaps prevent an adverse finding, but they also prevent trust.

My 36-month base case is that Floracene broadens into general ASC contract and spend analytics and remains below $1 million ARR because implant switching proves harder than price comparison. I pass today. One verifiable result flips me: three independent ASCs each document at least 50 prompted procedures in which surgeons select a lower-cost equivalent at least 30% of the time, producing audited implant savings above 10% under an explicit clinical-validation protocol. That would reveal the weird, costly behavior that matters—not viewing prices, but changing a trained surgeon’s choice.
