# twentyfour26 -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Automated machining software could let hardware teams buy one-off prototypes without paying repeatedly for manual engineering work; twentyfour26 becomes a large company only if that automation materially improves the economics of small orders. That is the controlling belief. The dossier identifies repeated quoting, programming, setup, and inspection as the problem, but does not establish how much labor disappears per conforming part. Owning a CNC gives the company control over production. It does not, by itself, establish an economic advantage.

The market timing cuts both ways. By September 2026, CloudNC reported serving more than 1,000 shops with overlapping programming automation. That supports technical feasibility while giving existing manufacturers access to part of the same improvement. Twentyfour26 needs an advantage beyond automated toolpaths: lower total delivery cost, including setup, inspection, scrap, and machine utilization. The $132.9 billion precision-machining estimate does not answer that question, particularly when the stocked material is currently 6061-T6 aluminum. There is no quantified cost or margin change here to justify a technology-shift premium.

The strongest bull case is that integrating software and manufacturing eliminates coordination costs that a software vendor or supplier marketplace cannot remove. Twentyfour26 checks STEP geometry, selects stock and tooling, simulates machining, and generates machining plans for its own equipment. That is substantive workflow ownership. Better planning technology could improve a system that remains responsible for delivering the physical part. This fits my willingness to fund physical businesses when software changes their economics. However, the founders still run production and inspection while developing additional autonomy. The integrated architecture earns credit; autonomous margins and a proprietary process-data advantage remain unproven.

There is an early demand signal, but its information content is limited. In the October 2, 2026 interview, Sam Mathew said the company had begun taking manufacturing orders. I give that more weight than launch attention, but it does not establish completed paid deliveries, repeat purchasing, or expansion. I would not borrow Xometry’s $686.6 million FY2025 revenue as evidence of demand for this particular operating model. Customers buying outsourced manufacturing is established; customers repeatedly choosing twentyfour26 at attractive economics is the company-specific question.

Hemant Mahendru’s aerospace training and industrial experience provide relevant context, and the founders have already launched an earlier product and pivoted. Those facts make the team credible enough to test the proposition. They do not document the costly commitment or specific prior-startup learning needed to raise my founder score above its default. I also see no documented vanity-project behavior or premature liquidity. The exception for research-stage companies does not resolve this decision: the dossier offers neither rare frontier research nor an institutional opening that would justify underwriting ahead of economic proof.

I pass on a personal check today. Twentyfour26’s strongest evidence is control of a real production workflow; its weakest link is demonstrating that this control changes manufacturing economics. My flip condition is an auditable 90-day ledger of fulfilled orders from repeat customers showing at least 40% gross margin after charging founder production labor at replacement cost and including scrap and machine depreciation. That is my underwriting threshold, not a reported company result. It would demonstrate that the software is creating a different business rather than helping two founders operate a conventional shop.
