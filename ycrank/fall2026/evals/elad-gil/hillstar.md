# HillStar -- Elad Gil Evaluation

HillStar’s opening is that AI can process utility procurement documents faster while utilities face pressure to buy equipment and services. The controlling variable is **expert labor required per defensible award decision**. If that falls substantially, HillStar can expand procurement capacity and capture part of the savings. If experts still reconstruct every comparison before approving an award, the software accelerates document processing without changing the economics. The dossier establishes the problem and a plausible mechanism, but no measured threshold at which the buyer’s economics work.

The strongest technical evidence is the company-published account of 22 submissions, approximately 19,800 pages, evaluated in nine minutes. That is meaningful evidence of processing capability. It does not establish total labor saved: an 11-person expert team participated, and the comparison baseline comes from experience with comparable procurements. HillStar’s identification of two bidders lacking mandatory certification, including the cheapest bidder, shows why correctness matters more than raw speed. Committee confirmation and source citations are sensible controls. Their value depends on making verification cheaper than repeating the original work.

There is some customer pull here. An unnamed electric utility reportedly completed a sourcing cycle in seven days across approximately 250 equipment SKUs per vendor. An actual procurement outcome is more informative than a demo or waitlist. I discount it because the account is company-published and does not establish payment, repeat purchasing, or expansion. Missing revenue is ordinary at this stage; the important uncertainty is whether utilities repeatedly entrust HillStar with procurement work after the first successful engagement.

The product also reaches beyond document summarization. Solicitation preparation, bid normalization, evaluator judgments, exceptions, award rationales, and supplier outcomes could form a recurring operational loop. Better models should improve that loop while customer-specific decision history remains useful. But the dossier documents advertised capabilities, not longitudinal dependence or integration depth. Fairmarkit already has enterprise sourcing workflows and ERP integrations; Keelvar has sourcing optimization. Neither needs to reproduce an entire utility’s history to compete for its next procurement. HillStar has not established how much time, money, or privileged access reproducing its advantage would require.

The founders make the strongest case against waiting. Nooshin Behroyan founded a utility-services business and reported an exit in 2025; Kenny Stone co-founded PlanGrid, acquired for $875 million in 2018, and subsequently worked in geothermal software. That is documented startup learning with relevant industry context. It fits my pattern of backing software that captures a physical-industry transition. The objection is substantial: these founders may know how to turn an initially assisted implementation into repeatable distribution. However, their prior exits do not establish desperation, and the advertised platform-plus-professionals offering leaves a specific red flag unresolved—whether revenue scales with software usage or procurement staffing.

I would monitor HillStar rather than write a personal check today. The research-stage exception does not apply: the uncertainty is commercial workflow economics, not a frontier scientific capability. The physical-industry exception remains plausible, but requires evidence that technology changes delivery costs. My flip condition is **one referenceable utility renewal supported by an audit of three completed sourcing cycles showing lower total expert labor per award, including HillStar’s service labor, without missed mandatory requirements**. That would connect the processing result to the economic variable that matters and make this a materially stronger investment.
