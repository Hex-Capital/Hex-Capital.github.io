# GrayPass -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A finance employee changes a payout destination, and the application checks whether the person who logged in is still controlling the session before releasing money. GrayPass’s short-lived, action-bound signed proof is a little piece of that future. Getting there requires applications to register consequential actions, enforce the checks, and distinguish ordinary changes in human behavior from account takeover. The dossier describes the first pieces, but behavioral continuity remains experimental and unapproved for live enforcement. The distance between observing a suspicious cursor and safely stopping a payment is the investment question.

Why should logging in confer trust for everything that happens afterward? GrayPass has found a genuine architectural assumption worth questioning. But who pays for that assumption, how much, and whose control improves? The dossier supplies no measured fraud loss, authentication delay, or worker time recovered. Its $500 monthly starting price tells me what the company charges, not what burden it removes. Continuous observation could give people safer control over their accounts; it could also give employers another instrument for watching them. I cannot award the agency benefit before seeing which relationship the product creates.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that somebody has already moved beyond polite interest. The founders report European pilots, a customer won during a San Francisco trip, and “five figures” in revenue. Those are meaningful signals for two people at pre-seed. They are also founder reports without a revenue period, customer identity, or repeated deployment behavior. I want to inhabit this product: change devices, injure a finger, use assistive software, then attempt a sensitive action. Does GrayPass protect me without making me fight my own account? Paid evaluations establish willingness to investigate that question; they do not yet establish the answer.

Mathur has built software beyond this company, including a fashion app reported to have reached 3,000 users in three weeks. Ranjan brings substantive computational neuroscience research through SickKids and a UBC fellowship. Their relationship dates to high school in 2021, with subsequent projects together. That is more interesting than two impressive résumés placed beside each other. What remains missing is evidence of sustained obsession with this particular security problem. My pre-revenue exception at **CosmicBrain AI** rested on a functioning prototype and a lifelong robotics record; GrayPass’s early commercial signals are stronger on revenue, but its disclosed technical validation and problem-specific founder history do not support the same override.

The SDK, API, and claimed proprietary models fit my pattern of looking beneath the visible application. Financial platforms, business software, and developer tools could all need this layer. Yet GrayPass has not demonstrated control of a scarce dependency: TypingDNA and Twosense already overlap its behavioral inputs and authentication functions. Nor do I see a demonstrated severe privacy violation or founder leadership shell game. The Illinois notice, affirmative-consent, deletion, and revocation provisions are constructive, although the dossier does not establish the broader data-custody architecture. Those controls keep the opportunity open; they do not prove defensibility.

I would pass with my own money today. GrayPass has early evidence that customers will explore the product, while the promised transfer of safety and control remains unmeasured. My flip condition is one independently referenceable production deployment showing repeated enforcement of sensitive actions, measured takeover detection and legitimate-user rejection rates, and documented consent and deletion controls. That would show whether this small artifact belongs to the future I can imagine.
