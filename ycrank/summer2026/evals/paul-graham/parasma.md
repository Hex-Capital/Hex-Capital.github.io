# Parasma -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Sean Cole trained roughly 200,000 neurons to play Doom, but Parasma still has a team size of one. Parasma is therefore a maximal-schlep artifact inside a minimal organization: unusually hard technical work produced by a real hacker, with the weakest possible founder configuration. That combination is exciting because animals leave artifacts, and dangerous because biological compute requires more kinds of work than one animal can do.

**Coinbase** is the high rung on the schlep ladder: compliance was not incidental to the product; enduring it became the advantage. Parasma has the same promising inversion. Noise, non-stationarity, limited observability, wet-lab protocols, and even consciousness questions are precisely the work mainstream AI founders will avoid. Parasma diverges from Coinbase because Cole does not control the substrate: the Doom experiment ran on Cortical Labs’ CL1, while competitors such as TBC have raised $25 million to build integrated platforms. The schlep is real, but Parasma has not yet captured it.

Parasma’s team size of one is the lowest part of the signature. The failure mechanism is not loneliness; it is insufficient surface area. Parasma simultaneously needs biological experimentation, reinforcement-learning research, hardware integrations, and commercial discovery, yet Parasma is advertising 1%-3% equity for a founding scientist instead of showing a cofounder who already crossed one of those gaps with Cole. **Loopt** was an exception because Sam Altman’s force of personality overrode weak timing. The dossier shows Cole’s technical force, but no equivalent evidence that he can recruit, sell, and operate around the science alone.

**Dropbox** is the clean parallel for resourcefulness: Drew Houston shipped the demo before asking anyone to believe the deck. Cole likewise skipped the bachelor’s route, finished an AI MSc with distinction, implemented Doom on the CL1 in about a week, published the code, earned 250 GitHub stars, and attracted coverage from The Guardian and Scientific American before Parasma had a product or revenue. Parasma has not produced an Airbnb-style revenue stunt, but Parasma has produced the more important pre-seed thing: an artifact strange enough that nobody could have made it by merely spotting a market.

Sean Cole’s MSc work on spiking neural networks makes Parasma adjacent to his own intellectual life, but the dossier never says Parasma solves a problem Cole personally suffered as a user. This is organic research, not yet an organic product. The score would flip upward if Parasma’s token-prediction system emerged from a tool Cole repeatedly needed while running CL1 experiments, or if another biological-compute operator repeatedly used Parasma’s training stack without being recruited into a collaboration. Until then, Parasma resembles a fascinating capability looking for its Dropbox USB-stick moment.

Parasma’s 250-star `doom-neuron` repository is credible hacker genealogy rather than a degree used as costume. Over the next 18 months, that matters because Parasma can turn public experiments into a recruiting and iteration loop: each benchmark attracts wetware researchers, each researcher exposes another failure mode, and each failure mode can become training infrastructure. Parasma loses that compounding loop if Cole retreats into closed demonstrations or hires credential-first scientists who do not ship. The open code is not a moat, but it is evidence of the sort of founder who may build one.

Cortical Labs’ CL1 dependency points to a narrower 36-month outcome: by July 2029, Parasma will have pivoted from “AI compute” to wetware-training and benchmarking software, remain below $1 million in annual recurring revenue, and derive most commercial usage from Cortical Labs or its customers. Parasma’s schlep depth should produce useful tools; Parasma’s solo configuration and lack of substrate ownership make a general-purpose biological-compute platform unlikely within that period.

Parasma’s strongest bear case is brutal: no revenue, no defensibility, public training code, a solo founder, a $25 million integrated competitor, and no independent proof that the neurons rather than the surrounding software learned Doom. Parasma could be a celebrated science demo that Cortical Labs absorbs as an API feature. The bear case loses narrowly because Cole built the artifact before Parasma existed, added random- and zero-signal ablations, and chose a problem whose unpleasantness filters out ordinary AI-wrapper founders. I would write a small check, not a heroic one. If controlled experiments fail to show living-neuron learning beyond those ablations, or if Parasma cannot recruit a complementary cofounder-level scientist, I would flip to Pass.
