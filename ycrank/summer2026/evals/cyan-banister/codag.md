# Codag -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Michael Zhou’s solo-founder Codag has unusually clean authority and unusually little physical-world texture: one former Okta and Shopify infrastructure engineer controls Codag, while Codag itself remains an agent-facing compression layer that cannot be ridden, photographed, or encountered in the wild. That pairing makes Codag a disciplined infrastructure craft project, not yet a magically weird object. Biz and Tiz are visible; Rizz is unobservable. I see a founder who has touched infrastructure, but not yet the autobiographical scar that explains why logs became his life’s work.

**Uber** is the high rung for unambiguous authority: Travis Kalanick was unmistakably the human driving the machine, which made disagreement legible even when the ride was chaotic. Codag shares that mechanism because Michael Zhou is the sole named founder and CEO authority cannot deadlock across a 50/50 partnership. Codag diverges because the dossier provides no founder-vesting, succession, or conflict protocol; solo control avoids the **HQ Trivia** fracture, but it does not prove Zhou has built the governance muscles that Codag will need after the first senior hire.

**DeepMind** is the low rung on my frontier-and-regulation ladder: pure software intelligence with none of the rockets, oil fields, drones, ketamine clinics, or ugly regulated surfaces I normally use as accumulating moats. DeepMind escaped that weakness because Demis Hassabis was a genuine polymath; Codag offers a computer-science and neuroscience degree plus infrastructure work, but no comparable cross-domain compounding. Codag’s fine-tuned models, JSON capsules, and PII redaction are useful engineering, yet Axiom and the $8 million-funded Sazabi can absorb the same functionality into broader observability platforms. The red flag is model-and-workflow defensibility without a hostile physical or regulatory environment protecting Codag.

Michael Zhou’s Okta and Shopify infrastructure work places Codag above the market-analysis founder who discovered observability from a TAM slide. Over 18 months, that experience can compound through better failure taxonomies, production-safe PII handling, and line-level evidence attribution—if Codag gains access to real incidents. The unstated pattern is still missing: Codag’s dossier contains no specific night Zhou spent drowning in a million-line outage, and the 638 GitHub stars belong to Codag’s prior workflow visualizer rather than the current product. Zhou is adjacent to the well pad; Codag has not shown that he lived on it.

Axiom’s native MCP support and Sazabi’s agent-optimized compression make Codag look more timely than non-consensus. For Codag’s weirdness score to flip into my invest range, credible observability buyers would need to reject Codag because the compression architecture looked dangerous or alien—and then independent diagnosis results would need to show those buyers were wrong. Today Codag publishes a company-controlled reduction from 26.6 million tokens to 3,317 in under one second, while no current customer, revenue figure, or independent benchmark is public. Codag is clever compression in a market already announcing that agents will query telemetry; that is a rebuttal to commoditization, not yet a rebuttal to consensus.

**Niantic** is my single anchor for physical verifiability because I could play Ingress before I needed a spreadsheet to believe John Hanke. Codag’s open-source CLI and one-line installer give Codag more tactile truth than a closed B2B API: an engineer can run logs through it, inspect the IncidentCapsule, and follow cited source lines within 48 hours. But Codag’s artifact remains visible mainly to infrastructure engineers, and the crucial claim—whether discarded lines conceal the real cause—cannot be felt the way a ride, delivery, camera, or game can. Installation is observable; diagnostic reliability is still founder testimony.

Codag’s 1.2-million-line demonstration points to my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Codag will have pivoted from standalone log compression into an agent-oriented observability ingestion component and will be acquired by Axiom for under $30 million after failing to exceed $1 million ARR. Codag’s compression can create value, but native MCP distribution and bundled telemetry economics favor the platform already holding the logs. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing; the likely asset is Codag’s compression machinery and Zhou’s infrastructure talent, not Codag’s current pricing page.

Codag’s strongest bull case starts with the 638-star prior repository, a one-line installer, a solo technical founder, and a claimed 8,000-fold token reduction: perhaps Zhou repeatedly ships developer products, transfers that distribution skill into Codag, and produces an **HQ Trivia**-style exception where intense product pull overrides my dislike of abstract software. That case stings because agent context is expensive and Codag solves a problem engineers can test immediately. The evidence that wins today is that the 638 stars predate the pivot, the benchmark is company-controlled, and Sazabi and Axiom already bundle overlapping capabilities. Verified current-product ARR above $1 million from at least 25 recurring organizations by July 2027 would flip my verdict; until then, my own money stays in my pocket.
