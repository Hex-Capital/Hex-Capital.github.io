# Tilion -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Tilion has built something difficult. What I cannot yet see is what the founders did when something difficult went wrong. Those are separate questions. Fortress is working browser infrastructure, with native Chromium patches and an external benchmark reporting 156 ms median lifecycle latency and 99 successful runs out of 100 on October 22. But the dossier contains no specific sequence of a founder encountering a blocker, finding a workaround, and changing the outcome within days or weeks. I give the technical proof substantial credit. I leave resourcefulness at its missing-evidence default.

The most promising part is where the team chose to work. Browser agents fail at an awkward boundary between software and the systems trying to identify it. Tilion is modifying the browser itself rather than merely adding another automation interface. That fits my preference for technical founders building primitives other developers can compose. Arham maintains Fortress releases, and the founders have shared research and a college relationship behind them. This is more substantial than a team assembled around an application. It still does not establish that they encountered this particular problem in their own work, or that they have changed their approach after a failed hypothesis.

Who needs Fortress badly enough to keep using it? Engineers running browser agents are a plausible narrow well. The reported 100,000 installs and use by engineers at 170 companies suggest that the product has reached that well. They do not tell me how deep it is. Downloads can accumulate while usage disappears. “Millions of operations daily” could be powerful evidence, but without a workload breakdown it cannot distinguish many dependent users from a few heavy experiments. I give demand credit above the default, while keeping it below the level earned by repeat usage, retention, or payment.

There is a serious case for investing anyway. Tilion fits two patterns I like: a programmable interface for newly autonomous users, and concrete technical proof before a financing story. A working product can also justify an exception when founder history is thin. The strongest argument against my hesitation is that developers have already selected the product, and public information simply trails reality. But the independent benchmark establishes speed and reliability on its tested workload; it does not establish durable access across protected sites. Tilion’s own broader test achieved 86.4% success across 92 sites, and self-hosted users still need suitable network egress. The evidence supports an impressive component. It has not yet established an indispensable service strong enough to override the missing founder evidence.

The commercial question follows directly. Will users pay Tilion to absorb the continuing burden of keeping their workflows working? The $2,000 monthly organization license offers a way to test that. It is a price, however, rather than disclosed revenue. Earlier BSD releases remain available for forks, and competitors already patch browser engines. Tilion therefore needs continuing usefulness that makes maintaining a fork unattractive. The licensing change alone proves neither customer learning nor adaptability. The hosted product’s open-access announcement followed by a current waitlist also leaves the actual buying path unclear. I see no documented dependence on a grand launch or a large partner; the unresolved issue is whether ordinary users return and buy.

I would hold off on a check today. Tilion clears my technical threshold but does not clear my founder-resourcefulness gate, and the demand evidence is not yet strong enough to replace it. This is a neutral judgment with a specific route to conviction. My flip condition is one documented episode in which a paying customer’s production workflow breaks, the founders ship a measured fix within a week, and that customer renews afterward. That would connect the impressive software to the behavior I am actually investing in.
