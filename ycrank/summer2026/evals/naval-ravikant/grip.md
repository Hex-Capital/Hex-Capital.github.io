# Grip -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Jonas Gruetter achieved 70% boulder-removal success with a 12-ton autonomous excavator before applying reinforcement learning to irregular waste. Grip is a specific-knowledge founder wrapped in a low-network architecture: the intelligence lives in Grip’s machines, but each new waste operator does not automatically make every prior operator more valuable. Rare technical ability can create a good robotics business. It does not automatically create the asymmetric geometry I seek with my own money.

**Stack Overflow** is the high rung for specific knowledge: developers built for developers after years of lived immersion, then shipped the artifact into public view. Grip shares the load-bearing mechanism because Gruetter worked at Gravis Robotics and ETH’s Robotic Systems Lab and published research on real heavy machinery. Grip diverges where proof should escape the laboratory: no public repository, deployed fleet, throughput, accuracy, or contamination-tolerance result establishes that excavator knowledge transfers to wet organic waste. I score the founder near the high rung, not Grip’s product validation.

**Perplexity** sits at the low rung for direct user-added network value: one user does not automatically improve another user’s experience, so exceptional distribution and founder knowledge had to waive the network criterion. Grip has the same single-sided weakness without Perplexity’s permissionless product distribution or pre-consensus AI timing. Grip may learn from every successful and failed grasp, but that is an internal data flywheel, not a marketplace: facilities do not attract other facilities, and custom deployments may partition the data by feedstock and end effector.

Grip’s founder-led outreach to waste, composting, recycling, and biogas operators reveals how the leverage compounds over the next 18 months. Every Grip installation requires hardware, facility integration, maintenance, and operator permission; no visible repository, founder X audience, or accessible website supplies the permissionless distribution pattern running through my actual portfolio. Grip can use code to improve gross margins after deployment, but code inside capital equipment is not code as distribution. Against my unstated pattern, Grip has neither organic builder adoption nor media leverage; against my red flags, direct enterprise outreach precedes any disclosed unprompted adoption.

The **$962.3 million** waste-sorting-equipment market, projected to reach only **$1.43 billion by 2030**, gives Grip limited room for two failed ideas. Grip can move among composting, recycling, and biogas facilities, but those are adjacent buyers for similar equipment rather than a platform-shaped market. The score flips upward only if Grip’s learning-and-manipulation stack transfers without major hardware redesign into construction debris, mining, agriculture, or general material handling. Gruetter’s excavator work makes that counterfactual plausible; Grip’s current wet-waste positioning does not establish it.

**Notion** is the single-anchor parallel for the 10-year-partner test: Ivan Zhao’s long obsession showed intelligence and energy through repeated construction, while integrity became legible through years of behavior. Grip gives me evidence for two legs—Gruetter’s field robotics work and the 70% boulder-removal result—but almost none for the third. Grip discloses no co-founder history, prior venture outcome, or record of operating through conflict. Silence is not an integrity failure at pre-seed, so I apply the neutral default rather than inventing virtue or vice.

**ZenRobotics** has operated in waste sorting since 2007, which makes slow commercial adoption the base rate rather than an edge case. By July 2029, Grip will have pivoted from plastic removal in wet organic waste toward broader autonomous material handling in mining or construction and will still be below **$1 million ARR**. Grip’s strongest asset—Gruetter’s transferable robotics knowledge—pulls toward the adjacent market; Grip’s weakest asset—the absence of network distribution—keeps deployment revenue linear and slow.

**Anchorage Digital** supplies the strongest bull case: permission can become a moat when regulation, difficult operations, and proprietary data exclude weaker entrants. Grip could similarly turn wet-waste contamination into a difficult niche where every failed grasp improves a proprietary model, EU packaging rules create urgency, and Recycleye, EverestLabs, and ZenRobotics remain optimized for drier streams. The objection stings because Gruetter may possess exactly the rare knowledge that justified exceptions such as Anchorage and Perplexity. Grip still reports no customer deployment, dataset size, removal rate, throughput, or uptime, so today the “moat” is a sentence about future learning. I would flip to Invest if one named facility verifies a paid Grip deployment sustaining at least 95% plastic removal and 90% uptime for six consecutive months.
