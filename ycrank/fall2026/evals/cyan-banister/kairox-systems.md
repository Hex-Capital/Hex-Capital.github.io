# Kairox Systems -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A coastal unit launches one aircraft, sends it underwater to inspect a suspected mine, and retrieves it through the air. What disappeared? A second vehicle, a separate deployment, perhaps a support boat. That is a future I can inhabit. Kairox Systems has specified the sequence that could get us there: fixed-wing transit, water entry, submerged movement, and emergence into flight. But the distance between those verbs matters. Tempest is reported at TRL 4, with no published range, endurance, depth, or speed. The configuration gives me a concrete future to investigate; the dossier does not establish that combining these capabilities produces a useful mission envelope.

The founders have relevant operating tuition. Jorge’s control-law and hardware qualification work at Oligo Space matters more to me than the MIT label: this vehicle must survive transitions between environments that punish different engineering compromises. Joel’s mechanical background and Alex’s electrical and computer-science background give the team complementary coverage. What I cannot find is the earlier trail of obsession—dated experiments, stubborn independent builds, or recruitment around a problem they understood before starting Kairox. Their biographies support technical competence more strongly than the first-believer conviction I underwrite with my own money.

Why are aerial and underwater missions separate today? The dossier identifies separate operating and logistics requirements, but it does not tell me which costs are unnecessary and which purchase indispensable performance. Combining vehicles could return operational control to a small unit; it could also force that unit to accept shorter endurance in both environments. No quantified deployment cost or delay resolves that tradeoff. The pressure-vessel architecture and modular payloads represent tangible engineering, yet Kairox has not demonstrated a scarce enabling asset beneath several downstream businesses. My pattern of backing concealed infrastructure is only weakly activated here. SubUAS’s existing air–water platform makes the distinction especially important: crossing the boundary is already possible; Kairox must make crossing it operationally preferable.

The strongest argument against passing is wonderfully concrete: three people built an initial prototype with a $14,000 grant and report Marine Corps engagement. Modest resources have already become hardware, and military users are reportedly participating in validation discussions. **CosmicBrain AI** is the relevant exception to my usual demand for user proof: a physical prototype can justify investing before revenue when founder obsession and committed industrial testing bridge the uncertainty. Kairox shares the prototype-led path, but this dossier supplies neither that lifelong build history nor signed proofs of concept. QLab acceptance and a conference showcase help the team reach believers; they do not establish that an operator has repeatedly entrusted a mission to Tempest. I give no extra user-behavior credit to those introductions.

I pass on Kairox Systems at this evidence level. Defense is not the objection, and absent revenue at pre-seed does not trouble me. The decisive gap is whether one machine actually removes a deployment burden after accounting for its compromises. Joel is identified as CEO, and the dossier establishes no affirmative governance or privacy red flag; those gates do not explain this decision. One verifiable event would reopen it: a named military unit paying for repeated, representative air–water–air missions that demonstrably replace its separate aerial and underwater deployment workflow. That would turn the appealing shape of this machine into evidence that someone’s operating world has changed.
