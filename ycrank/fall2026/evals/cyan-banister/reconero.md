# Reconero -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. An officer finishes an incident, checks a report against its underlying evidence, and returns to work without spending the evening reconstructing events. Reconero’s Alyx points toward that world: search departmental records, extract information from body-camera footage, prefill forms. But the distance matters. Reliable evidence extraction must become reliable reporting, and reliable reporting must survive departmental implementation and legal scrutiny. The dossier establishes the proposed functions; it does not yet establish those transitions. I can imagine the destination. I cannot treat my imagination as Reconero’s evidence.

Why does the paperwork consume so much time? Reconero claims over **40% of an officer’s day** goes into documentation. That is a concrete place to look for wasted human life. But how much comes from duplicate entry, necessary evidentiary detail, or incompatible systems? Those causes require different remedies. The dossier identifies manual documentation in legacy software without establishing who captures an avoidable rent or how much time Alyx actually returns. Open-format exports are a welcome design choice. They are not yet proof that departments can escape an incumbent’s operating constraints.

The founders give me a stronger reason to keep looking. Riwan Azeroual previously built a sentiment-analysis business reporting **$150K ARR**; that belongs to his earlier company, but it demonstrates more commercial initiative than a list of internships. Edward Liu co-developed ReconeroRMS for the Gemini Live Agent Challenge, and the two met through their high school’s Hack Club. There are builders here, with a relationship preceding the fundraising story. What is missing is a documented, sustained obsession with policing’s particular operating problems. Shipping software and understanding what makes a police report defensible are separate accomplishments.

The strongest objection to passing is that these founders are already learning inside the institution. **September 2026 reporting describes police-unit trials and iterative officer feedback.** That is more informative than a waitlist: someone has let the product encounter actual work. It fits my pattern of finding opportunity in unglamorous workflow repair. The police-data mechanism also warrants checking the **Flock Safety** exception: a specific technical insight can make an uncomfortable category investable. The material difference is that Reconero’s dossier establishes no comparable technical advantage. Unnamed trials, unspecified repeat usage, and overlapping offerings from Mark43 and Axon leave that exception unearned.

Then there is the evidence itself. Who may search it? Which generated sentence came from which recording? Can an officer’s correction be distinguished from the original observation? Reconero requires review and signature, but those controls do not answer the custody and surveillance questions. The dossier does not demonstrate severe privacy misconduct; it leaves my privacy gate unresolved. Likewise, CEO and CTO titles identify apparent responsibilities without establishing a governance problem. I will not invent either a violation or a clean bill of health. My pattern of backing infrastructure beneath a visible application also offers little affirmative support here: a combined records-and-dispatch ambition does not establish control of a scarce enabling asset.

I pass on Reconero today. The founders have shown initiative, and the trial feedback deserves attention, but I cannot yet connect this product to a distinctive future that they are equipped to own. My flip condition is an independently audited, 90-day departmental deployment demonstrating sustained officer use, a substantial measured reduction in documentation time, and source-traceable reports under enforced access controls. One such operating record would tell me far more than the **$20.4B** category estimate: whether Reconero has discovered a repeatable way through the institution’s hidden complexity.
