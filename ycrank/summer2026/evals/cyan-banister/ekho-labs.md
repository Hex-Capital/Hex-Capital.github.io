# Ekho Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and ocean-freight teams should not be reading disruption alerts; their systems should already be comparing routes, carrier capacity, sanctions, weather, and delivery commitments before presenting the operator with a decision. Ekho Labs has one artifact leaking from that world: a reported 12-day median warning lead time paired with PO-level reroutes, carrier alternatives, and booking windows. But the dossier does not map the intervening transitions from today’s advisory product to that future. Ten thousand data sources are an impressive ingestion problem, not yet proof that Ekho owns the decision layer.

Alina Park is the strongest part of the bet. She was founding technical lead at Inca Digital, built enterprise financial-intelligence systems, and developed multilingual text-geolocation technology at Yachay AI; the public repository has 160 GitHub stars. That is real technical continuity across intelligence ingestion, location inference, risk detection, and enterprise deployment. It is not yet the full first-believer story: I cannot see a dated freight obsession, a pre-company shipping artifact, or evidence that she recruited the three LinkedIn-listed employees around a hard-won view of ocean operations. The Biz is plausible; the dossier has not shown me enough Tizz or Rizz.

The broken-system mechanism is legible but incomplete. Late carrier and TMS warnings narrow routing options, forcing emergency freight spending, missed commitments, and manual firefighting. Ekho returns some agency to freight operators by moving the decision earlier. Yet it does not quantify the emergency premium, identify which intermediary captures it, or explain why existing systems structurally cannot act sooner. If the answer is merely that Ekho reads more feeds faster, Everstream’s 200-plus enterprise customers and several heavily financed competitors make this a feature race rather than a broken rule being removed.

The strongest bull case is that the working artifact is already better than the public proof suggests. Ekho refers to enterprise customers, reports seven confirmed Hormuz forecasts—including a ceasefire call 21 days early—and has joined the CMA CGM-supported ZEBOX Club. A three-person company integrating sanctions, AIS, carrier EDI, port, weather, government, labor, and social data may have accumulated operating complexity that is difficult to see from the outside. This also fits my unglamorous-workflow pattern: a painful decision buried inside global logistics can become important infrastructure without looking magical in a demo.

But the costly user behavior is missing. I do not know whether a customer changed a booking, paid an emergency premium to avoid a larger loss, renewed a contract, or trusted Ekho over its incumbent visibility stack. The forecast claims are company-authored, with no disclosed false-positive or false-negative rate. Worse, the future moat is explicitly inferred: no proprietary outcome corpus is documented, while the product depends on licensed and public feeds whose availability and latency Ekho does not control. This is beneath the visible AI wave, but it has not yet shown control of the scarce layer beneath it.

I would not write the check today. There is no governance shell game—Park is the sole active founder—and no severe privacy architecture appears in the dossier, so my hard red flags are not triggered. The concern is simpler: relevant founder, credible machinery, crowded market, and no observed customer sacrifice proving that the recommendation changes reality. One verifiable condition flips me: a named freight customer publishes production results showing repeated Ekho-directed reroutes, measured savings, and forecast error rates across both disrupted and normal lanes.
