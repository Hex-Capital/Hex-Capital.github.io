# Cyclon -- Paul Graham Evaluation

What happened when Cyclon’s founders first tried to make an agent operate a real phone? That is the missing story I most want. The dossier gives me a proposed interface, a detachable camera, and two relevant technical backgrounds. It gives me no named obstacle followed by a founder-built workaround and an observable result. I cannot infer resourcefulness from Google or Berkeley. This leaves my most important criterion at its missing-evidence default. It does not establish that the founders are passive.

The strongest case for Cyclon is that these particular founders might actually build it. Thomas Kiefer worked on Android at Google. Adam Zinebi combines microtechnology training with research on LLMs and robotic agents, and previously sold music-generation models. That combination deserves attention. But Cyclon’s hardware remains a concept in development, and its ten public repositories include LineageOS and microG forks. Those establish neither a functioning phone nor reliable agent execution. My pattern of backing technical founders in unfamiliar sectors depends on concrete proof. Here I would want to watch the phone complete a difficult task through an existing account and see what happens when the task fails.

The central product question is why someone would tolerate a crude replacement phone to get this interface. Cyclon offers transportation and directions as examples, but identifies no narrow group whose need is desperate enough to justify switching devices. Its October 27, 2026 waitlist has no disclosed count or repeat-use evidence. Brain’s phone reached Japanese retail through SoftBank in April 2026, and Nothing already offers natural-language app creation. Their existence does not disqualify Cyclon. It makes the founders’ specific discovery about users more consequential. A detachable camera supplies a difference; the dossier does not show that it supplies a reason to switch.

There is substantial schlep here: hardware, account permissions, service integrations, and recovery from unintended actions. Willingness to confront that work could become an advantage. But the dossier does not connect it to a problem either founder personally encountered, or document a product revision prompted by users. Both dimensions therefore remain at their defaults. Nor would I turn absent cofounder history into an accusation about their relationship, or a waitlist announcement into evidence of dependence on a ceremonial launch. The economics are similarly untested: prototype ownership appears in the terms, but no price or revenue is disclosed. Gartner’s $393.3 billion adjacent market forecast cannot tell us whether anyone urgently wants this particular phone.

I would pass on the present evidence. The strongest objection is that a small, appropriately skilled team can produce a surprising prototype before public traction appears. That is true, and it is why this is a pass rather than an adverse judgment about the founders. But the exceptions in my framework require a working prototype, validated technical result, or unmistakable user pull; Cyclon supplies none of those yet. My flip condition is one documented deployment in which the founders overcome a specific blocking failure within two weeks and ten identifiable users subsequently return to the repaired workflow daily for a month. That would establish both what the founders do under resistance and who needs what they built.
