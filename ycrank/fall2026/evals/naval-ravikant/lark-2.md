# Lark -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Lark’s strongest credential is a distributor using what the brothers built. Interline Hardware reports purchasing workflows covering **60,000+ inventory items**, approximately **60 hours saved monthly**, and first use within a month of kickoff. Jason’s supply-chain agent work at Traba adds relevant experience. The deployment supplies something more valuable than employment history: evidence that this team can translate messy operating knowledge into working software. His claimed 400K ARR at Datost belongs to another business; it establishes no revenue for Lark.

The ownership question is harder. If a competitor copied the application, what would Lark retain? Customer-specific ERP mappings and purchasing rules exist, but the dossier establishes neither exclusive rights nor measured replacement costs. Configuration can become an asset. It can also remain work that another implementation team performs again. I give the founders substantial credit for shipping and retain the absent-evidence default for durability. Counting the same deployment as both execution and moat would manufacture conviction.

The strongest case against passing is that Lark has already crossed the boundary where many agents fail: useful action inside a customer’s operating system. Interline’s named testimonial matters; the separate, unnamed claim of **50 hours saved weekly** adds weaker corroboration. Capturing experienced buyers’ rules also fits my preference for turning individual knowledge into scalable output. But this remains leverage within each customer. There is no evidence that adding one distributor improves another distributor’s forecasts, acquisition, or purchasing power. Fragmented offline commerce creates an opportunity for a network; Lark has demonstrated workflow automation.

Purchasing supplies a concrete entry point. Forecasting, sales, and analytics are advertised adjacencies, but the dossier does not establish demand for that expansion or the economic breadth it unlocks. I therefore keep market expansion at its default rather than crediting the product menu. Subscription terms likewise identify an intended charging mechanism without establishing a paying customer, price, or realized revenue. Hours saved are customer value. The fraction Lark can capture remains unknown. Didero’s existing procurement execution makes that distinction consequential: useful automation already has competing suppliers.

I would **pass today**. Nothing supplied triggers an integrity or artificial-deadline veto. I also checked the exceptions: early revenue can be dispensable when self-reinforcing adoption is visible, or when a distinctive prototype and demonstrated capital efficiency justify backing the builders before distribution emerges. Lark’s deployment is encouraging, but those override mechanisms are unproven here. My verifiable flip condition is a matched deployment study showing that reusable mappings and purchasing rules from earlier customers materially improve later customers’ forecasting accuracy without increasing implementation labor. That would show customer work becoming a compounding asset.
