# Stoa -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what broke. Stoa has routed more than $300 million in RFQs during its first month, but the dossier never identifies an obstacle the founders encountered, the workaround they built, or how quickly it changed user behavior. The amount is striking; the causal story is not. I cannot infer relentless resourcefulness from three strong résumés or from leaving an xAI opportunity. This matters because founder behavior is the gate, not an ornament added after market analysis.

There is nevertheless a real product here. Buyers specify hardware, vetted dealers submit blind firm quotes, and the system records awards, shipping, inspection, and settlement on a 48-hour RFQ cycle. That is substantially more than a diagram. The founders also have unusually relevant ingredients: Kaan Yigit built trading and pricing systems at Uniper, Eren Berke Saglam traded derivatives at Citi, and the three have known one another for more than ten years. But the proof stops halfway. Stoa discloses neither executed volume nor revenue, its H100 index is unavailable, and it explicitly declines responsibility for whether hardware exists, arrives, or matches its description. They have built the exchange’s software before proving the exchange’s transaction.

The narrow user niche is credible: institutional GPU buyers and dealers dealing in private quotes and forwarded inventory lists. A first-month $300 million RFQ notional suggests these users will at least try the crude product. It does not yet show that they pull it back. An RFQ can be inexpensive curiosity; a settled order exposes the buyer to money, delivery, authenticity, and counterparty risk. No repeat usage, payment, referral, or executed trade is disclosed. The difference between a marketplace and a lead-generation form is what happens after the quote.

The strongest bull case is that Stoa matches an important unstated pattern: the team has shipped a programmable market interface and is already collecting the proprietary bid and offer data from which a benchmark could emerge. The market is also expanding, with AI data-center chip shipments projected from $123 billion in 2024 to $286 billion in 2030, while Blackwell upgrades push H100s into secondary circulation. If the $300 million represents genuine institutional demand, the founders may have assembled the substrate before outsiders can see the network. Their decade-long relationship also avoids the red flag of an application-assembled team.

But the schlep has not disappeared merely because the interface exists. Compute Exchange already spans physical GPUs, reserved capacity, and forward contracts across more than 100 providers; Procurri performs inspection, logistics, and asset disposition. Stoa instead relies on contracts, counterparty screening, a 12-month non-circumvention clause, and disclaimers against the hardest hardware risks. That creates both commoditization and disintermediation risk. There is no documented product-route change after evidence, no founder-origin story tied specifically to GPU trading, and no known exception strong enough to override the failed resourcefulness gate: RFQ notional is not unmistakable real-world pull.

I would not write the check today. My forecast is that Stoa is more likely to become a specialized GPU brokerage and pricing-data service than a liquid exchange, because its strongest evidence is the trading workflow while its weakest evidence concerns overcoming the physical transaction’s blockers. One result would flip me: three named institutional buyers each completing and then repeating an inspected, paid settlement through Stoa within 90 days. That would prove users need the whole mechanism rather than merely wanting another quote.
