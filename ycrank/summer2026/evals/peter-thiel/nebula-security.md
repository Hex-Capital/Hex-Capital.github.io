# Nebula Security -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Nebula Security’s implicit contested truth is that autonomous source-level agents can reproduce elite offensive-security research—not merely flag suspicious code—and continuously produce validated exploits and patches. The falsifier is straightforward: Vega fails to reproduce its kernel-and-browser results on ordinary customer repositories without extensive intervention from the four researchers. The dossier does not name a belief rejected by competitors; OpenAI, Microsoft, RunSybil, XBOW, Depthfirst, and Hacktron are all pursuing versions of the same automation thesis. This is therefore a technically supported proposition, but not yet a secret.

The technical evidence is unusually substantive for pre-seed. Nebula reports 1,393 validated findings, including 1,356 Linux-kernel bugs, 98 public CVEs, and eight Chrome zero-days; the team previously earned more than $400,000 in Google bug bounties, while its exploit repository has 1,300 GitHub stars and 364 forks. Those are harder facts than an AI-security demonstration. But they do not establish whether Vega autonomously generated the results, whether the findings generalize beyond the founders’ specialties, or whether its performance is ten times better than competing systems. Elite research ability creates value; it does not automatically create a company that captures it.

The proposed market is also too broad to be a convincing beachhead. Nebula simultaneously advertises operating systems, browsers, web infrastructure, agentic systems, Solidity, and EVM software, then sells metered repository scanning, enterprise plans, and human-assisted audits. That is a catalogue, not a monopoly sequence. The inferred feedback-data moat could eventually matter, but no customer-scale scanning history, exclusive dataset, dominant segment, or measured retention supports it today. Competitive convergence is particularly damaging because frontier-model vendors can absorb generic code-scanning features, while well-financed offensive-security startups already occupy adjacent positions.

The founding structure is the strongest evidence in the dossier. Eten Zou and Yuan Tan coauthored systems-security work at UC Riverside; Xiaochuan Yu and Frank Wu share the r3kapig operating community; all four have directly relevant exploit, fuzzing, CTF, or program-analysis records. Core technical work appears to be in-house, and no part-time founder, outsourced engineering, excessive salary, secondary sale, or investor-dominated capitalization is documented. Missing evidence remains missing, however: the dossier does not verify that every founder is full-time, that the whole group has previously shipped together, or that founders retain material ownership.

The strongest bull case is the known-exception mechanism: an independently verified zero-to-one technical result can outweigh a crowded market. Eight Chrome zero-days and 98 CVEs could be precisely that result if Vega produced them autonomously and repeatedly. The current evidence cannot separate proprietary automation from an exceptional research team using automation, and the commercial record is thinner still. An enterprise private beta followed a YC-company launch, but there are no named paying customers, prices, conversion rates, sales cycles, margins, or implementation costs. My 36-month base case is $3 million to $8 million in ARR, with most revenue still coming from researcher-assisted audits rather than a compounding self-serve product.

I would investigate, but not invest today. Nebula fits the increasing importance of adversarial information systems and has genuine technical substance; it does not trigger the usual organizational red flags. Yet the distinction that matters is still unresolved: proprietary system or talented consultancy, monopoly or commodity. One condition would flip the decision: two independent enterprises each paying at least $100,000 ARR and verifying over six months that Vega repeatedly found critical, exploitable vulnerabilities missed by Codex Security or MDASH.
