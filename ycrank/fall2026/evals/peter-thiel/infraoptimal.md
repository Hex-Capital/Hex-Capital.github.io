# InfraOptimal -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

InfraOptimal has demonstrated that agents can find a compiler improvement. It has not demonstrated the important truth that makes a separate inference company necessary. Its September experiment reduced decoder time by approximately 3–5.6% on an Apple M2 Pro; the current product sells self-optimizing dedicated endpoints. The missing proposition connects those two facts: what can autonomous optimization consistently achieve that existing inference platforms cannot? No named peer rejects a precise claim in this dossier. I would give the secret the absent-evidence default rather than invent one for the founders.

The distinction matters because InfraOptimal enters an existing purchasing decision. Baseten, Fireworks, and Together already sell inference infrastructure with performance optimization, while AWS supplies kernel-writing and profiling agents. Continuous optimization search could improve the product, but the dossier identifies neither a bounded customer segment InfraOptimal can dominate nor an observed mechanism that keeps competitors out. Retained transformations are potentially useful intellectual property; cross-customer compounding remains an inference. This is an adverse monopoly signal, sufficient for a presumptive pass.

The founding structure is substantially better than the market structure. Cassidy and Kocheta describe being friends since fourth grade and leaving school to work full-time. Their compiler, processor-validation, and research-systems backgrounds make the published engineering work credible. These are affirmative commitment signals, not résumé decoration. Ownership, economic exposure, and a history of shipping together remain undocumented, so I cannot give the foundation full marks. But nothing here supports the convenience-cofounder or part-time-founder red flags.

The strongest objection to passing is that InfraOptimal may be exposing the first small result of a much larger optimization system. The scoped Z3 proof, reproducible experiments, and acknowledgment of hardware-dependent regressions suggest founders willing to distinguish a measured result from a universal claim. If their system repeatedly discovers transformations that manual teams miss, a growing library could eventually make each subsequent deployment cheaper to optimize. That would fit the pattern of owning an essential layer of AI production. Today, however, the measured experiment was agent-assisted, concerned a decoder on an M2 Pro, and did not establish autonomous GPU-endpoint performance. The bull case requires a consequential technical bridge that the dossier has not yet crossed.

The commercial model does not resolve that gap. Prepaid, minute-based capacity billing is one coherent revenue engine, but public evidence supplies no contract values, gross margins, or customer expansion arithmetic supporting a power-law outcome. Charging for idle capacity also leaves utilization economics with the buyer. A self-service console, coding-agent integration, and consultation bookings are concrete acquisition routes, worth modest credit; conversion, implementation burden, and repeatability are unproved. Competitors’ billion-dollar revenue figures establish demand for their offerings, not value capture for InfraOptimal.

I would pass with my own money. The known exceptions require something decisive—an independently demonstrated technical discontinuity, persistent customer transactions, or a concrete deployment path that removes the central risk. None is established here, and the broader allocation toward the AI production stack cannot substitute for it. One verifiable event would reopen the decision: an independently audited, paid 90-day deployment in a defined workload segment showing at least twice the throughput per all-in dollar of the customer’s best incumbent configuration, with equivalent output quality and without ongoing manual tuning. That would turn an optimization promise into evidence of proprietary advantage.
