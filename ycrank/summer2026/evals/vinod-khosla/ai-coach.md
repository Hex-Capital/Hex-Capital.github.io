# COACH -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

COACH is already a good pre-seed business. It is not yet a consequential technology bet. The founders report €1 million ARR within eight months, thousands of reps, 74,473 analyzed meetings, and a 32% conversion-rate lift. Those numbers establish demand. They do not establish that success rewrites a major cost pool by 10x or 100x. The quantified market is a $6.9 billion sales-enablement category, while the product improves coaching inside that category. This is useful productivity software, not yet an option on a radically different world.

The technical case is weaker than the commercial case. Rilla and Siro already record, transcribe, analyze, and coach face-to-face sellers. COACH relies on Anthropic for AI and transcription, publishes no model-performance benchmark, and offers no validated evidence that its “one skill at a time” method produces a discontinuity rather than better implementation. The claimed 27%–32% conversion lift could matter, but without cohort definitions, controls, or comparison against Rilla, Siro, and ordinary manager coaching, it is marketing evidence—not a 5–10x technical result. A corpus of 74,473 meetings may become a learning loop, but possession of data is not proof that the data compounds into proprietary capability.

The founders are the strongest part of the case. Mathieu and Thomas operated a 44-rep field-sales business that generated €3.5 million in 14 months, so they lived inside the workflow rather than discovering it through market research. Mathieu closed Lucy and redirected the team into COACH; that is evidence of tactical revision. Recruiting Nicolas, a mobile engineer with ten years of software experience, also mapped a complementary skill to the product. Yet the team has not documented a belief changed because of product data, nor recruited an exceptional owner for the largest remaining risks: defensible modeling, consent-compliant capture, and rigorous outcome measurement.

The bull case stings. Execution-heavy businesses can become enormous without scientific novelty, and €1 million ARR, 100% pilot conversion, 0% reported churn, and 120% monthly growth are extraordinary signals at this stage. Five large French customers could provide a beachhead, while 200-company usage could turn COACH into the system of record for field-sales behavior. In 36 months, I can plausibly see $15–30 million ARR and a serious U.S. expansion. But that forecast describes a strong SaaS company competing through sales execution and workflow polish. It does not justify my preferred option economics when two heavily financed companies already pursue substantially the same mechanism.

Capital allocation is also unconvincing. The dossier names consent exposure, processor dependency, metric inconsistency, and commoditization, but supplies no ranked kill risk, decisive experiment, threshold, cost, or stop condition. Hiring a designer and expanding through forward-deployed pilots may accelerate revenue before proving durable technical advantage. SaaS delivery should permit attractive unsubsidized economics, but COACH discloses no gross margin, inference cost, implementation burden, retention cohort, or manager-hours saved, so economic scalability remains an assumption.

I pass despite admiring the founders’ velocity. The known exceptions do not rescue the decision: strong operators and a real value pool can override weak initial technology only when the product has room to evolve into much broader infrastructure. COACH currently presents a narrower coaching application surrounded by well-capitalized substitutes. One result would flip me: a preregistered, independently auditable comparison showing that COACH delivers at least a 5x improvement over Rilla, Siro, or conventional ride-along coaching in conversion gain per manager-hour, sustained across multiple customer cohorts, with the advantage increasing measurably as its proprietary corpus grows.
