# CONUS -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI retrieval makes fragmented device documentation easier to navigate; CONUS gives hospitals access and asks manufacturers to help finance the network. The controlling variable is whether recurring hospital use produces recurring manufacturer revenue at a delivery cost that falls as the network grows. That could create a valuable distribution business. The dossier establishes paid manufacturer services, but does not establish renewal economics or the cost of maintaining each hospital’s knowledge. I would pass on a personal check today.

The strongest argument against passing is that CONUS already has both sides of the market moving. It reports more than 20 European hospitals live and relationships with Johnson & Johnson, Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, and B. Braun. Its August 2026 institutional terms explicitly identify paid manufacturer services as a financing source. Those are meaningful pre-seed signals, substantially stronger than a waitlist. But free hospital deployment does not establish recurring clinician use, and named manufacturer relationships do not establish recurring commercial contracts. The unspecified starting date for the claimed six-month deployment period also limits what I can infer about adoption speed.

The product has more substance than a medical document summarizer. Structured manufacturer information, hospital-specific knowledge, and answers linked to original source pages give CONUS useful context that a general model does not automatically possess. Better models should improve retrieval and synthesis. The unresolved issue is how much of the operational loop CONUS owns afterward. Its terms require source verification and exclude patient-specific product-selection decisions. That leaves an important boundary between answering a preparation question and controlling the implant-selection or procurement workflow. C8 Health and Incision already approach this customer through adjacent workflows.

The reported 500,000 papers and 2,000 manufacturer documents do not establish a data moat. I would want the time, money, and commercial permissions required to reproduce the useful portion of that corpus. Nor does broader medical-AI adoption establish CONUS’s economic step change: OpenEvidence’s reported 1.12 million verified US clinicians demonstrates category adoption, not lower implementation costs or higher margins here. This fits my pattern of looking for software that performs institutional work, but the dossier currently demonstrates knowledge access more clearly than completed work. Manufacturer-funded distribution could support adjacent products; expansion into selection would require resolving the product’s current scope restrictions. The prohibition on preferential sorting is constructive, although it does not establish buyers’ willingness to renew.

The team has relevant capabilities: Paffrath’s AI and healthcare strategy experience, Becker’s automation work, Schulz’s biomedical engineering, and five chief-surgeon founders or advisors. Those credentials support execution credibility. They do not document costly personal commitment or prior startup learning, so I would keep that dimension at its stated default. I also see no basis for a known-exception override: this is neither documented frontier research requiring investment ahead of demand nor demonstrated reconstruction of incumbent unit economics. **My flip condition is a verified renewal cohort across the existing hospital network showing repeat clinician use, renewed manufacturer contracts, and positive contribution margin after document maintenance and hospital onboarding.** That would connect the promising distribution footprint to the economic mechanism I need to underwrite.
