# Aucert -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Aucert’s consequential possibility is making expert mobile verification cheap enough to run continuously. That could change how much software a small engineering team can safely ship. But the dossier establishes an $11.45 billion testing market, not the fraction of engineering labor Aucert can eliminate or the additional software that becomes economical to build. I can describe the upside mechanism. I cannot yet quantify an outcome large enough to justify buying a very unlikely success with my own money.

The technical question is whether autonomous exploration removes the expensive human work of specifying what should happen. Aucert learns approved expectations and remembers screen graphs and paths. That is a plausible mechanism. It also leaves a difficult boundary: navigating an app is different from knowing that its behavior is correct. If customers must continually teach expectations and adjudicate findings, labor moves rather than disappears. BrowserStack already offers testing agents, while Drizz supports English-directed visual tests. There is no comparative evidence of defect recall, false-positive burden, coverage, or engineering hours saved. I will not infer either a breakthrough or a commodity wrapper from that absence.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders. Vivek Soneja was a founding mobile architect at PhonePe; Rajesh Kumar spent 11 years at PayPal and worked on patented automated testing. These are people who have encountered the expensive failure modes inside the workflow. Their complementary mobile and testing experience makes the autonomous-verification thesis more credible than a generic agent pitch. PhonePe’s 500 million-plus users establish the environment Vivek worked in, however, not Aucert’s reach. Nor does the record document a belief revised after an experiment or a difficult recruit brought in to own a missing capability. Relevant experience earns my attention; it does not supply those separate proofs.

Aucert also fits my broader pattern of backing execution-heavy software when a narrow entry point can become infrastructure. Organization-specific expectations and verdict history could support that expansion, but customer-owned records do not yet demonstrate a proprietary learning advantage. I checked the exception case: exceptional instigators addressing an enormous existing value pool can justify investing before technical superiority or business economics are established. Here, the domain credentials are substantial, but the dossier does not establish that wider ambition or mechanism. Conversely, there is no evidence of exit fixation, stubborn rejection of data, or spending on distribution ahead of acknowledged technical risks. Competitive overlap is a concern, not proof of those red flags.

The next dollars should test whether useful autonomy survives contact with real releases. Draft terms acknowledge missed defects, but disclose no decisive experiment, threshold, budget, or response to failure. Unredacted screenshots reaching AI providers also create a concrete adoption question for the payments customers these founders understand. The free scan and October 2026 launch establish availability, not repeat usage or purchasing commitment. Undisclosed subscription prices and absent inference, device, and human-review costs leave economic replacement unproven. None of this requires a larger team or years of revenue history to resolve.

I pass at 25/100. The current evidence supports experienced builders addressing a real expense; it does not yet establish a discontinuity in that expense. My one verifiable condition for reopening the decision is an independently auditable trial across several customer apps showing at least 10x lower total verification cost than the customers’ existing workflows, at equal or better recall of independently confirmed release-blocking defects, including expectation setup and human review. That would turn autonomous exploration from an attractive description into evidence that expertise can become radically cheaper.
