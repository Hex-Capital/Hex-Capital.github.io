# Greypoint Industries -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Darcy Zhang served as a Canadian Armed Forces infanteer, while Greypoint Industries has already signed its first ARROW contract. Greypoint Industries is a schlep-heavy startup whose founders have not yet shown that the idea is organic to their own pain. Military procurement, export controls, hardware manufacturing, and autonomous interception are an impressive pile of unpleasant work. But none of the four founders is described personally needing LEGION or building ARROW for himself before deciding to start a defense company. I see animals running toward a hard problem. I do not yet see the lost USB stick.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness: the compliance grind was not an obstacle around the product but part of the product. Greypoint Industries has the same structural advantage. Canada’s competitive qualification process, controlled-goods registration, export permits, field testing, sensor integration, and interceptor manufacturing will repel founders who merely want to train models. Greypoint Industries diverges from Coinbase because ARROW has no published range, speed, accuracy, test volume, or deployment result. The signed contract proves willingness to enter the schlep; it does not yet prove mastery of it.

**Loopt** (2005) exposed the low-rung failure mechanism on organic founder pain: exceptional founder force can override a product whose need has not become concrete enough. Greypoint Industries has unusually relevant biographies—Zhang’s infantry service, Jordan Khoo’s uncrewed-aircraft work, and Nic Chu’s General Dynamics experience—but Greypoint Industries never supplies the decisive sentence: “We encountered this failure ourselves and built the first version to fix it.” That omission matters. Counter-UAS is a fashionable and rapidly funded category, and a defense résumé is not the same thing as an organic idea. Loopt is the known exception, not the model I want to repeat.

Greypoint Industries’ first counter-UAS contract, 2025 Defence Tech Hackathon victory, and UBC People’s Choice award are tracks left by resourceful founders before substantial funding. Over the next 18 months, that resourcefulness compounds only if Greypoint Industries converts the contract into flight tests, procurement learning, and a second customer before perfecting the hardware. Winning a hackathon is weaker than Airbnb selling 1,000 cereal boxes for $40 each, but combining a physical interceptor with a contract before YC is considerably stronger than presenting credentials and a rendering. The unanswered question is whether the founders found a side door through procurement or merely entered through a newly opened government program.

Steven Xu led Sailbot simulator and controls work, interned in embedded systems at Bosch, and joined Khoo’s electrical-engineering and control-systems background inside Greypoint Industries. The hacker-genealogy score would jump if Greypoint Industries showed a pre-company flight controller, an autonomous interception video, public technical work, or a strange prototype assembled before anyone authorized it. It would fall if LEGION and ARROW remain website nouns supported mainly by engineering degrees. No verified founder GitHub repositories exist, but Xu’s controls work and Khoo’s UAS experience are real artifacts rather than credential-only signals. These founders probably can build; I have not yet seen the equivalent of Drew Houston’s demo.

**Airbnb** (2009) had three complementary founders whose relationship could absorb years of rejection. Greypoint Industries has four founders, and the dossier places Khoo, Chu, and Xu together at UBC and on the 2025 founding roster, but it does not establish a long working relationship with Zhang. Greypoint Industries therefore gets credit for complementary technical and operational coverage without receiving the full pair-or-trio premium. Four founders can work, but four loosely connected roles can also conceal the absence of a single animal who owns the whole machine.

Fortem’s more than 4,500 captures make the next 36 months unusually falsifiable: I predict Greypoint Industries will reach $3 million to $8 million in annualized defense-contract revenue by July 2029, primarily through Canadian or allied counter-UAS programs, while remaining an independent specialist rather than displacing Anduril’s platform. Greypoint Industries can reach that range because procurement and qualification reward founders willing to endure the schlep, even before ARROW becomes technically dominant. The same missing organic-pain signal makes me less confident Greypoint Industries becomes the defining battlefield operating system rather than a capable contractor.

Anduril’s approximately $2.2 billion of 2025 revenue is the strongest objection: Greypoint Industries may be entering a category where incumbents already possess deployed interceptors, battlefield integrations, manufacturing scale, and operational data, while Greypoint Industries publishes no performance results or present moat. That bear mechanism stings because hardware procurement can turn schlep into incumbency rather than opportunity. Greypoint Industries nevertheless has a signed contract, a defense-hackathon win, infantry experience, and embedded-controls depth before YC; those are better evidence than a fashionable market slide. I would invest, but not strongly. If Greypoint Industries has neither an independently witnessed interception test nor a paid follow-on or second government contract by July 2027, I would flip to Pass.
