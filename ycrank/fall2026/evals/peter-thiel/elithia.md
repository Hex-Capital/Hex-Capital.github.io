# Elithia -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Elithia would need the following secret to be true: physician expert review has requirements that broad legal-AI platforms cannot satisfy economically, creating a separate market its specialist can own. The dossier establishes the workflow—reconstructing patient histories from thousands of pages—but does not establish that contested proposition or identify peers who reject it. If existing platforms deliver equivalent clinician review at comparable cost, the proposed secret fails. Missing evidence does not refute Elithia’s opportunity; it leaves the reason for forming an independent company unproved.

The clinician beachhead is intelligible. Its monopoly mechanism is not. Elithia removes duplicates, organizes records, and produces source-linked chronologies with Word/PDF exports. DigitalOwl already offers chronologies and evidence-linked record chat, and its October 2025 acquisition by Datavant connects analysis to retrieval infrastructure. That is affirmative competitive overlap, beyond merely an undisclosed moat. Expert corrections could eventually produce proprietary data, but unverified collection rights and hypothetical performance gains confer no present ownership advantage. Elithia risks doing valuable work while the company controlling record access captures the value.

Sarthak Jain leaving MIT Sloan’s MBA to build the company is the strongest affirmative evidence of commitment. Oxford computer science and Winton experience make technical execution credible, although credentials do not establish a secret or economic alignment. A solo founder needs no invented co-founder history; the relevant unanswered questions concern ownership and who builds the essential product. The dossier provides no evidence of part-time employment, outsourced core engineering, or investor-dominated capitalization, so those founding red flags should not be presumed. His decision to leave school earns credit; the remaining founding structure remains unverified.

The strongest objection to passing is that Elithia has already sold to physician expert witnesses and medical-legal firms. These buyers may value professional review tools that plaintiff-law platforms subordinate to case management. The first 1,000 pages free provides a concrete trial, and Jain’s 2026 Medico-Legal Conference speaker listing under Minerva Labs supplies access to a concentrated professional audience. This is more informative than a generic enterprise-sales ambition. It also fits my less-public preference for software that makes contested information legible. But neither that pattern nor reported sales establishes repeat acquisition economics. Paid rates, customer counts, margins, and expansion behavior remain undisclosed; insurer and TPA expansion is still a proposed route. There is no grounded arithmetic for a power-law outcome.

I would pass at present. Elithia’s central problem is documented product overlap without a demonstrated mechanism for retaining value. My exceptions for a proven technical discontinuity or persistent demand overcoming an identified barrier do not resolve that problem: the dossier contains neither independent technical results nor customer behavior demonstrating resistance to substitution. I would reopen diligence if one independently audited, blinded benchmark showed Elithia completing physician expert review at one-tenth the reviewer time of DigitalOwl, with no increase in material omissions, and an ablation demonstrated that the advantage depended on an exclusive, rights-cleared correction dataset. That would turn a plausible specialization into evidence of something Elithia could own.
