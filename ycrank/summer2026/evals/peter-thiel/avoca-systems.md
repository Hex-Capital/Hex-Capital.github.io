# Avoca Systems -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

**Max Shand was Afterpay’s first employee and sold Serenade after serving 347 artists, while Avoca Systems publishes no definite multi-year plan.** Avoca Systems is an operator-pedigree healthcare workflow company whose commercial execution has outrun its intellectual specification: 250+ clinics, six named healthcare-system integrations, and call abandonment reduced from roughly 30% to 2%, but no articulated secret about why AbbaDox or OpenDoctor structurally cannot follow. Avoca Systems has discovered a painful business; it has not yet shown that it has discovered a monopoly.

**Anduril is the high rung because Palmer Luckey combined shipped-to-scale operator history with direct adjacency to the technical system he intended to replace.** Avoca Systems shares the revealed-preference mechanism: Shand helped scale Afterpay and completed a prior exit, while Campbell Mercer-Butcher reached principal engineer and tech-lead roles before encoding Avoca Systems’s radiology workflows. The divergence is scale and specificity. Luckey had built Oculus into a billion-dollar hardware outcome; Serenade served 347 artists, and neither Avoca Systems founder has documented radiology employment. This is credible operator lineage, not yet the pedigree override that excused Asana’s otherwise consensus SaaS profile.

**Quora’s low-rung weakness was an indefinite consumer roadmap whose exception depended on Adam D’Angelo’s Facebook CTO pedigree and a knowledge-network compounding thesis.** The same weakness appears more starkly at Avoca Systems: Avoca Systems names completed integrations, certifications, and workflow capabilities, but no two-to-five-year architectural commitment, no milestone beyond geographic expansion from Australia into the United States, and no proposition the founders refuse to pivot away from. Existing execution prevents the minimum score, yet execution is not a definite plan. A product roadmap hidden from public view cannot be credited with private-money conviction.

**AbbaDox already markets VoiceAI, FaxAI, scheduling, results sharing, and referrer communications, which makes Avoca Systems’s secret the decisive missing mechanism.** Over the next 18 months, Avoca Systems’s customer-specific rules could compound into implementation knowledge: every modality constraint, staffing qualification, and RIS write-back could make the next deployment faster. But implementation learning is not automatically proprietary learning. Without exclusive data rights or a named architectural reason AbbaDox and OpenDoctor cannot encode the same rules, Avoca Systems risks becoming “AI for radiology” with unusually good execution—mimetic convergence wearing a vertical-software costume.

**More than 250 clinics would flip Avoca Systems’s monopoly score upward if those deployments generated a proprietary rules graph whose accuracy and deployment speed improved measurably with network scale.** Avoca Systems instead discloses switching costs from Visage, Karisma, Comrad, Epic, Oracle Health, and eClinicalWorks integrations, while the dossier explicitly finds no exclusive access, proprietary data ownership, or structural barrier to incumbent imitation. The counterfactual is precise: demonstrate that clinic 251 can be deployed materially faster because clinics 1–250 created reusable operational intelligence, and Avoca Systems begins to resemble a narrow monopoly. Without that proof, negotiated enterprise integration supports retention, not pricing power.

**Palantir is the relevant single-anchor parallel because regulated data, integration depth, and customer-specific operational knowledge can become a moat long before they resemble atoms.** Avoca Systems processes protected health information, holds SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 certifications, claims HIPAA compliance, and escalates out-of-parameter clinical interactions to humans. Avoca Systems therefore sits well above ordinary software-only SaaS on the regulated-sector axis. The parallel stops where Palantir’s begins: radiology networks are fragmented commercial buyers, not intelligence agencies with extreme trust barriers and decade-long procurement entanglement. Compliance creates a tollbooth; it does not by itself create Mordor’s one ring.

**The 250-clinic footprint leads to a falsifiable 36-month forecast: Avoca Systems reaches $6–12 million ARR as a durable radiology-access vendor but remains independent and sub-monopoly, competing feature-for-feature with AbbaDox rather than controlling the category.** Avoca Systems can produce that outcome because reducing abandonment from 30% to 2% gives buyers an immediate economic justification, while six integrations and site-specific booking rules slow replacement. Avoca Systems remains below power-law shape because the same implementation-heavy mechanism that retains customers also limits distribution velocity, and the published $780 million category is too narrow unless Avoca Systems expands from scheduling into the radiology network’s broader operating layer.

**An 86% call-resolution rate and greater than 80% eligible-call booking conversion form the strongest bull case against that forecast.** Avoca Systems may already have found the Palantir-like wedge: begin with an unglamorous regulated workflow, encode institutional knowledge across 250+ clinics, then become the system through which every patient-access decision passes. That case stings because Shand’s Afterpay experience and prior exit could provide the operator-pedigree exception seen in Asana and Quora. The dossier still loses on the condition that matters: AbbaDox and OpenDoctor publicly offer overlapping radiology-specific automation, while Avoca Systems names neither their structural incapacity nor a proprietary compounding asset. Verified evidence that deployment time or clinical accuracy improves with accumulated cross-customer data—and that Avoca Systems contractually owns the resulting model or rules layer—would flip my verdict to Invest. Until then, I would not write the check. Competition is for losers; Avoca Systems has shown it can compete, not that it can escape competition.
