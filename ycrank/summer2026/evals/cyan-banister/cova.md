# Cova -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A daughter discovers that Medicaid can pay her to care for her mother, verifies eligibility that afternoon, and begins paid work before an exhausted family abandons the process. Cova has found a credible leak from that world: it is already a licensed Michigan provider, and one prospective caregiver reported entering enrollment with a quoted $22 hourly rate. But the dossier does not yet map the intervening steps from one AI-operated agency to a national care system. Multi-state licensing, payer contracts, clinical accountability, and exception handling are unnamed bridges, not details.

The broken system is unusually concrete. Enrollment takes weeks, four state forms must be photographed and texted page by page, state caseworkers and physicians sit in the loop, and caregivers can wait unpaid. Cova captures reimbursement as the provider while returning income and occupational control to relatives already performing the labor. That is much more compelling than selling another efficiency dashboard to agencies. The agency is the product, and the software has to survive the consequences.

Jordan Ibe and Ansh Tandon have real operating tuition: both worked on Palantir’s healthcare team, spent the preceding year building AI systems for home-care agencies and hospitals, and converted Omnition into Cova’s internal operating system. Computer science, deployment work, and bioengineering are complementary. Still, this is documented professional proximity followed by a one-year build, not yet proof of a long pre-company obsession, exceptional recruiting pull, or commercial mastery of reimbursement. The founders know the machinery; I do not yet know whether they are possessed by this particular problem.

The sharpest bull case is that licensing and operating care create the training ground a software-only competitor cannot cheaply simulate. Every rejected form, payer exception, and caregiver support call could improve the system while Cova earns provider revenue. The sharpest bear case stings more: Aidaly already covers 8.5 million lives across five states, while Cova discloses no payer contract, revenue, measured automation advantage, or proprietary dataset. “No non-caregiver headcount” is currently a claim sitting beside telephone support and manual photographs of four forms.

This also touches my privacy gate. Families are transmitting health and benefits documents by text, yet the dossier gives no consent, custody, retention, or human-review architecture. Healthcare automation does not become safer because the interface feels ordinary. Add two co-founders with no disclosed decision tiebreaker, and I have unresolved governance and surveillance questions precisely where an operator assumes direct responsibility.

My current expectation is that Cova can become a valuable regional care provider before it proves it is a defining enabling layer. I am Neutral, not because pre-seed revenue is absent, but because the strongest evidence establishes the rent being removed while the weakest evidence concerns durable control beneath the wave. One result flips me to Invest: an audited cohort of 100 families with a median application-to-first-paid-shift below seven days and less than one employee-hour of back-office work per enrollment.
