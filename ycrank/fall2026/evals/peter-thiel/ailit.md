# Ailit -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Ailit’s potentially important truth is that freight rail can be electrified economically without replacing locomotives or installing continuous charging infrastructure: move the batteries, preserve the locomotive, and sell energy below diesel prices. That proposition is falsifiable. A representative route could reveal that battery inventory, charging, swaps and equipment depreciation consume the advertised 20% saving. It earns some credit for specificity. But the dossier identifies neither a prevailing belief that informed opponents reject nor an operating result that challenges it. Ailit has a definite engineering hypothesis; its secret remains unproved.

The ownership question is harder. Superwagon supplies an existing diesel-electric locomotive from interchangeable battery packs, but Dayton-Phoenix already reports integration testing of a modular battery tender. The distinction is operational: can swapping produce an advantage competitors cannot economically reproduce? AilitOS and a future network might eventually do that. Neither currently demonstrates proprietary performance, exclusive access or network effects. Class I/II railroads describe prospective customers, not a bounded market Ailit can dominate. Competition does not prove the architecture is a commodity, but the dossier supplies no affirmative monopoly mechanism.

The strongest argument for the founders is Emil Tagesson’s reported experience taking Candela’s electric ferry through certification and production, alongside work on Koenigsegg’s battery team. Someone who has completed that journey deserves more attention than someone who has merely modeled it. That experience strengthens the execution case; it does not establish founding alignment. Shared KTH backgrounds do not document Lind and Tagesson’s working history, and Banderet’s reported co-founder/adviser status needs clarification. Full-time commitment and ownership are undisclosed. I would retain the stated founding-structure default, without converting an ambiguous title into evidence of a part-time founder.

Ailit also has a serious answer to the usual infrastructure objection. Existing locomotives and diesel backup could permit a limited deployment before a complete network exists. Customers buying energy rather than equipment could avoid a difficult capital-budget decision. This fits my preference for neglected physical infrastructure, and the reported SEK10M of earlier financing plus $500K from YC gives the team resources to attempt it. The relevant exception is Panthalassa: interdependent hardware and infrastructure can be investable when a concrete pilot and deployment path make the coordination problem tractable. The material difference is that Ailit’s first full-scale prototype remains under construction, targeting summer 2027, with no named customer commitment or validated operating unit in the dossier. The exception is possible, but not established.

The commercial tradeoff is that removing equipment purchases from the railroad’s balance sheet puts batteries and swapping sites onto Ailit’s. One energy revenue engine is intelligible; its power-law economics are not yet calculable. Pricing 20% below diesel says nothing about the remaining margin after electricity, degradation, spare packs and infrastructure financing. Likewise, a historical commercial hiring page seeking railroad relationships describes work still to be done. Juergen Wilder’s advisory involvement could help open doors, but neither that relationship nor conference participation demonstrates a repeatable sales route.

I pass on the present evidence. The decisive gap is the connection between successful electrification and durable value capture: a working tender alone would not establish a monopoly. My single flip condition is a paid, independently audited deployment on a defined freight corridor demonstrating reliable swaps, positive fully loaded energy margins at the promised diesel discount, and contractual exclusivity over its exchange sites. That would turn Ailit’s proposed network from a financing obligation into evidence of an ownable market.
