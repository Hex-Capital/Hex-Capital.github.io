# Definite -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Definite’s immutable snapshots, per-value lineage, and hash-chained receipts reveal a real secret: finance agents fail because books are fragmented before intelligence ever reaches them. Definite is strongest on that contrarian diagnosis and weakest on a definite plan—the YC launch sold regulatory reporting for banks, while the current website sells customer-built agents across finance. Definite therefore looks like a potentially important context layer searching for its first monopoly wedge. A secret without a plan is philosophy, not yet a business.

Stripe named legacy processors’ indifference to developers; developer-first distribution then compounded into the payments default. Definite similarly names n8n and Workato’s assumption that records arrive normalized, while Rillet and Puzzle demand ledger replacement. The analogy is load-bearing because Definite places the overlooked context layer—not the model—at the center. But Definite diverges from Stripe where the failure mode becomes institutional: the dossier gives no reason Numeric, FloQast, or an ERP vendor cannot add lineage and normalized entities. The secret is specific, but not yet structurally exclusive.

Quora’s indefinite roadmap was tolerable only because Adam D’Angelo’s Facebook CTO pedigree and a prospective knowledge-graph network effect overrode it. Definite shows the same weak dimension without the override: no two-to-five-year milestones, no architectural commitment beyond read-only connectivity, and a visible transition from bank regulatory reporting to broad back-office agents. Definite may call itself definite, but the dossier records iteration between wedges. The name is currently an aspiration.

NetSuite, QuickBooks, Sage, Workday, ADP, Stripe, Bill.com, and Ramp could create an integration flywheel over the next 18 months: every mapping rule could lower onboarding cost, while historical reconciliation records could improve canonicalization. Definite loses the monopoly test if those assets remain customer-specific implementation work, and no connector volume, accuracy rate, switching evidence, or pricing-power hypothesis is public. Definite has the beginnings of integration depth, but Numeric’s $89 million and Rillet’s more than $100 million make “competition validates the market” precisely the wrong conclusion. Competition is for losers unless Definite can explain why competitors are trapped.

Mazin Al-Ani’s Optiver and Boosted.ai experience, Farhan Ur Rehman’s Meta recommendation work, and Gurshabd Singh Varaich’s BitGo controls work give Definite credible technical proximity to the problem. Definite would move into an invest tier on founder lineage if one founder had owned a financial system at scale, shipped a prior product to meaningful adoption, or demonstrated proprietary reconciliation infrastructure beyond internships and employment. Waterloo is a signal; it is not the PayPal Mafia. Potential is not pedigree.

Palantir turned regulated customers into a moat because integration depth, security clearance, procurement trust, and mission dependence accumulated over years. Definite gestures toward that structure by seeking introductions at banks, credit unions, fintechs, and insurers, while deterministic checks and read-only access answer finance’s trust objection. Definite remains pure software, however, and the current website broadens beyond the regulated wedge. Without named financial institutions, certifications, or contracts, regulation is a customer attribute rather than a barrier to entry.

Definite’s shift from end-to-end bank regulatory reporting to a general finance-agent context layer predicts that by July 2029 Definite will have pivoted back to compliance and regulatory reporting for financial institutions, reaching roughly $1 million to $3 million ARR rather than becoming a horizontal operating layer for finance. Definite’s secret is sharp enough to find a business, but its weak definite plan and read-only action boundary make horizontal control unlikely. The likely outcome is a narrow evidence-and-reconciliation product—not the one model of the books.

Numeric’s $89 million, Deloitte’s finding that trust is the principal adoption barrier, and Definite’s deterministic verdicts form the strongest bull case: Definite could become the trusted system of intelligence above every ledger without replacing any of them. Definite’s read-only posture could accelerate adoption, while source lineage and hash-chained receipts could create switching costs before incumbents react. The case fails today because Definite discloses no customer, revenue, mapping accuracy, or proprietary data scale, and because the product-positioning transition signals search rather than commitment. Five named banks on multi-year contracts producing at least $2 million ARR from one repeatable regulatory workflow would flip my verdict to Invest.
