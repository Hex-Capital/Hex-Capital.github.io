# Stratum Industries -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The decisive missing fact is what happened when Stratum Industries first collided with government. The dossier names formidable obstacles—procurement, program-specific rubrics, impact assessments, testing, appeals, and human oversight—but not one obstacle the brothers encountered and worked around. A public launch in July 2026 is not such evidence. I cannot infer resourcefulness from Raj’s sales record or Vihaan’s engineering résumé, so the founder-action criterion remains at its missing-evidence default.

There is at least a product-shaped technical idea. Stratum says it reads applications, routes steps among models, requests missing information, and produces cited summaries for human officers. That is more concrete than saying “AI for government,” but there is no deployed agency workflow, measured accuracy result, processed-case count, or working-system demonstration in the dossier. The strongest unstated pattern in my recent checks has been proof before the round. Here the architecture is asserted, while the proof is missing.

Nor is there yet a narrow well of users. The launch asks adjudicators to request meetings and other people to supply introductions. No agency, officer, applicant class, pilot, LOI, repeated use, or revenue is named. This matters especially because GovWell already serves more than 150 agencies across 5,000 processes, while Polimorphic and Clariti have each raised $28 million. “The entire U.S. government backlog” is broad enough to conceal whether anyone urgently wants the crude product now.

The bull case is the team. Raj reports averaging 220% of quota at Deel and ranking among its top five representatives out of more than 1,000. Vihaan was Cadastral’s third founding engineer before its June 2026 acquisition by Legora, and the founders are brothers rather than application-matched strangers. The April and May 2025 federal policies also create a real opening. These facts make it plausible that one founder can build while the other penetrates a difficult sales channel. But they do not show why these founders encountered adjudication personally, or that they changed Stratum’s route after learning something from an agency.

The schlep is real, which earns some credit. Different agencies impose different evidentiary standards, integrations, audit requirements, and consequences for error. Those burdens could become the company’s advantage. They could also make the horizontal thesis incoherent: a system safe enough for disability benefits is not automatically credible for patents, permits, and drug approvals. The current breadth looks more like a category claim than the result of one agency pulling the product into an adjacent workflow.

I would pass today. The known exceptions do not rescue it: there is no working prototype with unusual measurable performance, no unmistakable user pull, and no independent proof capable of replacing the failed founder-action gate. One verifiable fact would flip me: within six months, show a named agency repeatedly using Stratum on live cases, with the founders having personally worked around a documented procurement, integration, or accuracy blocker and producing a measured reduction in adjudication time without a higher correction rate.
