# Obia -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The useful detail about Obia is the logistics operator. The company says it screened zoning, considered combining sites, and compared projected throughput per dollar of rent. That is a specific job someone might urgently need done. But the October 23 account stops before the part I most want to hear: what prevented the customer from finding space, what the founders themselves built to get past it, and how quickly that changed the outcome. Research performed for a customer gives me some evidence of demand. It does not yet establish founder resourcefulness.

The strongest case for Obia is that industrial tenants need someone to reconcile facts that live in different places. A building can look suitable until its permitted uses make it useless. Samuel Cho’s engineering background at Amazon and BIASLab makes the automation plausible, while Jason Henkel’s conversational-AI work is relevant to collecting requirements. Brokers handling tours and negotiations could let the founders learn the whole problem by doing it manually. There is real schlep here. But neither biography documents a prior collision with this particular problem, and the dossier contains no observed product revision following customer feedback. Those remain unknowns.

The technical distinction matters because Obia could complete this research with considerable human effort. Its account does not isolate what the software accomplished, show a working artifact, or measure accuracy or time saved. I therefore cannot count the logistics engagement again as technical proof. Nor does the reported accumulation of search requirements establish the difficult proprietary substrate I sometimes back: there is no dataset size or demonstrated advantage. Crexi already supplies overlapping research tools. Obia’s opportunity would be to learn something through representing tenants that software sold to existing brokers does not capture.

That makes the brokerage economics central. Landlords pay the fee, so a tenant accepting free research is a weaker signal than a tenant repeatedly relying on it to make consequential decisions. The unnamed logistics engagement earns limited demand credit; the CoreNet sponsorship earns none. Exhibiting does not establish that the founders depend on a ceremonial launch, either. Likewise, missing cofounder history is not evidence that the relationship was assembled hastily. The strongest bull case is founder-led service becoming a learning machine, but no completed transaction, returning customer, or measured technical result yet supplies an exception to my investment gate.

I would pass at 31. The decisive gap is the missing account of these founders overcoming resistance, rather than their team size or lack of published revenue. My flip condition is one independently checkable customer history: a specific zoning obstacle, a founder-built workaround shipped within two weeks, and repeated customer use with broker-verified results that lead to a signed lease. That would connect the people, the software, and the need. Today those connections remain largely proposed.
