# Perceptron ML -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is what the brothers have built, not where they studied. Michael converted production-agent and formal-verification experience into three apparently deployed legal systems for timekeeping, monitoring, and research. That is action-revealed knowledge, but only partially in the exact problem: neither founder has practiced law, the firms are anonymous, and the dossier provides no usage or accuracy outcomes. The artifacts raise the founder prior; they do not yet prove deep knowledge of legal work.

Perceptron’s source-gated generation attacks a real failure mode. Customer-controlled deployment, firm-specific matter training, and auditable primary-source grounding could matter in a profession where fabricated authority is unacceptable. But copy the code and little demonstrably remains. Harvey already embeds legal engineers and supports more than 25,000 customer-created agents; Thomson Reuters and LexisNexis control legal corpora; Perceptron has disclosed no exclusive data, proprietary model, validated benchmark, or compounding asset. Private customer data may improve each installation without improving Perceptron itself.

The product therefore compounds labor more clearly than leverage. “Built to order” systems and founder-led calls can win early firms precisely because 69% of legal professionals use generative AI personally while only 34% of firms have adopted legal-specific AI. The same customization that unlocks procurement can turn the company into an implementation shop: every new customer may require another integration, another workflow reconstruction, and another trust exercise. No user currently creates value or distribution for another user.

The bull case is that three deployments are unusually concrete for pre-seed, and the prototype-before-network exception deserves consideration. Legal buyers may pay heavily for privacy and verification before public metrics appear. Yet the dossier gives no price, named payer, repeat usage, or revenue, while the current competitors already demonstrate that legal AI is a funded and legible category. The $3.918 billion projected 2030 market is real enough, but Perceptron has not named an adjacent market or a reusable capability that expands beyond bespoke law-firm automation.

The July-to-August repositioning from a cross-industry event engine to legal AI is not itself a veto; intelligent founders update. It does weaken the claim that this exact market was learned through sustained action, and the dossier supplies no evidence separating prior-product traction from the three current case studies. There is no documented integrity problem, artificial deadline, or reassurance-seeking founder behavior. The red flag is structural rather than personal: a fashionable AI category, rapidly reproducible software, and distribution that scales through principals selling services rather than users strengthening a system.

I pass today. The verifiable flip condition is five named law firms paying recurring subscriptions for the same core product, with an independently reproducible citation-accuracy benchmark and declining deployment hours per firm. That would show direct value capture and a reusable asset rather than three custom projects.
