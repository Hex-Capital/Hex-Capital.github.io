# Codag -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The strongest fact is not the phrase “log compression for agents.” It is that Michael Zhou has already shipped the whole path from a no-account CLI to an API and MCP server, then published artifacts measuring 98–99% incident-answer rates with 5.9–7.5 times fewer tokens than raw logs plus grep. The incidents were synthetic and the evaluation internal, so this is not independent validation. But it is still a working, measurable object built before customers or institutional permission. Zhou looks more like a fierce nerd than a person arranging boxes on a market map.

The missing fact is what broke for him. His work at Okta and Shopify makes the origin plausible, but the dossier never identifies a production incident Zhou personally encountered, the blocker it exposed, or the workaround he shipped in response. It says he made five pivots and moved from a workflow visualizer in February 2026 to log compression in May. That shows speed. It does not show whether he changed routes because users taught him something or merely kept trying nouns. Resourcefulness requires resistance, not motion alone.

There is also no narrow well yet. The current repositories have 45 and 20 stars, with 8 and 7 forks, but no named developer repeatedly using Codag, no paid account, and no production incident resolved. The prior visualizer’s 655 stars belong to a different product. The strongest bull case is that the same founder has now produced two developer tools people voluntarily noticed, while the current one offers a programmable primitive that agents can consume directly. That matches my pattern of liking interfaces technical users can compose into larger systems. The known exception for a solo founder with a striking working prototype therefore deserves consideration. But a synthetic benchmark cannot provide the part of that exception supplied by unmistakable founder energy and real user response.

The schlep could become the company. Operational logs contain secrets and personal data; Codag already promises transient processing, PII scrubbing, no third-party LLM provider, and VPC peering for enterprise customers. Those burdens could keep casual competitors away. Yet the deterministic core is an MIT-licensed adaptation of Drain3, while Sentry, Honeycomb, Dash0, and Cribl already own telemetry relationships and can bundle overlapping diagnosis. The $19 Pro and $499 Team plans prove that the product can be priced, not that anyone will buy it. Without customer evidence, the ugly work may be a barrier to Codag rather than its wedge.

I do not see the usual active red flags. Distribution does not depend on a ceremonial launch or an unnamed major partner, and Zhou has shipped instead of proposing to hire people who will ship. The more subtle danger is five pivots becoming a substitute for learning. A running back changes direction because a defender moved. The dossier does not yet identify the defender.

I would not write the check today. Codag has unusually good technical proof for a pre-seed company, but it fails my people-first gate because neither the named-blocker story nor current-product pull is documented. The verifiable flip condition is simple: show me three unrelated engineering teams that have used Codag in production for at least four consecutive weeks, plus the specific product change Zhou shipped after one team’s logs defeated the original compressor. That would simultaneously establish a narrow well, action under resistance, and evidence-driven route correction.
