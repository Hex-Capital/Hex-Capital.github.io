# Derya -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have earned specific knowledge through action. Oguzhan Karaca worked inside CEVA/CMA CGM, specialized in maritime and trade law, and previously sold consulting to six-figure ARR. Mert Turna built ship-operations software used across more than 30 vessels representing $200 million in fleet value. Their eight-year relationship and prior business together reduce coordination risk. This is not a pair of tourists attaching agents to a fashionable industry; each founder has operated on one side of the exact problem.

Customers are already paying for that knowledge. Derya contracted $80,000 in its first month, sourced more than $3.2 million in freight opportunities, and signed TVO for 20 containers. The flat monthly plan makes the payer and value proposition unusually legible for pre-seed: the customer buys an outsourced logistics office, not an AI demo. But contracted revenue is not yet recurring revenue, and the dossier gives neither plan pricing nor gross margin. Specialists still execute the services, so volume could increase labor and compliance exposure as quickly as revenue.

The central problem is that the company currently compounds work, not leverage. If a competitor copies the software tomorrow, Derya has no demonstrated exclusive data, proprietary model, carrier rights, workflow lock-in, or customer-created network. Flexport, Forto, and Nuvocargo already coordinate digital freight, while 5U AI automates quoting, booking, tracking, and reconciliation for incumbent forwarders. The inferred shipment-history moat is plausible but nonexistent as evidence. A future data asset is not a present advantage.

The market supplies room to maneuver: freight forwarding was estimated at $225.4 billion in 2025, and digital forwarding at $42.22 billion with 21.77% projected annual growth through 2030. Procurement, assembly, production, storage, and distribution are credible adjacencies because they share documents, vendors, routing decisions, and exception handling. Yet this breadth is also unresolved positioning. A company cannot simultaneously be a software agent, freight forwarder, and outsourced supply-chain department without deciding which economics govern it.

The strongest bull case is that the founders have found fragmented offline supply, made prices visible, and converted direct operational knowledge into revenue before conventional validation. That matches a real pattern in permissionless marketplaces. But Derya is not yet permissionless: introductions are founder-led, operations reviews are company-delivered, and one customer does not improve service or acquisition for another. The relevant Notion exception does not rescue it. Both began with unusually capable, capital-efficient builders before a user loop was visible; the material difference is that Notion’s product could replicate at near-zero marginal cost, while Derya currently incurs specialists, customs work, storage coordination, and shipment exceptions with every account. No public evidence raises an integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-stability red flag; the objection is structural, not personal.

My 36-month base case is a $3 million–$8 million ARR outsourced freight operation with useful automation but service-like margins, rather than a self-reinforcing software network. That can become a good business without becoming a nonlinear angel outcome. I would not invest yet. One condition flips the decision: a customer cohort showing that accumulated shipment data has reduced human work per shipment by at least 70% while gross margin and retention improve.
