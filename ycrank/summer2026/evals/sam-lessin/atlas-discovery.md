# Atlas Discovery -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and use of funds. Atlas discloses backing from Y Combinator, Pear, and Glasswing, but neither price nor evidence that this is a scarce first check. That earns no inference of cheapness. More importantly, the company has not named the finite experiment this round purchases. “Build partnerships” is an activity; it is not a venture moment with a dated pass/fail result.

The technical work is real. Atlas reports 0.7–0.9 retrospective AUROC across several cancer and autoimmune trials, including 0.760 on 358 treated UNIFI patients and a modeled reduction of 458 participants at matched power. The three founders have also shipped two preprints and working open-source code. But Atlas itself supplies the decisive caveat: the UNIFI model was trained and evaluated within the same trial, and cross-trial generalization is not currently usable for stratification. The product has demonstrated retrospective pattern recovery, not prospective clinical value.

The missing asset is the dark pool. The model requires linked molecular, treatment, and outcome data, yet Atlas owns no disclosed exclusive dataset and is still requesting access from hospitals and biopharma. Owkin already claims relationships with more than 800 hospitals; Unlearn and PhaseV have raised $134.85 million and $65 million, respectively. Publishing the DCM code and weights may be good science, but without proprietary outcome access it leaves Atlas selling a model into a market where better-capitalized competitors own more of the scarce input.

The bull case is that unusually technical founders have found a useful biological representation before securing commercial access. Sanjukta Bhattacharya brings AI-biology doctoral work, Shaamil Karim has worked with Cellarity and Asimov, and the entire team coauthored the underlying research. This could resemble the exception I make for an AI company when measured accuracy becomes an operating business. The material difference is that Atlas has no paid pilot, no prospective prediction, no conversion, and no retention. A same-trial retrospective AUROC cannot carry the same evidentiary weight as a buyer repeatedly paying for a forecast that survives deployment.

Deleting the next financing makes the problem sharper. There is no public pricing, revenue, gross margin, burn, or controllable cash metric, while obtaining regulated clinical data and proving prospective validity can consume years. The likely failure mode is not that the science is fake; it is that Atlas becomes a capable research team waiting for data rights, regulatory credibility, and another round while incumbents compound their hospital relationships. Within 36 months, my base case is a pivot toward fee-for-service biomarker analysis or acqui-hire rather than a standalone business above $3 million ARR.

I pass today. The one condition that flips me is a paid biopharma pilot granting Atlas exclusive rights to linked pretreatment-and-outcome data and prospectively achieving at least 0.75 AUROC on a held-out trial. That single result would prove customer action, create proprietary access, and define the truth this seed round is financing. Until then, Atlas has an interesting model but no owned workflow, no purchase, and no disclosed economic path.
