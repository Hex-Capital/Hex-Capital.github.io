# Gradon -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Gradon says a 50-person civil firm completed a project in 180 hours against a 750-hour manual estimate. That is the fact I would investigate first. But whose economics improved? The engineering firm potentially recovered 570 hours; Gradon has not disclosed what it collected or spent delivering that result. A valuable customer outcome can support a business. It does not establish that this particular equity is worth buying. Post-money valuation, competing offers, and whether my check supplies genuinely scarce capital are all undisclosed. I cannot call the round overpriced, and I cannot underwrite it as cheap.

The strongest case for Gradon is that its workflow is already being exercised inside an actual engineering firm. Civil 3D, Revit, ETABS, and Bluebeam sit close to deliverables customers must produce, and incorporating historical projects and firm standards could make delegation substantially more useful than generic chat. The reported project completion earns credit for customer action. It does not yet establish that proprietary access caused a purchase: payment, repeat usage, and retention are undisclosed. Moreover, the contract prohibits training on customer content. Account-specific configuration may become sticky, but I cannot assume Gradon owns a reusable data advantage, particularly when SWAPP already advertises firm-standard validation and workflow memory.

The founders give that possibility substance. Nour Zahzah has performed mechanical design; Yousef Helal has engineering experience at Apple and AMD and shipped Vendo, whose repository attracted 633 stars. There is a plausible bridge between engineering work and software execution here, consistent with my preference for people who can operate across systems. Still, the Vendo stars belong to the prior product. They establish shipping ability, not demand for Gradon, and the dossier does not document sustained operation inside an AEC practice. Childhood friendship also tells me nothing about decision rights; the named CEO and CTO roles provide no basis for inventing a founder-governance problem.

What does new capital prove? The dossier identifies no funded experiment, deadline, or pass/fail milestone. That receives the missing-evidence default, rather than a penalty for an imaginary hiring spree. The economic question is similarly unresolved. Unlimited-usage seats, on-site implementation, and mandatory licensed review leave inference, customization, and checking costs to be measured. None proves negative margins. None supplies the seed-funded route to profitability I need if I delete the next financing from the model.

The objection to passing is serious: recovering most of an engineering project's production time could generate enough customer surplus to finance onboarding and leave excellent margins. Gradon could become the trusted execution layer inside firms whose standards are difficult for outsiders to learn. That fits my pattern of backing software that reorganizes difficult physical operations. I also allow exceptional conversion and operating evidence to override missing deal information. Here, however, the headline percentage and the 180-hour example are company-reported outcomes, with the latter compared against an estimate. They do not establish paid conversion, repeatability, or supplier economics. The AI red flag remains a question about dependable delivery costs, not a demonstrated defect.

I pass on the evidence available. My one verifiable condition for reopening underwriting is an audited cohort of three independent AEC firms renewing paid Gradon deployments at positive contribution margins after implementation, inference, support, and required review costs. That would demonstrate that the time savings support a repeatable business and give me a reason to consider overriding the missing gates. Today, Gradon has a promising customer result; the investable economics remain unproven.
