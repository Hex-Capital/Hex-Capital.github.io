# Tilo -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Tilo is asking developers to rebuild applications around an agent’s needs. That could remove real waste from computer use. It also creates a capital-allocation problem: who pays for the migration before an application ecosystem exists? The proposed post-money, competing offers, and financing plan are undisclosed. I cannot establish that my check buys a cheap, neglected belief or funds one finite experiment. YC participation establishes neither scarcity nor an auction. Those two primary gates remain at their missing-evidence defaults.

The strongest argument for Tilo is that its founders have built something more substantial than an automation demo. Typed actions, shared workspace state, permissions, history, and undo address the coordination between people and agents. Quinten Wolff also built patent-workflow automation at BDPE during 2025–2026. That connects operating experience inside an actual workflow with the team’s published software infrastructure. It fits my pattern of backing founders who translate between systems. I give that shipped work meaningful credit, while stopping short of assuming they have already demonstrated enterprise adoption or reliable production operation.

The difficulty is turning that bridge into owned access. Tilo’s deeper functionality requires native applications to describe their resources and actions; existing Linux applications receive less functionality. Developers therefore incur integration work before the ecosystem provides much leverage. Browser Use offers structured interaction with existing websites, which gives customers an alternative to adopting a new application framework. The public SDK makes Tilo accessible, but it does not establish a proprietary distribution channel. Three stars on a GitHub mirror are neither a verdict on the product nor evidence that its workflow causes purchases. No documented customer action supports lifting that dimension above default.

The bull case deserves attention: managing actions and state could produce durable value even as models improve, and the founders’ BDPE experience gives them a concrete place to understand useful automation. Tilo need not be a generic AI wrapper. But the claim of up to 10,000× lower computer-use cost lacks an independently reproduced benchmark, and cheaper execution does not establish a profitable business. There is no disclosed pricing, margin, burn, or seed-funded break-even plan. The October 21 catalogue authorization expiry and unfinished hosted-operator isolation also identify operational work that must be funded. They do not prove bad economics; they prevent me from assuming the architecture has already resolved them.

I pass at **31/100**. None of my exceptions supplies an override here: the dossier contains no documented paid conversion and retention, acquisition offer, or prior exit paired with a financing structure that removes repeated fundraising. I also see no evidence of auction pressure or equal-founder paralysis; those red flags should not be invented. The decisive problem is that Tilo has demonstrated engineering without demonstrating what scarce capital purchases. My verifiable flip condition is a signed, customer-funded production deployment showing that a bounded native integration produces recurring positive contribution cash after hosting, support, and integration costs. That would turn the ecosystem proposition into an underwritable business experiment.
