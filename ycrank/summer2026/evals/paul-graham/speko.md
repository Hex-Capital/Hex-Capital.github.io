# Speko -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Beknazar Abdikamalov spent four years selecting voice stacks by trial and error, yet YC lists him as Speko’s only active founder. That pairing is Speko’s signature: an unusually organic idea attached to a dangerously incomplete founder configuration. Speko looks like a tool its founder genuinely needed, not a market report converted into an API. But Speko also asks one founder to keep 56 providers and 159 models working while LiveKit and Vapi attack from adjacent layers. The product passes the organic-idea test. The founder configuration does not yet pass the animal test.

**Dropbox** (2007) is the high rung for organic ideas: Drew Houston lost USB sticks, built the thing he wanted, and produced a demo other hackers immediately wanted. Abdikamalov’s four years of voice-stack bake-offs and prior work as Hupo’s CTO give Speko the same important starting mechanism—repeated personal pain becoming software. Speko diverges where the artifact meets users. Dropbox’s demo created unmistakable pull; Speko has 90 weekly MCP downloads, 85 SDK downloads, and two GitHub stars per principal repository. Speko’s origin is highly organic, but the public response is still closer to experimentation than need.

**Coinbase** (2012) initially exposed the solo-founder failure mechanism: Brian Armstrong needed an Android wallet strong enough to override the configuration I normally dislike, and he subsequently found Fred Ehrsam. Speko remains on the dangerous side of that pattern. YC names Abdikamalov as Speko’s sole active founder despite four listed employees, and Speko supplies no cofounder pipeline or prior builder relationship. Employees are not cofounders. Speko needs a peer who owns half the problem when several provider APIs fail simultaneously, not merely people who can be assigned parts of it.

Seven dated benchmark boards are evidence that Speko shipped before merely describing the idea, but they are not yet evidence of animal resourcefulness. Speko has built TypeScript and Python SDKs, LiveKit and Pipecat adapters, and a hosted MCP server; those are real artifacts. What is absent is the peculiar track animals leave: a named customer won through an unscalable stunt, revenue improvised before funding, or a rejection overcome by something as concrete as Armstrong’s wallet. If Speko showed that Abdikamalov manually rerouted ten production customers through overnight provider failures before YC, this dimension would move an entire tier. The dossier does not show it.

Fifty-six providers and 159 models create a genuine schlep because every model release, API change, language regression, and latency spike expands Speko’s maintenance burden. If Speko observes enough real failures, Speko can turn unpleasant integration work into a routing dataset that compounds over 18 months. But public benchmarks may not transfer to customer audio, SOC 2 Type 1 remains in progress, and LiveKit already performs cross-provider inference routing. The schlep is real; ownership of the schlep is not. A 5% routing fee may be too thin to finance the work unless the $0.09-per-minute hosted layer becomes the real business.

**Stripe** (2010) had hacker genealogy visible before the company: Patrick Collison’s Lisp work, prior shipping with Auctomatic, and a trail that other hackers could inspect. Speko has respectable substitutes—Abdikamalov’s CS degrees, Amazon engineering experience, Hupo CTO role, and working SDKs—but Speko has no public personal repositories or recognized hacker-community footprint. Two stars on each principal SDK repository do not yet establish technical taste in the way Stripe’s prehistory did. Speko has a technical founder by occupation; the dossier does not prove a hacker by compulsion.

LiveKit’s $181.3 million in funding makes the next 36 months fairly legible: by July 2029, Speko will have pivoted from a general voice-model router into enterprise voice evaluation and observability, reaching roughly $1 million to $3 million in ARR rather than becoming an independent routing default. Speko’s organic insight should keep Speko alive because benchmark drift is a real pain. Its solo-founder configuration and dependence on external providers make it unlikely to out-maintain every adjacent platform at the broad gateway layer. The benchmark machinery is more naturally sold as the instrument panel than as the engine.

Speko’s 175 combined weekly package downloads form the strongest bull case because they accompany seven benchmark boards, 159 integrated models, prior voice-AI experience, HIPAA compliance, and a working public product. The **Airbnb** (2009) exception warns against dismissing an awkward-looking artifact before discovering whether the founder is an animal; Speko could become OpenRouter for a modality whose reliability problems are worse and therefore more valuable. But Airbnb had cereal-box revenue, while Speko has no disclosed revenue, named production customer, SLA, proprietary dataset, or public evidence that automatic fallback improves customer outcomes. Speko would flip me to Invest if ten unaffiliated production customers generated at least $25,000 MRR and published data showed Speko’s routing materially reduced failed calls. Until then, I would not write the personal check.
