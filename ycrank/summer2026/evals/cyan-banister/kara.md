# Kara -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. AI racks, RF systems, spacecraft, and quantum machines are no longer designed around whether heat can escape; diamond layers sit beneath the hottest components as an ordinary engineering input. The path there requires repeatable large-crystal growth, economical processing, and customer qualification. Kara can name the present-day leak: Blackwell racks already demand up to 120 kW, and Akash introduced diamond-cooled MI350X servers in March 2026. That is more concrete than a fashionable “AI infrastructure” story, although the dossier does not yet show how far Kara has traveled through those three transitions.

The team makes me lean forward. Aarin Jhaveri is a fifth-generation diamantaire who has sold more than $5 million of diamonds across the value chain. Anuveer Chadha worked on diamond quantum-sensing systems at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, while Arjun Lakhanpal supplies the software and data competence that process manufacturing eventually needs. This is not three people discovering advanced materials after reading a market report. It is complementary commercial and technical exposure. What is missing is first-believer proof: no dated pre-company reactor work, independently documented build, unusual recruiting act, or customer encounter shows that the obsession had already become an artifact.

Kara’s strongest quality is its position beneath several visible waves. It claims to grow, process, and characterize single-crystal diamond in-house, controlling orientation, roughness, impurities, and isotope fraction for thermal, power-electronic, aerospace, and quantum applications. That is a real enabling-layer shape, not a thin interface. But “we operate our own reactors” is the beginning of the moat question. Without wafer dimensions, defect density, throughput, yield, or qualified specifications, I cannot distinguish controlled process knowledge from an expensive catalog that established suppliers can reproduce.

The most painful bear case is brutally physical. More than a dozen firms are pursuing single-crystal semiconductor diamond; Diamond Foundry has raised $315 million, Diamfab €8.7 million, and Akash combines funding, grants, tax credits, and systems integration. If Kara’s crystals cannot clear customer qualification at useful size and yield, superior thermal conductivity is laboratory trivia. The company could spend years perfecting material that purchasers will not redesign packages around. My 36-month base case is therefore narrow and falsifiable: Kara reaches $1 million to $3 million in annualized revenue from custom diamond components, but has not yet become a wafer-scale platform.

I also cannot identify the broken rule or captured rent. Heat is a severe engineering constraint, but the dossier does not quantify who extracts an artificial fee, which gatekeeper preserves it, or whose occupational control Kara returns. Nor is there costly or weird customer behavior: the company apparently has functioning reactors and an NDA-gated specification process, yet no named pilot, paid sample, qualification result, repeat order, or production shipment is public. The exception for a pre-revenue materials company would be a working prototype paired with signed industrial proofs of concept. Kara currently supplies only the first half.

The red-flag scan is otherwise clean. Aarin is identified as CEO, the team does not dismiss incumbent complexity, and Kara controls physical production rather than depending on one closed AI supplier. Its hidden-infrastructure profile fits me unusually well, but fit is not proof. I would not write the personal check today. One event flips me: a named semiconductor or power-electronics customer converts a paid qualification into a repeat production order with disclosed size, defect-density, and yield requirements. That would show both that the material works and that Kara can manufacture the specification rather than merely describe it.
