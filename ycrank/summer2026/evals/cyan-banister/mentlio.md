# Mentlio -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Autonomous agents are consuming most engineering tokens, choosing models task by task, and leaving managers unable to distinguish productive compute from expensive thrashing. Mentlio could become the control layer beneath that behavior. The leak is already visible: median monthly AI spend reportedly climbed from roughly $5 to $81 per user in ten months, while Mentlio’s local router preserved 98.93% of Fable 5’s Terminal-Bench 2.1 accuracy at 24.90% lower cost. But the dossier does not yet map the intervening transitions—what agents control, which purchasing boundary shifts, and why this team owns the resulting layer rather than an AI vendor or existing engineering-intelligence platform.

The founders have technical ingredients, not yet an obsession story. Ashank Shah researched biomedical generative and graph-based learning; Ahmet Demirbas has five listed publications spanning ML, NLP, sensing, and digital twins. Their token-compression work is relevant. Yet three months before Mentlio they were building Adjoint, a Unity plugin. That abrupt pivot does not prove opportunism, but it prevents me from treating their biographies as evidence that they independently chased this system leak before AI-cost management became fashionable. Caltech credentials are not Tizz, and a 13,000-view launch post is not Rizz or Biz.

The system wound is real but only partly exposed. Engineering leaders are spending more without knowing whether the expenditure improves delivery, and reproducing Coinbase-style routing, caching, and observability internally consumes scarce platform labor. Mentlio returns some control by connecting spend to pull requests and routing prompts toward cheaper capable models. What remains unnamed is the rent: no customer’s gateway cost, wasted-token bill, integration burden, or delivery improvement is quantified. “AI spend is rising” identifies pressure; it does not yet reveal who captures the excess or how much autonomy Mentlio returns.

The strongest bull case is that this is not merely another management dashboard. Local scoring protects prompts and source code, the router has a measurable technical result, and the product spans context optimization, caching, response control, and log compression. DX’s $1 billion acquisition also shows that engineering intelligence can become strategically valuable. If deployment data compounds across prompt characteristics, routing choices, savings, and delivery outcomes, Mentlio could occupy a useful layer beneath autonomous coding rather than compete screen-for-screen with Jellyfish or Swarmia.

But that data flywheel is currently hypothetical. There is no disclosed customer, repeat audit, production workload, revenue, or engineer who changed behavior because of the product. One benchmark against one comparison model cannot establish a routing advantage across Claude, OpenAI, Cursor, and rapidly changing workloads. Worse, ranking engineers by output, prompt quality, and token efficiency creates a surveillance-shaped adoption problem even if raw code stays local. Privacy architecture helps with custody; it does not resolve whether employees consent to being scored or whether managers trust the score.

I would not write the check today. Mentlio looks like an enabling layer searching for its first costly user behavior, and the competitive boundary can collapse quickly if model vendors bundle routing or established platforms copy local optimization. The verifiable flip condition is three named organizations with at least 15 engineers completing 90-day deployments that reduce total AI-coding spend by 20% or more without worsening accepted-PR cycle time, while renewing on paid contracts and documenting employee-level consent controls. That would turn the benchmark into an operating artifact and show that the future has begun leaking through real budgets.
