# OpenHack -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a small engineering team letting software write, attack, and repair its applications continuously. The interesting transition is from receiving security warnings to trusting machines with permission to prove and close vulnerabilities. OpenHack’s application modeling and sandbox exploitation point toward that world. But that future is my extrapolation. The dossier does not establish the intermediate steps from today’s beta verification to that level of delegated trust. I cannot give my largest criterion credit for a story I supplied myself.

Ananay Arora is the reason I keep reading. ASU’s April 2023 account establishes a security-research background before OpenHack’s June 2026 announcement; his bug-bounty work and `apple-auth` library, with 330 stars, add substance beyond an Apple job title. Security appears to be something he practiced before it became this company’s category. That earns meaningful founder credit. The record is thinner on independently documented early experiments, recruiting complementary talent, and command of the commercial machinery. A solo founder identified as CEO presents no affirmative evidence of the equal-control or leadership-shell-game problems I reject.

Why does an engineer have to investigate a scanner’s accusation before knowing whether anything is actually exploitable? OpenHack attacks that wasted attention, alongside the gaps between periodic pentests. Giving developers a reproducible exploit could return control over what gets fixed first. This fits my quieter pattern of backing irritating workflow repairs. But the dossier does not quantify the investigation burden or identify an artificial gatekeeper collecting a rent. Free entry pricing and custom Enterprise credits describe how OpenHack charges; they do not establish how much agency or money it returns.

The strongest argument against passing is wonderfully concrete: an authentication bypass reported March 1, 2026, and patched March 2. OpenHack’s published reproduction of CVE-2026-36755 is more persuasive than an “AI security engineer” slogan. There is working machinery here, plus 430 stars, 65 forks, and six displayed customer logos. Still, a successful disclosure establishes technical usefulness; it does not establish that customers repeatedly entrust releases to the product. The logos disclose neither payment nor behavior. My pre-revenue exception applies when a functioning prototype accompanies a well-supported future path and unusually deep founder obsession. Missing revenue is acceptable; the other evidence must carry the check. Here it does not yet do enough.

OpenHack also resembles the concealed infrastructure I like, but resemblance earns little. Its MIT-licensed scanner is a controlled technical artifact, not an established scarce dependency beneath several downstream markets. Aikido already combines autonomous pentesting with broader security coverage, making independent distribution consequential. The source-code deletion and no-training commitments address my privacy concern directionally; the dossier establishes neither a severe violation nor complete custody safeguards. I would **pass with my own money today**. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: three unrelated engineering teams paying for verification on every release for 90 consecutive days, with records showing that reproduced findings led to shipped fixes. That would demonstrate OpenHack becoming an operating habit rather than another destination for alerts.
