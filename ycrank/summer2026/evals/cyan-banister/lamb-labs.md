# Lamb Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Useful models live inside robots, medical devices, factories, and air-gapped systems without asking a distant GPU cluster for permission. Lamb Labs has found a plausible physical constraint on that future: retrieving weights can consume 100–1,000 times the energy of arithmetic, while some GPUs use only 20–40% of their compute. A company-reported dev board running a 7-billion-parameter model at 6 W is a real little leak from that world. What is missing is the bridge: no foundry, process node, tape-out status, yield plan, or shipment date explains how this artifact becomes repeatable silicon.

The product is below the application wave, which is where I want to look. Model–chip co-design, on-chip weights, quantization, an RL architecture-optimization environment, and diffusion conversion could serve private data centers, regulated deployments, robotics, wearables, and consumer hardware. Those are multiple downstream markets sharing one bottleneck. But “could” is doing dangerous work. Lamb Labs has not disclosed protected IP, proprietary data, or manufacturing know-how, and Taalas has already fabricated a 6-nm chip using the closely corresponding hardwired-model approach. This is enabling infrastructure without demonstrated control of the constraint yet.

Kotecha and Lanning have unusually relevant intellectual equipment: reinforcement learning and graph-neural-network research on one side, mathematical physics and CERN machine learning on the other. I can believe they understand the machinery. I cannot yet see the pre-company obsession. The dossier contains degrees, scholarships, and research credentials but no dated trail of chips, compiler artifacts, independent builds, customer encounters, or complementary recruiting. That distinction matters when the proposed competitors have raised between $219 million and roughly $925 million. Clever architecture is not enough; semiconductor founders must show how every dollar crosses the manufacturing chasm.

The bull case stings because the team may have built two businesses at once. Its post-training conversion reportedly makes existing models run twice as fast on ordinary GPUs, while the 6 W board suggests the same optimization machinery can eventually emit specialized silicon. If the software generates deployment feedback and revenue before fabrication, it could finance the harder destination. But there is no reported customer, paid conversion, signed proof of concept, or repeated user behavior. Four hundred two company followers and 382 reactions to a launch post are attention, not someone rearranging a system around the product.

I also do not yet know what agency Lamb Labs returns. Lower power and avoiding subscriptions or API charges could let regulated and air-gapped users control inference locally, but that business model is inferred, and the dossier identifies no buyer currently paying a quantified cloud rent or waiting behind a specific institutional gate. Privacy-sensitive deployment is potentially attractive only if custody and consent are designed explicitly; “private” is a market label, not an architecture.

My own money stays out today. The verifiable flip condition is one named customer repeatedly operating the 7B system in a power-constrained or air-gapped production workflow, paired with independently reproduced performance and a disclosed tape-out path. Without that, I expect the valuable piece to become GPU conversion or model-compilation software rather than a shipped custom-chip company. The future is visible; Lamb Labs has not yet shown that it owns the road there.
