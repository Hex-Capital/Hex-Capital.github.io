# Experiential Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Companies no longer treat every agent interaction as a disposable API call; the failures, corrections, and edge cases accumulate into an operating asset they can move between models. The path is legible: production traces become simulations, smaller models become sufficient for narrow tasks, and routers reserve frontier intelligence for the difficult tail. Experiential Labs already has a small leak from that world—a customer-specific 9B model reportedly ran seven times faster than Opus 4.8 at 97% lower cost. The dossier maps the mechanism better than it maps the timeline, and the result still lives inside a simulated environment, but this is more than consensus “models are getting cheaper” language.

The broken rule is unusually clean. High-volume agent operators pay GPT or Claude repeatedly, while the endpoint owner retains the leverage and the customer’s accumulated task experience does not become portable capital. Experiential Labs converts that rent into weights the customer owns. That returns both economic agency and switching power, particularly if learned behavior really transfers when base models change. The unnamed deployment’s claimed 97% cost reduction gives the rent a number; what remains missing is evidence that customers retain the same control over their raw traces, deletion policies, and derived training data.

Kion Fallah and Silen Naihin have paid real technical tuition before this company. Fallah led mixed-reality simulation for autonomous-vehicle testing at Waabi; Naihin contributed to AutoGPT, agent benchmarks, continual learning, interpretability, and DOE AI-for-science work. They researched together for six months before forming the company and shipped World Model Optimizer into public scrutiny, earning 337 GitHub stars. That is strong pre-company obsession and credible technical complementarity. The weaker part of Rizz–Tizz–Biz is Biz: one unnamed customer and an inferred enterprise-contract model do not yet show that the founders can turn technical performance into repeatable deployments.

The bear case bites directly into the product’s heart. The public case study evaluates 1,000 generated tasks with an LLM judge inside an LLM-simulated environment. An independent review found the trained Qwen model solved only 27.5% of trials against its teacher’s 49.0%, while maximum routing savings reduced measured quality by 0.54 points. If the simulation rewards behavior that production punishes, Experiential Labs is not compounding experience; it is manufacturing a cheaper benchmark score. Together AI, internal ML teams, and other open-model platforms can already combine fine-tuning, evaluations, and routing, so simulation fidelity must be substantially better than a reproducible workflow.

Still, this sits beneath the visible agent wave rather than merely decorating it. The controlled assets are customer-specific traces, simulations, evaluation histories, and portable weights—not a thin interface to one closed model. That matches the concealed-infrastructure pattern, and publishing workflow components may be useful distribution rather than surrender if the difficult asset is the longitudinal production loop. But there is no demonstrated structural barrier yet, and the absence of published retention controls around production telemetry activates a serious privacy and custody concern. No evidence identifies a 50/50 governance problem or leadership shell game; Fallah is explicitly CEO, although the contractual control structure remains unreported.

There is also no exceptional user ritual, signed industrial proof, or production-scale cohort that would justify waiving the validation gap. My 36-month bet is that Experiential Labs reaches $3 million to $8 million in ARR through managed agent-cost optimization, but remains service-heavy until it proves that simulations predict live outcomes across customers. I would invest a small personal check because the founders, ownership transfer, and lower-stack mechanism are unusually aligned. My verifiable flip condition is one named 90-day production cohort: if the system cannot reduce all-in inference cost by at least 70% while keeping task success within five percentage points of the frontier baseline, I pass.
