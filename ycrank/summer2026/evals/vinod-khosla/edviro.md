# Edviro -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Edviro may become useful software, but the dossier does not yet describe a consequential energy company. The $11.079 billion figure measures spending on building-energy-management systems, not the energy wasted by buildings, the population affected, or the cost pool Edviro could eliminate. Seven live school sites and a claimed $400,000 saved establish a real problem. They do not establish an orders-of-magnitude consequence. The downside is one check; the upside currently looks like a competent facilities-software business, not a 100x option.

The strongest evidence is economic. Edviro reached $20,000 ARR in three months while claiming $400,000 in customer savings, connects to installed systems without rip-and-replace hardware, and has 29 sites described as expanding beyond the seven already live. That is potentially excellent customer value for a pre-seed company. It also matches the useful pattern of improving existing industrial infrastructure rather than demanding new equipment or a green premium. But aggregate savings without named case studies, deployment costs, or an IPMVP baseline cannot yet prove repeatable unsubsidized economics.

The technical case is weaker. Edviro names Clockworks, 75F, GridPoint, Siemens, and Trane, but supplies no measured improvement in savings, deployment time, fault-detection accuracy, or control performance against any of them. Connecting fragmented data, detecting anomalies, issuing work orders, and verifying results is a sensible product loop. It is not yet a demonstrated 5–10x discontinuity. The danger is that the “world model” is an ambitious label around capabilities that building-management incumbents can add through software and distribution.

The founders have done more than produce a presentation. Hursh Shah found approximately $360,000 in school utility overbilling, and the two-person team converted that observation into live deployments, early revenue, and a construction-company M&V pilot. Shah’s research and prior startup work, combined with Tanuj Siripurapu’s ML and infrastructure experience, support execution. There is still no documented belief revision, complementary magnet hire, or explicit assignment of ownership for cybersecurity, integration, simulation error, and savings verification. Biography is not learning rate.

The bull case stings: a tiny team has found a painful installed-infrastructure wedge, generated revenue quickly, produced apparent customer savings twenty times current ARR, and begun accumulating intervention-outcome data that could become proprietary. This is the plausible exception—a modest initial product evolving into the control and capital-planning layer for a large existing value pool. I cannot underwrite that exception yet because the dossier identifies the kill risks but gives no cheapest decisive experiment, numerical threshold, cost, timing, or stop condition. Capital appears positioned to expand audits and deployments before the technical moat has been separated from implementation labor.

My 36-month base case is $1–3 million ARR concentrated in schools and adjacent facilities, with Edviro operating as a valuable energy-operations platform but not yet a defensible world-model company. I pass today. One result flips the decision: an independently verified study across at least 50 heterogeneous buildings showing at least 30% energy-cost reduction, deployment in under two weeks using existing systems, and sustained performance materially exceeding Clockworks or Siemens on the same sites.
