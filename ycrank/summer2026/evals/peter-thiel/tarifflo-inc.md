# Tarifflo Inc. -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Tarifflo’s contested truth is that customs compliance is not merely an administrative cost: import records contain enough systematic, recoverable errors for software to fund its own distribution through contingency fees. The obvious falsifier is a representative audit cohort producing recoveries too small—or too frequently rejected by CBP—to cover review, filing, and customer-acquisition costs. The dossier does not name the prevailing belief it rejects, but it supplies more than a slogan: over 1 million products audited and a reported $1.3 million recovery for Orly after reviewing 10,000-plus items. This is an incomplete but testable secret.

The beachhead is reasonably definite: U.S. importers with historical entries worth auditing, especially those already working through freight and customs brokers. Tarifflo has an ABI filer code, a direct CBP connection, 10 million linked shipment records, and licensed-broker review. Those are real implementation assets. They are not yet a monopoly. Zonos has CBP-selected infrastructure, KlearNow connects directly to customs systems in five countries, and Altana already sells trade intelligence to enterprises and government agencies. The decisive question is whether Tarifflo owns exclusive learning from classification decisions and correction outcomes. The dossier explicitly cannot establish that ownership or any measured learning effect.

The contingency model is clever because it converts distrust into distribution: the importer pays nothing upfront, while a free sample audit reveals whether money has been left behind. Alba Wheels Up filing Orly’s petitions shows that the partner route can complete a real workflow. But a partner retaining the filing relationship may also retain the customer. Tarifflo has not disclosed its recovery percentage, rejection rate, sales cycle, acquisition cost, or repeat engagement rate. A mechanism that wins isolated refunds can become a profitable consultancy; a mechanism that accumulates direct filing relationships could become infrastructure. Only the second outcome supports last-mover economics.

Bryce Judy has unusually exact founder-market fit: KPMG customs work, a CBP broker license, information-systems training, and published HTS-classification benchmarking research. Yet domain knowledge is not founding alignment. YC lists Judy as the sole active founder after he met historical co-founder Tanner Helms at a coding competition, while the dossier provides no evidence about the five-person team’s shared history, ownership, full-time status, or whether core engineering is in-house. This resembles a founding-structure red flag without proving it. Missing evidence is not refutation, but it prevents conviction.

The strongest bull case is that Tarifflo is an adversarial information system: it makes a contested regulatory reality legible, has already surfaced a seven-figure customer recovery, and could compound every reviewed entry into proprietary compliance intelligence. Rapid tariff changes in 2025–2026 increase the value of such a system. But none of the known exceptions applies. There is no independently verified zero-to-one technical result, persistent transaction behavior overcoming legal hostility, or qualified production unit that overrides the unresolved monopoly question. Policy turbulence creates demand, but it also creates maintenance costs available to every funded competitor.

Within 36 months, I expect Tarifflo to reach roughly $3–8 million in annualized contingency and filing revenue but remain an acquisition candidate—most plausibly for Zonos—rather than an independent monopoly. My judgment changes if Tarifflo verifies one condition: at least $5 million of annualized net revenue from 50 or more importers, with over 70% repeat usage and contractual ownership of the resulting classification and correction data. Until then, this is a promising secret attached to an unproven value-capture system: investigate, but do not yet invest.
