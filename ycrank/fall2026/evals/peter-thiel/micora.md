# Micora -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Micora’s potentially important secret is that deploying remote power can become a repeatable machine operation rather than a construction project. Its claim of autonomous installation at **100 kWp per day** makes that proposition testable. It fails if site preparation, human intervention, and commissioning consume the labor supposedly eliminated by array deployment. The dossier establishes a concrete technical claim, but neither a measured result nor a prevailing belief that named competitors reject. I give the claim some credit; I cannot yet treat it as a discovered secret.

The investment question turns on who captures the savings. BoxPower already supplies prefabricated microgrids, so putting solar, batteries, and electronics into a convenient package does not establish a monopoly. Micora’s deployment automation could create a proprietary advantage, but the current documentation describes forklift placement, and the October 21 LAKI collaboration concerns planned robotic deployment. These descriptions could represent complementary steps in one process; they do not prove deception or technical failure. They also do not establish an advantage competitors cannot reproduce. Pacific islands and manufacturing sites identify possible customers, without yet identifying a bounded market Micora can dominate.

The strongest argument against passing is the unusually relevant combination of operating experience. Akemoto shipped heat pumps and worked in space robotics; Choo has both robotics manufacturing experience and Tesla solar/BESS deployment experience. Micora could be attacking a coordination problem these founders understand unusually well: making otherwise available components work as one rapidly deployed system. Its claimed deployments suggest there may already be something to inspect. That fits my unstated preference for businesses extending technology into physical production and energy infrastructure. But expertise does not establish founding alignment. Shared schools between Akemoto and Mareels do not document prior work together, and full-time status, ownership, and control of essential engineering remain undisclosed. Those receive the cautious default, not an invented red flag.

Micora also has one coherent product engine: sell or lease remote power systems. Purchase and **12- or 24-month leases** are alternative financing arrangements for that engine. The unresolved issue is whether rapid installation produces attractive company economics after equipment, delivery, commissioning, and any retained financing exposure. Final pricing follows site review, and land and ongoing operations are excluded. Without a monetary quote or margin structure, the $43.47 billion microgrid market cannot supply the missing arithmetic. Direct configuration inquiries and founder requests for introductions to diesel users identify an initial sales route, but provide little evidence about its repeatability or cost.

My coordination-failure red flag is relevant because robotics, electrical integration, and site delivery must work together. It is not yet established in its strongest form: the dossier does not show Micora needing several simultaneous scientific breakthroughs. Nor do the known exceptions justify overriding the gaps. There is no independently demonstrated technical event, documented persistent customer purchasing, or sufficiently concrete production path that resolves the central risk. The likely intermediate outcome is a useful microgrid integrator whose customers continue buying substantial engineering work with each installation. That could be a sound business while leaving little proprietary value for my equity.

I would pass on a personal check today. One verifiable event would move me to investigation: **an independently audited, paid customer deployment demonstrating the claimed 100 kWp daily rate and materially lower fully commissioned cost than a comparable BoxPower installation under matched site conditions**. That would connect the proposed secret to purchasing advantage. Today, Micora has a plausible claim to test; it has not demonstrated the mechanism that lets it keep the value.
