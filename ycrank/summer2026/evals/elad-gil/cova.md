# Cova -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: AI can remove administrative labor from a growing $162.35 billion home-care market, families can enroll relatives as paid caregivers, and Cova becomes large only if automation produces materially better contribution margins per active caregiver while preserving reimbursement compliance. “No non-caregiver headcount” is the right falsifiable proposition. The dossier does not yet supply the reimbursement rate, caregiver payout, exception rate, enrollment throughput, or administrative cost needed to show that it is true.

Owning the Michigan agency is important. Cova controls intake, benefits coordination, enrollment, insurance, billing, and delivery instead of formatting model output for another provider. The license and direct payer relationship give its agents permissions and responsibility inside an end-to-end workflow. Better models should therefore expand the work Cova can automate rather than erase the product. But applicants still complete four state forms and send photographs of every page, and there is no disclosed integration, reliability measurement, or exception-handling rate. This is a credible operating system under construction, not yet a demonstrated autonomous agency.

The economic discontinuity remains the weak point. A prospective caregiver’s quoted $22 hourly Medicaid rate says nothing about Cova’s retained margin. The company discloses neither payer contracts nor payer mix, and the broad market’s forecast 10.0% growth does not establish software-like economics. Aidaly already covers 8.5 million lives across five states, while Honor and Cera show that funded providers can add technology to care delivery. Cova’s claim of zero administrative headcount could change the incumbent margin structure, but without cohort economics it could equally conceal founder labor, outsourced work, or accumulating compliance exceptions.

Product-market pull is similarly preliminary. Being licensed and live is operational proof, and the June 2026 caregiver report confirms that at least one real enrollment entered the system. It is not evidence of completed enrollment, reimbursement, repeat referrals, retention, or a scalable acquisition channel. The absence of revenue is normal at pre-seed; the problem is that there is not yet an informative substitute such as a named MCO agreement, repeated discharge-planner referrals, or a documented enrollment bottleneck created by demand.

Jordan Ibe and Ansh Tandon have unusually relevant implementation experience: both worked in Palantir Healthcare, they combine software and bioengineering backgrounds, and they spent the preceding year building AI systems for agencies and hospitals before turning Omnition into Cova’s internal system. That supports technical execution and domain fluency. It does not establish second-time-founder learning or costly personal commitment, so I would not let biography override the unresolved market economics.

The strongest bull case is that Cova is an early AI-native service business whose first licensed agency becomes reusable distribution for additional payer and care products. If the founders can reproduce the Michigan operation state by state without adding administrative labor, the physical-service exception applies because technology has actually changed unit economics. Today, however, the dossier documents neither that step change nor a structural barrier preventing Aidaly or another licensed provider from reproducing the automation. The inferred workflow data moat has no stated reproduction cost, which is precisely where service-heavy AI businesses tend to overclaim defensibility.

I would not write the check yet. My base case is that within 36 months Cova reaches $3 million to $8 million in annualized care revenue but remains a regional, operationally intensive provider rather than demonstrating venture-scale software economics. The single condition that flips me is three consecutive months with at least 100 active paid caregivers, greater than 30% gross margin after caregiver compensation, and non-caregiver operating labor below 8% of revenue. That would validate the one variable on which the company depends.
