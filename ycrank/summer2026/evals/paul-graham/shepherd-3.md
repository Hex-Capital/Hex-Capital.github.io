# Shepherd -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Philip Meng has four named ML publications, while Shepherd has no public evidence that any founder previously suffered the enterprise-memory problem. That pairing defines Shepherd: unusually technical young builders pursuing an idea that appears selected rather than discovered. Shepherd can probably build the artifact. The unanswered question is whether Shepherd’s founders need it badly enough to keep digging after the fashionable version stops working.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for hacker genealogy: the Collisons had shipped technical products, written Lisp, and accumulated hacker credibility before payments became a company. Shepherd shares the raw mechanism. Philip Meng published at NeurIPS, ICLR, EMNLP, and AAAI; Elijah Renner was a Regeneron STS Scholar and ISEF winner; Ishan Ramrakhiani built computational-biology models. But Shepherd diverges where Stripe became convincing: Ishan’s best public repository has 4 stars, Elijah’s has 3, and Philip has no public repository. Research credentials show intelligence. They do not yet show the animal programmer who ships something strangers adopt.

**Kiko** (2005) shows the low-rung failure mechanism for organic ideas: a plausible software product can lose momentum when the founders have no enduring personal pull toward the problem. Shepherd’s founders worked in NLP, medical research, and computational biology, but Shepherd’s dossier contains no evidence that Philip, Ishan, or Elijah personally struggled to reconstruct decisions across Slack, schematics, firmware, and operations. Shepherd therefore gets the absent-evidence default here. No known exception rescues it: Airbnb had cereal boxes, Loopt had Sam Altman’s force of personality, and Stripe had the Collisons’ prior commerce hacking. Shepherd has no equivalent override signal yet.

Shepherd’s early-access launch and Ishan’s report that an outbound tool increased calls are faint tracks of resourcefulness, not an animal story. Over the next 18 months, this dimension compounds only if Shepherd’s founders perform the ugly manual work themselves: install connectors, repair permissions, reconstruct missing decisions, and sit beside users until the answers become indispensable. If Shepherd instead treats booked demonstrations and 381 LinkedIn followers as progress, the founders will optimize the appearance of motion while Glean and Dust absorb the ordinary customers.

Shepherd’s terms acknowledge employee plaintext access, stale outputs, source outages, permission changes, and connector rate limits. Those are genuine schleps. They could become Shepherd’s advantage because large vendors dislike deployments requiring source-by-source archaeology and bespoke security controls. The score would flip upward if Shepherd could name even three technical organizations where the founders personally completed these deployments and users repeatedly relied on the resulting decision graph. Without that evidence, Shepherd has found a schlep zone but has not proved it will enter the swamp.

**Airbnb** (2009) had three founders whose complementary skills and shared history survived repeated rejection. Shepherd also has three founders, and Ishan Ramrakhiani and Elijah Renner share a Dartmouth research connection. But Shepherd’s dossier says only that all three applied to YC together in March 2026; it does not establish years of building together or explain why CEO, CPO, and CTO are durable roles rather than titles assigned to three precocious researchers. Shepherd passes the numerical cofounder test. Shepherd has not yet passed the chemistry test.

Shepherd will, by July 2029, have narrowed from horizontal enterprise search into decision memory for biotech or hardware R&D teams and reached roughly $1–3M ARR, without becoming a general replacement for Glean. Shepherd’s technical and biomedical genealogy makes that vertical credible, while the absence of organic enterprise-search pain makes the present horizontal framing unstable. The likely path is not defeat by a better embedding model. It is discovering through installations that Shepherd understands research workflows better than it understands the generic enterprise.

Glean already exceeds $100M ARR, Dust has raised more than $60M, and Notion already distributes enterprise search inside an existing workspace. The strongest bear case is that Shepherd is a technically polished AI wrapper entering a consensus category with no moat, no revenue, and no demonstrated founder obsession—the precise red-flag pattern I normally avoid. Shepherd’s answer is the cross-domain decision trail spanning research, hardware, firmware, code, and agent sessions, but today that distinction is website language rather than user behavior. I would change my verdict to Invest if Shepherd produced five named technical customers totaling at least $250K ARR whose teams used its traced decisions weekly for six consecutive months.
