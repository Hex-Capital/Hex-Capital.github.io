# Pacific -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, compute should arrive more like manufactured equipment than a construction project: standardized pods move to powered sites, serve terrestrial AI workloads, and eventually operate where water and conventional cooling are unavailable. Pacific can name the steps—reuse dormant interconnections, factory-build the electrical stack, cool it with diamond wafers, then extend the architecture toward orbit. The leak from that future is real: 57% of data-center projects suffered delays of at least three months in 2025 while completed grid connections sat unused. But the distance between the leak and Pacific’s machine remains enormous.

Why does a powered site remain stranded? Pacific’s answer is better than “AI demand is large”: one-off construction, 33-week equipment lead times, permitting, and fragmented power infrastructure consume time that compute buyers cannot recover. A relocatable pod would return scheduling control to those buyers and potentially convert sunk interconnection capacity into revenue. The company’s claimed $1 billion weekly cost supplies magnitude, although it does not identify who captures that rent, and local approvals remain after the grid connection is solved.

The little object I want to touch is missing. Pacific publishes no operating pod, thermal-capacity test, power-density result, certification, patent, or field deployment. “Working with unnamed large aerospace companies” could mean anything from a conversation to a paid installation. The prior digital-twin product reached £10,000 MRR in May 2026, which proves some ability to sell, but the pivot means those customers did not perform a costly action around this physical system. Meanwhile, Armada has deployed modular units and Crusoe can attack the same opportunity with $3.9 billion raised. A diamond wafer is not a moat until it moves heat reliably outside a diagram.

Ralph Heward-Mills brings relevant thermal experience from Airbus and an ESA engineering challenge; Harper Dammann Smith combines aerospace training, deployed AI systems, and experience organizing an accelerator for more than 80 founders. That is credible adjacency, not yet pre-company obsession. I cannot see dated hardware builds, data-center operating tuition, manufacturing artifacts, or a history together that explains why these two will outlearn heavily capitalized infrastructure teams. The ambition to internalize transformers, switchgear, cooling, power distribution, compute operations, and eventually orbital deployment may be vertical integration—or six companies hiding inside one.

The bull case stings because pre-revenue industrial companies can deserve a check before revenue. Pacific has a technical founder, a quantified infrastructure wound, aerospace conversations, and a product that sits underneath several fashionable markets rather than wrapping one model. But the relevant known exception required a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a founder’s lifelong record of building robots. Pacific currently supplies none of those three. YC and NVIDIA Inception are endorsements, not substitutes for the machine.

My 36-month prediction is that Pacific retreats from the orbital story and becomes a bespoke managed-pod supplier to defense or aerospace customers, reaching roughly $1 million–$3 million ARR without yet proving repeatable mass production. I am neutral and would not invest my own money today: the system diagnosis is unusually good, but the working artifact—the thing that should compress this from professional daydreaming into conviction—is absent. One verifiable event flips me: a paying aerospace customer operating a Pacific-built pod continuously for 90 days with independently measured power density and waterless thermal performance.
