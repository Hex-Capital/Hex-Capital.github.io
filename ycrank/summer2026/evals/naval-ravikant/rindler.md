# Rindler -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have built close enough to the problem for their actions to matter. Arthur De Los Santos worked on robust vision-language navigation at MIT CSAIL; Michael Serrano combined CSAIL LLM research with production machine-learning work at Roblox. Rindler has converted that knowledge into verifier-gated actions running against Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, and state registries. This is stronger than résumé-based founder-market fit, although 38 internal runs across two grocery tasks do not yet prove that the team has endured the authenticated, adversarial web at meaningful scale.

The useful idea is not browser control. Browser control is becoming a commodity. The useful idea is that one verified mapping can serve every customer account on a multi-tenant platform, and a route repair can propagate centrally. If repair histories materially reduce future recovery work, Rindler accumulates an operational asset while customers sleep. But that asset is still asserted more than demonstrated: there is no published mapping count, longitudinal reliability, or evidence that competitors cannot reproduce the schemas. Browserbase, Kernel, Browser Use, and Deck already control adjacent layers, while Rindler depends on Kernel and Browserbase to operate.

The market has room to maneuver. Recruiting portals and business registries are merely the initial wedge into finance, compliance, payer, banking, and vendor workflows that remain trapped behind websites. The shared capability—turning unstable interfaces into typed, verified actions—could address a large transaction layer. Still, breadth is not the same as adjacency proven. Rindler has not shown that reliability learned on an ATS transfers economically to a bank, government registry, or bot-defended payer portal.

The strongest bull case is that this behaves like infrastructure before it looks like a network: a two-person team has shipped production workflows, exposed an MCP endpoint, published self-service pricing, and beaten Browser Use by company-reported margins of 4× speed, 3× cost, and 2× failures. A strange, capital-efficient product can deserve financing before its distribution loop becomes visible. The material difference here is that Rindler’s advantage sits above well-funded infrastructure providers capable of absorbing the same mapping layer; early product proof does not yet establish durable leverage.

There is direct monetization design—$100 per month for 100 runs and $1,000 for 1,000—but no disclosed payer, completed transaction, retention, or gross margin. More importantly, users do not yet create value for other users. The company performs the mapping and repair work centrally. That is reusable labor, not a permissionless contribution loop. Rindler resembles the unstated pattern of moving fragmented access into a structured system, but not yet the stronger pattern where outsiders compound supply or knowledge themselves.

I see no disclosed integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-morale red flag. The structural red flag is subtler: a fashionable agent company may mistake a maintained software library for a non-copyable substrate. I would not invest yet. One verifiable condition flips the decision: demonstrate that a growing corpus of at least 50 production portal mappings delivers over 95% successful completion across independent paying customers while shared repairs measurably reduce failure rates over successive quarters. That would turn the mapping thesis from code into compounding operational data.
