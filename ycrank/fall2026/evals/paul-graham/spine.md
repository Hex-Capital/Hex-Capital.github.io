# Spine -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The question I want answered about Spine is what happened when something broke. A carrier refused an enrollment. A payroll integration failed. What did Onur and Ece do next? The dossier describes an operating service, but supplies no specific episode connecting a founder’s response to an obstacle with a shipped solution. Their third company together is encouraging evidence of a durable working relationship. It does not establish resourcefulness under pressure. I would leave that criterion at its missing-evidence default.

There is considerably better evidence that someone wants what they have built. Spine reports 30+ startup customers, and Acadian reports commission revenue growing 4.4 times over six months through July 2026. That is roughly 6% compounded weekly, although two endpoints cannot establish steady weekly growth. The important detail is that employers can buy relief without replacing Rippling, Gusto, Deel, or ADP. Founders already have software. Spine offers to do the work. Carrier commissions mean the absence of employer service fees does not make this merely a collection of free users. This is substantial early demand evidence, with retention and customer concentration still unresolved.

The strongest case for investing is that the unpleasant work becomes the opportunity. Enrollment, carrier coordination, employee questions, and compliance are precisely the jobs software buyers discover they still own after buying software. An engineer and a lawyer who have operated companies together could be well suited to this. But the dossier does not document their own encounter with that problem, so I cannot award an organic-origin story by inference. Nor does the consumer health app establish an intelligent pivot: the observation that prompted the change and the timing are missing. Claims about AI plan analysis supply no measured technical proof. Those three dimensions remain at their respective defaults.

Spine also tests the limits of my preference for doing things manually. Manual service is valuable when it teaches founders how to build something much larger. It can also remain manual service. Acadian’s approximately 78% contribution margin is company-modeled; it does not establish that commissions cover the actual labor required as customers grow. Existing brokers can add support, while the HR platforms already occupy the workflow. The 50+ practitioner network may help distribution, but it is not yet the difficult proprietary substrate that has attracted me to other infrastructure businesses. Hiring for growth is not evidence that founders have delegated customer learning prematurely, and multiple acquisition channels weaken the concern about dependence on one large partner. Neither observation resolves the delivery economics.

I would pass today. The revenue growth is the strongest objection to that decision, and it deserves more weight than the funding round or founders’ credentials. I checked whether working-product, overwhelming-demand, or independently validated-proof exceptions justify overriding the founder gate. Spine has meaningful demand, but the dossier does not establish an exceptional substitute for the missing founder evidence. My flip condition is one documented renewal cohort showing the founders overcoming a specific carrier or HRIS blocker within weeks, retaining the affected customers, and earning positive contribution after all delivery labor. That would connect the attractive service proposition to the people and operating mechanism I would actually be backing.
