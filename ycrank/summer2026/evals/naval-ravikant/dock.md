# Dock -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Mike Molinet and Govind Kavaturi are repeat B2B operators building Dock as a single-sided AI workspace: unusually strong long-term-partner evidence attached to unusually weak network effects. Dock inherits six years of founder collaboration and experience scaling Branch past $100 million ARR, but each new Dock organization remains economically independent of the next. The signature is clear: proven operators applying code leverage to a product that does not yet compound through its users.

**Twitter** is the high rung on the founder-trio ladder: Jack shipped, Ev Williams had completed Blogger cleanly, and both could survive a decade-long game. Dock approaches that rung because Molinet hired Kavaturi as Branch’s first international employee, worked with him for six years, and later co-founded Thena with him; durable collaboration is harder to counterfeit than pedigree. Dock diverges because the dossier establishes intelligence and energy, not how Molinet and Kavaturi treated former teams or resolved adversity. The YC page duplicating Molinet while omitting Kavaturi is probably clerical noise, but integrity is verified through clean history, not assumed from $100 million ARR.

**Perplexity** is the low rung on network compounding: each user receives an answer without directly improving the next user’s product, so the investment required an exceptional founder-specific-knowledge override. Dock has the same structural absence more starkly—its dossier explicitly documents no adoption-based network effect or proprietary-data advantage. Dock’s tables, permissions, and audit history may produce switching costs, but switching costs are not network effects. Perplexity had Aravind Srinivas’s retrieval depth and pre-consensus product pull; Dock currently has six repositories, one star on the leading repository, and no equivalent exception strong enough to waive this dimension.

Dock’s $19-per-organization Pro plan sits on top of a free web tier, Mac application, npm CLI, MCP server, templates, REST, webhooks, and a ChatGPT integration. That is real permissionless code leverage: over 18 months, every additional client and template can lower adoption friction without proportional labor. Dock still depends on gatekeepers at important edges—ChatGPT, MCP clients, third-party agents—and one GitHub star shows distribution architecture rather than distribution momentum. Code can replicate Dock cheaply; it has not yet demonstrated that attention will replicate with it.

Mike Molinet scaled Branch past $100 million ARR, while Govind Kavaturi says he grew international revenue from zero to $100 million ARR across multiple continents. Dock therefore has non-trainable knowledge about operating and distributing B2B software, but Dock has not shown equally specific knowledge in agent runtimes, authorization systems, or collaborative databases; neither founder has public personal repositories. The score would move into the top rung if Dock showed that its actor-attribution and permission model came from sustained internal agent use, or if outside developers adopted its MCP workspace organically. Until then, Dock has founder-market adjacency rather than the builder-product identity that made Stack Overflow and Replit obvious.

The $182.97 billion projected AI-agents market gives Dock more pivot-room than its current multiplayer-workspace label suggests. **Notion** survived repeated rebuilds because the underlying surface—software for people creating and organizing work—was broader than the initial product; Dock can similarly pivot from shared documents into agent identity, audit infrastructure, workflow state, or an authorization control plane. Dock’s organization-based pricing and general MCP, REST, and webhook interfaces preserve those options. The risk is that Dust, Relevance AI, and CrewAI capture the application layer before Dock becomes the underlying protocol.

Dock will, by July 2029, pivot from general multiplayer surfaces toward an agent-identity and audit control plane and reach $3 million–$8 million ARR, without becoming a user-compounding network. Dock’s strongest asset is repeat-founder execution; Dock’s weakest is the absence of an Nth-user mechanism. Experienced B2B operators can sell a useful control layer, while agent permissions, activity history, and signed webhooks naturally pull the product toward enterprise infrastructure. That produces a credible business, though not yet Twitter-shaped upside.

Dust has raised more than $60 million, Dock remains in commercial beta, native WebAuthn is unfinished, and Dock cannot govern the third-party agents customers connect. The bear mechanism stings: model providers and orchestration platforms can absorb shared state and permissions, leaving Dock as a thin interface with one GitHub star and no network defense. Molinet and Kavaturi’s Branch record partially defeats that case because distribution and organizational execution are themselves specific knowledge, while Dock’s open MCP and REST posture allows it to sit across vendors instead of betting on one. I would write the wide-funnel angel check, not double down. If Dock has fewer than 100 weekly active organizations by July 2027, I would flip to Pass because the permissionless architecture will have failed to create permissionless pull.
