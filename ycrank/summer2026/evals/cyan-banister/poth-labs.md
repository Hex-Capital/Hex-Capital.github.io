# Poth Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the attractive version is clear: every company maintains a living model of why each customer adopted, expanded, or churned; when evidence is missing, that model asks the customer directly. But Poth Labs has not mapped the steps from today’s fragmented tools to that future. Its present-day leak is narrower: Inkle, Sol, Composio, UnCircuit, Illume, and several Summer 2026 YC teams have reportedly used a functioning product. That proves an irritating workflow exists, not yet that a new organizational behavior has arrived.

The founders understand the machinery. Matthew Wong worked on U.S. commercial data systems as a Palantir forward-deployed engineer and deployment strategist; Mojmír Horváth was a Tietoevry data scientist and coauthored research on a 4.5-billion-parameter multimodal model. Those are relevant technical and customer-deployment credentials. What I cannot find is the pre-company obsession: a dated artifact, independently pursued experiment, or operating wound that compelled them to build Poth. The soft pivot from adaptive surveys to Ask Poth may be intelligent customer discovery, but it also leaves open whether the team found its inevitable problem or followed the strongest current demand signal.

The system failure is real but insufficiently decoded. Customer knowledge is scattered across support tickets, calls, CRM records, surveys, documents, and usage analytics, so teams repeatedly reconstruct the same explanation by hand. Yet the dossier does not identify the rent: no quantified analyst hours, delayed roadmap decisions, research expenditure, or incumbent gatekeeper whose power Poth removes. It returns informational control to product and customer teams, but without measuring the burden, I cannot tell whether this is infrastructure they must own or convenient software they can replace.

The strongest bull case is the product loop. Poth does more than summarize stored feedback: it resolves entities, presents supporting and contradicting evidence, and launches adaptive interviews when the record is incomplete. Named teams suggest the artifact works, and the founders possess unusually relevant enterprise-data skills for a two-person pre-seed company. If those customers repeatedly permit Poth to initiate research and then use its conclusions to change products, that would be costly behavior with the beginnings of a proprietary evidence graph.

Today, however, the dossier supplies no frequency, retention, payment, data volume, or decision outcome. Enterpret already offers entity resolution, cited answers, a customer context graph, and more than 50 integrations with $25.1 million raised. Poth’s adaptive follow-up is a feature-level distinction until user behavior proves otherwise. Worse, the system ingests CRM, support, analytics, transcripts, and surveys through several processors. Saying data is neither sold nor used for model training is useful, but it does not answer consent, custody, deletion, access boundaries, or the consequences of an incorrect identity match. This is exactly where a seemingly mundane entity-resolution error becomes a surveillance or decision-quality problem.

I pass. Poth Labs currently looks like a capable team building a useful application inside a crowded present-day category, without a controlled lower-stack asset or a six-year causal map. The verifiable flip condition is specific: show that at least three named customers have paid for six months, allowed Poth-generated interviews to run repeatedly, and made documented product or retention decisions from the resulting evidence graph. That would turn “trusted by” into strange, consequential behavior—and show that adaptive evidence collection is the company rather than another AI research feature.
