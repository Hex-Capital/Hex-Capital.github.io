# Instance -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

10,000+ human-labeled episodes make **Instance** a technically serious, missionary-looking pivot whose strongest dimension is founder conviction and whose weakest is category fit. Claire Mao and Lucy Cai have the MIT, CSAIL, JPL, and SpaceX scar tissue to understand robot-policy evaluation, and **Instance** already beats Claude Opus 4.8 on its benchmark, 0.76 macro F1 to 0.53. But software for robots is still software. More precisely, **Instance** looks like capable founders constructing a useful AI tool inside a sleeve where I have neither a structural edge nor evidence that the asset deserves venture financing.

**Drop.io** is the high rung for asymmetric conviction: build a stubborn primitive against free incumbents, keep turning the crank, and let execution reveal the market. **Instance** shares some of that mechanism because Cai researched robot learning, Mao built physical-system simulations, and the founders assembled 10,000 labeled episodes across seven robot platforms before presenting a revenue story. **Instance** diverges where the analogy matters most: Mao was promoting an AI data analyst in February 2026 and a robotics verifier by July. A five-month pivot can be an excellent regatta tack, but without public category writing or evidence that robotics evaluation predates the pivot, it is not yet proof of a mission the founders cannot be talked out of.

**OpenAI** is the low-rung mechanism for my category-fit test: foundation-model economics, consensus attention, and no value-seed advantage for a small investor buying the derivative layer. **Instance** is not training a foundation model, which is the quick walk-back, but **Instance** is selling a fine-tuned verifier into the same commoditization current while Robocurve and Bifrost distribute open-source alternatives and Runway demonstrates world-model evaluation. None of my unstated sleeves appears here: no crypto economics, DTC brand, prescription rail, creator asset, holdco, or documented Wizard-of-Oz service evolving into software. The office-demo offer hints at founder-led service, but it is not evidence of six months manually running customer evaluations.

No public round price, financing instrument, tier-1 pass, or customer revenue exists for **Instance**, so no evidence — applying the 9/30 absent-evidence default. Over the next 18 months, **Instance** could accumulate proprietary verdicts, but YC robotics heat is more likely to raise the SAFE cap before those verdicts establish an underwriting edge. The 0.76 F1 benchmark improves the product; it does not misprice the security. Without a below-batch valuation or a visibly skeptical financing market, I am buying the hot deck rather than the illegible asset.

The public HTTP API could make **Instance** capital-efficient if per-rollout revenue compounds faster than inference and labeling costs. The counterfactual that flips the capital-structure score is straightforward: **Instance** reaches default-alive through usage revenue, or finances customer-specific evaluation rigs with a revenue-share instrument instead of repeatedly selling equity. Today there is no published pricing or revenue, while the autonomous workflow still requires an unlaunched second robot to reset scenes. That leaves a generic SaaS cap table attached to a workflow that may require hardware integration and several financing tacks.

**John Fish / Bookshelved** had distribution sitting on the cap table before the product existed; the person was the asset and the financing could be shaped around that fact. **Instance** has 393 company LinkedIn followers, Mao has roughly 844 X followers, and Cai has roughly 2,000—useful founder reach, not owned community. **Instance** must acquire robotics laboratories one relationship at a time, and 35 YC Launch votes do not create the customer-acquisition cushion that made the Bookshelved structure underwritable.

**Foxglove** is where I expect the legibility translation to end: by July 2029, **Instance** will have pivoted from standalone success detection into robotics-data QA and observability, then be acquired by Foxglove for technology and talent before reaching $2 million ARR. **Instance** has a credible verifier but only automates one narrow judgment, while Foxglove already owns the broader data workflow and has raised at least $58.7 million. The likely outcome is not that Instance’s model fails; it is that the model becomes a feature whose natural economic home is inside the system of record.

**Ro** is the strongest objection because capital intensity and operational complexity can become the moat when a new infrastructure layer clears work customers cannot do themselves. The bull case is that **Instance** turns 10,000 labeled episodes, seven robot platforms, and a 23-point F1 advantage over Claude into the proprietary cross-robot evaluation corpus every lab needs. The objection stings because Mao and Cai have unusually relevant technical histories, and the five-month pivot may be a fast tack rather than mercenary drift. But **Instance** currently judges only success, leaves physical resets unsolved, reports no production customer, and faces open-source and simulated substitutes; Ro had regulatory rails that competitors could not download from GitHub. I would flip to Invest if Instance reaches $1 million ARR from at least five named robotics fleets with 90%+ renewal and customer-approved verdict data accumulating into a proprietary corpus.
