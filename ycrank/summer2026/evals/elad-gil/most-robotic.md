# Most Robotic -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward but the company map is not: open physical-AI infrastructure now lets robotics teams compress training and evaluation from months to days, while the variable determining whether Most Robotic becomes large is whether it can generate proprietary deployment learning faster than capitalized competitors and convert that learning into superior task completion across valuable workflows. Most Robotic has not named that variable, its threshold, or even the customer and task against which it would be measured. “Research lab for physical AI” describes a technical domain, not a falsifiable company thesis.

The discontinuity itself is credible. NVIDIA released open Cosmos 3 reasoning, simulation, and action-generation components in May 2026, while Genesis AI, Skild AI, and FieldAI are simultaneously validating that significant capital is entering the layer. But open infrastructure cuts both ways: it lowers experimentation costs for this two-person team while also lowering reproduction costs for everyone else. With Skild reportedly at approximately $30 million in 2025 revenue and more than $2 billion raised, and FieldAI already deployed across hundreds of industrial environments, Most Robotic needs a narrow wedge where data acquisition, embodiment, or customer access compounds faster than competitor scale. Nothing public identifies one.

The strongest bull case is a frontier-research exception. The founders are MIT computer scientists, have robotics-research experience, are described collectively as having worked at NASA JPL, SpaceX, and AWS, and have known each other since middle school. A rare team can justify financing before commercial pull, as Anthropic did when its researchers’ specific frontier contributions were already documented. The material difference is proof: Most Robotic discloses no founder attribution, paper, benchmark, dataset, model architecture, hardware demonstration, or prototype. Employer logos establish plausible technical competence; they do not establish scarce research capability or the capital path required to compete in a crystallizing market.

There is also no owned workflow yet. The dossier cannot identify a robot embodiment, integration, customer action, feedback loop, or operational responsibility. A future proprietary interaction dataset is only a hypothesis, and Most Robotic has not estimated what it would cost a well-funded competitor to reproduce it. Better foundation models therefore might strengthen the company, erase part of its stack, or be irrelevant to its eventual product. Without a disclosed architecture or application, those outcomes cannot be distinguished.

No public traction is normal at pre-seed, so I assign the stated missing-evidence prior rather than treating silence as failed demand. The same applies to founder motivation: building a two-person robotics lab is directionally committed, but the dossier documents neither costly sacrifice nor prior-startup learning, and credentials cannot substitute for that evidence. The central problem is not missing revenue. It is that the company has not exposed the one proposition whose validation would make revenue likely.

I would pass today. The verifiable flip condition is a named industrial customer repeatedly deploying a Most Robotic system that beats an open-Cosmos baseline on a specified task while producing proprietary feedback data unavailable to competitors. That single result would define the wedge, demonstrate pull, and show that model improvement deepens an owned workflow rather than commoditizing it.
