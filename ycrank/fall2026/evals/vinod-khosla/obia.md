# Obia -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Obia could make industrial site selection cheaper by automating scarce expertise: interpreting zoning, reconciling operating requirements, and identifying usable properties. That matters when a bad recommendation means leasing a building the customer cannot operate. But the consequence demonstrated here stops at research assistance. The $292.6 billion brokerage-and-management proxy includes spending Obia does not address, and the dossier supplies no baseline connecting research hours saved to dramatically cheaper transactions or greater industrial capacity. I cannot price an extraordinary outcome from that number.

The most useful evidence is the company-reported logistics engagement: zoning screening, site-combination analysis, and throughput-per-rent comparisons. Those are concrete operational questions. They deserve more attention than the CoreNet booth. However, the engagement supplies no measured improvement in accuracy, elapsed time, or labor against conventional brokerage. Crexi already offers document extraction and market reporting; Tandem combines AI with office representation. Obia’s industrial scope could demand deeper capability, but scope alone does not establish a technical discontinuity. Replicable AI functionality is a live competitive concern; the dossier does not establish that Obia merely wraps a standard model.

The strongest bull case is that representation lets Obia capture transaction economics while learning from actual customer requirements. Automating research could let four people serve searches that conventional brokers find uneconomic. Repeated transactions could then produce proprietary evidence about which properties actually satisfy operational constraints. My unstated preference for execution-heavy marketplaces makes that possibility relevant. My exceptions also permit an initially ordinary product when a distinctive team can expand into a consequential existing value pool. Here, however, accumulated search requirements have no disclosed scale or exclusivity, and completed-deal feedback remains an inferred future advantage. The exception is conceivable, not evidenced.

Samuel Cho’s Amazon and research-lab background supports engineering competence; Jason Henkel’s conversational-AI work is relevant to intake. Neither establishes rapid belief revision or recruiting complementary people to resolve a specific weakness. Those dimensions receive the missing-evidence defaults. The same applies to risk sequencing: Obia’s terms acknowledge unreliable third-party information, but the dossier contains no decisive experiment, error threshold, budget, or stop condition. CoreNet sponsorship alone does not establish the red flag of scaling before resolving that risk.

The economics need equal discipline. “Free to tenants” identifies who pays; it says nothing about production cost. Landlord-paid commissions could support attractive margins, but unpublished fees and retained human tours and negotiations leave the cost curve unmeasured. There is no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, and no basis for crediting unsubsidized cost superiority. The contracting-entity discrepancy also needs resolution before I put personal money into the business.

I pass. Obia has a plausible service business and an interesting industrial wedge, but the evidence does not yet establish an option on radically cheaper expertise. My verifiable flip condition would be an independently audited cohort of 30 completed industrial searches demonstrating at least a tenfold reduction in total broker labor per transaction—including verification, tours, and negotiations—without a higher material zoning-error rate than conventional brokerage. That would change the mechanism I am underwriting.
