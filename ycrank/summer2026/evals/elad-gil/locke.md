# Locke -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Parth Badhwar’s White House, campaign, Deloitte policy, and Clipbook GTM experience makes Locke a catalyst-rich but distribution-poor government-affairs bet. Locke is skating toward a real puck—the rise in organizations lobbying Washington on AI from 158 in 2022 to 451 in 2023—but Locke currently looks more like an AI-enabled services firm than an index on regulatory complexity. That high-catalyst/low-index pairing is the analytical signature: the window may be open, while the business model captures it one retainer at a time.

**Anduril** is the high rung on market re-opening: Ukraine exposed a procurement failure, and Anduril already occupied the software path through defense modernization. Locke shares the recent-catalyst mechanism because AI companies expanded their use of outside lobbying firms during 2025, while policy volume increasingly overwhelms manual monitoring. Locke diverges because the catalyst primarily increases demand for lobbying rather than making Locke’s architecture newly possible; Quorum, FiscalNote, Plural, Polintel, and Sporos can all apply the same models. Locke therefore sits below Anduril but above a steady-state consultancy: a credible reopening, without exclusive access to the window.

**Lyft** exposed the low-rung index failure: being a credible participant in a growing category is not enough when value compounds toward a better-positioned platform. Locke shows the same structural problem in softer form. Quorum already serves more than 2,000 organizations, FiscalNote has $84.1 million of ARR, and Locke’s consultation-led model requires Locke to win each engagement through relationships and human delivery. Locke does not yet become automatically more valuable whenever a new regulation, RFP, or AI-policy dispute appears; today the category compounds faster than Locke’s distribution.

**FiscalNote’s $84.1 million of ARR** illustrates how revenue can compound once policy data is packaged into repeatable subscriptions, whereas Locke discloses only “first few partners” and a growing pipeline with no customer names, contract values, or repeat usage. Over 18 months, Locke’s revenue velocity will depend on whether briefs, stakeholder maps, and proposals become standardized software outputs or remain labor-intensive deliverables supported by hires earning $72,000–$200,000 and $100,000–$200,000. Locke has no visible curve, so “first few partners” remains a false signal until renewals or monthly growth demonstrate that Locke is just working.

**Parth Badhwar’s policy career** establishes domain fit but provides no evidence of the Perplexity pattern—specific artifacts shipped, tested with users, and rebuilt between conversations. Locke’s two job openings and functioning service menu show activity, but Locke does not document what Badhwar personally built at Clipbook, what product iterations the first partners caused, or whether the second founder supplies technical execution. The score flips into invest territory if Locke can show a sequence of shipped workflow changes tied to repeat partner behavior; absent that evidence, I apply the shipping default rather than converting credentials into execution.

**Color Genomics** is the useful single-anchor parallel: Color placed workflow and data infrastructure on top of commodity sequencing rather than requiring new chemistry. Locke similarly assumes that foundation models already work and adds monitoring, citation-backed briefs, stakeholder mapping, and human lobbying around them. Locke’s remaining risks—AI citation accuracy, Lobbying Disclosure Act compliance, and relationship-based delivery—are execution constraints rather than three independent scientific miracles. Locke passes the single-miracle test reasonably well, although combining a trustworthy software product, a regulated lobbying practice, and a scalable service operation is less clean than Color’s infrastructure abstraction.

**Quorum’s 2,000-organization installed base** makes my 36-month forecast that Locke reaches roughly $2 million–$5 million in annualized revenue as a technology-enabled government-affairs boutique, but does not become the default policy platform and either remains services-heavy or sells to a larger public-affairs firm. Locke can monetize the catalyst through retainers because Badhwar has direct policy relationships and Locke already claims initial partners. Locke’s low-index position caps the outcome: every new engagement adds delivery load unless the underlying workflow becomes a product that customers adopt without founder-led consultation.

**Locke’s first few partners** support the strongest bull case: Badhwar has unusually direct founder-market fit, AI lobbying demand has nearly tripled from the 2022 baseline, and Locke could use service delivery to accumulate proprietary stakeholder, outreach, and contracting-outcome data before Quorum or FiscalNote can reorganize around an AI-native operating model. That is the Color or Decagon path—start inside the workflow, then turn accumulated execution into infrastructure. The case fails today because Locke discloses no proprietary dataset, named customer, revenue, retention, or moat while five competitors already market AI policy workflows. I would flip to Invest if Locke reaches $100,000 ARR from a standardized product while sustaining more than 20% month-over-month growth for six consecutive months.
