# ObsessionDB -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

ObsessionDB has already built something customers use in production. What am I being offered to own it? The dossier supplies no post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or evidence that this is the first scarce check. It also names no experiment the round finances, with a deadline and a measurable result. Neither omission proves an auction or a financing treadmill. Both leave my primary gates unresolved: 6/28 for terms and 6/24 for the venture moment. A functioning database does not, by itself, tell me whether my money buys an attractive risk.

The founders are the strongest affirmative evidence. Aviles, Höffl, and Leuthardt worked together at Numia, and ObsessionDB productizes infrastructure developed there. They have operated the demanding customer workload and built the infrastructure underneath it. Aviles reports a previous business reaching $1.5 million ARR and five billion monthly API requests; those are founder receipts, not ObsessionDB revenue. This is the outsider-insider bridge I look for, with shipped work rather than résumé inference. It earns 9/10.

Their access has also produced a concrete customer action. Numia moved 200 TB from BigQuery and reported monthly workload costs falling from approximately $8,000 to $2,000 in the [June case study](https://obsessiondb.com/blog/how-numia-cut-costs-75-percent-bigquery). Production API workloads provide further evidence that the product serves an operating need. I give the owned-workflow dimension 16/20: the originating relationship and workload knowledge helped establish a real deployment. The limitation is whether that access travels beyond the founders’ existing ecosystem. Proprietary SharedMergeTree and cache routing represent engineering, but the query engine remains ClickHouse’s, and the upstream supplier competes directly. Customer-specific operating knowledge could compound; exclusive access has not been established.

Now delete the next financing. Can ObsessionDB support these workloads profitably? The indicative $4,090 monthly configuration tells me what a customer might pay, not what remains after compute, storage, support, and migration work. Numia’s savings belong to Numia; they do not establish the vendor’s margin. Without burn, runway, or contribution economics, this dimension stays at its 7/18 default. Unsupported native backup and restore operations and inconsistent encryption disclosures add specific questions about support costs and enterprise adoption. They do not prove an uneconomic business.

The strongest objection to passing is substantial: these founders turned their own infrastructure problem into proprietary software, won paying production usage, and demonstrated material customer savings. That fits the pattern of funding an overlooked operating system, and it weakens the generic AI-application objection—the product has utility even if the agent narrative disappears. But the known exceptions require unusually hard evidence that overrides a missing gate. Here, deployment evidence is stronger than the evidence for durable customer conversion, cash generation, or financing structure. A named CEO also gives me no basis to invent an equal-founder governance problem. The unresolved issue is underwriting the check.

I pass on the evidence supplied. ObsessionDB could become a useful infrastructure business while still being a poor personal investment at the offered price. My one verifiable flip condition is a documented round below $10 million post-money whose contracted customer revenue and cost schedule show that this check funds cash break-even within 18 months. That would connect price, a finite capital test, and survival without another raise.
