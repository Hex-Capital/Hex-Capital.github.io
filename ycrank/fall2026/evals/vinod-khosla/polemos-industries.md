# Polemos Industries -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Polemos Industries wants to remove a real constraint: armed forces can identify more targets than they can afford to engage. If its manufacturing approach radically reduces precision-strike costs, the consequence could be substantial. But “cheap enough to field in numbers” supplies neither today’s cost baseline nor the proposed reduction. The cited $44.3 billion precision-guided-munition market measures spending across a broader category. It does not establish how much capability Polemos unlocks per dollar. I cannot price the upside simply by attaching a large market to an affordability claim.

The strongest case for Polemos is that the breakthrough could reside in production rather than exotic technology. Short-range ballistic missiles, compact launchers, existing reconnaissance drones, and existing production lines describe a potentially practical way to expand supply. This fits my unstated preference for rebuilding physical infrastructure through compatibility with what already exists. Long qualification cycles and manufacturing difficulty are acceptable risks. However, the dossier establishes the proposed configuration, not its advantage. There is no measured comparison against a named incumbent, and Ares also emphasizes simplified manufacturing. Different propulsion and launch configurations distinguish products; they do not establish superior economics.

The March 2026 agreement to quadruple PrSM production capacity strengthens the argument that munition supply matters to buyers. It establishes no procurement demand for Polemos specifically. Likewise, October 23 blog coverage is attention, not customer validation. I attach no negative weight to a three-person pre-seed team having no disclosed revenue or open jobs. What remains unknown is whether its next dollar buys evidence against the largest uncertainty or merely advances the product. The dossier contains no founder-owned risk-removal plan, budget, decisive threshold, or response to failure. Export authorization is another unresolved dependency, not evidence of regulatory misconduct.

Founder information could materially change this assessment. Names and backgrounds are unavailable, so I cannot credit exceptional learning, complementary recruiting, or demonstrated willingness to revise a plan. My known exceptions allow investment before commercial milestones when an extraordinary instigator or a quantified, enormous consequence justifies purchasing the option. Neither is documented here. Conversely, Polemos shows no affirmative evidence of exit obsession, rejection of contrary evidence, or premature spending on organizational scale. Missing information receives the stated defaults; it does not become a founder indictment.

I pass with my own money on the available evidence. The decisive gap is the connection between existing production lines and repeatable economic advantage: no unit costs, yields, utilization assumptions, or manufacturing agreements establish that connection. My one verifiable condition for reopening the decision is an independently audited, customer-accepted production lot demonstrating repeatable delivered-cost advantage against a named incumbent alternative at comparable customer requirements. That would turn Polemos’s central proposition into evidence. Today, I have a consequential demand pool and an unverified mechanism for serving it.
