# RouterPlus -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: a small developer buys reliable agent execution without becoming an expert in model pricing, GPU availability, and supplier negotiations. I can inhabit that future. RouterPlus would need to turn workload evaluations into dependable purchasing decisions, then accumulate enough procurement power that providers compete for its customers. The dossier establishes today’s procurement service; it does not establish that compounding path. I won’t award my imagined future to the company as evidence.

Why does the customer need a broker? RouterPlus identifies real friction: different provider prices, peak-capacity failures, and engineers arranging their own backups. Provider bidding and one API could return time and purchasing control to developers. But there is no quantified procurement markup, contractual restriction, or demonstrated saving showing precisely which rent disappears. Prepaid token pricing and separately contracted reserved capacity explain how customers buy; they do not yet show how much economic power changes hands.

The founders make this considerably more interesting than an interchangeable routing interface. Lakshmi Narayana spent four years building Samsung’s ML-platform GPU infrastructure and previously served as Trupeer’s first hire and CTO. Alok Bishoyi worked on edge-AI deployment, and the earlier EVO repository has approximately 1,500 GitHub stars. Those are relevant operating experiences and a public building artifact. The May–June autoresearch updates, August router, and October RouterPlus launch show iteration, although they do not establish a long-running, independently derived obsession with inference procurement. I see technical tuition; I have less evidence of the commercial command needed to run this marketplace.

The strongest objection to passing is that production customers may already be pulling these founders toward the important problem. RouterPlus reports that its largest customer runs three million agent conversations daily, and a founder describes a dedicated deployment reducing one application’s P95 latency from 4.8 seconds to under four. Entrusting production performance to a young team is meaningful behavior. But the first figure describes the customer’s activity without establishing RouterPlus’s share, and the second lacks a named customer or sustained measurement period. I give those reports credit without converting them into independently verified adoption. The prior product’s 10,000 optimized repositories do not belong in that calculation.

Supplier procurement fits my less obvious preference for the machinery beneath a visible application wave. Forty-plus reported provider relationships also make the single-closed-supplier objection less applicable. Yet access is different from controlling a scarce dependency: no exclusivity, secured capacity advantage, or demonstrated proprietary matching advantage is established. OpenRouter already offers customer-specific evaluations and dedicated deployments. RouterPlus could become valuable precisely because these negotiations are difficult, but the dossier has not shown why that value will accumulate here rather than remain contestable brokerage work.

I pass at this evidence level. Missing revenue is not the reason: my exceptions allow a pre-revenue check when a working artifact and unusually strong founder history make the destination tangible. Here, that combination is only partly established. CEO and CTO responsibilities are identified, with no documented governance contradiction, but nonconfidential intake and indefinite retention of de-identified requirements leave a concrete custody question; they do not establish a severe privacy violation by themselves. My one flip condition is a referenceable customer renewal showing that RouterPlus’s contracted capacity access delivered sustained price and peak-availability advantages that the customer could not obtain through OpenRouter. That would turn supplier relationships into evidence of an enabling asset.
