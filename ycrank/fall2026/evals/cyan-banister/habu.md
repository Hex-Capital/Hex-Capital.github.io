# HABU -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a small development team turning overlooked parcels into apartments because underwriting, design, procurement, and construction decisions finally talk to one another. HABU’s connected operating system points toward that world. But what has escaped from the future into Phoenix today? The dossier describes automated workflows without a completed project showing the transition from faster paperwork to more housing. That missing connection matters more to me than the size of multifamily capital flows.

Why does an apartment project require the claimed 1,000 hours of desk work? Which hours reconcile incompatible documents, and which represent someone accepting responsibility for a consequential decision? HABU could eliminate substantial coordination waste. Yet permits, architects, and builders still determine whether a project moves. Its fee-development model gives investors someone accountable for delivery, but the dossier does not identify a specific gatekeeper’s rent that disappears or establish that savings reach residents. Institutional investors are the immediate beneficiaries; broader housing agency remains a possibility.

Bryan Tharalson gives this more substance than an automation pitch assembled from outside the industry. His reported management of $80 million-plus in development projects is relevant operating experience, although the separate $310 million-plus figure needs its scope clarified. Being Mutiny’s fifth employee and first sales hire also suggests experience selling before a company has much machinery around it. Junhao Chen brings AI operations and Navy aviation logistics experience. These are complementary backgrounds for coordinating messy systems. They do not yet establish the dated experiments, independent obsession, or recruited believers that would make me follow these founders into a difficult six-year journey.

The strongest argument against passing is that HABU can learn by being the developer. Selling software leaves adoption and execution with somebody else; accepting delivery responsibility could expose the team to every expensive exception. That fits my attraction to mundane workflow repair and to businesses whose initial service conceals a deeper infrastructure opportunity. The no-revenue exception is relevant here: a functioning prototype and committed counterparties can be enough. But this dossier supplies neither documented project use nor a signed development mandate. Listed backers establish investor interest, not that exception.

I also cannot turn the claimed 90% automation, 95% faster processing, and 10%–15% lower project costs into product evidence without supporting results. They are unverified claims, not demonstrated falsehoods. TestFit and Mosaic show that feasibility automation and software-assisted operations already have alternative implementations. HABU must establish what it controls that those alternatives cannot readily provide. A proprietary internal system is a starting point; an exclusive learning asset beneath multiple development workflows has not been demonstrated. The dossier identifies a CEO and CTO and establishes no governance or severe privacy violation, so those red flags do not drive my decision.

I would pass with my own money today. My flip condition is an independently documented repeat mandate from an investor after a completed Phoenix project, showing that HABU’s controlled workflow produced the claimed cost savings while preserving quality and earning a development margin. That would connect the software to physical outcomes and costly customer belief. Until then, I see relevant operators pursuing a credible efficiency business, with the larger enabling layer still unproven.
