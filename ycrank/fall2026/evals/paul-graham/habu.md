# HABU -- Paul Graham Evaluation

What did Bryan Tharalson do when a development project got stuck? That is the question I would ask first. He has managed more than $80 million in development projects, according to his [founder profile](https://www.ycombinator.com/founders/bryan-tharalson). That puts him close to real problems. But HABU’s dossier contains no specific obstacle, founder-built workaround, or elapsed time. I cannot turn the dollar value of projects he managed into evidence of resourcefulness. There is also no documented rejected hypothesis and subsequent product change. Both judgments remain at their missing-evidence defaults.

The strongest case for HABU is that these founders may understand work that software founders overlook. Tharalson combines development experience with being Mutiny’s first sales hire. Chen brings AI operations experience from Meta and aviation logistics experience from the Navy. Their decision to perform development themselves could let them discover which pieces are worth automating. Permits, contractor coordination, and design review are precisely the sort of unpleasant work that can conceal an opportunity. This earns credit for a grounded, schlep-heavy wedge, though the dossier never identifies the particular incident that caused them to start HABU.

The decisive question is whether their software changes what they can deliver. HABU describes an internal system spanning sourcing, underwriting, diligence, construction tracking, and disposition. Its [YC page](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/habu) claims over 90% task automation, five times more projects per employee, and 10–15% lower project costs. Those would be consequential results. But no project-level measurements establish them. Faster document processing saves money only to the extent that it removes work or changes decisions on the actual project. TestFit already sells feasibility automation, and Mosaic combines software with construction operations. HABU needs evidence that taking responsibility for the whole job produces an advantage. A described architecture leaves technical proof at its default.

Phoenix Class A multifamily investors supplying $5 million–$20 million equity checks are a specific prospective customer group. The dossier identifies no investor who has commissioned a project, returned, or paid HABU. Its listed backers establish financing interest; its 271 LinkedIn followers establish little about demand. The fee/merchant model could become a good business, but undisclosed fees and project economics leave the path to exceptional equity returns unresolved. My unstated preference for concrete proof before a round therefore remains unmet. The launch, investor introductions, and planned hiring do not establish the corresponding red flags: there is no documented dependence on one partner or premature delegation by the founders.

I would pass at **32/100**. The bull case has substance: experienced operators may be building the machinery while outsiders see only an ordinary developer. But the known-exception tests require a working prototype, independently validated proof, or unmistakable user pull strong enough to replace a missing prior. None is documented here. My operational gate fails on founder resourcefulness and on both technical proof and demand. One verifiable flip condition would be a documented paid Phoenix project where a named blocker led to a founder-shipped workaround within two weeks, followed by the same investor commissioning a second project. That would show the founders learning under resistance and a customer wanting the result again.
