# Datoric -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders learned this problem by attacking it. Nikhil Reddy automated more than 15,000 hours of annotation work by bypassing platform QA systems in 2023; Jeffrey Lin worked in ML and robotics and co-authored Datoric’s procedural-video benchmark. They have since turned that knowledge into private capture systems, a 250,000-trace computer-use dataset, roughly 15,000 hours of screen activity, and nearly seven figures of revenue in 30 days. Specific knowledge is visible in the artifacts and transactions, not inferred from credentials.

The revenue matters because it is realized value capture rather than an LOI or a free-user proxy. But one impressive month is not yet a business model. Datoric discloses neither customer count nor pricing, repeat purchases, gross margin, or revenue concentration. Nearly $1 million from several recurring lab customers would be powerful; the same amount from one bespoke collection would describe a project business with a temporarily large invoice.

If a competitor copies the software, Datoric keeps a network of more than 300,000 active contributors, project-specific collection experience, and sample-level consent and provenance records. Those are real assets. What is not yet proven is that they compound: the dossier provides no contributor exclusivity, qualification history, customer retention, or evidence that each completed project makes the next collection materially faster or better. Scale AI, Turing, Mercor, and Prolific already possess larger operating networks. Private apps are architecture; accumulated proprietary judgment is a moat.

The wedge has room to maneuver. The same rights and capture system can serve voice, video, computer-use, robotics, and world-model teams inside a training-dataset market projected to grow from $3.2 billion in 2025 to $16.3 billion by 2033. The 250,000-trace repository proves that computer-use data is more than a slide. But Datoric is not yet a self-reinforcing network: contributors produce inventory for customers, while each new engagement still appears to require specifications, collection, and QA from the company. A large supply base creates capacity; it does not automatically create leverage.

The bear case is sharp. Two founders moved from the announced Arzule product in Winter 2026 to Datoric in Summer 2026, sell labor-intensive data into a field dominated by capitalized incumbents, and disclose no continuity between the products or customers. More seriously, “bypassing” annotation-platform QA may represent unusually valuable adversarial knowledge—or conduct that harmed another principal. Integrity is not a score adjustment; it is a veto. The unstated pattern is only partially present: outsiders contribute useful work, but the system is private and managed rather than demonstrably permissionless, and no participant-to-participant loop has been observed.

I would invest a bounded seed check. The combination of exact-problem action, deployed technical artifacts, 300,000 contributors, and immediate revenue is too information-dense to dismiss as another AI wrapper. My 36-month base case is $40–80 million ARR, still partly services-heavy, with the outcome determined by whether provenance and contributor-performance records become proprietary infrastructure rather than operational paperwork. One verifiable condition flips the decision: evidence that the 2023 QA bypass involved deception or uncompensated harm to platforms, workers, or customers would make this an immediate pass.
