# Vernius Systems, Inc. -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Cheap interceptors are produced by many airframe manufacturers, but they cannot depend on a human pilot visually finding a jet-powered target in the final kilometer. The important layer is the seeker that connects a distributed cueing network to terminal guidance. Vernius Systems has found a small leak from that future: 45 units sold to special forces, an Archimedes 1A integrated with Ukraine’s STING interceptor, and a frontline field test completed within an eight-week build cycle. The dossier does not fully map the intervening six years—production scale, electronic-warfare resilience, and integration standards remain unnamed—but this is more than a fashionable defense story looking for a product.

The irritating system is unusually concrete. Existing radar seekers can cost six figures, while manually piloted FPV interceptors must visually acquire targets that are increasingly too fast for that workflow. Vernius moves range and bearing onto an adaptable onboard module, potentially giving interceptor builders a capability previously confined to expensive missile architectures. I understand the rent and the constraint. I am less certain who captures the savings: without disclosed pricing, manufacturing cost, or procurement structure, the company has not yet shown whether it owns the economics or merely performs bespoke integration work.

This team has paid relevant operating tuition. Arthur Nguyen-cao brings more than six years of RF PCB work and led 14 engineers at Defense Tech for Ukraine; Joe Gonzalez architected secure Tomahawk-fleet communications at Sandia; Arthur and Hisham El-Halabi had already built a company together. Those biographies are not ornamental credentials. They connect directly to the artifact the three founders produced and fielded. The missing piece is a dated record of seeker-specific obsession predating Vernius, plus evidence that one leader can make the final call. Three executive titles are clear; governance and control are not.

The strongest bear case is painful because the headline numbers could be telling two different stories. Forty-five sold units and one field test indicate real behavior, but $500 million of 2027 LOIs is wildly ahead of disclosed deliveries, pricing, and independently verified performance. Detection ranges are company-reported, autonomous operation still depends on an external cueing network and a terminal-vision handoff, and Cambridge Aerospace, Mara, MatrixSpace, and Echodyne are attacking adjacent pieces with substantially more capital. Vernius could become an integration shop squeezed between airframe vendors and sensor suppliers rather than the standard beneath them.

I still lean invest. The automated seeker-engineering pipeline, eight-week prototype cycle, modular 24 GHz hardware, and direct work with frontline operators make this an enabling-layer bet rather than a thin application riding current defense enthusiasm. The users have spent money and placed hardware into a consequential environment; that is costly behavior, not a waitlist. My flip condition is specific: if Vernius cannot produce an independently documented repeat order within twelve months that includes successful operation in realistic clutter or jamming conditions, I would withdraw the invest judgment.
