# RATLOG -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

RATLOG’s proposed secret is that rotating an engine to pressurize propellant can eliminate the turbopump without surrendering the performance and reliability required for reusable heavy lift. What would prove that wrong? Bearing loads, engine mass, or operating life that consume the savings from fewer components. The reported hot-fire—achieved in four months for under $30,000—makes this a technical proposition worth examining. It does not establish that the proposed 90% reduction in engine parts survives flight conditions. Nor does the dossier identify an opposing engineering consensus that this experiment has overturned.

The strongest evidence concerns the people pursuing that proposition. Weise, Kunhart, and Kelm previously worked through Innuendo, with responsibilities spanning propulsion and electronics. That is evidence of a team formed around actual work rather than assembled around a fundraising opportunity. Driesnack brings operating experience from Lio. I give the founders credit for their shared technical history; full-time status, ownership, and economic alignment remain unverified. University affiliations alone establish neither divided commitment nor exceptional commitment.

The business question is harder: what small market could RATLOG actually own? A 20-tonne launcher addresses a substantial capability, but payload capacity is not a beachhead. Satellite operators, space stations, and orbital data centers describe several customer populations with different requirements. Stoke is already pursuing full reusability, while Isar illustrates that European launch diversification has attracted substantial financing. RATLOG has not yet demonstrated exclusive access, a protected customer segment, or a measured operating advantage that would let it retain the value its engine might create. Fewer parts could produce a monopoly mechanism; the component count itself does not establish one.

The $400 million in signed launch reservations is the strongest objection to passing. Together with more than 70 customer discussions, it suggests the founders have found buyers willing to engage before an orbital vehicle exists. That matters disproportionately at pre-seed. But reservations with undisclosed payment and cancellation terms cannot establish the economics of delivery. Direct launch-planning calls constitute a real acquisition route; their repeatability remains unknown. The proposed per-kilogram business is coherent, but without numerical pricing, launch costs, or reuse assumptions, I cannot derive a power-law outcome from its unit economics. The absence of current revenue is not the problem.

RATLOG fits my preference for neglected physical frontiers, although the dossier does not show the concentrated operator network behind some such company formations. The relevant red flag is coordination: propulsion, vehicle integration, recovery, financing, and launch-site access must converge, with Oman and UAE arrangements still exploratory. The strongest applicable exception is Panthalassa: both pursue an unusual physical architecture against an immediate capacity constraint, but that exception included a specific pilot-factory and deployment path. RATLOG’s inexpensive hot-fire validates an important first step; it does not yet resolve the route from engine experiment to delivered launch capacity. There is no documented technical retraction or founder misconduct here—only a substantial gap between the demonstrated unit and the commercial system.

I would pass on writing a check today. This is a credible founding team investigating a potentially important natural secret, with ownership of the resulting market still unproved. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an independently witnessed, full-duration test of a flight-representative engine demonstrating the claimed component reduction while meeting disclosed thrust, mass, and repeated-operation targets. That would test whether RATLOG has removed complexity or merely relocated it.
