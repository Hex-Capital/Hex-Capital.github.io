# Helios Intelligence -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Abudayyeh and Gootenberg have acquired specific knowledge through work that is expensive to fake. They have built molecular-diagnostics ventures, and both co-authored the May 2026 mAge research integrating proteomics, wearables, and mortality outcomes. That matters more than their academic titles. Helios Intelligence starts with founders who understand the underlying science. It does not yet establish that the resulting scientific advantage belongs to this company.

Copy the dashboard, the integrations, and the recommendations. What remains? Potentially, an assay measuring 1,000+ biomarkers from a home-collected blood sample. That would make the physical measurement layer the scarce asset. But the dossier supplies neither product-specific validation nor company-exclusive rights. The mAge paper’s reported 21% reduction in mortality-prediction error supports the founders’ relevant research experience; it does not validate Helios’s collection method or establish a proprietary commercial model. I give the founders credit for their work without awarding the company an asset it has not demonstrated.

The market has more substance than the moat. Function’s estimated $100 million annualized revenue in February 2025 and Superpower’s reported $52 million in July 2026 indicate that consumers already spend on preventive testing. Those figures are competitor evidence, with different levels of verification, rather than Helios traction. Repeated molecular monitoring could expand the frequency and usefulness of that spending, but the dossier does not establish a path into additional buyers or transactions. Likewise, combining customers’ measurements could eventually improve predictions for everyone. Without contribution rights, outcome linkage, or measured improvement, that remains a proposed feedback loop.

Roon gives Helios a live consumer storefront and an advertised subscription price starting at $9.16 per tin. It establishes an offer, not realized revenue or a demonstrated financing engine for molecular research. The strongest bull case is that these founders use a simple consumer business to finance a difficult measurement platform, then compound longitudinal biological data. That would fit my preference for infrastructure bottlenecks. The uncomfortable alternative is two businesses competing for attention: supplement acquisition requires ongoing marketing, while assay development requires patient scientific validation. Neither a permissionless contribution mechanism nor customer-created distribution is demonstrated.

I am neutral at this evidence level. My exceptions for early products depend on something concrete already working exceptionally well—a prototype, unusually efficient execution, or self-reinforcing user behavior. The dossier establishes scientific depth, but not those overrides. It also contains no documented integrity violation, artificial financing deadline, or reassurance-seeking behavior; missing product evidence is not a founder red flag. One verifiable result would change my decision: independent validation of a Helios-controlled assay reproducing the claimed 1,000+ biomarker measurements across realistic home-collection and shipping conditions. That would turn the central advantage from a research possibility into a company asset.
