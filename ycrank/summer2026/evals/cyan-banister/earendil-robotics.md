# Earendil Robotics -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A small infantry unit cannot wait for a distant radar operator to classify every cheap drone overhead; its soldiers see the threat through worn sensors, launch an interceptor locally, and keep moving without exposing themselves. Earendil Robotics has a credible leak from that future: Russia launched roughly 6,500 one-way attack drones in March 2026, while NATO exercises are already testing interceptor counter-UAS systems. The causal chain—rising drone volume, decentralized detection, edge classification, then autonomous interception—is visible. What remains unproven is whether Earendil owns the critical step rather than merely assembling today’s available components.

The hardware is more than a rendering. IVAS combines night vision, thermal imaging, laser ranging, and edge AI; Aeglos claims autonomous locking above 300 km/h; and the team exhibited at DPRTE Farnborough alongside development work involving Vulcan Threat and Risk Management, Heckler & Koch UK, and Steel Core Group. But a booth and a collaborative showcase are not costly user behavior. There is no independent intercept result, deployed unit, paid trial, repeat operator use, or disclosed dataset. The most important artifact is therefore still company-published performance, not a soldier choosing to trust the system twice.

James Walton and Dan Komorny are the strongest part of the case. Walton was a British Army UAS specialist with Ukraine experience and previously founded two IT or telecom companies; Komorny brings a decade across drones, security, AR, optics, and payload hardware. That is an unusually coherent pairing for this product. It is not yet proof of pre-company obsession, however: the dossier supplies no dated interceptor builds, field experiments, recruitment history, or prior artifact connecting their separate careers to Earendil’s specific architecture. I see earned domain intuition, but not yet the first-believer trail that tells me they found this system before the market made counter-drone defense fashionable.

The bull case is the pre-revenue exception: a functioning physical prototype, domain-obsessed founders, and institutional proofs can outweigh missing revenue, as they did with **CosmicBrain AI**. The shared mechanism is expert founders turning an urgent industrial need into working hardware. The material difference is that CosmicBrain had signed proofs of concept and a documented lifetime of building; Earendil currently has an exhibition, collaborators, and unverified specifications. I cannot convert proximity to defense institutions into product validation without seeing who operated Aeglos, under what conditions, and what happened.

The hidden-system economics are also incomplete. Earendil identifies the operational failure—small units face massed drones while defenses are layered and centralized—but does not quantify the response delay, per-engagement cost, procurement bottleneck, or agency transferred to the squad. Nor is this yet the concealed enabling layer beneath the wave. Anduril, Fortem, MARSS, and Epirus already cover autonomous interceptors or counter-swarm defeat; Earendil is presenting another vertically integrated system, not a scarce component several downstream platforms must buy. My barbell pattern likes difficult physical infrastructure, but this sits in today’s hottest defense category without a demonstrated controlled bottleneck. UK export licensing and dependence on opaque procurement compound that concern, although I see no disclosed privacy, founder-control, or leadership-shell-game red flag.

My 36-month base case is that Earendil becomes a paid subsystem or integration supplier to a UK or NATO prime at $1 million–$4 million in annual revenue, rather than winning as an independent small-unit defense platform. I would not write the check today, but I would keep the door open. The flip condition is verifiable: an independently documented live intercept exercise with a named allied military unit, followed by a paid repeat order for the same configuration. That would prove both the artifact and the institutional agency shift that are currently inferred.
