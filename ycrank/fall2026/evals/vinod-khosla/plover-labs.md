# Plover Labs -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Removing human approval could change the economics of software production. Plover Labs has demonstrated something narrower: agents completing engineering tasks inside a customer’s workflow. The difference determines whether I am buying an option on abundant software or another enterprise productivity tool. The dossier does not quantify engineering spending displaced, review hours eliminated, or the population newly able to build software. I will entertain an improbable breakthrough. I still need a consequence I can calculate.

The strongest evidence is Fieldguide’s evaluation: 37 of 40 historical tasks produced merge-ready results, with a 28-minute median completion time. That is useful technical evidence at pre-seed. But production review and some browser and document checks were excluded, and human review remains the final gate. The customer’s 40% increase in total merged PR throughput is observational; the separate company-reported tripling of PRs per engineer is not a controlled comparison. Neither establishes a 5–10x discontinuity against human-reviewed development. Sonar already supplies deterministic semantic checks. Plover must show what its accumulated conventions and verifiers can establish that those checks cannot.

The bull case deserves more than a polite concession. Envoy and Fieldguide reportedly signed five-figure monthly contracts. Customers are spending money, and Fieldguide documents a feedback loop that turns failures into regression cases, Blueprints, and environment definitions. I back execution-heavy businesses as well as scientific breakthroughs; customer-specific learning can make an ordinary-looking wedge become infrastructure. Plover could accumulate the evidence required to remove human approval while competitors keep selling assistance. But the dossier establishes useful accumulated artifacts, not that those artifacts reliably capture missing requirements or remain defensible against delivery platforms adding verification. Calling this merely a wrapper would overstate the evidence. Calling it autonomous shipping would do the same.

The next dollar should test whether the gates approve dangerous changes that a reviewer would reject. Historical task completion does not answer that question. There is no disclosed experiment with an acceptance threshold, cost, deadline, and stop-or-change response for removing human review. Edward Ahn’s CMU safe-reinforcement-learning research and Apple agent work provide relevant technical context, but they do not document rapid belief revision or complementary recruiting. Likewise, contract prices establish willingness to pay; they do not establish delivery cost, maintenance labor, or an unsubsidized advantage over human review. Those are missing facts, not evidence of bad founders or bad economics.

I pass with my own money. My exceptions for small execution teams attacking enormous existing spending pools remain relevant, but Plover has not yet connected its paid wedge to a quantified transformation. Nor does the dossier establish the extraordinary consequence that would justify overlooking both technical and economic uncertainty. I would reverse the decision after one independently audited production trial across at least 1,000 changes demonstrates fivefold lower fully loaded engineering cost per shipped change, with human pre-merge approval removed and no increase in severe escaped defects against a matched baseline. That would turn the central promise into evidence worth financing.
