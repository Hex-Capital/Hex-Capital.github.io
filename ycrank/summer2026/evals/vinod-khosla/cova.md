# Cova -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Cova’s pivot from selling AI software to operating a licensed Michigan provider makes Cova a high-learning-rate, low-technical-risk company. That pairing matters. Jordan Ibe and Ansh Tandon recognized that agencies were not merely missing software; fragmented intake, enrollment, insurance, and billing demanded an operator. But Cova has changed the organizational wrapper around home care without yet changing its underlying economics. The economic mechanism that breaks is agency administration, not caregiving itself. That is useful. It is not yet civilizational.

**Slash** is the high rung for learning rate because its documented Kanye West pivot exposed founders who metabolized an unexpected market signal and rebuilt the business around it. Cova shares the load-bearing mechanism: after a year building AI for home-care agencies and hospitals, Cova abandoned the easier software-vendor position and assumed licensure, caregiver management, payer compliance, and service delivery. Cova diverges because no customer behavior, revenue, or measured operating result proves that the provider pivot was learned from reality rather than inferred from frustration. A pivot is evidence of learning only when the new thesis produces a result the old thesis could not.

**HubSpot** represents the low-rung failure mechanism: commodity AI workflow automation becomes an incumbent feature before the startup accumulates proprietary advantage. Cova reports AI agents across intake, benefits coordination, enrollment, insurance, and billing, but Cova discloses no proprietary model, dataset, performance benchmark, or patent. Michigan’s electronic-visit-verification rules and the federal 80% direct-care compensation requirement create operational difficulty, not a scientific miracle. Consensus investors may dislike the margins, but they will not be repelled by an unsolved technical frontier. Regulatory inconvenience is not **Commonwealth Fusion** physics.

**$107.07 billion** of U.S. home-healthcare spending gives Cova a consequential substrate, but Cova’s mechanism compounds only if lower administrative cost recruits more caregivers, accelerates enrollment, and makes previously uneconomic patients serviceable. Over 18 months, each case could improve eligibility, scheduling, and billing automation; that data could then lower cost per case and widen access. The dossier supplies no case volume or measured cost advantage, however, while Michigan permits caregivers to enroll directly and Aidaly and Entyre already pursue family-caregiver compensation. At maximum disclosed ambition, Cova becomes a more efficient agency inside a $107 billion system. It does not yet make the incumbent system disappear.

**Jordan Ibe’s** VA medical-center exposure and Ansh Tandon’s bioengineering and data-science background support founder-market fit, while Cova’s decision to operate care rather than sell software contradicts the fashionable horizontal-AI playbook. Cova still has no documented position that experts said was impossible or economically irrational. The counterfactual that changes the score is precise: Cova must show that AI-operated agencies can comply with Michigan EVV requirements while cutting administrative cost by more than 50% and paying caregivers more under the 80% compensation rule. That would be a falsifiable assault on industry orthodoxy. Without it, Cova’s founders are thoughtful operators, not yet instigators.

**Commonwealth Fusion** required a $115 million Series A because capital depth was inseparable from proving the physics. Cova has no comparable capital mechanism: no plant, fleet, model-training program, drug pipeline, or stated requirement exceeding $50 million before a commercial milestone. Cova may consume working capital as Medicaid receivables and caregiver payroll scale, but financing reimbursement timing is not my capital edge. I would be over-resourced for Cova today, just as I would be for a conventional agency with better software.

**Michigan licensure** makes my 36-month forecast falsifiable: by July 2029, Cova will operate as a technology-enabled regional home-care provider in Michigan and one adjacent state, producing $6 million to $12 million in annualized service revenue, but Cova will not demonstrate a defensible standalone AI platform or a national winner-take-most moat. The learning-rate signal drives expansion; the low technical-risk signature caps differentiation. Cova can build a good business faster than it can build a defining technology company.

**DoorDash** is the strongest objection because DoorDash lacked a science miracle yet converted operational execution into a $72 billion outcome; my own unstated pattern includes capital-light consumer and fintech bets such as DoorDash, Affirm, Instacart, and Block. Cova could similarly turn caregiver liquidity, payer administration, and family trust into a distribution network, with the provider pivot functioning as its equivalent of owning delivery rather than selling restaurant software. The objection stings, but Cova currently has 227 LinkedIn followers, no disclosed revenue, no structural enrollment barrier, and an 80% Medicaid compensation constraint that leaves little room for administrative economics. I pass today. Cova would flip my verdict by reaching $10 million in annualized net revenue across at least two states while documenting positive contribution margins and a greater-than-50% administrative-cost advantage over traditional agencies.
