# FlowManual -- Paul Graham Evaluation

David Shijoon Bae and Michael Lin are Harvard-dropout roommates who began building FlowManual on April 18, 2026. FlowManual’s signature is a strong founder pair searching its way into someone else’s schlep: co-founder configuration is the highest signal, while organic founder pain is the lowest. Roommates can become animals together. But hundreds of contractor interviews are still evidence of learning a problem, not evidence that FlowManual’s founders had the problem themselves.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for founder configuration because the Collison brothers had years of shared history and had already built Auctomatic together. FlowManual has the important first half of that mechanism: Bae and Lin lived together, cover CEO and CTO roles, and both dropped out of Harvard. FlowManual diverges where the evidence becomes decisive. The dossier names no prior artifact they built together and does not establish a two-year working history. This is a promising pair, but not yet a Collison-like unit.

**Loopt** (2005) is the low rung for organic pain because founder force had to override a product whose infrastructure was not yet usable; the exception worked because Sam Altman was unusually forceful, not because the idea visibly emerged from his daily life. FlowManual’s construction knowledge appears to come from interviewing hundreds of contractors rather than from Bae or Lin working inside a contractor’s back office. FlowManual may have discovered a genuine wound, but discovering a wound is different from bleeding. No comparable Altman-style animal evidence justifies overriding that distinction.

$500,000 arrived from YC roughly one month after FlowManual began development, and FlowManual reportedly interviewed hundreds of contractors during that interval. Over the next 18 months, resourcefulness would compound if Bae and Lin personally annotate bad PDFs, reconcile invoices, and sit beside estimators until contractors refuse to work without them. The dossier instead names no paying customer, signed LOI, repeat user, or unscalable rescue. Building quickly earns credit. Animals leave clearer tracks than a funding announcement and 202 LinkedIn followers.

Document Crunch has raised at least $37 million around construction contract intelligence, while FlowManual also attempts bid comparison, purchasing history, invoice checking, takeoffs, and submittal review. FlowManual would move up a tier on the schlep dimension if Bae and Lin were personally recovering missed $5,000–$10,000 scope items for named contractors and absorbing the ugly onboarding required to earn access to contracts and vendor prices. AES-256-GCM encryption, human confirmation, and clause-level citations show that the schlep is real. They do not yet show that the founders, rather than the customer, are carrying it.

**Dropbox** (2007) had the hacker proof before the check: Drew Houston’s working demo reached Hacker News. FlowManual has a weaker version in Michael Lin’s IEEE IV 2025 paper and software internship associated with Dijie’s growth from $3,000 to $336,000 MRR. FlowManual has no public GitHub repositories, while Bae’s disclosed artifact is the educational project FilmEnvisioned. Technical credentials suggest they can build; they do not yet establish the hacker genealogy that makes me stop reading the application and watch the demo.

Hundreds of contractor interviews should push FlowManual toward a narrower product as users expose which back-office mistake hurts most. By July 2029, FlowManual will have narrowed from “AI for Construction’s Back Office” to bid-to-contract scope reconciliation for specialty contractors and reached $1 million–$3 million in ARR. FlowManual’s close founder pair can iterate fast enough to build a useful business, but the lack of lived construction pain will make customer behavior choose the wedge for them.

Michael Lin helped at a business reportedly growing from $3,000 to $336,000 MRR, and the strongest bull case is that FlowManual combines his shipping ability with Bae’s willingness to interview hundreds of contractors. Airbnb is the uncomfortable precedent: outsiders can look wrong until an animal act reveals that the founders matter more than the initial idea. FlowManual has not supplied its cereal-box moment—no revenue, named customer, repeated usage, or founder-run recovery of a costly scope error. Three named contractors each paying at least $25,000 annually and using FlowManual weekly to approve contract or invoice findings would flip my verdict to Invest.
