# Spine -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Spine’s decisive variable is service cost per covered employee. Rising insurance premiums give startup founders a reason to reconsider benefits, and carrier commissions let Spine offer operational support without charging an administration fee. This becomes a large technology business if support costs grow materially slower than commission revenue. Otherwise, customer growth also buys a proportionate increase in specialist workload.

The market mechanism is credible. Spine operates customers’ existing Rippling, Deel, Gusto, and ADP systems, removing a migration barrier while taking responsibility for enrollment, carrier coordination, and employee questions. But the approximately 78% contribution margin cited by Acadian is company-modeled. It supports a hypothesis about attractive economics; it does not demonstrate that AI has changed the cost of delivering brokerage services. The 6% increase in family-health premiums in 2025 strengthens the purchasing incentive without establishing a technical discontinuity.

Actual demand is the strongest evidence. Spine reports 30+ startup customers, and Acadian reports approximately 4.4× commission-revenue growth over six months through July 2026. Those are meaningful signals at this stage, although an undisclosed starting base limits what the growth multiple establishes. I exclude the consumer app’s 582 ratings from this assessment because they concern a different product. The conflicting 15% and 25% savings claims also prevent treating advertised savings as a reliable measure of customer value.

The strongest case for investing is that brokerage already supplies monetization, while operating the existing HR stack supplies distribution into additional workflows. The reported network of 50+ referral practitioners could make that channel more efficient. This fits my pattern of backing a first product that creates access for subsequent products. It also exposes the central objection: broader operational responsibility may increase labor faster than cross-selling increases revenue. Spine has documented actions and customer responsibilities beyond an AI interface, but the dossier does not establish proprietary workflow state, automated completion rates, or a feedback loop that competitors would find expensive to reproduce.

Onur Kardesler and Ece Cokar have built companies together before; Acadian describes Spine as their third shared company. That earns credit for startup experience and an established working relationship. Their engineering and legal backgrounds are relevant, but neither credentials nor repeat founding establishes exceptional commitment or successful lessons applied here. My willingness to fund businesses that reconstruct incumbent economics could justify this investment, but modeled margins do not yet qualify Spine for that exception. The frontier-research and scarce-model-talent exceptions also have no supporting evidence in this dossier.

I would monitor Spine. There is enough pull to take the business seriously, and insufficient evidence to underwrite technology-driven margin expansion with my own money. The risk is a successful brokerage whose operating requirements constrain its scale. One verifiable result would flip my decision: a six-month customer cohort maintaining at least 70% realized contribution margin, including fully loaded operational labor, while covered employees per specialist double. That would connect the existing demand signal to the economic mechanism that matters.
