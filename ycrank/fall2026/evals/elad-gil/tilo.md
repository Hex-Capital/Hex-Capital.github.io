# Tilo -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Tilo’s market opens if deploying agents makes application interfaces an economic bottleneck: agent builders become buyers, and the controlling variable is whether execution savings exceed the cost of adopting Tilo-native applications. Typed actions could eliminate repeated screenshot interpretation and clicking. But the savings must justify moving workflows into a new environment when Browser Use and Simular work with existing software. That adoption tradeoff determines whether Tilo becomes infrastructure or an elegant alternative desktop.

The product has meaningful architectural substance. Applications expose actions while humans and agents share workspace state, permissions, history, and undo ([developer documentation](https://tilo.so/docs/)). Better models could complete more work while Tilo retains the operational state surrounding that work. This fits my preference for owning a recurring workflow and my unstated pattern of backing infrastructure around a technology transition. The unresolved revocation and hosted-isolation boundaries limit how much responsibility the platform can safely assume today; they do not erase the architecture already documented.

The economic discontinuity is less established. Tilo claims computer use can be up to **10,000× cheaper**, but the dossier supplies no independently reproduced comparison. Direct action invocation provides a credible cost-reduction mechanism. MCP’s November 2024 introduction also supports the timing. Neither establishes the economic advantage after application integration, maintenance, and failures. The headline claim therefore earns partial credit. The quoted **$16.7 billion** AI operating-system market spans mobile and embedded deployments and does little to define Tilo’s reachable buyer or revenue pool.

The strongest case against passing is that Tilo makes the application itself agent-operable, potentially turning every integrated application into another reason to adopt the platform. Browser access and the public SDK provide plausible entry points. If developers build on that shared state, subsequent model improvements could increase the platform’s usefulness. The difficulty is distribution: external application adoption is unreported, and the GitHub mirror’s **three stars and zero forks** establish neither demand nor its absence. YC backing and October 19 coverage also do not demonstrate repeat usage. The exceptional-team override does not apply on this record: there is no documented frontier research or unusually scarce technical capability sufficient to substitute for market evidence.

Quinten Wolff’s **2025–2026 patent-workflow automation work at BDPE** gives the team relevant experience with institutional tasks. Both founders’ TUM backgrounds support technical context. They do not establish prior startup learning, costly commitment, or personal need of the kind that would raise my founder-conviction score. Nor does the dossier substantiate fundraising vanity, excessive liquidity, or dependence on a frozen model. The applicable red-flag question is whether improving general-purpose agents reduce the advantage of rebuilding applications; Tilo has not yet supplied comparative evidence resolving it.

I would pass on writing a personal check today despite the promising workflow architecture. The market gate fails: there is no demonstrated adoption threshold, and the economic step change remains largely a company claim. My verifiable flip condition is **a 90-day paid deployment cohort of three independent agent teams demonstrating at least a 10× reduction in fully loaded cost per successfully completed recurring workflow, including application integration costs**. That would connect the technical advantage to a reason customers actually switch.
