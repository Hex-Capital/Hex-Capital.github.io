# Mireye -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most useful fact is not that Mireye has an MCP server. It is that Ansh Chokshi had never sold, manually identified 50 prospects, varied the message until 10% replied, and narrowed the first wedge to data-center developers. That is a real obstacle followed by founder action and a measured change. It is weaker than a dated account of a customer rejection forcing a product rewrite, but it clears my resourcefulness gate. He is learning the conversion step himself instead of hiring someone or waiting for a partner.

The technical proof is stronger. Two people have assembled 85 sources and more than 300 provenance-linked fields, with source URLs, fetch timestamps, and confidence values exposed through an API and MCP server. The catalog was reportedly growing 20% weekly in August 2026. This is not merely an agentic-GIS diagram. It is a working programmable primitive built from county records, utility portals, maps, and government documents that were not designed to interoperate. That matches my unstated preference for founders who construct a difficult substrate before asking permission to call it a platform.

The narrow well may be data-center land acquisition. First customers reportedly used Mireye to source off-market sites 100 times faster than their previous process. This identifies both a user and an urgent job, but “100 times faster” is a testimonial, not yet evidence of payment, retention, or repeated weekly use. The $19 and $99 self-service plans prove that Mireye can charge, not that anyone is paying. Its 426 LinkedIn followers are nearly irrelevant. The important unanswered question is whether the same developers keep querying the catalog after completing one site search.

The strongest bear case is that Mireye has built an impressive adapter over sources it does not control. Mapbox already has global hosted and self-hosted MCP access; CARTO and Fused cover adjacent agentic workflows; Mireye remains largely US-only and inherits government refresh schedules. At $1 per additional 1,000 credits, a useful feature could become a cheap commodity before the request-derived catalog becomes a compounding asset. There is also no evidence that the founders have changed product route after contrary usage, and the second teammate’s identity and working history are unclear. A good runner has to do more than run quickly in a straight line.

The cofounder uncertainty resembles the risk YC accepted with Dropbox, but the material difference is that Dropbox already had an unusually convincing prototype and visible user pull. Mireye has the prototype and founder motion, but not equivalent pull. Still, no exception is required here: Chokshi already passes the operating gate through direct sales learning and a functioning technical artifact. The usual red flags are mostly absent—distribution does not depend on a ceremonial launch, a large partner, or prematurely hired salespeople.

I would write a small check, but near the bottom of the invest range. My 36-month base case is that Mireye becomes a $2–5 million ARR vertical data platform for data-center site selection rather than a universal physical-world API. The verifiable flip condition is retention: if fewer than five of the initial data-center customers are still making weekly production queries after 90 days, I would pass, because that would turn the apparent narrow well into a sequence of one-off research projects.
