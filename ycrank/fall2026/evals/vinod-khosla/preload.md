# Preload -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Preload’s consequential opportunity is to make human dexterity cheaply transferable to machines. Video shows motion; contact and muscle signals could reveal what makes that motion succeed. If those signals radically reduce robot training requirements, the company could remove a bottleneck in automating physical work. But the dossier does not quantify that reduction, the labor cost displaced, or the population reached. A $1.31 billion robot-training-data market estimate measures spending on an input. It does not establish the consequence of Preload succeeding.

The central technical question is whether EMG adds transferable information after video and tactile sensing are already available. Axonnic captures EMG; Human Archive collects multimodal wearable data. Preload combines modalities that these competitors separately emphasize, but collecting more channels does not establish better robot policies. Human hands can demonstrate contacts that a robot cannot reproduce. Touch2Robot supports the relevance of contact-aware learning; its results are not Preload’s results. I have no measured advantage against robot teleoperation or a competing collection stack to underwrite.

The founders make the strongest case against passing. Leo Li’s Neuralink and computational-neuroscience background, Surya Pandiaraju’s neural-interface experience, and Tony Wang’s EMG and hardware research address real parts of this problem. This fits my unstated preference for teams assembled around difficult scientific workflows. It could become a repeatable production platform rather than a collection service. But the dossier documents neither a proprietary learning loop nor evidence of fast belief revision or recruiting someone to own a missing capability. Relevant scientific experience earns a serious conversation; it does not establish those behaviors.

Preload also leaves the decisive experiment unspecified. The largest apparent kill risk is human-to-robot transfer, followed by whether EMG earns its collection and calibration costs. There is no disclosed test budget, numerical threshold, or stop condition. Likewise, custom dataset licensing is an inferred business model, with no disclosed cost per usable demonstration or successful robot task. Those are missing facts, not evidence of bad spending or permanent subsidy dependence. The October 1 launch video’s reported 246,186 views do not resolve either uncertainty, and I do not require revenue from a three-person pre-seed team.

I checked the exceptions before deciding. An extraordinary instigator or a quantified societal consequence can justify buying an option before milestones or economics exist. This dossier establishes specialized founders, but neither override. The competitive overlap raises the possibility that implementation labor becomes the business; it does not prove that outcome. There is also no evidence of exit fixation, refusal to adapt, or premature organizational scaling. My **Pass** rests on unproven consequence and technical leverage, rather than an active founder red flag.

One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independently reproduced customer benchmark showing at least **5x lower fully loaded training cost to reach a fixed success rate on held-out manipulation tasks**, versus robot teleoperation, with an ablation demonstrating that EMG materially contributes. Include sensing, calibration, human collection, compute, and robot fine-tuning costs. That would establish that Preload’s additional signals buy useful capability economically. Today, I would keep my personal check unwritten.
