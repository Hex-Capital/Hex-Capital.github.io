# Routant -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Routant’s possible secret is that enterprise agent deployment requires an independent trust boundary between vendors and customers, rather than authorization embedded in each vendor’s product. That is a proposition worth testing. It would be weakened if customers could deploy the same agents through existing identity providers without additional integration or governance friction. But this is my reconstruction: the dossier supplies neither a named opposing belief nor an experiment establishing that Routant has discovered something its competitors misunderstand. The secret remains unspecified.

The more decisive problem is ownership of the opportunity. Routant proposes one vendor integration through which customers govern resources, human approvals, and audit trails. Keycard already documents policy-bound credentials and delegation chains; Arcade documents action-level authorization, approvals, and audits; Descope provides agent identities and access policies. These are concrete substitutes for important parts of the proposed product. Their funding totals do not settle the competition. Their capabilities do establish that Routant needs a demonstrable mechanism for winning a bounded market. “Vendor-to-customer deployment” describes a focus; it does not yet establish proprietary technology, network effects, or control of that market.

The strongest objection to passing is that this boundary could become the control point for agent commerce. A vendor’s single integration might reach many customers, while customer policies could eventually make replacement costly. The underlying obstruction is real: the January 2026 Okta-commissioned survey found that security concerns slowed agent adoption for 69% of 150 respondents. This also fits the broader move toward infrastructure supporting the complete AI production stack. Yet the survey validates the problem, while the integration and switching-cost mechanisms remain unverified. None of my exception mechanisms applies: Routant has no documented independent technical breakthrough, persistent customer transactions, or validated deployment path that removes the competitive risk. The dossier also supplies no proprietary founder network or unique information asset supporting the relevant unstated patterns.

I cannot assess the foundation of the company because founder identities, shared working history, commitment, and ownership are unavailable. The reported team size of zero is not evidence that nobody works there. Those unknowns receive the ordinary prior, rather than an invented founding-team red flag. Similarly, absent revenue is normal at this stage. The economic limitation is narrower: without pricing, billing units, margins, or customer expansion evidence, I cannot construct one revenue engine with power-law potential. The adjacent market estimate—$210 million in 2026, projected to reach $1.02 billion in 2031—cannot substitute for that arithmetic. No verified acquisition channel or delivery economics resolves distribution either.

I would pass on Routant today. The adverse evidence is competitive overlap; the other gaps remain unknowns. My one verifiable condition for reopening the decision is a customer-confirmed production deployment showing that Routant satisfies a consequential vendor-to-customer security requirement that evaluated alternatives could not satisfy. That would turn its proposed boundary into evidence of a distinct business.
