# Perit.AI -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Perit.AI’s possible secret is that transaction-critical speech failures require data authored by sector practitioners because general crowd raters systematically miss what matters. That proposition is testable: compare downstream model performance using practitioner data against matched general-rater data on previously unseen conversations. The dossier supplies no such result and identifies no competitor that rejects the proposition. Its public sample—45 conversations totaling 5.10 hours—is scripted, studio-clean US English. A useful dataset establishes that something was produced; it does not establish the secret. I assign the absent-evidence default here rather than mistake an untested claim for a refuted one.

The stronger evidence concerns selling and delivering the product. Six major AI labs and “multiple millions in ARR,” although self-reported, are substantial signals for a Fall 2026 company. The acquisition route is concrete: buyers submit model failures, scope a pilot, and commission continuing deliveries. The reported 4,000 audio hours across nine locales make this more credible than an enterprise-sales aspiration. Distribution is Perit.AI’s strongest dimension. But reaching sophisticated customers and becoming indispensable to them are separate achievements.

Who owns the resulting advantage? Perit reports 650 transcribers and aligners, yet no exclusive contributor access; non-exclusive licensing is the default. Mercor and Turing already organize expert labor and could extend into speech workflows. Their ability to match Perit’s quality is unproved, but the dossier identifies no structural obstacle to trying. Calibration and Foundry may improve execution without making the company a last mover. This is affirmative competitive exposure, which puts the monopoly score below its missing-evidence default. Custom quotes separately itemize contributor compensation, and reported ARR comes without gross margins or a distinction between recurring commitments and annualized project work. One recognizable revenue engine exists, but its power-law economics remain unestablished.

The founders deserve a different treatment. Kshitij’s prior operating experience and Poorva’s engineering work at Google, Atlassian, and Uber support their ability to build an organization. They do not establish longstanding collaboration, full-time commitment, ownership, or economic exposure. Attending IIT Kharagpur together cannot substitute for that evidence. I use the founding-structure default; I do not infer divided commitment from a separate GitHub project or manufacture a red flag from missing cap-table information.

The strongest objection to passing is that Perit.AI may already be acquiring the scarce asset: trusted access to frontier labs’ actual failure distributions. Six customers could finance increasingly specialized rubrics and make the supplier progressively harder to replace. This fits the broader pattern of investing across the AI production stack, and a repeat operator has already found paying demand. It also tests my known exceptions: exceptional technical proof or persistent customer behavior can outweigh an initially weak monopoly. Here, however, there is no comparative technical result, documented renewal behavior, or demonstrated ownership of the accumulated learning. The explicit competitive exposure survives that exception test. **I pass.** My one verifiable flip condition is a procurement audit showing that at least three existing lab customers renewed at premium pricing because Perit controls an exclusive evaluation asset that competing suppliers could not reproduce. That would turn successful delivery into evidence of durable value capture.
