# Qlo -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important missing sentence is what broke and what the founders did next. Qlo has shipped something substantial, but the dossier does not name a customer rejection, accuracy failure, integration obstacle, or procurement blockage followed by a dated workaround. Ryan Hu’s earlier insurance company reached roughly $500,000 ARR and received acquisition term sheets, which shows he can build in this market. It does not show how he behaves when Qlo itself hits resistance. For a people-first investor, that distinction is decisive.

The product is stronger evidence than the founders’ descriptions of it. Qlo is not merely extracting fields into a demo. It executes customer-approved Excel rating logic, preserves source references, and produces quote-through-renewal artifacts alongside the existing policy system. An enterprise customer uses it for daily quoting, and Distinguished Programs reports roughly three times the primary-quote throughput. This is a fierce-nerd proof: a deployed system doing accuracy-sensitive work inside an ugly production workflow. It also matches the pre-round proof pattern I like, though the throughput claim remains a customer testimonial rather than an independently reproduced benchmark.

There is a narrow well of demand. Commercial MGAs and program administrators have too many submissions and underwriters still move data among email, spreadsheets, raters, and policy systems. At least one named customer tolerates Qlo’s current product every day. The 30-day paid pilot, parity check, refund promise, and two-year design-partner structure are sensible because they force the product to prove itself against existing outputs. But no price, renewal, or second production customer is disclosed. One deep well may become a market; it may also be a bespoke implementation.

Hu encountered this problem through roughly 2.5 years of work across MGA, carrier, and broker workflows. That is an organic origin, not an “AI for insurance” brainstorm. The wedge is full of schlep: macro-enabled raters, endorsements, policy documents, customer-specific pricing logic, auditability, and integration without replacing the policy administration system. The usual partnership red flag does not fire because the founders are selling and implementing directly. Nor does Hu’s dropout status carry much weight either way; his prior insurance revenue is the relevant evidence.

The strongest case against Qlo is that its apparent software proof is actually concentrated services work. A two-person team promises two-week setups while Federato, Sixfold, and Kalepa already cover overlapping ingestion, rating, and quote-and-bind workflows with far more capital. Each customer may bring a new spreadsheet language, document corpus, and parity burden. The absence of any documented history of Hu and Liv Huang building together adds another uncertainty. The exceptional proof that could override these gaps would be unmistakable pull or independently validated performance across several customers. One testimonial and one daily deployment do not yet supply that override.

I would not write the check today because Qlo fails my operational gate on documented resourcefulness, despite clearing the technical and user-pull side of it. This is a promising company with unusually real pre-seed product evidence, not yet a proven pair of running backs. I would flip to Invest if the founders document one customer-specific parity or integration failure, a shipped workaround within fourteen days, and continued paid daily use by that customer afterward. That single episode would show both that the product survives contact with insurance complexity and that the founders do too.
