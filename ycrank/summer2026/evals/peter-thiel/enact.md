# Enact -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Enact’s implied secret is that robot developers will improve policies faster by purchasing one integrated loop—failure discovery, targeted demonstrations, retraining, and evaluation—than by assembling those functions themselves. What would prove it wrong? If developers obtain comparable improvement from Instance, Axis, NVIDIA Cosmos, or internal tooling without sending deployment-specific data to Enact. The dossier reports no policy-success delta, evaluation-accuracy benchmark, dataset scale, or cross-embodiment result. This is therefore a fashionable proposition about physical AI, not yet a falsifiable secret proved by Enact.

The competitive facts are worse than mere silence. Instance has tested success detection on more than 10,000 held-out episodes across seven robot platforms; Axis offers human-gated post-training; NVIDIA and Runway can push overlapping capabilities down from the platform layer. Enact identifies no exclusive data, 10x technical result, network retention, or bounded segment it already controls. Its integrated loop is broader than any one competitor’s stated product, but integration is a feature until customers repeatedly generate proprietary failure-and-recovery data that competitors cannot reproduce. Today this is competition without a monopoly mechanism.

The strongest bull case is that general-purpose robotics models create an expanding post-training layer, just as model proliferation created valuable infrastructure elsewhere in AI. The physical-AI training-platform market was estimated at $3.2 billion in 2025 and growing 41.35% annually; real-world recovery data is expensive and deployment-specific, so the apparent collection burden could become Enact’s proprietary asset. This also fits the emerging complete AI production stack—models, data, evaluation, and physical deployment—better than a superficial “AI copilot” does. A verified zero-to-one result could override current competitive overlap.

But the exception requires proof, not proximity to an important trend. Enact has disclosed no customer, revenue, repeat usage, exclusive dataset, or technical measurement. Its “Request data” workflow suggests direct enterprise selling to robotics-model developers, yet there is no named buyer, contract value, sales cycle, implementation burden, or evidence that accumulated data lowers subsequent acquisition or delivery costs. My 36-month base case is therefore that Enact either pivots into a managed robotics-data service or remains below $2 million ARR, rather than becoming the proprietary post-training layer.

The two-person team receives neither founder credit nor founder condemnation. There is no public evidence about biographies, prior collaboration, full-time status, ownership, or whether core engineering is internal. None of the explicit founding red flags is established, but neither is the long alignment required to entrust them with a difficult coordination loop spanning real-world rollout access, failure judgment, data collection, retraining, and evaluation. Missing evidence is not refutation; it is also not a foundation.

I would pass today. Two primary gates fail affirmatively: the dossier presents a consensus opportunity without Enact-specific technical proof, and it names several substitutes while identifying no mechanism for value capture. The one verifiable flip condition is a customer-controlled benchmark showing that Enact’s targeted recovery loop improves policy success by at least 10 percentage points over the customer’s existing pipeline across three robot platforms, accompanied by two paying renewals. That would convert the pitch from slogan to secret and the data workflow from service labor into a possible monopoly asset.
