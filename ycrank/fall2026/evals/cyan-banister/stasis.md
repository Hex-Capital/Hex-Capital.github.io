# STASIS -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine glasses quietly negotiating with every screen around me: noticing when my eyes struggle, changing the light, and letting me keep doing what I care about. STASIS proposes pieces of that world—inward-facing sensors, automatic tinting, Bluetooth screen adjustments. But the important transition is from measuring eyes to making a useful intervention. The dossier does not establish that its measurements reliably identify strain or that its adjustments relieve it. That future is imaginable; the evidence that it has begun is still thin.

The founders are the strongest reason to keep looking. Soham Chakraborty and Neil Goradia worked together on GlaucoGlasses before STASIS, and Berkeley independently documents that collaboration. Bioengineering, neurology-AI research, EECS, and previous ocular-device work form a coherent trail. These are people who were already building around eyes, rather than discovering eyewear in an accelerator application. I give that real credit. What remains unproven is the commercial half: which measurement matters enough that someone will buy glasses, wear them every day, and eventually pay $15 a month?

Why does the screen user hurt today? Is the problem illumination, an unsuitable prescription, sustained near work, or something else? STASIS names static filters, reminders, and manual brightness adjustments, but the dossier does not isolate the failure those alternatives leave behind. Nor does it identify a gatekeeper extracting a specific rent. Returning comfort and control to an individual would interest me; I cannot yet tell how much control this product actually returns. Conflicting $95 and $240 preorder copy also leaves the initial economic bargain unclear, without proving anything adverse about the founders.

The strongest argument against passing is that the team may already possess the difficult practical knowledge while public evidence trails behind. A reported 30-day personal wear test is more informative than a rendering. Prior GlaucoGlasses work suggests persistence, and its reported 348 physician intent forms and seven distributor LOIs totaling $168,000 suggest experience approaching prospective buyers. Those commitments belong to a different product, however. My pre-revenue exception for **CosmicBrain AI** depended on a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept; STASIS shares the physical-product ambition, but lacks comparable external validation in this dossier. Missing revenue is not the obstacle. Demonstrated usefulness is.

STASIS also touches two patterns I like: mundane daily repair and physical technology beneath a more ambitious experience. Neither resemblance establishes the investment. Claimed sensor patents have no verified identifiers or scope here, and a proposed longitudinal dataset is not yet a controlled enabling asset. JINS MEME’s discontinued consumer offering makes sustained usefulness a concrete question, although it does not establish why that product ended. Inward-facing sensing also makes consent and data custody consequential; the dossier establishes neither a severe privacy violation nor an architecture resolving that concern. Likewise, two co-founders do not establish equal control or a leadership problem.

I would pass with my own money today. I like the builders more than I can underwrite the consumer behavior. My single condition for reopening the decision is an independent 30-day controlled trial showing that users repeatedly choose paid daily wear and experience meaningfully less discomfort with STASIS’s sensor-driven adjustments than with tint-only glasses. That would turn an appealing future into an observed reason to put something on your face every morning.
