# Hubble -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that Hubble calls itself an intelligence layer. Healthcare APIs stop at the edge of the useful data, so the founders built browser and voice agents to cross the remaining portals, phone systems, and release queues. The result is more than 40 production tools spanning Epic, Cerner, athenahealth, UnitedHealthcare, and Aetna. That is a real shipped workaround to a named obstacle. But the dossier does not reveal the first failure, how quickly the founders responded, or whether a customer forced the response, so I cannot give them top credit for resourcefulness.

The technical proof is Hubble’s strongest evidence. Deterministic identifiers, source traceability, audit logs, and normalized output form a working system rather than a proposed architecture. Its customers report records delivered within a week, $340,000 in denied claims recovered over 90 days, and patient-estimate accuracy rising from 50% to 94%. These are unusually concrete pre-seed results. They remain company-published testimonials from anonymous customers, however, with no disclosed retrieval volume, completion rate, or independent benchmark. Hubble has proved that the machine works in production, not yet how reliably or broadly it works.

There is also a plausible organic origin. Prabha Dublish built healthcare products at Grow Therapy, while Aaron Leon built data and EHR infrastructure at Amazon Care and One Medical. They did not approach healthcare interoperability as tourists. They also built a nonprofit together in college and have known each other for more than a decade, eliminating the application-assembled-cofounder concern. But the dossier never identifies the particular incident that made either founder need Hubble, nor a rejected product hypothesis followed by a dated change of route. Domain history earns credit for the schlep-heavy wedge; adaptability remains at the missing-evidence default.

The user evidence is stronger than a waitlist and weaker than a narrow well visibly pulling the product back. Legal teams want records without hours of paralegal work. Dental groups want denied claims recovered. Benefits platforms want accurate estimates. Those are urgent jobs with measured outcomes, but the customers are unnamed and Hubble discloses neither payment nor repeat usage. The patient service is free, while B2B pricing and revenue remain unpublished. The evidence shows value delivered, but not yet that users repeatedly buy the crude product or refer others to it.

The strongest objection is that Hubble may have built an impressive collection of brittle automations rather than a compounding platform. Its completion time still depends on portals, phone trees, providers, and consent rules it does not control. Particle Health, 1upHealth, Health Gorilla, and PicnicHealth have each raised between roughly $49 million and more than $100 million. Forty integrations could become proprietary infrastructure, or merely forty things that break whenever an external system changes. Direct demo outreach is sensible at this stage, but 453 LinkedIn followers and an oversubscribed undisclosed round are not distribution. The concrete pre-round proof spike fits my actual pattern of investing; the missing growth, retention, and accuracy data prevent a stronger conclusion. No known exception is needed here—the team is experienced, technically credible, and longstanding—but none supplies an override if usage proves shallow.

I would write a small check. The founders have already crossed enough ugly boundaries to pass the resourcefulness gate, and the production system clears the technical-proof gate. My 36-month base case is a focused healthcare-data operations business reaching $3 million to $8 million in ARR, rather than a universal patient-information layer. One verifiable condition would flip me to a pass: by August 2027, fewer than ten paying organizations performing repeat record retrievals through Hubble.
