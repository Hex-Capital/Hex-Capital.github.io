# Whitespace -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first numbers I want are missing: proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely the first scarce check. Financing silence does not prove an auction, but it does not establish cheap non-consensus entry either. More importantly, Whitespace names no finite use-of-funds experiment—only a broad product spanning inventory, procurement, sales, service, finance, and planning. I cannot tell what one card this round flips, by what date, or what result would dramatically change the company’s value.

The founders are the strongest part of the case. Alex Tung grew up around wholesale distribution, advised manufacturers and distributors at BCG, and helped Ankar reach seven-figure ARR within a year. Leon Yao combines machine-learning research with logistics optimization at InstaDeep and previously shipped a product serving more than 1,000 users across three schools. This is a credible outsider-insider pair connecting distributor operations with applied automation. Tung is identified as CEO, which is better than an anonymous 50/50 committee, although the dossier does not establish who has final authority over product, hiring, and financing.

There is also more than pre-seed theater in the customer evidence. Kingfisher Direct and Warwick are named deployments covering order intake, customer service, and inventory planning. Whitespace reports two-to-four-week implementation, greater than 80% productivity gains in selected workflows, and three-month customer payback. The customer-specific knowledge base and correction history could become a proprietary workflow asset. But the causal chain stops early: the dossier does not disclose whether these deployments are paid, whether usage persists, or whether corrections improve results over time. Two deployments establish adoption, not yet a durable dark pool that repeatedly causes purchases.

The economic issue is different from customer ROI. A distributor recovering its cost in three months does not tell me Whitespace’s implementation labor, gross margin, burn, or route to break-even. Usage- and workflow-based pricing may align value and revenue, but supporting several ERP-connected functions can also turn nominal software revenue into integration-heavy services. With Plato, Proton.ai, Canals, and Conexiom already financing overlapping workflows, breadth is not itself a moat; it may simply multiply the surfaces on which accuracy failures and incumbents can attack.

The strongest bull case is that these are unusually informative pre-seed deployments: named customers, active operational workflows, human review, fast implementation, and reported ROI. That resembles the mechanism that justified the AI exception in Ando—enterprise value observed in operation rather than promised through model improvement. The material difference is that Ando documented forecast accuracy, complete pilot-to-paid conversion, and retention. Whitespace supplies company-reported outcome claims without cohort conversion, retention, or vendor economics. The exception is possible, but the evidence required to invoke it is not yet present.

My 36-month forecast is that Whitespace reaches roughly $3 million–$6 million ARR by August 2029 as an implementation-heavy vertical application, but does not become the distributor “operating system” or demonstrate profitable, one-and-done scaling. The team understands both sides of the system, so I do not forecast immediate failure. I forecast a real product trapped between customer-owned workflow data and heavily funded competitors, with the next financing remaining necessary.

I am passing. One verifiable result would flip me: a six-month cohort of at least ten distributors showing paid conversion, full retention, software-like gross margins, and repeatable implementation within four weeks, financed at a sub-$10-million post-money valuation. That would turn the claimed workflow advantage into a measured venture moment at a price that preserves convexity.
