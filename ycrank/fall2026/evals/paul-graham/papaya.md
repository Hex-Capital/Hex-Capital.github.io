# Papaya -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Papaya’s most interesting fact is that the founders built an analytics agent and then started selling its optimization harness. Tools you build because you need them can become better companies than the original product. But the important question is what forced that discovery. Which failure blocked the analytics agent, what did the founders build to get past it, and how quickly did it work? The dossier establishes a change of direction without documenting that sequence. I cannot award resourcefulness points merely because the destination looks sensible.

There is actual software here. Papaya has a public tracing SDK and describes a system that analyzes workflows and delivers approved pull requests. That earns more credit than an architecture diagram. The reported 20%+ quality improvement and 30% latency reduction could be substantial, but there is no disclosed benchmark methodology or independent reproduction. The 1.1M conversations analyzed establish reported processing volume, not how reliably the proposed fixes improve an agent. Technical proof is Papaya’s strongest dimension, with a substantial certainty discount.

The named customers make the opposing case harder to dismiss. Sunny Health’s Ayush Tomar reports fewer inference error states; Hamlet’s Jeff Hui reports useful recommendations and easier trace inspection. Those are specific jobs for a specific niche: engineers already maintaining production agents. Papaya could discover that these teams urgently want someone to finish the debugging work their tracing tools expose. But neither testimonial establishes repeated use, payment, or dependence. The $500 monthly Business plan is a price, not revenue. I want to know whether customers bring the next broken workflow back without being asked.

The founders have credible reasons to understand this work. Niyaz spent more than ten years at Amazon across ML and agent evaluation; Faiz brings data infrastructure experience. April 2026 documentation of Nile Local gives their earlier product a dated artifact, supporting an origin in building rather than merely selecting a fashionable category. Trace integrations, customer-specific rubrics, and sensitive data handling also contain real schlep. Macy’s experience helping SafeBase grow from $0 to $10M ARR through acquisition strengthens the enterprise execution case. It does not establish Papaya’s demand. Likewise, the disclosed pivot earns partial credit for changing routes, while its missing date and triggering observation limit what it proves about learning speed.

Papaya fits my unstated preference for tools technical users can build into their work. It has not yet shown the accompanying proof spike: developers repeatedly using a new primitive or forcing unexpected workflows. LangSmith Engine’s overlapping fixes matter because Papaya must learn something from users that the larger vendor misses. Competition alone is no reason to pass. Nor does the dossier establish dependence on a major partner, premature delegation, or an artificially assembled founding team. The working-product exception deserves consideration, but the current artifact and testimonials do not supply unmistakable pull or independently validated results strong enough to replace the missing founder-behavior evidence.

I would hold off on writing a check. Papaya has enough substance for a neutral judgment, but it fails my resourcefulness gate. My flip condition is one auditable customer episode: a named production failure, a founder-built workaround shipped within two weeks, and that customer continuing to use the fix and paying for Papaya over the following month. That would connect the software to both founder action and real need. Today those pieces remain insufficiently connected.
