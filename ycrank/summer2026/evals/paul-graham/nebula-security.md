# Nebula Security -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Eten Zou’s $400,000 in Google bounties makes **Nebula Security** a hacker tribe with an unusually weak conventional two-or-three-founder shape. **Nebula Security** has the artifacts animals leave behind: 1,162 validated findings, eight Chrome zero-days, 98 public CVEs, Linux contributions, DEF CON finals, and a 1,186-star exploit repository. The anomaly is not whether these people can hack. It is whether four accomplished researchers can become one founder organism instead of a research collective.

**Stripe** is the high rung for hacker genealogy: the Collisons had been building commerce software before Stripe existed, and their technical taste became a product ordinary programmers could use. **Nebula Security** is at least as convincing on the first half. Yuan Tan contributed to Linux and built VEGA; Frank Wu led DARPA program-repair work; Xiaochuan Yu reached three DEF CON finals; Eten Zou published kernel-vulnerability research. The divergence is the important one: Stripe’s hack became a simple API with users, while **Nebula Security** still has no disclosed paying-customer count. These are first-rate hackers. They have not yet shown that they are product hackers.

**Kiko** illustrates the low-rung failure mechanism in founder configuration: several talented hackers can build together without becoming a durable company, and founder count does not substitute for founder cohesion. **Nebula Security** is better situated because Eten, Yuan, and Frank overlapped at UC Riverside, while Frank and Xiaochuan competed through r3kapig. But **Nebula Security** has four specialized researchers and no evidence yet of who turns research arguments into product decisions or who does the humiliating enterprise follow-up. I prefer pairs and trios because ambiguity has fewer places to hide. Four is not fatal, but it is the clearest structural weakness here.

**Nebula Security’s** 1,162 validated findings show resourcefulness compounding in a way credentials alone do not. Every exploit gives **Nebula Security** another test case, another public proof, and another reason for a security engineer to trust VEGA. Over 18 months, that loop can turn $400,000 of bounty hunting into a proprietary corpus of failures and patches. The danger is that humans still review every report and write every patch. Animals do manual work at first; manager-mode companies mistake permanent manual work for a product. The next evidence I want is not another CVE. It is ten repositories being protected repeatedly without researcher hours rising in proportion.

**Nebula Security’s** founders have spent years inside kernel exploitation, browser security, program analysis, and automated repair, so the idea is close to their own lives. The missing piece is a specific Dropbox-style origin story: no dossier fact says Eten built VEGA because a security workflow repeatedly hurt him. **Nebula Security** would move to the very top of the organic-idea range if Yuan’s initial VEGA prototype began as a tool the founders used during their own bounty work and the $400,000 was earned materially faster because of it. If VEGA was instead assembled after observing that “AI security” was fundable, the score would fall a tier. The artifacts make the first story more likely, but they do not prove it.

**Coinbase** is the useful parallel for Nebula Security’s schlep. Coinbase’s advantage was not merely software; it was Brian Armstrong’s willingness to endure compliance work everyone else avoided. **Nebula Security** is choosing exploit reproduction, false-positive removal, responsible disclosure, and human-written patches—the dirty work between a scanner alert and a vulnerability an engineer believes. That schlep can distinguish **Nebula Security** from Codex Security, ZeroPath, and Semgrep. It can also become a throughput ceiling. The moat exists only if VEGA learns from the researchers quickly enough that the humans validate exceptional cases rather than every case forever.

**Google’s** $400,000 of prior bounty payments makes my 36-month forecast unusually concrete: by July 2029, **Nebula Security** will be acquired by Google for roughly $150–300 million before exceeding $15 million in ARR. **Nebula Security** has exactly the sort of exploit corpus and researcher density Google can absorb, while the four-founder shape and human-validation bottleneck make an acquisition more likely than an independent Stripe-scale outcome. The mechanism is hacker quality outrunning commercial machinery.

**XBOW’s** $120 million Series C is the strongest bear case against that forecast and against investing in **Nebula Security** now. Codex Security overlaps with discovery, validation, and patching; Semgrep already spans roughly 40 languages; **Nebula Security** discloses no revenue; and every VEGA report still receives human review. The stinging version is that four famous security researchers have built an excellent consultancy wrapped around a model feature. But 98 public CVEs, eight Chrome zero-days, and $400,000 in bounties are not deck claims, and they distinguish **Nebula Security** from the polished AI wrapper I would normally pass on. I would reverse the verdict if **Nebula Security** cannot show by July 2027 either $1 million in ARR or ten paying repositories with repeat usage and declining human-review time per validated finding.
