# Palette -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

$500K of YC financing and a two-person MIT founder base make **Palette** a lean financing object trapped inside my least-convicted category: consensus AI orchestration. **Palette** scores highest on capital structure because consumption pricing and 130+ regression tests can support cheap pre-Series-A tacks; **Palette** scores lowest on category because routing Seedance, Veo, Kling, MiniMax and LTX is software built on rented intelligence. Software is no longer investible—fine, provocative overstatement—but horizontal wrappers without owned distribution or proprietary supply are especially hard to underwrite.

**Teamshares** is the high rung on capital structure because the structure itself—employee ownership, operating cash flow and a rollup other funds struggled to classify—created the mispricing. **Palette** shares one useful mechanism: $0.01-per-credit pricing can turn variable model costs into self-financing usage rather than force every experiment through another equity round. The analogy breaks where it matters: **Palette** discloses no current-product revenue, gross margin or retained usage, and its suppliers control both availability and pricing. Teamshares owned the underlying cash-flow assets; Palette owns a router that Higgsfield or Krea can reproduce.

**OpenAI** is the low-rung category mechanism—not because OpenAI fails, but because seed investors paying consensus prices for commodity exposure around a dominant foundation layer usually fail to capture its economics. **Palette** sits directly in that blast radius: Higgsfield already reports a $200M annual revenue run rate, Krea has raised $83M, and both combine third-party models with increasingly proprietary products. **Palette** fits none of my unstated sleeves—pre-consensus crypto, DTC brand, prescription rails, creator ownership, Wizard-of-Oz service or unusual financing—and triggers the explicit red flag of an undifferentiated AI layer whose upstream providers can absorb its features.

$500K led by YC tells me almost nothing about **Palette**’s entry price because no valuation, outside demand or visible investor rejection is disclosed, so I apply the price-discipline default. Over the next 18 months, every improvement from Veo or Kling will lower **Palette**’s orchestration friction while simultaneously lowering customers’ switching costs; Palette then has to spend its next financing tack proving workflow retention before the routing advantage expires. A disclosed sub-batch valuation plus a strategic pass from a tier-one fund would make that illegibility attractive. Today, Palette looks more like a hot YC deck operating inside the fastest-marking consensus market.

Serena Pei’s diffusion, computer-vision and multimodal research gives **Palette** genuine technical competence, while Josephine Lee’s Broad Institute work shows she can ship data products. The founder-conviction score would flip upward if **Palette** could show that Lee or Pei had spent three years publishing a non-obvious thesis about creative production, or had manually operated customer video workflows before automating them. Instead, the indexed June HR-agent page followed by a July multimedia product suggests a recent tack without evidence of the missionary obsession that justified my Pinterest exception. Palette may have found a better market, but the dossier does not show that Palette was inevitable for these founders.

**John Fish / Bookshelved** worked because the distribution asset existed before the financing instrument: the creator and the customer channel were already the same cap-table asset. **Palette** has almost the inverse profile—Lee has roughly 3,000 LinkedIn followers, Pei has 964, neither has a public Twitter account or public GitHub repository, and no newsletter, channel or engaged creative community is identified. Palette’s self-serve access, API and book-a-call funnel are product surfaces, not owned distribution; Palette must still rent attention in a market where far better-capitalized suites already have creator mindshare.

The 130+ regression tests and claimed 50% cost reduction lead to a falsifiable forecast: by July 2029, **Palette** will have pivoted from a horizontal multi-model video suite into a narrow managed enterprise creative-operations service or API and will be between $1M and $3M ARR. **Palette** can probably make routing useful, but without community or proprietary models the horizontal product will not compound; the viable tack is selling accountable workflow outcomes to a specific enterprise buyer, effectively becoming more Wizard-of-Oz and less platform.

Higgsfield’s $200M annual revenue run rate is the strongest objection because it proves AI-video aggregation can become enormous, and **Palette**’s MIT founders, 35% faster generation claim and source-linked project context could identify the right workflow before incumbents do. Robinhood is the relevant Known Exception: an early enough $500K entry can absorb later consensus even when the category becomes hot. The objection loses because **Palette** has disclosed neither Robinhood-like entry terms nor Higgsfield-like usage, and Palette’s benchmarks are internal while its website identity, product category and domain routing were still discontinuous in July 2026. I would flip to Invest if Palette reaches $1M ARR with at least 60% gross margin and six-month enterprise retention while preserving the current $500K-scale capital base.
