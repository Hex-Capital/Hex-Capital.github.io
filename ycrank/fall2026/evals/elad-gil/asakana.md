# Asakana -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Asakana’s opportunity is that AI can let food distributors accept orders through existing customer messages while removing the labor of entering them into an ERP. The controlling variable is the fully loaded cost per correctly processed order, including human review and integration maintenance. One distributor receives approximately 60 orders daily, some exceeding 75 lines, and spends approximately 30% of representative time processing them. That establishes a meaningful labor pool. Asakana becomes a large business only if it can capture part of those savings while making each additional distributor inexpensive to serve.

The company-reported 40%+ order-desk time returned to selling is the strongest economic evidence. It suggests a change in how work gets done, rather than another interface for the same work. But time released is not automatically payroll eliminated or incremental sales generated. Transaction-volume commissions also create a specific tradeoff: revenue follows merchandise value, while processing costs may follow order complexity and exceptions. The dossier does not connect those two curves. Google’s October 2025 computer-use release supports the general timing, but Asakana’s undisclosed model stack prevents attributing its economics to that release.

The product has substance beyond extracting text. Customer histories, catalog matching, ERP entry, and escalation before uncertain orders reach the system form an operational workflow. Better models should improve completion rates while those connections remain useful. That is affirmative architectural evidence. It does not establish an exclusive data asset: customer-specific context may be reproducible, and the dossier provides no estimate of the time or money required to recreate it. Choco’s claimed 200-plus ERP connections make integration coverage an existing competitive advantage Asakana must overcome.

The strongest case for investing now is that a narrow seafood entry point can outperform a broad platform on the orders that actually cause expensive mistakes. Live US deployments and the founder’s report of broadline customers suggest the product is already crossing beyond its initial niche. Yudai’s seafood-distribution experience and Ben’s eight years at Google, including Gemini and Project Mariner, make that execution case credible. However, the deployments establish usage, not yet paid expansion or repeatable acquisition. Pepper’s broader commercial suite creates a distribution problem: a useful standalone automation product can still lose to a vendor already serving the buyer. Asakana’s first product could become a channel for adjacent back-office tools, but that distribution advantage remains prospective.

I would keep founder motivation separate from founder competence. The dossier documents relevant operating and technical experience; it does not document prior startup learning, a small exit, or costly personal commitment sufficient to score the desperation criterion above its default. Nor does the rare-team exception for frontier research apply: this is an application business with live deployments, so operating economics should determine conviction. The relevant red flag is custom integration work becoming a service burden behind software pricing. There is no evidence that this has happened, but maintained connections alone do not resolve it.

I would monitor Asakana rather than write a personal check today. It owns enough workflow to be interesting, and the reported customer benefit is substantial. The unresolved issue is whether that benefit produces repeatable vendor economics against established competitors. My flip condition is a six-month paid distributor cohort demonstrating at least 70% contribution margin after charging all onboarding, human-review, and integration-maintenance labor. That is my underwriting threshold, not a claimed industry benchmark; meeting it would directly validate the variable on which this investment turns.
