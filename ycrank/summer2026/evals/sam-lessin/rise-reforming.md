# Rise Reforming -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I cannot underwrite the deal before I know the deal. Rise Reforming has raised more than $1.7 million, but the dossier gives no post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or evidence that this is a scarce first check. That is not proof of an auction; it is missing evidence on my primary gate. Funding raised, YC admission, and 587 LinkedIn followers do not substitute for price.

The actual venture moment is unusually legible. Rise has delivered a container to a Chicagoland wastewater plant after more than 1,800 hours of stable syngas production, and the next experiment is whether that system can operate on contaminated live biogas in 2027. Success would retire a real technical risk—moisture, sulfur, VOCs, and siloxane poisoning—not manufacture another engagement metric. But the company has not specified a quantitative pass/fail threshold, dated operating duration, chemical yield, or how much of the round is reserved for reaching it. This is a named experiment, not yet a properly priced card flip.

The strongest part of the story is the translation layer. One binding biogas-supply agreement, multiple producer MOUs, and a conditional DME offtake connect wastewater operators holding low-value gas with downstream chemical buyers. That fits the pattern of software and machinery reorganizing neglected physical infrastructure. It is not yet a proprietary dark pool that causes purchases: the live-feedstock pilot has not started, the offtake remains conditional, and no commercial chemical volumes or revenue have been reported. M2X already has multiple field deployments and $40 million of Series B financing, so merely proving that modular conversion is possible will not establish ownership of the market.

The economics are the sharper problem. Rise proposes to finance plant CAPEX and maintenance, pay hosts for biogas, and recover everything through commodity chemical sales. Without module cost, yield, gross margin, burn, or host-level payback, deleting the next financing from the model leaves a blank page. This is the relevant capital-factory red flag: each new site may consume more balance-sheet capital before the prior site proves cash generation. The company could ultimately use project finance, but resemblance to a financeable asset is not evidence that lenders will fund it.

The founders have credible technical receipts. Lucas Zubillaga built and operated the bench-scale reformer, and the team’s work across molecular engineering, nuclear waste, offshore wind, carbon capture, and electrochemistry maps well to process development. What is missing is shipped operating experience on both sides of the bridge: no founder has yet deployed chemical infrastructure at commercial scale or sold commodity output through an offtake portfolio. The CEO, CTO, and COO titles reduce—but do not resolve—the concern about final decision authority among three university co-founders.

The bull case stings: 1,800 stable operating hours, a delivered pilot, binding feedstock access, conditional demand, and process-IP work are substantially more than a pre-seed climate presentation. Still, none of the known exceptions applies because Rise lacks the hard external override—realized purchases, retained commercial operation, or extraordinary acquisition evidence—that could compensate for unknown pricing and economics. I am passing today. I flip if the delivered module completes 1,000 continuous hours on raw wastewater biogas and sells at least $250,000 of resulting chemical at positive site-level contribution margin under a non-auctioned, sub-$10 million post-money round.
