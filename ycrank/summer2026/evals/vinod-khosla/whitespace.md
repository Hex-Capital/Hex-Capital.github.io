# Whitespace -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Whitespace can remove a meaningful amount of administrative labor, but the dossier does not yet show an option on an orders-of-magnitude consequence. Wholesale distributors reportedly devote 30–50% of staff capacity to back-office work, and Whitespace claims productivity gains above 80% in selected workflows. That is a credible efficiency pool. It is not yet a quantified societal cost pool: there is no distributor spending baseline, affected population, or evidence that the mechanism can make an essential service 10–100x cheaper. The upside currently reads as better distribution operations, not a rewritten cost curve.

The technical claim is stronger than the consequence claim, but still inadequately measured. Whitespace integrates with inboxes and ERPs, constructs customer-specific knowledge, and performs order intake, customer service, and inventory planning within two-to-four weeks. Two named customers—Kingfisher Direct and Warwick—make this more than a presentation. But “80%+ productivity” lacks a task definition, baseline, sample size, error rate, and comparison against a named incumbent product. Human review may contain mistakes; it does not establish a discontinuity. Meanwhile, Plato, Proton.ai, Canals, and Conexiom already cover overlapping workflows with substantially more capital.

The strongest case against passing is the team. Both founders grew up in wholesale-distribution families. Alex Tung advised manufacturers and distributors at BCG and helped Ankar reach seven-figure ARR within a year; Leon Yao combines machine-learning research, logistics optimization, and experience building a product used by more than 1,000 people. Their twelve-year relationship and complementary commercial and technical backgrounds fit the execution-heavy industrial-software pattern that can outperform grander science projects. Two live customers and a claimed three-month payback are unusually useful pre-seed evidence. But the dossier documents neither a material belief revised after customer evidence nor recruiting that assigns exceptional owners to security, integrations, and automation reliability.

The largest kill risk is not whether distributors dislike spreadsheets. It is whether an agent can execute financially consequential workflows across heterogeneous systems with an error rate low enough to earn expanding authority. Whitespace provides human-review routing and correction-based learning, but no pass/fail threshold, experiment cost, or observed error trajectory. Expanding simultaneously across inventory, procurement, sales, service, finance, and planning risks funding feature surface area before proving trustworthy autonomy. Capital should first establish whether correction data creates a repeatable performance curve or merely produces customer-specific implementation labor.

The economics are promising but incomplete. A three-month customer payback would beat the incumbent labor cost without subsidy, yet pricing is undisclosed and there is no bottom-up model for deployment labor, inference cost, support burden, or gross margin across custom ERP integrations. Keeping each customer’s data out of cross-customer training may be correct for privacy, but it weakens the proposed learning moat: every account could remain its own integration project while competitors accumulate broader workflow knowledge.

My 36-month base case is a useful, integration-heavy vertical SaaS company at $3–8 million ARR, not an AI operating system with a compounding technical advantage. I pass with my own money today. One result flips the decision: an independently auditable cohort of at least ten distributors showing greater than 5x throughput, fewer than one financially material autonomous error per 10,000 transactions, and implementation payback within three months without increasing deployment labor per customer.
