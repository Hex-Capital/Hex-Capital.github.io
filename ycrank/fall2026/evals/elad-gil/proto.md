# Proto -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Proto is entering design software just as agents gain the ability to edit canvases and generate code; product teams become the buyer if this reduces the engineering work required to turn a design decision into a shipped interface. The controlling variable is the engineering time remaining after Proto submits a pull request. Generating more prototypes does not necessarily create a large business. Consistently removing implementation work could. The dossier establishes that engineers still complete the remaining connections, but supplies no measurement of that residual work. I can formulate the market thesis; I cannot yet underwrite it.

The timing is real, although the economic advantage remains unproven. Figma opened its canvas to agents in March 2026, and Magic Patterns introduced overlapping canvas and coding-agent handoff capabilities in October. These developments establish technical feasibility while narrowing Proto’s window of differentiation. The adjacent $590.38 million prototyping-market estimate does little to resolve the opportunity: capturing implementation spending could expand the boundary substantially, but that expansion requires demonstrated labor savings. There are no measured completion times, delivery costs, or pricing data supporting that step change.

The strongest bull case is that Proto retains the context that other tools lose between requirements, visual exploration, and engineering. Albert’s demonstrations describe generation from Linear tickets or Notion requirements, automatic interface states, comments that restore prototype state, and decomposition into submitted pull requests. Those are concrete workflow features, and they deserve more credit than a generic generation demo. Better models could make this workflow more useful while Proto retains the review history and design decisions. However, accepting requirements as inputs does not establish a persistent integration, and submitting code does not establish responsibility for the completed result. The evidence supports partial workflow ownership, with the most valuable boundary still unresolved.

Albert is unusually relevant to that boundary: Stamper combined visual artboards with programming, and his work includes being an Avenue founding engineer and launching Browserbase’s Director. That makes the product direction credible. It does not establish repeat-founder learning, personal sacrifice, or a prior founder exit. Similarly, alpha access through founder DMs is a sensible starting channel, but the 65 company-account followers and October 31 product coverage provide no evidence of recurring customer demand. I assign the missing-evidence defaults here; neither a small audience nor undisclosed revenue is evidence of failure.

I would pass with my own money. Proto fits my pattern of exploring several products around a technology discontinuity, but this market is already crystallizing around competitors with distribution. Its first product has not yet demonstrated a reusable customer channel. The relevant red flag is convergence: stronger models may improve Proto while also making its differentiating features cheaper for established tools to reproduce. The research-stage talent and physical-economics exceptions do not apply; this dossier documents an application workflow rather than frontier research or reconstructed industry margins. My one verifiable flip condition is a dated study across five paying teams showing that, for eight consecutive weeks, Proto-originated interfaces require at least 50% fewer engineering hours to reach production than those teams’ existing Figma-to-code workflow. That would test the variable that matters.
