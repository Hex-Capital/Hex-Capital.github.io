# OpenVector -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Andrey Gizdov’s CVPR 2025 research and Harvard withdrawal make OpenVector a high-conviction technical founder paired with a low-distribution business: OpenVector has a real intellectual asset, but no owned audience capable of pulling that asset into the market. Gizdov’s approximately 8K LinkedIn followers, 39 X followers, and undisclosed design partners are reach without demonstrated community. OpenVector therefore looks like a research lab searching for its venture shape, not yet a default-alive operating system for physical businesses.

Drop.io is the high rung for asymmetric conviction because I kept turning a technically awkward file-sharing primitive against free incumbents until the asset became legible to Facebook. OpenVector shares that mechanism: Gizdov worked on compute-efficient vision at Harvard, MIT, and Weizmann, published at CVPR, and then left Harvard to build OpenVector with a collaborator he had already won the MIT Grand Hack Google Prize alongside. OpenVector diverges from the high rung because the conviction currently points in three directions—camera-to-action automation, bio-inspired architectures, and the separate Foveal Vision compute-reduction product—so I see missionary intensity without evidence that the mission has collapsed into one durable tack.

Fin is the low rung for founder-owned distribution: an impressive Wizard-of-Oz operation still had to pivot into workplace analytics because manual intelligence did not create a community that pulled the product forward. OpenVector shows the same failure mechanism in earlier form. OpenVector offers 20-minute demonstrations, a waitlist, and refundable $100 reservations, but Gizdov’s 39 X followers and Urlam’s approximately 3K LinkedIn followers are not an owned customer channel; no newsletter, operator community, or engaged industry network predates OpenVector. The founder-as-brand asset is effectively absent, so I apply nearly the absent-evidence default.

The $500,000 YC investment tells me nothing about whether OpenVector is mispriced, while the words “AI,” “agent,” and “physical world” tell me the auction could become expensive very quickly. Over the next 18 months, that mechanism compounds badly: Spot AI has raised $93 million, Coram AI $66 million, and Ambient.ai more than $72 million, so OpenVector may be financed as a scarce physical-AI platform before OpenVector proves that camera-triggered actions are safer or more valuable than detection. No valuation, visible tier-one pass, unusual instrument, or below-batch pricing is disclosed; price discipline is the alpha here, so OpenVector receives the absent-evidence default rather than credit for merely being early.

The $449 monthly station or $6,499 upfront purchase is the most promising structural fact because OpenVector could become an appliance business with recurring software economics instead of a five-round SaaS regatta. The counterfactual that flips OpenVector into Invest is straightforward: station prepayments fund deployment, one narrow workflow reaches payback inside twelve months, and OpenVector becomes default-alive before institutional Series A capital. Today the $100 reservation is refundable, deliveries do not begin until fall 2026, and no paid deployment or revenue is disclosed, so OpenVector has a venture-shaped possibility rather than a demonstrated venture-shaped capital structure.

Sublime Security is the closest single-anchor parallel: an enterprise-security product bought before the next consolidation wave because operational complexity could become an illegible asset worth underwriting. OpenVector has an analogous wedge in connecting existing cameras to DRB SiteWatch, POS systems, calls, and webhooks, with compute-efficient vision research potentially lowering edge costs. OpenVector does not yet fit one of my revealed-preference sleeves, however: there is no manual Wizard-of-Oz operating history, creator distribution, crypto activity, DTC brand, or unusual holdco structure, while the current framing remains uncomfortably close to foundation-model infrastructure applied to cameras. AI is just more internet—too glib, obviously—but OpenVector still needs to prove that its physical integration burden creates a proprietary business rather than an expensive wrapper around improving VLMs.

Spot AI’s $93 million of funding makes my 36-month forecast specific: by July 2029, OpenVector will have abandoned the horizontal “any camera” operator and pivoted into licensing compute-efficient edge-vision models to camera or security-platform vendors, with less than $3 million in ARR. OpenVector’s strongest asset is Gizdov’s vision architecture, while OpenVector’s weakest asset is customer ownership; that pairing naturally pushes value upstream toward OEM technology rather than downstream toward a broad operational platform. The product-positioning split between OpenVector and Foveal Vision is already the first tack in that direction.

Ro is the exception that makes the bull case sting: capital intensity and regulatory complexity looked like founder drainage until prescription rails became the moat, and OpenVector’s autonomous-action risk, edge station, and industry integrations could create the same kind of venture moment. If OpenVector accumulates proprietary correction data across restaurants, car washes, warehouses, and gyms, the 92% confidence threshold could improve while switching costs compound, turning today’s deployment burden into tomorrow’s barrier. The dossier still gives me undisclosed design partners, refundable reservations, no proprietary dataset, and three conflicting product narratives; those facts beat the exception today. I would flip to Invest if OpenVector reports 25 paying multi-site operators using camera-triggered actions in production with at least 80% six-month customer retention.
