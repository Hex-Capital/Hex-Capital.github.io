# Orgo -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, a small-business owner might give a new worker a computer, a budget, and a task without hiring another human. That worker needs somewhere to leave its files overnight. Orgo is building that somewhere. Its October 19 templates-and-triggers release is a small, concrete artifact of this transition: repeatable environments that could turn individual agent sessions into continuing operations. The remaining steps are consequential—reliable unattended execution, bounded permissions, and economical recovery when something breaks. The dossier establishes the beginnings of that chain.

Why must someone assemble virtual machines, streaming, storage, and isolation before delegating ordinary computer work? Orgo says that assembly takes months. Its API gives developers control over a desktop without first becoming infrastructure operators, with subscriptions starting at $29 a month. That returns engineering time and makes experimentation accessible to smaller teams. But the dossier identifies an integration burden rather than a gatekeeper collecting an artificial rent. The agency returned is real; the economic magnitude remains unmeasured.

The founders have encountered this work from useful directions. Nick Vasilescu previously operated computeruse.agency; Spencer Kinney brings software engineering experience, and their Berkeley hacker-house relationship predates joining forces. Tiger Wang supplies a clearly identified technical counterpart. That is more persuasive than three biographies stapled to an AI slogan. What I cannot establish is a sustained sequence of pre-company experiments demonstrating independently derived obsession. The repositories show builders, but their star counts do not establish when or why that obsession began.

Orgo has also crossed the line from narrated demo to actual use. Peter Steinberger is identified as its first paying customer, and Phil describes agents operating inside three businesses. Those are more informative than follower counts. The reported 1,000-plus teams and thousands of production desktops suggest breadth, but provide no cohorts, duration, or expenditure with which to distinguish experimentation from dependency. I credit working infrastructure and payment; I cannot yet credit deeply repeated behavior. The disclosed paid-account freezes also matter: a worker whose computer disappears after a billing webhook error returns operating responsibility to the customer.

The strongest argument for writing a check is my recurring preference for the concealed infrastructure beneath a visible application. Agencies selling business agents could carry Orgo into many customers while the desktop becomes the durable asset underneath. That is compelling. Yet E2B and Daytona already offer overlapping environments, and open-source components make self-hosting possible. Orgo controls a functioning runtime, but the dossier does not demonstrate control over a scarce dependency. My exception for backing an early prototype before revenue would not resolve that question: payment already exists here; the missing evidence concerns durable advantage and founder depth. Nor should infrastructure enthusiasm waive the privacy gate. Customer-owned keys and VM isolation help, while screenshot transmission still requires scrutiny of consent, retention, and downstream access.

I am **Neutral** with my own money. Orgo is useful enough to investigate closely, but usefulness alone does not establish that it owns a necessary piece of the future. My flip condition is an independently documented customer migration test showing that a paying agency cannot economically reproduce its essential persistent workflows on E2B or Daytona and consequently expands its Orgo deployment. That would reveal the dependency currently asserted by the infrastructure story.
