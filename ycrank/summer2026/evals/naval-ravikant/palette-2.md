# Palette -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Palette’s 130+ regression tests reveal a code-leverage company without a network. Palette’s highest dimension is permissionless leverage: self-serve access, public APIs, and software routing work across Seedance, Veo, Kling, MiniMax, and LTX at near-zero replication cost. Palette’s lowest dimension is compounding user-added value: one creator’s generation does not make another creator’s generation better. This is an intelligent model router and creative interface, not yet a platform. Code scales. Aggregation commoditizes.

**Twitter** is the high rung because every new user created media for every prior user; distribution and network value were the same machine. Palette shares Twitter’s permissionless surface—anyone can try Palette, buy credits at $0.01 each, and reach Palette through an API—but Palette diverges where the leverage becomes rented. Seedance, Veo, Kling, MiniMax, and LTX own the underlying models, pricing, and availability. Palette’s reported 35% speed and 50% cost advantages are internal benchmarks, not structural control. Palette sits below the pure code-and-media architecture of Twitter and closer to an application-layer bet: highly scalable software, but software whose strongest inputs require upstream permission.

**Perplexity** is the low network rung: each user can receive value without making the next user’s product materially better. Perplexity survived that weakness because Aravind had rare retrieval knowledge, product distribution was permissionless, and category timing was pre-consensus. Palette has the same single-sided geometry but weaker evidence for the exception. Palette could accumulate routing outcomes, failures, refunds, and project context, yet the dossier labels that data advantage inferred and reports no usage scale. Shared credits are billing infrastructure, not a network. Unless Palette turns creators, reusable assets, templates, or workflow data into user-contributed supply, the Nth Palette customer adds revenue but little value for the first.

Serena Pei’s diffusion, computer-vision, and multimodal research at CSAIL, Media Lab, and LIDS gives Palette genuine specific knowledge. Over the next 18 months, that knowledge can compound through evaluation systems: Palette’s 130+ regression tests can improve model selection every time providers change quality, latency, or price. Josephine Lee’s Broad Institute software work strengthens the ability to build data products, but neither founder has a named creative tool, public repository, or multi-year video-production obsession predating Palette. Palette therefore has technical specific knowledge, especially in Pei, but not yet the founder-as-user inevitability of a builder who has been unable to stop building the product.

The $42.3B projected global AI-video market gives Palette room to pivot across marketing, entertainment, training, customer support, image generation, music, and structured content. For Palette to move from a broad market into a top-tier market score, the June HR-agent page would need to represent deliberate learning rather than opportunistic repositioning, and one adjacent workflow would need repeat usage without enterprise services. If Palette’s context layer became the operating system connecting source documents, storyboards, assets, video, and training material, the market would be platform-shaped. If Palette remains a multi-model video interface competing with Higgsfield’s $200M annual run rate and Krea’s integrated suite, the large TAM belongs mainly to the model owners.

**Notion** is the relevant 10-year-partner parallel: young founders can pass without a prior exit when depth appears through persistent building and clean behavior. Palette names Josephine Lee as CEO and Serena Pei as CTO, avoiding the unresolved-authority red flag, and Palette shows energy through a working paid product, public endpoints, and 130+ tests on $500K of funding. Intelligence is legible. Integrity is neither positively nor negatively established, so I apply the neutral prior rather than inventing character evidence. Palette’s web-identity confusion—the redirect to a domain-for-sale page and an indexed HR-agent page weeks before the multimedia launch—reads as execution disorder, not dishonesty, but long-term games require a cleaner trail.

Higgsfield’s $200M annual run rate makes the next 36 months legible: Palette will pivot toward enterprise training and customer-support content workflows and reach roughly $1M–$3M ARR, but Palette will not become a defining horizontal AI-video platform. The mechanism is the high-low signature. Permissionless code lets Palette ship and acquire some self-serve users cheaply; absent network effects and dependence on third-party models push Palette toward workflow integration, custom quotes, and service-assisted enterprise revenue. Palette becomes a useful business. Useful businesses and power-law angel investments are different species.

Palette’s strongest bull case is the **Perplexity** exception: a single-sided AI application can win when technical specific knowledge, permissionless distribution, and category timing overwhelm the missing network. Pei’s multimodal research, Palette’s public API, 50% claimed cost reduction, and a $42.3B forecast market make that case sting. The evidence that wins today is that Palette exposes no organic adoption, public technical community, customer count, revenue, or proprietary model; Palette also carries the red-flag shape of pedigree before artifact—MIT, Amazon, and research institutions are named more clearly than a shipped creative product users love. The unstated pattern is missing: no open-source contributions, founder media leverage, or community distribution predates the round. I would flip to Invest if Palette demonstrates six consecutive months of at least 25% organic self-serve revenue growth while showing that proprietary routing data—not enterprise labor—sustains its 35% speed and 50% cost advantages against Krea and Higgsfield.
