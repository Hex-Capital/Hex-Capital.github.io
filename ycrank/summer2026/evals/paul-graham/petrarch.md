# Petrarch -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The strongest fact is not that Petrarch calls itself a marketplace. It is that the founders sourced, redacted, and transferred several thousand construction plans in one week. That is a real artifact assembled before anyone granted them scale. But the dossier omits the crucial scene: what broke, which founder intervened, and what changed because of that intervention. The week-long execution earns substantial resourcefulness credit, not the maximum. I can see the running back moving; I cannot yet see whether he changed direction after being hit.

The idea did not come merely from noticing that AI needs data. Samuel Hahn worked on PII-redaction and compliance products, Ian Lee built training-data infrastructure and advised Scale AI, and Sudhish Swain worked with hospital computer vision and cancer datasets. They encountered both sides of the problem before starting Petrarch. More importantly, they chose the ugly part: locating obscure owners, establishing chain of title, restructuring heterogeneous files, removing PII, and negotiating a transfer. Legal clearance and redaction are not decorations on this business. They are the business. That is exactly the sort of schlep that can hide an opportunity from people who see only a $4.44 billion market forecast.

The technical proof is real but incomplete. Petrarch has completed transactions involving construction plans, autonomous-driving recordings, and codebases, and its in-house workflow processed thousands of files quickly. Yet transfer volume is not dataset utility. There is no measured improvement to a buyer’s model, no independently tested redaction accuracy, and no evidence that the same buyer returned. The reported customer range—from first-time founders to Fortune 500 enterprises—is too broad and anonymous to identify the narrow well. Completed transactions beat a waitlist, but one repeat paid buyer would teach me more than 1,091 LinkedIn followers.

The bear case is that Petrarch is a services-heavy broker temporarily disguised as a marketplace. Suppliers can transact directly after discovery, providers retain responsibility for submission rights, and Troveo, Defined.ai, Datarade, and Cloudflare already overlap with parts of the workflow. Hiring remote fellows to source buyers and sellers is especially dangerous if the founders are delegating customer discovery before learning it themselves. The dropout story does not rescue them, either. Their relevant work experience and shipped transactions offset the age concern, but they do not yet have the independently measured technical result that would make youth irrelevant.

Still, the pre-round proof spike matters. These founders have already made three types of dormant assets cross organizational and legal boundaries, including one batch of several thousand plans in a week. My 36-month expectation is that Petrarch reaches $3–6 million in annualized net revenue as a high-touch industrial-data broker, rather than becoming a self-serve marketplace. The missing evidence is adaptability: the dossier contains no rejected hypothesis or product route changed after buyer feedback. I would write a small check because the founders have entered the schlep and shipped through it, but not a large one. One named AI buyer making a second paid purchase and reporting a measured model-performance gain would flip this to a strong investment.
