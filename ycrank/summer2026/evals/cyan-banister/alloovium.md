# Alloovium -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Every construction decision is traceable to a clause, drawing, email, and site record; compliance paperwork assembles continuously instead of consuming 40–50 people at submission time. Getting there requires trusted ingestion, source-level provenance, institutional acceptance of AI-assisted records, and project memory that improves across jobs. Alloovium has early pieces of that chain—sentence-level citations, read-only integrations, review-gated routines—but the dossier does not show that it owns the transition. The $2.5 million, 400-page management-plan example proves an extravagant present-day leak. It does not yet prove that Alloovium is the inevitable layer beneath the future.

The leak itself is wonderfully concrete. Contractors pay people to reconcile disconnected contracts, drawings, specifications, correspondence, and management systems because errors create contractual and professional liability. Alloovium returns some of that time to project teams through clause-by-clause checks and cited drafts while preserving human sign-off. That is more interesting than generic “construction is inefficient” language. The remaining ambiguity is who captures the current rent and whether the product removes a structural gate or merely makes an unavoidable compliance function cheaper.

Zander Schweitzer has worked on civil-marine sites, and Cielo Nicolosi brings machine-learning training, four founding-engineer roles, and 48 public repositories. Zander recruited complementary talent after Startmate, and the two moved to Sydney to build together. Those are real acts of commitment. They are not yet evidence of a long pre-company obsession: the dossier shows no dated construction-software experiments, customer-support scars, or prior artifact that independently pulled either founder toward this exact problem. Zander is clearly presented as CEO, so I see no leadership shell game, but the ownership and tiebreaker arrangement remain undisclosed rather than affirmatively safe.

The strongest case against my caution is that institutions are already taking the artifact seriously. It is deployed across more than $400 million of active projects, including a BMD subsidiary, and a 30-year auditor is helping test the ISO-compliance product. SOC 2 Type I certification, encrypted read-only access, source tracing, and mandatory review address the privacy and liability gates more thoughtfully than most pre-seed AI products. These deployments could be the first strange behavior: contractors entrusting a young company with consequential project records before the company has publicly disclosed pricing or revenue.

But deployment value is not the same as habitual user behavior. The reported tenfold increase in professionals lacks a starting count, the subscription terms prove that Alloovium can charge rather than that customers have paid or renewed, and no evidence shows teams repeatedly running routines, reducing submission labor, or expanding across projects. Meanwhile, Trunk Tools, Document Crunch inside Trimble, and Nomic already overlap the retrieval, drafting, compliance, and drawing layers. Organization-specific correction memory could become a controlled asset, but today Alloovium depends on Procore, Autodesk, Aconex, SharePoint, and other systems without demonstrating a scarce technical bottleneck those platforms cannot absorb.

I am neutral with my own money. This is credible, unglamorous workflow repair with unusually solid institutional proof for its age, but it currently looks more like a useful construction-software feature set than a six-year enabling layer. One verifiable condition would flip me: three unrelated general contractors renewing and expanding Alloovium after independently documenting at least a 50% reduction in management-plan labor. That would convert the $2.5 million wound from a vivid anecdote into repeated costly behavior—and show that the correction memory compounds outside one friendly deployment.
