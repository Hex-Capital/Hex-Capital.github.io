# Decent -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Decent’s potentially important claim is that a small employer’s insurance renewal can be changed by intervening in claims throughout the year. What would prove that wrong? Comparable employers receiving ordinary annual brokerage advice would achieve the same renewal outcomes after adjusting for coverage and funding arrangement. The dossier supplies no such comparison and identifies no prevailing belief that Decent has overturned. The reported $60,000 savings at Serif Health and more than $100,000 at Locate demonstrate useful plan shopping; they do not yet establish the proposed secret about continuous intervention.

The decisive problem is ownership of the result. Nava already offers year-round advice, AI tools, and bill audits. Comparing more funding arrangements than Thatch distinguishes the service, but does not establish proprietary technology, network effects, or exclusive access. Decent’s licensed brokerage and quoting relationships enable participation in this market. They supply no documented mechanism for dominating it. This is affirmative competitive overlap, rather than merely an undisclosed moat, and it fails my monopoly gate.

The founding relationship is substantially more encouraging. Soman and Budker met building Kindle approximately sixteen years before this launch. That is meaningful evidence of prior collaboration, well beyond assembling a team for an application deadline. Soman’s earlier health-plan business and Budker’s production AI background also fit the repeat-operator pattern I favor. But the September 2026 company is a new entity: the earlier business’s customers and economics cannot be carried forward. Full-time status, ownership, and compensation remain unverified. Indexed affiliations elsewhere are not sufficient evidence of part-time work, and prior acquisitions alone do not establish an intention to sell this company.

The strongest objection to passing is that Decent may already have changed brokerage’s cost structure. A two-person team reports working with 67 companies and automating 89% of brokerage work. If that automation claim survives measurement, experienced operators could deliver substantial service with very little incremental labor. The named customer testimonials make the bull case harder to dismiss. Yet the automation percentage has no published methodology, and the customer savings establish neither a technical discontinuity nor an advantage competitors cannot reproduce. The evidence supports a potentially efficient brokerage more strongly than a last mover.

Distribution is concrete enough to deserve some credit: employers can request quotes, consult the founder, receive renewal reminders, and generate rewarded referrals. Those routes address an identifiable purchase occasion. But referrals are rewarded for eligible quotes, while revenue depends on variable provider commissions and bonuses. Without quote-to-binding conversion, acquisition cost, or commission yield, I cannot connect the channel to profitable customer acquisition. There is one coherent revenue engine here, but no disclosed account economics from which to derive a power-law outcome. Employer premium savings are not Decent’s revenue.

I pass on the current evidence. The finance-oriented and repeat-operator patterns favor taking this team seriously; the documented competitive overlap remains decisive. None of my exception mechanisms removes that risk: there is no independently verified technical breakthrough or customer behavior establishing a position competitors cannot contest. Claims review also excludes fully insured and PEO arrangements, limiting the reach of the central product promise. One verifiable condition would reopen diligence: an executed carrier agreement granting Decent exclusive claims access for a commercially meaningful employer segment. That would provide a concrete mechanism for retaining the value its service creates.
