# Composal -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Composal is attacking a bottleneck that could become more expensive as code becomes cheaper: humans still have to determine whether the software works. Removing that constraint could multiply engineering capacity. But the dossier gives no baseline for verification spending, human hours displaced, or the fraction of releases agents could verify autonomously. I can identify the consequence-of-success hypothesis. I cannot yet quantify the option I am buying.

The technical question is whether autonomous exploration finds important failures that existing testing misses, reliably enough to replace human work. Composal’s local benchmark passed 157 of 166 attempts and recorded one false success. That is useful engineering disclosure. It establishes neither a 5–10x advantage over Momentic or mabl nor reliability on unseen applications. A false declaration of success is particularly dangerous here: customers could remove the human check while retaining the defect. Persistent journey maps might improve performance, but the dossier does not establish a proprietary learning advantage. The AI-wrapper red flag remains an unresolved concern rather than a demonstrated diagnosis.

I give the team modest credit for testing a consequential uncertainty and publishing the limitations. Its October research also exposed a concrete deployment barrier: authentication or network restrictions stalled half of ten setup calls. These are useful experiments for a three-person company. They tell the founders where the product breaks. They do not yet constitute a risk-removal plan with a ranked existential uncertainty, a decisive threshold, an experiment budget, and an explicit response to failure. The next dollars should purchase evidence about trustworthy verification on unfamiliar applications.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ proximity to the problem. Zembashis’s Cosine CTO experience, Davies’s product engineering background, and Moehlmann’s commercial experience form a credible combination for building and distributing this product. Nine interviewed teams had already built internal QA agents; that is evidence of an existing need and a potential customer-built substitute. CLI, MCP, and repository integrations provide a plausible platform entry point. This fits my pattern of backing small teams around important technical workflows. However, eight stars on the associated Roder harness do not establish a committed technical community, and the source-control product history does not document why the founders changed their beliefs. Relevant experience earns attention; the specific evidence of learning and difficult recruiting remains absent.

The economics need the same discipline. A $500 subscription including 3,000 browser minutes is a price, not a production-cost model. Extra minutes at $1 each reveal little about inference costs, retries, human review, or customer setup labor. The economically meaningful denominator is a correctly verified release. Composal could sell inexpensive browser activity while leaving expensive human assurance intact. There is no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence, but there is also no demonstrated path to replacing the incumbent workflow at lower total cost.

I pass with my own money today. My exceptions for unproven products require an extraordinary instigator addressing a quantified enormous value pool, or visible capability progress sufficient to justify the uncertainty. This dossier establishes neither override. It does establish a technically relevant team asking an important question. My flip condition is one independently audited production study across ten previously unseen customer applications showing at least a 10x reduction in fully burdened verification cost versus QA Wolf, with equal or better escaped-defect rates over 90 days. That would connect capability, consequence, and economics in one result worth financing.
