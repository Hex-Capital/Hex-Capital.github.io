# Risklytics -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Risklytics could remove a real bottleneck to deploying robots: customers cannot use machines they cannot insure. That consequence matters more than faster insurance applications. But the dossier does not quantify how many deployments are blocked, what productive capacity they represent, or how much insurance costs must fall to unlock them. The adjacent forecast of $4.8 billion in annual AI insurance premiums by 2032 measures spending on insurance, not economic value Risklytics can capture. I see a potentially consequential constraint. I do not yet see evidence of an orders-of-magnitude consequence.

The strongest evidence is that the founders move. Gold and Risio abandoned wildfire modeling during YC, built a brokerage, and [reported obtaining coverage for three previously declined companies](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49451495). Risio acquired producer licensing rather than waiting for the insurance industry to cooperate. Those are concrete changes in activity, although the dossier does not establish what evidence drove the pivot or demonstrate difficult complementary recruiting. The placements also remove a little existential uncertainty: some carriers will cover these risks. They do not establish repeatable capacity, acceptable exclusions, or a program that spends against the largest risk with explicit thresholds and a bounded budget.

The product currently drafts applications and supports licensed humans who approach insurers and inspect wording. The [736-filings exclusion tracker](https://www.risklytics.ai/ai-exclusions/) is useful work. Neither establishes a measured technical discontinuity against RiskCube, Koop, or conventional brokerage. I would not infer that the founders lack technical ability; I would assign the missing-benchmark default. The decisive dependency is external underwriting authority. If Risklytics makes submissions dramatically easier while carriers retain the same exclusions, pricing, and appetite, the company has accelerated the paperwork around the bottleneck.

The bull case deserves more weight than “another insurance broker.” Three successful placements after earlier declines suggest that specialized interpretation and carrier access can unlock transactions that generalists lose. Almost 50 application users suggest a reachable customer base, although they are not 50 bound policies. My unstated willingness to back execution-heavy financial businesses applies here, and insurance can become infrastructure for physical autonomy. But the known-exception override still needs a credible mechanism connecting this wedge to a much larger value pool. The dossier establishes neither proprietary loss learning nor exclusive capacity. The relevant red flag is differentiation resting on application automation and implementation work; there is insufficient evidence to accuse the team of merely wrapping a standard model.

I would pass with my own money today. Carrier commissions of 10–20% and no brokerage surcharge establish how Risklytics gets paid, not whether total customer cost falls or service economics scale. There is no disclosed placement-cost model, so economic parity receives the absent-evidence default; there is also no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence. One result would reopen my decision: a carrier-backed, independently audited pilot showing that a proprietary Risklytics risk model cuts the full cost of insuring comparable robot deployments by at least 5x without narrowing coverage or subsidizing premiums. That would demonstrate a mechanism for changing deployment economics. Today, the evidence supports capable, adaptable brokers serving an emerging need.
