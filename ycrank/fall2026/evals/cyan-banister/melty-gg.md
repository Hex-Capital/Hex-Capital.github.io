# Melty -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years out, I can imagine friends treating games as ingredients: someone invents a ridiculous combination, sends a link, and everyone plays that evening. Melty’s agent-assisted publishing and multiplayer invitations are small pieces of that world already visible. The interesting transition is from installing someone else’s mod to making and distributing your own play experience. But easier creation must lead to reliable shared play, and shared play must survive publisher restrictions. The dossier establishes the beginnings of that path, not its completion.

Why does assembling the fun require so much housekeeping? Melty’s Windows launcher downloads mashups, installs dependencies, and launches games people already own. That is useful work. Yet the installation burden is inferred rather than measured, and owning a game does not confer permission to modify it. Melty has not demonstrated that it removes the underlying gatekeeper. Meanwhile, its October 18 terms require exclusive marketplace distribution during listing and for twelve months afterward. Creators retain ownership, but lose distribution freedom. That makes me less comfortable with the story that this becomes their playground.

David Miller’s prior coaching of millions of gamers, reported by YC, gives him meaningful contact with the audience. I would rather see that than a founder who discovered gaming through an AI market report. It earns some founder credit. What I cannot establish is the pre-company obsession expressed through dated experiments, successive builds, or recruiting others into an independently discovered idea. The dossier’s conflicting team counts do not tell me whether he can recruit, and the archived prior company names do not establish opportunism. Those questions remain open.

The strongest argument for Melty is that strange consumer behavior can become legible before the business model does. A downloadable product, creator publishing, public lobbies, and a reported 10,611-member Discord give that possibility somewhere to happen. Free access is perfectly sensible at this stage. But the Discord figure is a third-party snapshot from October 26, not evidence that people repeatedly create mashups or rearrange their evenings to play them. I can waive revenue when a working artifact and unusually strong founder evidence make the future tangible; here, neither repeated user behavior nor that deeper founder record is established. My pattern of backing hidden infrastructure offers limited additional support: installation tooling occupies an enabling position, but the dossier demonstrates no scarce compatibility asset Melty controls.

Privacy could change the decision more abruptly than monetization. The recording allegations reported by Dexerto are unverified; I will not turn them into a finding of misconduct. The October 12 policy does describe selected launcher-window recordings, excluding games and hiding typed input. Those limits matter, but they do not establish explicit consent or data custody. For software invited onto a gamer’s computer, that architecture belongs inside the product thesis. Nor does contractual catalogue exclusivity substitute for a technical advantage over Nexus Mods, Overwolf, or mod.io.

I pass with my own money today. Melty has a recognizable future hiding inside a practical launcher, but the evidence stops before the behavior that would make me believe. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an independently checked eight-week creator cohort in which a majority publish a second mashup and bring returning players with them. That would show creators choosing to spend more labor here after the novelty wears off.
