# Hemlock -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Julian Olschwang has built something worth examining. I still cannot tell what he does when he gets stuck. Hemlock identifies a concrete limitation—video demonstrations omit force—and proposes a wrist-worn EMG device that avoids covering the hand. But the dossier contains no episode showing an obstacle, Julian’s response, and how quickly that response worked. The bracelet earns credit as a technical artifact. Inferring resourcefulness from its existence would count the same evidence twice.

The technical result is the strongest reason to take Hemlock seriously. The company reports **10× better egg pick-and-place success and 2.7× better lightbulb installation** when demonstrations include Bracelet data. Those are sensible tasks: seeing where a hand moves does not necessarily tell a robot how hard to squeeze. Yet these are company-reported comparisons without absolute success rates, trial counts, or independent replication in the dossier. A tenfold improvement can describe very different products depending on where it starts. I give this meaningful, discounted technical credit.

Hemlock also fits a pattern I like: a technical founder building a potentially useful input for other builders. But a programmable primitive becomes interesting when people start depending on it. Here, the named audience is manipulation researchers and robotics data teams; there is no documented repeat use by a particular lab. The inferred licensing business could accumulate a valuable corpus, but neither collection agreements nor dataset exclusivity are established. MANUS offers another capture route, while FACT’s EgoForce research shows that force supervision is attracting independent effort. Hemlock needs to discover which data a lab repeatedly wants enough to obtain from this particular supplier.

The strongest argument against passing is Julian himself. His **2024 Davidson Fellowship for a sign-language glove**, together with his UCLA work on robotic training data, gives him relevant experience across both halves of this problem. A young founder with that background and measurable hardware results could be much further along than the public record reveals. The known exceptions for impressive prototypes and validated technical proof deserve consideration. Here, however, the reported task gains do not establish independent validation or unmistakable user pull strong enough to replace the missing founder evidence. His prior work establishes competence; it does not document how he encountered this particular need. Likewise, there is no evidence-driven product revision to score, and no evidence of obstinacy to penalize.

I would **pass today**. Hemlock falls below my resourcefulness gate, despite a promising technical signal. Dropping out supplies no additional evidence either way. The launch film is not evidence of demand, but neither does it establish that Julian mistakes publicity for distribution; no disclosed dependence on a major partner triggers that red flag. One verifiable result would change my decision: a named robotics lab documenting a collection problem, Julian shipping a workaround within weeks, and that lab subsequently reordering the resulting force-annotated data. That would connect the founder’s behavior to a customer’s need—the missing link in this otherwise interesting machine.
