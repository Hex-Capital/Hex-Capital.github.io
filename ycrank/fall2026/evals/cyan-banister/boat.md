# Boat -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, an agent’s computer should feel like a workshop: leave a job unfinished, return tomorrow, find the tools and materials where you left them. Getting there requires dependable persistence, safe execution of untrusted code, and environments that survive changes in the agent software above them. Boat has a small artifact from that world: OpenInspect announced support for its images, tunnels, and filesystem snapshots on October 23. Another product is beginning to treat Boat as its execution layer. That earns my attention; “billions of agents” alone would not. The remaining distance is making those environments dependable enough to entrust with ongoing work.

Why should every agent developer become a part-time cloud operator? Boat packages Ubuntu, Chrome, Docker, and fleet management into something a developer can provision directly. That returns engineering time and control. Its quoted default price of $0.036 an hour, versus $0.331 for E2B and Daytona, suggests a substantial cost difference, although the comparison is vendor-authored and configurations need verification. The dossier identifies infrastructure assembly work and a compute bill; it does not identify an artificial restriction or establish who captures an excessive rent. I see useful friction removal, with less evidence of a system whose rules Boat can overturn.

The founders have encountered this problem from inside the workload. Nougaret reports building cloud coding agents before extracting their infrastructure into Box, and his public Rust deep-learning framework is a tangible technical artifact. Five years of knowing his co-founder also matters more to me than their school names. But the record does not establish a long, independently pursued obsession with execution infrastructure or unusual recruiting ability. The October 24 claim of more than 1,000 active paying users and the reported six-figure ARR show customers spending money on the current product. They remain founder-reported signals without cohorts, workload histories, or evidence of repeated dependence. Payment gets my attention; watching someone repeatedly trust Boat with an unfinished job would tell me considerably more.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that Boat fits two patterns I like: an operating wound becoming a product, and an unglamorous enabling layer beneath a fashionable application wave. Separate kernels, nested virtualization, and dedicated AMD Ryzen hosts show actual engineering beneath the interface. The objection that stings is that Google already offers agent sandboxes and snapshots, while Modal has added VM sandboxes. Boat could execute well, attract customers, and still have its price advantage competed away. It controls an implementation, but the dossier establishes no scarce resource or technical dependency that competitors must come through Boat to obtain. My base case is a useful specialist provider; becoming indispensable requires another step.

I would also inspect what happens to the workshop keys. European compute with snapshot copies permitted outside the EU leaves custody questions for customers, and stopped disks disappear after 14 days unless preserved through snapshots. Neither fact establishes severe privacy abuse, but both matter to the promise of a persistent computer. SOC 2 being in progress is ordinary at this stage. The dossier names a CEO and CTO and supplies no evidence of a leadership shell game or equal-control deadlock. My willingness to back a prototype before revenue does not resolve the central uncertainty here: Boat already has reported revenue, while evidence of durable dependence remains thin.

I am **Neutral** with my own money today. The product is real enough to take seriously, and the founder’s route into it makes sense. One verifiable result would flip me toward investing: a paid customer cohort retaining at least 80% of its workloads for 90 days after a comparable competitor matches Boat’s effective price. That would show developers are keeping their workshops here for something deeper than cheap rent.
