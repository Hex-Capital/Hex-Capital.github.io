# 0.security -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

0.security has shown it can find vulnerabilities. It has not shown what my check buys. Post-money valuation, competing term sheets and whether this is the first scarce check are undisclosed. So is the capital-funded experiment: no budget, deadline or pass/fail milestone connects this round to a specific change in business value. I will not infer an auction from YC membership, but I cannot infer a cheap deal either. Both financing dimensions receive their missing-evidence defaults.

The founders are the strongest part of this dossier. Doruk’s cyber-defense work at Migros, credited JSONata vulnerability research and shipped security tools connect operational security needs with software development. Fabian brings institutional-finance experience and reports a prior exit, although the dossier gives little detail about that business’s operating results. This partly fits my pattern of backing people who translate between systems: developers want to ship; security teams need defensible evidence that shipping is safe. The advisory is an actual receipt. The reported exits add less without knowing what was built and sold.

The question is who owns the valuable information after 0.security performs that translation. Persistent repository context and investigation history could become useful private assets, but the dossier does not establish exclusive access, measurable compounding or customer dependence. The 526 GitHub stars and 58 forks show interest in a public tool. The Spotify engineer’s report of scanning almost 30,000 files shows someone used it; it does not establish Spotify procurement, retained usage or a purchase caused by proprietary access. Reported EY work deserves diligence, but its commercial scope is unspecified. These facts do not yet establish a dark pool that causes customers to buy.

The strongest objection to passing is that security research can generate its own distribution: discover a real vulnerability, earn developer trust, enter the repository and expand into recurring enterprise work. Doruk’s advisory credit and the team’s reported enterprise work make that mechanism credible. **NanoCo** is a relevant exception because open-source security adoption and enterprise demand justified overriding my usual price discipline. The material difference is that its evidence included thousands of users, early enterprise customers and a rejected acquisition offer. Here, the commercial bridge remains unverified. Aikido’s overlapping code-security and autonomous-testing products also mean that useful orchestration alone does not establish durable ownership of the customer.

I would separate that competitive concern from an accusation that the product cannot work. Deterministic scanning, execution sandboxes and vulnerability verification are substantive design choices; the dossier does not show that margins depend on errors disappearing. But the $30 subscription, $50 monthly free usage and credit pricing reveal the charging mechanism, not contribution profit. Inference costs, support burden, burn and break-even requirements are absent, so economics receives its default. Delete the next financing from the model and there is no documented cash plan to evaluate.

I pass on the evidence available. The technical team merits attention; the investment case has not connected its work to scarce capital, owned demand and financing independence. My verifiable flip condition is completion of a seed-funded, six-month enterprise experiment, available to me below a $10 million post-money valuation, in which three named customers renew and their recurring gross profit covers the company’s operating burn. That would turn the proposed workflow advantage into a demonstrated business and show exactly what the initial capital proved.
