# Resin Technologies -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: a small robotics team describes a machine, receives editable electronics designs, and orders boards that work on the first fabrication run. Resin Technologies has pieces of that world on display: requirements-to-schematic generation, datasheet-to-footprint creation, and rule-constrained routing. The difficult transitions are reliable electrical verification and demonstrated agreement between the design and the manufactured board. The dossier establishes neither transition. I can imagine the destination; Resin has not yet supplied the evidence connecting its present artifact to that future.

The founders give me the strongest reason to keep looking. Koosha Rezaiezadeh has worked in robotics, haptics, and electronics at A&K Robotics, Intelligent Haptronic Solutions, Bosch, and Kodak. Anmol Jawandha brings enterprise AI deployment experience from You.com and a public biped-simulation project, `locomotion`. These are complementary skills for a product that must understand both instructions and physical consequences. Their relationship also predates the company, beginning at UBC. What remains missing is the particular obsession: which boards did they struggle to build, what did they repeatedly try before Resin, and what convinced another engineer to follow them? Relevant experience earns credit; it does not establish that history.

Why does an engineer have to buy another manufacturing cycle to discover a mistake? That is the ordinary, irritating question here. Resin could return time and design control by making errors visible before fabrication. But the dossier identifies labor and rework without quantifying the delay or identifying an artificial restriction or rent collector. Much of this friction may be the cost of getting physics right. Resin’s terms acknowledge incorrect wiring, unsuitable components, and misread datasheets. If reviewing generated work consumes the time saved drawing it, the apparent transfer of agency disappears.

The strongest argument against passing is that Resin fits my pattern of finding useful infrastructure underneath a visible wave. Its own editor and company-described proprietary routing engine represent engineering beyond access to general-purpose models. Better electronics tooling could serve robotics, industrial equipment, and other physical products. That possibility matters. Yet the dossier does not demonstrate that Resin controls a scarce constraint: routing remains in beta, comparative performance is unavailable, and Flux already offers an integrated ECAD workspace with AI editing. My exception for pre-revenue technical companies requires other decisive evidence, such as validated prototypes, committed industrial users, or unusually well-documented founder obsession. Resin’s dossier does not supply that substitute.

I also cannot turn a price into a customer. The $199 monthly Pro plan and $149-per-user Team plan establish an offer; the October 2026 launch and 176 LinkedIn followers establish visibility. Neither shows engineers repeatedly trusting Resin with consequential work. Customer-design privacy is a separate gate: disclosed model providers and a prohibition on training on customer designs are encouraging architectural commitments, while unfinished independent security reviews leave custody diligence open. There is no documented severe privacy violation, founder-control deadlock, or leadership shell game here, and I will not invent one.

I pass with my own money today, at **39/100**. The founders make a credible specialist engineering business plausible, but the evidence does not yet support the enabling-layer bet I would underwrite. My forecast is that by October 2029, Resin will still require engineers to approve its designs and will have less than $2 million ARR. One verifiable result would change my decision: a documented production pilot in which the same independent hardware team uses Resin across three successive board releases, demonstrates fewer fabrication respins, and renews a paid subscription. That would show the product changing an expensive real-world habit.
