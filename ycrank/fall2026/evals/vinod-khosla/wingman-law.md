# Wingman Law -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Wingman Law addresses a $57.8 billion legal-services pool. That establishes existing spending. The investment question is how much legal expertise becomes cheaper, faster, or newly accessible if its agents work. The dossier supplies no estimate of underserved claimants reached, attorney capacity multiplied, or client costs reduced. Automating personal injury representation could matter enormously. Today, the consequence is an unquantified possibility, which earns my absent-evidence score rather than credit for a breakthrough.

Owning the practice gives Wingman control over whether automation actually changes delivery. That is a useful design choice. But Supio already markets agents spanning intake through resolution, and incumbent firms can purchase overlapping capabilities. Wingman supplies no measured advantage against those alternatives: no attorney-hours benchmark, error rate, or case-throughput comparison. Its public visualization uses licensed litigation data. A proprietary learning loop from insurer responses and recoveries is conceivable, but remains an inference. I see neither demonstrated technical discontinuity nor evidence sufficient to dismiss the product as a generic wrapper.

The strongest argument for investing is the founders’ combination of operating knowledge and direct control. Nicholas Le’s 700-plus prior injury cases expose him to the workflow’s actual failure points. Sun Woo Lee has litigation experience and reportedly sold $350,000 of legal technology during law school. Those are prior accomplishments, not Wingman revenue. Lee recruiting Le as her first hire and subsequently making him a co-founder is an observed team-building action, although it does not establish rapid belief revision or exceptional technical recruiting. My broader pattern of backing execution-heavy businesses makes this relevant. **Block** is the applicable exception: an ordinary initial product can become a platform by controlling delivery within a giant spending pool. Wingman shares that potential starting mechanism; the material difference is that this dossier provides no evidence of expanding capability or changing economics beyond the initial service.

The first dollars should determine whether attorney supervision consumes the labor the agents supposedly eliminate. Wingman describes attorney-led oversight, but discloses no decisive experiment, threshold, budget, or response to failure. California’s accuracy and confidentiality obligations make that test consequential; they are engineering requirements, not reasons to reject legal automation. Contingency billing also leaves the economic question unanswered. No upfront fee is an established payment arrangement, not evidence of lower production cost. Attorney review, acquisition expense, case costs, unsuccessful matters, and the delay before recovery all belong in the calculation. Missing current revenue is normal at this stage; missing proof of those economics leaves the investment thesis unestablished.

I pass at 27/100. The technical risk I like is uncertainty about achieving a consequential capability; Wingman has yet to define that capability quantitatively. Its branded-car advertising does not establish the red flag of scaling acquisition before testing product risk, and representing injury victims does not fall into my excluded advertising-tool or debt-collection categories. Nor does the dossier establish the scientific progress or quantified societal consequence that justified my other exceptions. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independently audited, matched cohort of resolved injury cases showing at least 5x lower attorney-and-staff hours per case, comparable net client recoveries, no increase in material errors, and positive contribution after acquisition and case costs. That would turn an automation proposition into evidence of a different legal-delivery system.
