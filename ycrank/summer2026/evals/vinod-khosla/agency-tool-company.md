# Agency Tool Company -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Jack Morrison and Davis Foster spent eight years building and deploying hundreds of Scythe Robotics machines before its 2026 acquisition. That makes **Agency Tool Company** a high-learning-rate founder profile trapped inside a low-capital, modest-consequence business. **Agency Tool Company** knows the problem unusually well, but knowledge is not consequence. The economic mechanism that breaks is SSH-and-Docker-based robot deployment—not agriculture, construction, logistics, or the labor embodied in those industries. “Twenty times faster” is an optimization claim. My first question is what disappears if they win. Today, the answer is some scripts and spreadsheets.

**Slash** is the high rung on founder learning rate because its documented Kanye West pivot demonstrated that the founders could absorb reality and rewrite the thesis. Morrison and Foster share the repeat-founder mechanism: they shipped hundreds of robots, completed the Scythe acquisition, and started **Agency Tool Company** to build the platform they had lacked. That experience places **Agency Tool Company** above a merely linear first-time team. The divergence from Slash is important: the dossier documents adjacency, not a moment when Morrison or Foster admitted “we were wrong” and changed direction. I score the learning evidence strongly, but I will not manufacture a pivot that is not there.

**DoorDash** is the low rung on required-capital fit: capital-light execution with no scientific miracle, a profile that normally makes me over-resourced for the deal. **Agency Tool Company** has the same mismatch without DoorDash’s demonstrated ambition to reorganize consumer commerce. Deploy is beta, Build is “coming soon,” and nothing in **Agency Tool Company** requires a $50M first check, a $115M science round, or even a disclosed hardware fleet. Commodity software economics are not intrinsically bad. They simply mean any competent generalist can fund the company, while my capital adds little.

The $703.46M fleet-management market, forecast to reach $1.91B by 2032, constrains the consequence mechanism unless **Agency Tool Company** expands far beyond deployment tooling. Over the next 18 months, histories, device configurations, and failure handling could compound into switching costs across Burro, Tempo Works, and Gather AI. That compounding would make **Agency Tool Company** valuable infrastructure, but value is not civilizational consequence. The current product accelerates delivery of containers, models, configurations, and operating-system images; it does not yet automate the physical labor performed by the robots. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself” does not mean make a better transfer protocol and extrapolate a great company from it.

ATC Deploy’s delta updates, resumable downloads, fleet targeting, and rollback place **Agency Tool Company** above a generic GPT wrapper on technical substance, but below capital-hostile science. The score flips a tier only if **Agency Tool Company** proves that reliable heterogeneous robot deployment requires proprietary safety verification, hardware-in-the-loop simulation, or autonomous recovery that Formant, InOrbit, Balena, and internal platform teams cannot reproduce. Today the named miracle count is zero. Deployment safety is difficult engineering, but the dossier identifies no new physics, foundation model, proprietary dataset, patent, or technical barrier. A 20-times-faster transfer is falsifiable and useful; it is not QuantumScape.

Davis Foster is an RIT dropout and 2019 Thiel Fellow, but he and Morrison are also career robotics operators applying the playbook learned at Scythe. **Glean** is the relevant single-anchor parallel: Arvind Jain’s domain-expert continuity was acceptable because the consequence and capital scale could overwhelm my skepticism of experts. **Agency Tool Company** receives credit for Foster rejecting the credential path and for both founders building real machines, yet **Agency Tool Company** has not documented a controversial technical thesis that robotics experts dismiss. The founders are defying employment convention more clearly than expert consensus. Experts extrapolate the past; so far, these founders are productizing their past.

Three launch partners give **Agency Tool Company** enough founder-customer fit to build a real business. My 36-month forecast is that **Agency Tool Company** reaches $2M-$5M ARR as a respected robotics deployment tool and is acquired by InOrbit before becoming an independent category-defining platform. The mechanism is the dimensional signature: exceptional operator learning converts painful field experience into a good product quickly, while low required capital, feature overlap, and a $1.91B forecast market compress the independent outcome. Useful infrastructure gets bought. Civilizational infrastructure buys industries.

Burro’s partner fleet exceeds 750 machines, and the strongest opposing case is the **DoorDash** exception: a capital-light YC company can begin with an operational wedge and become the default coordination layer for a much larger economic system. Morrison and Foster have an exit, eight years of shared execution, hundreds of deployed robots, and three cross-industry users—precisely the quiet prior-exit pattern I often favor despite my rhetoric. **Agency Tool Company** could become the deployment control plane for physical AI as robot fleets scale from thousands to millions. The objection stings, but the dossier still shows overlap with Formant and InOrbit, no structural barrier, no revenue, and a product framed around updates rather than labor displacement. **Agency Tool Company** flips my verdict if it reaches at least $10M ARR across 50 customers and 10,000 independently operated robots while demonstrating that its deployment history materially prevents failures competitors cannot.
