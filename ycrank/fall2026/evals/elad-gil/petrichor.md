# Petrichor -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Petrichor targets frontier AI laboratories whose models need experimental feedback faster than an internal wet lab can be built. The controlling variable is the fully loaded cost of a reproducible, model-useful experiment delivered within the customer’s required turnaround time. If outsourcing improves that variable enough, laboratory execution becomes recurring infrastructure spend. The dossier establishes the proposed service, but supplies neither an economic threshold nor operating results showing that Petrichor clears it. Years-long laboratory construction creates an opening; it does not establish who captures the margin.

The strongest evidence is the product’s scope. Scientists execute instructions while photographs, raw instrument files, timestamped actions, and failures stream back. Petrichor is taking responsibility for physical execution and its records, which is substantially deeper than formatting model output. Better models could generate more experiments and make those records more valuable. That earns workflow credit. However, customer training integrations, persistent feedback loops, and measured reproducibility are undocumented. Customers also retain their commissioned data, and Petrichor promises against sharing or training on it. Any durable advantage therefore needs to come from execution quality and integration, rather than an assumed pooled-data moat.

The strongest bull case is that scientist execution is the right initial architecture. It could accommodate changing protocols before robotic infrastructure becomes economical, letting Petrichor discover demand while customers avoid building laboratories. Lila’s reported hundreds of thousands of AI-driven experiments in September 2025 makes the broader feedback-loop opportunity concrete. This fits my pattern of backing infrastructure around a technological discontinuity. But Lila’s activity establishes neither outsourced demand nor Petrichor’s economics. ECL and Strateos already offer remote experimentation, and the dossier does not quantify the effort required for either to supply comparable recording. Human flexibility could be a useful wedge while leaving labor costs as the dominant scaling constraint.

There is no documented current-product customer usage, revenue, or renewal evidence, so I use the absent-demand default rather than infer rejection. YC participation and October 11 founder coverage do not substitute for purchasing behavior. The earlier Qokedas instrument-data business provides relevant context, but appears to be the team’s earlier identity; it does not establish a separate startup outcome or documented operating lessons. Nor does the dossier establish costly founder commitment or wet-lab credentials. Those are unknowns. The frontier-research exception lacks documented rare research capabilities and a capital path here; the physical-business exception lacks demonstrated changes to incumbent unit economics. Neither overrides the market gate.

I would pass with my own money today. Petrichor’s strongest attribute is ownership of execution, while its weakest underwriting area is evidence that customers want to buy that execution repeatedly. The service-heavy economics red flag remains unresolved, rather than proven: nothing establishes commodity margins, but nothing supports a technology-driven margin premium either. My verifiable flip condition is a frontier AI laboratory renewing a training-loop execution contract supported by records showing at least 50% contribution margin after scientist labor, consumables, and failed-run rework. That is my proposed underwriting threshold, not a reported result. It would turn the central proposition from an attractive workflow description into an economically credible infrastructure business.
