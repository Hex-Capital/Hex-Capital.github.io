# Nodus Compute -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The secret Nodus Compute would need to establish is that unreliable, fragmented GPU supply can become reliably cheaper completed computation through superior orchestration. What would prove that wrong? Savings that disappear after checkpoint overhead, failed runs, and Nodus’s fees—or that an incumbent can reproduce with ordinary scheduling improvements. The dossier describes a testable product proposition, but it does not identify a prevailing belief that named peers reject. Cost-to-completion routing is an intelligible thesis; its contrarian substance remains unestablished.

The decisive problem is ownership of the advantage. Nodus restores saved files on replacement machines and documents checkpoint integrity checks and generation fencing. These are useful engineering controls. They do not establish proprietary technology, exclusive supply, or a network effect. Modal already pools capacity across clouds; SF Compute already supports resale of committed capacity. Their products are not identical to Nodus, but they occupy the mechanisms from which its advantage would have to emerge. “Startups needing cheaper GPUs” also fails to define a beachhead the company could plausibly dominate. This is affirmative competitive pressure, rather than merely missing moat disclosure.

The strongest objection to passing is the founder-reported result: 10 startup pilots and compute savings above 30%. If those customers repeatedly entrust real workloads to Nodus, the team may have discovered an execution problem that larger platforms systematically neglect. The proposed workload-to-research feedback loop could then turn initial savings into a compounding technical advantage. That possibility deserves attention. But the pilot claim comes through a mirrored search snippet, with neither payment status nor measurement methodology specified, while the feedback loop remains a research agenda. My known exceptions require a demonstrated technical discontinuity, persistent transaction demand, or a concrete deployment path that removes the relevant risk. These pilots do not yet establish an override of the monopoly concern.

Kotra and Varshney met during an internship and continued discussing GPU-market problems afterward. That is more encouraging than assembling a team solely to apply to YC, but it does not establish prior joint delivery, full-time commitment, or economic alignment. I retain the founding-structure default; I do not infer part-time work or an adverse cap table from silence. Their infrastructure backgrounds make the implementation credible, without independently proving the secret. Nodus fits my unstated interest in the AI production stack, but operating in compute does not by itself confer the advantages of a company formed around a proprietary operator network.

The business has one coherent usage-based engine. Its published $2.600 H100 SXM hourly price, however, is a supply price before Nodus fees—not evidence of retained revenue or gross margin. There is insufficient arithmetic to underwrite a power-law outcome, so that dimension also receives its default. Distribution has somewhat more substance: a working signup path, a public Python SDK, and outreach to holders of idle reserved GPUs provide concrete entry points on both sides. They merit limited credit, although no channel is tied to demonstrated conversion or customer acquisition economics. Pilot SLA limitations make workload selection consequential: customers buying cheap, interruptible computation have different requirements from customers buying assured completion.

I pass on Nodus Compute today. The company may create meaningful customer savings while leaving the enduring economic advantage with compute suppliers or established execution platforms. One verifiable result would reopen my decision: an independently reproduced benchmark showing that Nodus’s proprietary placement technology delivers a tenfold cost-to-completion advantage over Modal for a defined workload at equivalent reliability, including Nodus’s fees. That would establish a technical reason for this company to own its market.
