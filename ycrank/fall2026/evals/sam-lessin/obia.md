# Obia -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Obia could make money helping businesses find industrial space. My question is what buying its equity adds to that proposition. The proposed post-money, competing-term-sheet count, and capital plan are undisclosed. I cannot call the round auctioned merely because it is YC, but I also cannot underwrite a scarce first check. There is no named experiment with a deadline and pass/fail metric explaining what my money would establish. Both financing dimensions receive their missing-evidence defaults.

The strongest case is that Obia owns the brokerage relationship and uses increasingly cheap research to earn a transaction fee. Its reported work for a third-party logistics operator—zoning screening, site combinations, and throughput-per-rent comparisons—is substantially more interesting than a generic property-search interface. Operational requirements could become proprietary demand data, and successful placements could generate referrals. That fits my preference for translation layers inside difficult physical systems. But the [October 23 announcement](https://obia.io/news/corenet-global-summit-2026) establishes a research engagement, without documenting a completed transaction or showing that exclusive access caused customer action. Accumulating search requirements is the beginning of a possible dark pool; it does not establish one. CoreNet sponsorship buys exposure, with conversion still unreported.

The economics could work without extraordinary software defensibility. Landlords pay Obia’s fee, tenants receive representation free, and brokers still handle tours and negotiations. A focused brokerage might fund expansion from commissions while automation reduces research labor. That is the bull case that most challenges my pass: Obia may need only a few valuable transactions to become a viable business. Yet commission rates, delivery costs, collection timing, and burn are undisclosed, so I cannot establish that path. The economics receive the default, without assuming either profitability or a financing treadmill.

The AI risk is specific. Crexi already supplies overlapping research tools, and Tandem combines AI with brokerage. If research becomes broadly available, Obia must retain the customer relationship and enough commission after paying for local execution and verification. Its use of human brokers avoids assuming every agent error disappears, but those humans also remain a delivery cost. Samuel Cho’s Amazon and research background and Jason Henkel’s conversational-AI work are relevant credentials; neither documents shipped operating results spanning software and commercial brokerage. The contracting discrepancy between Obia Inc. branding and sole-proprietor terms also needs resolution. It does not establish an equal-founder governance problem.

I pass on the present evidence. I checked the exception routes: the dossier supplies neither demonstrated conversion and retention, acquisition-backed demand, nor a prior exit paired with an explicit alternative financing structure sufficient to override the missing gates. This is an underwriting gap, rather than evidence that a four-person pre-seed company should already have mature metrics. My one verifiable condition for reopening diligence is a reconciled ledger for Obia’s first completed industrial-placement cohort showing positive cash contribution after broker compensation, research, and verification costs. That would establish a functioning base business worth pricing; today, I have an operating concept and one reported engagement.
