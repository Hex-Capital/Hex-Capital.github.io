# Kairox Systems -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Kairox Systems needs a secret more specific than “drones can fly and swim.” SubUAS has already demonstrated that. The investment thesis would have to be that fixed-wing transit combined with submerged operation creates a decisive mission advantage over separate vehicles and existing amphibious designs. That proposition is testable: compare completed missions at equivalent payload, range, reliability, and cost. Tempest’s assembled prototype and reported TRL 4 establish development activity, but the dossier supplies neither that comparison nor a prevailing technical belief the founders have overturned. The secret remains unidentified, rather than disproved.

Can Kairox own the market its engineering might create? Coastal reconnaissance, mine countermeasures, and strike are three mission categories, each with different requirements; they do not yet identify a beachhead the company can dominate. Pressure-vessel architecture, modular payloads, and claimed GPS-denied autonomy describe a product architecture without establishing proprietary superiority. SubUAS’s reported Navy Phase III contracts exceeding $4 million by 2020 make the competitive question concrete. They demonstrate that another organization can develop and sell an air–water vehicle, although they do not prove Tempest is interchangeable. I therefore assign the absent-mechanism default, rather than the harsher commodity score.

The strongest argument for Kairox is that unusually inexpensive experimentation could expose a valuable configuration before established suppliers recognize it. Building the first prototype with a $14,000 grant is meaningful at this stage. Mechanical, electrical, aerospace, and controls backgrounds give the three founders relevant capabilities, while reported Marine Corps engagement provides a route to discovering actual mission requirements. This fits my less visible preference for companies building neglected physical infrastructure. But the dossier does not establish formation through a proven operator network, and meeting at MIT establishes neither long working alignment nor its opposite. Full-time status, ownership, and prior joint execution remain unknown; the founding-structure score stays at its specified default.

Military access is Kairox’s clearest affirmative commercial signal. Reported end-user discussions go beyond merely writing “government sales” on a slide, but neither QLab acceptance nor a conference showcase identifies a budget owner, procurement cycle, or repeatable acquisition process. Vehicle and payload sales are themselves an inferred business model. With no documented price, production margin, or purchasing volume, I cannot construct the arithmetic for one revenue engine producing a multibillion-dollar company. Zero disclosed revenue is ordinary at pre-seed; the issue is that the economic engine remains unspecified.

I would pass on writing a check today. My exceptions for complex hardware require concrete technical validation or a credible deployment path that resolves the central coordination risk; Kairox’s reported engagement does not yet supply either. Equally, there is no evidence of part-time founders, damaging capitalization, or a withdrawn technical claim, so those red flags should not be invented. The unresolved problem is the sequence from reliable air–water transitions to mission qualification, production, and procurement. One verifiable event would reopen the decision: an independently documented, paid Marine Corps trial showing repeatable complete missions with a pre-agreed operational and cost advantage over the best available alternative. That would turn an unusual vehicle into evidence for a business worth forming.
