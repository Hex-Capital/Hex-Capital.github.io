# Boat -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Boat’s possible secret is that persistent agent computers can be delivered at radically lower cost without sacrificing isolation or reliability. Its published $0.036/hour default VM price makes that proposition testable. But the comparison with E2B and Daytona at $0.331/hour establishes advertised prices, not equivalent workloads or underlying costs. A matched production test could disprove the advantage. The dossier supplies no evidence that named competitors reject Boat’s technical proposition; I would leave the secret unproved rather than mistake a pricing table for its discovery.

The founding relationship is more encouraging. Anicet Nougaret and Kirill Makarov report meeting at INSA Lyon five years ago, which removes some concern about founders assembled for an application deadline. Nougaret also encountered the infrastructure problem while building cloud coding agents. This fits my preference for companies formed around an operating problem their founders have actually experienced. It does not establish five years of working together, however, and full-time status, ownership, and economic exposure remain undocumented. Those are diligence questions, not evidence of a damaged foundation.

The decisive problem is ownership of the market. Separate Linux kernels and nested virtualization are real capabilities; they are not demonstrated proprietary technology. Google offers sandbox provisioning and snapshots, and Modal offers VM sandboxes. Boat has identified a workload, but the dossier does not identify a bounded customer segment it can dominate or a mechanism preventing competitors from serving it. Snapshots might eventually make migration inconvenient. An inferred switching cost earns no monopoly credit today. Infrastructure can become essential while its suppliers remain interchangeable.

The strongest objection to passing is that customers are already choosing Boat. More than 1,000 active paying users, although founder-reported, is meaningful evidence at this stage. Founder outreach, X posts, the July 22 Product Hunt launch, and OpenInspect’s October 23 integration provide concrete routes to developers. Separately, reported six-figure ARR and consumption pricing establish one coherent revenue engine. This could become a substantial business. But the dossier provides neither channel conversion and retention evidence nor margins and customer expansion from which I can underwrite a power-law outcome. Low prices attract customers; whether they finance durable expansion depends on the cost underneath them.

Boat fits the broader pattern of investing in the AI production stack, but category fit cannot override competitive economics. I checked the exceptions: there is no independently demonstrated technical breakthrough here, and current customer adoption does not directly resolve the market-ownership risk. There is also no documented founder commitment or capitalization red flag. My pass rests on affirmative competitive overlap, not missing pre-seed polish. One result would reopen the decision: an independently reproduced production benchmark showing Boat delivers equivalent isolation and reliability at one-tenth the fully loaded cost of E2B and Daytona, while earning a positive gross margin at its published price. That would turn cheap compute into evidence of proprietary technology. On this dossier, I would keep my money.
