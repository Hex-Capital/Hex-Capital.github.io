# FlowManual -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when something broke. David Bae and Michael Lin began building on April 18, 2026, interviewed hundreds of construction professionals within two months, and shipped a hosted product covering document comparison and invoice reconciliation. That shows speed. But the dossier never names an obstacle, the workaround they built, or the resulting change in a customer’s behavior ([David Bae LinkedIn, Jul 2026](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/shijoonbae_we-started-building-on-april-18-a-month-activity-7469855846215229440-gUNz)). I cannot infer relentless resourcefulness from elapsed time alone.

The strongest evidence is the artifact. FlowManual already extracts construction documents, identifies changes at the source line, compares bids with contracts and buyouts with invoices, and maintains vendor-price history ([FlowManual, Aug 2026](https://flowmanual.com/)). The team built this before completing SOC 2 Type II or an independent penetration test, then added tenant isolation, encrypted storage, audit logs, zero-retention processing, and optional local parsing ([FlowManual Security, Aug 2026](https://flowmanual.com/security)). This is functioning software, not a diagram. But there is no measured accuracy, independently verified outcome, or disclosed contractor usage, so it is an intermediate technical proof rather than a fierce-nerd proof spike.

Hundreds of interviews are learning, not pull. No named contractor is reported to use the product repeatedly, pay for Pro, refer another contractor, or tolerate an error-prone early version because the problem hurts enough. The free tier offers every feature and unlimited projects and uploads, but the dossier does not establish that anyone returns to it ([FlowManual pricing, Aug 2026](https://flowmanual.com/pricing)). Meanwhile, Document Crunch reports more than 500 customers and is being acquired by Trimble, while Trunk Tools has raised $70 million. A free product in a crowded market can produce trials without producing a narrow well.

Construction paperwork contains real schlep: changing scopes, inconsistent exclusions, invoice reconciliation, security reviews, and professional liability when a clause is missed. But neither founder is documented as having worked for a contractor or personally encountered this failure. Their engineering backgrounds and customer interviews may let them learn the market, but they do not yet prove a founder-originated need. Nor is there evidence that a rejected product hypothesis caused a material route change. For now, both the origin story and running-back test remain at roughly their missing-evidence defaults.

The bull case stings because two college roommates shipped a surprisingly broad system within months, chose founder-led demos instead of hiring salespeople, and are entering an administratively ugly industry. If several interviewed contractors are already sending live bids and invoices through it, the public dossier could badly understate the company. But FlowManual does not match the unstated pattern that would override this uncertainty: there is no server-breaking launch, numerical benchmark, indexed proprietary substrate, or other concrete pre-round proof spike. The known exceptions likewise depended on unmistakable working proof or user behavior. Fundraising, 202 LinkedIn followers, and interviews cannot substitute for either. There is no major-partner dependency or premature sales delegation, but broad workflow scope risks becoming a polished list of features before the founders discover which single discrepancy users urgently pay to catch.

I expect that by August 2029 FlowManual will have narrowed to bid-to-contract discrepancy review and remain below $1 million ARR. I am passing today because the technical artifact is ahead of the evidence that these founders can force adoption when the product meets resistance. One result would flip me: a documented contractor objection followed within two weeks by a founder-built change that causes at least three named contractors to use the product weekly and pay for it.
