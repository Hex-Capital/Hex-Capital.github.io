# Omanta -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A patient with recurrent cancer does not carry records between specialists or commission an improvised research project; tissue, multiomic results, treatment evidence, and clinical decisions update as one living program. That future is specific enough to interest me. Omanta’s dossier, however, does not show the steps that make it inevitable or the mundane artifact proving it has begun. Falling sequencing costs and the FDA’s January 2026 guidance are enabling conditions, not evidence that patients or clinicians have adopted this new behavior.

The present-day wound is clearer. Patients who have exhausted standard options may be forced to coordinate tissue logistics, laboratories, specialists, evidence, and treating clinicians themselves. Omanta proposes to absorb that fragmentation and return some control to the patient and physician. But why does the fragmentation persist? The dossier identifies CLIA requirements and uncertain clinical-decision-support classification, yet it does not identify who captures an economic rent, quantify the delay or expense, or explain how Omanta crosses those institutional boundaries more effectively than a determined human case manager.

The strongest bull case is that the breadth is the product. Tempus generated $1.2718 billion in 2025 while concentrating on testing, data, and AI; xCures structures records; Massive Bio matches patients to trials. None is described as owning the whole patient-specific research program. A four-person team could discover that desperate families will pay for coordination across those seams, producing a valuable longitudinal dataset before larger platforms recognize the category. The precision-oncology and rare-disease-diagnostics markets—$115.8 billion and $21.6 billion respectively in 2024—are certainly large enough to contain that wedge.

But breadth without a working artifact can also mean an expensive services bundle masquerading as a system. There is no disclosed case volume, repeat usage, revenue, named referral source, laboratory partner, validation study, or exclusive relationship. The website was inaccessible during research and its domain appeared in a recent sale listing. Those facts do not prove the product is dead, but they leave me unable to perform the immersion test: I cannot see one patient surrendering sensitive records, one clinician changing a treatment roadmap, or one laboratory repeatedly entering the workflow.

The missing founder record matters even more here than it would for ordinary software. No identities, clinical credentials, research histories, prior employers, or division of responsibility were retrievable. I therefore cannot tell whether this program was specified by a lived operating wound or assembled from an attractive list of healthcare bottlenecks. Nor can I verify one accountable leader. The Known Exception for a pre-revenue company would require the substitutes Omanta does not disclose: a functioning prototype, institutional proofs of concept, and unusually deep pre-company obsession.

Genomic and medical data also activate my privacy gate. HHS treats identifiable genetic information as protected health information, yet Omanta provides no public consent, custody, security, or data-limitation architecture. My 36-month base case is that the company narrows into a high-touch oncology case-management service for specialist practices and remains below $1 million ARR rather than becoming a defensible research platform. I would flip to investing upon verification of one condition: at least ten completed patient programs from two named clinical referral partners, with repeat physician use, documented treatment-roadmap changes, and explicit consent and data-custody controls.
