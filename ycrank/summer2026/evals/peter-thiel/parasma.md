# Parasma -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Sean Cole trained approximately 200,000 neurons to play Doom, yet Parasma owns neither those neurons nor the CL1 substrate beneath them. Parasma is an atoms-heavy technical wager with an unusually weak monopoly position: the science is non-mimetic, but the value-capture architecture is inverted. Cortical Labs owns the biological computer and API; Parasma supplies open-source-adjacent training software. The highest signal is physical difficulty. The lowest is pricing power.

**SpaceX** turned atoms-heavy difficulty into monopoly because it owned the rockets, factories, launch data, and multi-year reusability roadmap. Parasma shares the load-bearing premise that computation can improve through a physical breakthrough rather than another layer of software; biological noise, non-stationarity, and limited observability are genuine technical barriers. Parasma diverges where it matters most: Cole’s Doom demonstration ran on Cortical Labs’ CL1, while Parasma discloses no proprietary hardware, patents, or exclusive biological substrate. SpaceX owned the machine. Parasma presently rents it.

**Quora** exposed the low rung of the monopoly ladder: real technical ambition, but competing knowledge platforms and no durable pricing-power mechanism. Parasma has the same structural weakness in sharper form. Cole’s `doom-neuron` code is public, Cortical Labs exposes programmable hardware, TBC has raised $25 million to integrate biological computing with AI workloads, and Intactis is building automated biohybrid processors. Parasma has named competitors but not their institutional blind spot. Competition is for losers; dependency on a competitor is worse.

Parasma’s contrarian secret is directionally powerful: living neurons may become a trainable compute substrate while the AI industry assumes scaling must remain silicon-bound. Over the next 18 months, that secret compounds only if Parasma can isolate learning inside the neurons, demonstrate an energy or adaptation advantage, and accumulate biological-response data competitors cannot reproduce. The 601-run software replication could not establish that the living neurons performed the learning. Until Parasma answers that objection, the secret remains a provocative experiment rather than a named failure mode in Nvidia, Cortical Labs, or conventional neural-network architecture.

Parasma’s founding-scientist posting identifies protocols, benchmarks, noise, variability, and non-stationarity, but Parasma discloses no definite two-to-five-year sequence of technical milestones. The score flips an entire tier if Parasma commits to measurable gates: independently isolated biological learning, repeatable performance across multiple cultures, integration with at least three substrates, and a specified energy advantage over GPUs by a named date. Without those commitments, hiring a scientist to “develop protocols and benchmarks” reads as indefinite discovery. A difficult experiment is not yet a definite plan.

**Anduril** is the relevant founder parallel because Palmer Luckey converted an eccentric technical identity into a product that incumbents structurally could not build. Cole has a weaker but authentic version of that outsider signal: he skipped a bachelor’s degree, earned an AI MSc with distinction, researched spiking neural networks, and personally shipped the Doom implementation that received Guardian and Scientific American coverage. Parasma benefits from verified technical agency, not résumé theater. Parasma lacks the decisive Anduril override, however: Cole has not previously scaled or exited a company, and the dossier shows no PayPal-Mafia-style operator channel.

Cortical Labs is the likely endpoint within 36 months: Parasma will remain below $1 million ARR and either pivot into biological-compute benchmarking or be acquired by Cortical Labs for Cole’s training expertise after failing to establish substrate-independent pricing power. Parasma’s atoms-heavy insight creates scarce technical talent; Parasma’s weak ownership position prevents that talent from becoming an independent monopoly. The One Ring belongs to whoever controls the substrate, not whoever writes an interesting spell for it.

Parasma’s strongest bull case is **Stripe**: an independent software layer can become the developer default even when another party owns the underlying rails. Parasma already has token-prediction and reinforcement-learning demonstrations, 250 GitHub stars, and a credentialed-outsider founder; a “Stripe for wetware” could standardize training across Cortical Labs, TBC, Intactis, and future substrates. That exception stings, but Stripe named the processor failure mode and built a proprietary integration network, while Parasma has one external hardware dependency, open code, no revenue, and no demonstrated biological-compute advantage. I would flip to Invest if Parasma secures three independent hardware integrations and repeat paid workloads showing at least a 10x energy-efficiency or adaptation advantage over conventional compute.
