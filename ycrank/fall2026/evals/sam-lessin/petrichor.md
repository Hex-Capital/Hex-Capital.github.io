# Petrichor -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Petrichor gives the customer ownership of the experimental data and promises not to train on it. That may be exactly what gets a frontier laboratory comfortable buying. It also means I cannot underwrite this as a proprietary training-data pool. The investable asset would have to be the execution business: trusted scientists, reliable experimental records, and a workflow customers keep paying to use. Who owns the learning that makes the next experiment cheaper or better? The dossier establishes the confidentiality promise; it does not establish that compounding advantage.

The deal is equally unresolved. Proposed post-money, competing term sheets, and whether my check supplies genuinely scarce capital are all undisclosed. Fall 2026 YC participation establishes neither an auction nor an overlooked opportunity. I use my missing-evidence default. More consequentially, there is no stated capital-funded experiment with a metric and deadline. “Build wet lab infrastructure” describes an activity. I need to know what finite amount of money establishes a truth that materially changes Petrichor’s value—and why customer payments cannot fund that test.

The strongest argument against passing is that scientist execution could be the practical advantage. Petrichor describes streaming photographs, raw instrument files, timestamped actions, and failures while experiments run. Those intermediate records could matter more to a model developer than a clean final result. Emerald Cloud Lab and Strateos emphasize automation; a human execution layer might accommodate changing experimental instructions sooner. This fits my pattern of backing businesses that connect software demand to difficult physical operations. Customer ownership of the resulting data could reinforce trust and make outsourcing preferable to building a laboratory over several years. That is a coherent purchasing mechanism.

But the dossier supplies no observed purchase through that mechanism. There is no named customer, paid experiment, repeat usage, or measured throughput. Qokedas’s 48 X followers in September 2026 belong to the earlier instrument-data offering and establish no current customer action. Nor does Lila Sciences’ $550 million financing establish demand for Petrichor: an integrated laboratory operator may choose to own the entire loop. My known exceptions require operating evidence strong enough to override an unresolved deal or capital plan—documented conversion, retention, acquisition interest, or a proven founder with an explicit financing structure. None is documented here.

Delete the next financing and the economics remain unknown. Scientist-executed work creates a direct labor requirement, but that alone does not establish bad margins; commissioned experiments could support a sound service business. Prices, scientist utilization, gross margin, burn, and a seed-funded break-even plan are absent, so economics receives the default rather than an adverse score. The founders’ earlier instrument-data work is relevant context, but the record does not document a shipped operating result or wet-lab deployment. Wilhelm Hedenskog is identified as CEO; three founders who were high-school classmates do not, by themselves, establish the equal-founder decision problem I worry about.

I pass on this evidence. Petrichor has a plausible bridge between model builders and experimental biology, but neither ownership of the resulting data nor demonstrated ownership of the purchasing workflow. My one verifiable condition for reopening is completion of a paid pilot in which a named frontier AI customer purchases a second experimental cycle after using the first cycle’s streamed records. That would establish that the distinctive output changes purchasing behavior. Today, it remains a product description.
