# Kairox Systems -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Kairox Systems built its first prototype with a $14,000 grant. That is the most interesting capital-allocation fact here: the founders have turned very little money into hardware. But proposed post-money valuation, competing term sheets, and the current round’s use of proceeds are undisclosed. I cannot establish whether my check buys a neglected experiment at an attractive price. YC membership proves neither an auction nor scarce capital.

The potential venture moment is concrete enough to investigate. Can Tempest’s fixed-wing transit, submerged operation, and return to flight replace two vehicles on one economically valuable military mission? Kairox reports TRL 4 and improved prototype testing, but publishes no range, endurance, depth, or speed. There is no disclosed round-funded test with a deadline and measurable acceptance threshold. Technical development creates an opportunity to define that experiment; it does not establish that this round finances one. The $14,000 grant also leaves open whether the next experiment needs equity at all.

The strongest argument against passing is that these founders could resolve the engineering risk unusually cheaply. Jorge Cavazos has documented control-law and hardware qualification experience at Oligo Space, beyond an aerospace degree. The team has assembled a prototype and reports Marine Corps engagement. This fits my interest in founders connecting difficult physical systems with neglected operating workflows. The missing half is demonstrated military operating or procurement access that produces customer action. Draper, Lincoln Lab, MITRE, and NASA affiliations do not establish that bridge by themselves.

SubUAS’s reported Navy Phase III contracts exceeding $4 million by 2020 make the bull case more serious: a buyer has funded an air–water capability before. Those awards belong to a competitor, however, and they also mean Kairox must prove why its configuration changes mission economics. Reported validation discussions, QLab acceptance, and a conference showcase establish exposure, not proprietary distribution that converts. The dossier contains no purchasing commitment or retained deployment. Nor does it supply pricing, margins, burn, or a seed-funded route to break-even. I leave those economics unproven rather than assume hardware necessarily requires endless financing.

I pass on the evidence available. My exceptions to missing gates depend on unusually hard conversion evidence, demonstrated cash economics, or a financing structure that removes repeated fundraising; none is documented here. The red flag would be treating military conversations as purchases or prototype progress as a complete capital plan. I am not alleging an auction, dysfunctional founder governance, or uneconomic operation from silence. My single condition for reopening is a signed, paid Marine Corps field-trial agreement specifying a dated mission-performance acceptance test and a priced purchase option upon success. That would connect engineering progress to a customer’s capital allocation—the connection Kairox has yet to demonstrate.
