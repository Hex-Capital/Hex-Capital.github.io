# Stoa -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Stoa’s contested truth is that institutional GPU hardware is becoming a financial market before its participants have built the exchange infrastructure required to price it. The falsifying event is simple: if the reported $300 million in first-month RFQs produces little executed volume, no usable price index, and repeated off-platform trading, then this is brokerage theater rather than price discovery. Today, the H100 index is unavailable and settlement value is undisclosed. The secret is precise enough to test, but it has not yet been proved.

The founders are the strongest evidence. They have known one another for more than ten years rather than assembling for YC. Kaan Yigit built trading, pricing, and data systems at Uniper; Eren Berke Saglam traded interest-rate derivatives at Citi; Berat Celik is a repeat founder who reportedly declined an xAI opportunity. This is an unusually coherent team for the problem: software, market structure, and trading operations are all in-house. Their ownership, compensation, and exact full-time commitments are undisclosed, but there is no affirmative founding-structure red flag.

The more difficult question is whether Stoa owns a narrow market or merely introduces its participants. Institutional physical-GPU RFQs form a credible beachhead, and contractual ownership of bid, offer, volume, and settlement data could create a compounding information advantage. But a right to data is not yet a data monopoly. Compute Exchange already spans physical GPUs, reserved capacity, and forward contracts across more than 100 providers; Procurri supplies inspection, logistics, and asset disposition. Stoa disclaims responsibility for hardware existence, authenticity, condition, delivery, and payment—the operational layer where trust may actually accrue. Its 12-month non-circumvention clause is revealing: legal friction is being used where network effects have not yet made departure irrational.

The bull case stings because $300 million of RFQs in one month is far too large to dismiss as ordinary pre-seed demand. If even a modest share settles, the resulting transaction history could improve benchmarks, attract lenders, reduce dealer search costs, and generate still more proprietary data. The team’s trading backgrounds make that loop more plausible than a generic marketplace pitch. Yet none of the known exceptions rescues the missing proof: there is no disclosed persistent transaction volume comparable to demand surviving hostility, no independently verified technical breakthrough, and no deployed unit that collapses a coordination stack. RFQs measure interest; fees require execution.

The revenue engine is at least singular: transaction fees on institutional hardware trades. The underlying chip market—$123 billion in 2024 and projected at $286 billion by 2030—permits a power-law outcome, but top-down shipment value does not establish Stoa’s take rate, eligible secondary-market volume, gross margin, or retention. Distribution is similarly concrete but unfinished. Direct inquiries, calls, referrals, and waitlist onboarding have reached buyers, dealers, neoclouds, operators, liquidators, and lenders, yet the dossier provides no conversion rate, sales cycle, acquisition cost, or repeat settlement behavior.

I would investigate, not invest today. My 36-month base case is that Stoa becomes a useful GPU brokerage and pricing-data business with $3–8 million in annualized revenue, but does not become the dominant exchange because inspection, settlement trust, and counterparty relationships remain fragmented. One condition flips the decision: disclose at least $100 million of completed, repeat transaction volume across multiple quarters, with counterparties returning through the platform and a published index demonstrably derived from settlements. That would convert the pitch from secret to evidence and the marketplace from contractual enclosure to an emerging monopoly.
