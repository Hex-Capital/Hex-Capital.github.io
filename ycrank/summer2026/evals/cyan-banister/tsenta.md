# Tsenta -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the attractive version is obvious: people specify the work they want, software negotiates the bureaucratic maze, and job applications stop consuming hundreds of human hours. But Tsenta’s dossier does not work backward from that world. Its “why now” is the familiar claim that browser agents became capable in 2025–2026; it does not identify the next institutional transition, explain how hiring changes in response to abundant automated applications, or show why Tsenta controls that future. The present-day leak is real, but the six-year map is missing.

The broken system is much more concrete. Students submit 200–400 applications, spending 15–30 minutes repeatedly feeding identical facts into ATS forms. That is roughly 50–200 hours of clerical labor per search. Tsenta returns that time and gives applicants visible receipts, editing, and approval across 30-plus ATS platforms. The team understands the wound because the founders personally submitted more than 3,000 applications. What remains unanswered is who ultimately captures the rent: employers and ATS vendors do not benefit from receiving thousands of low-cost applications, so they have incentives to strengthen CAPTCHAs, detection, and other countermeasures.

The founders have earned a meaningful prior. Agnay Srivastava built Android device trees used by more than 2,000 ROM builders; Pulkit Gupta reports a language application with more than one million downloads and Bhasha Sangam with 500,000-plus users. They did not merely graduate into a fashionable category—they lived the application problem, had already shipped software to substantial user populations, and then built coverage across 50,000 career pages and more than two million jobs. I still lack a dated record of years-long obsession or evidence that they recruited exceptional complementary talent beyond the founding pair, so this is strong first-believer proof rather than the top band.

The strongest evidence is behavioral. Tsenta reports 45,000-plus users, 9,000 customers, monthly user doubling, and fivefold month-over-month revenue growth. People pay $19–$99 to delegate 600–4,500 applications every 30 days. That is wonderfully strange behavior: users are paying software to represent them thousands of times in a high-stakes market. The independent footprint—5,000-plus Android downloads, 1,000 Chrome users, and dozens of mobile ratings—confirms a functioning artifact even though it does not validate the reported 70%-plus paid-user interview rate.

Here is the objection that stings: perhaps this behavioral density becomes the moat. Every completed form expands ATS coverage, every correction improves reliability, and 45,000 users could generate an outcome dataset that competitors cannot cheaply recreate. But Tsenta said in June 2026 that it did not directly observe job outcomes. Without that feedback loop, the company is accumulating submissions rather than proprietary learning. Simplify and Jobright already overlap the workflow, Teal can extend into it, and general browser agents can attack the same forms. Tsenta currently sits on top of the AI wave and depends on hostile third-party portals; it does not yet control an enabling layer beneath the wave.

My 36-month base case is a $3–8 million ARR consumer application business whose growth is constrained by worsening ATS countermeasures and commoditized submission agents—not a defining career infrastructure company. I would not invest my own money today. The verifiable flip condition is an outcome-linked six-month paid cohort showing durable retention and improving interview conversion as submission volume grows; that would prove the operational footprint is becoming a proprietary feedback system rather than an impressive integration treadmill.
