# Marengo -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have specific knowledge in simulation-led engineering, but not yet in the exact problem they are selling. Emil Ares worked on control systems for the UK Space Agency; Gad Marconi authored the HITEN orbital-dynamics library; together they demonstrated space-mission design software in April 2026. Those are artifacts, not résumé decoration. But Marengo repositioned toward data centers only months later, and neither founder has documented data-center design employment, completed facilities, or responsibility for power and cooling systems. The team has learned how to automate engineering. It has not yet proved that it understands where data-center designs fail.

This distinction matters because Marengo is entering a consequential workflow, not merely improving drafting speed. It claims twice the design speed at half the cost, while tier-four facilities target 99.999% reliability, yet it provides no accuracy benchmark, certification, completed-project case study, or disclosed BIM integration. “Working with some of the largest data center builders” is potentially strong evidence, but unnamed work reveals neither repeated usage nor whether customers trust generated designs beyond demonstrations. Selection among 16 Onstage finalists from 1,614 applicants validates attention, not engineering correctness.

The market is large enough to preserve option value: data-center design services were estimated at $21.93 billion in 2025 and are projected to reach $38.36 billion by 2030. Rising electricity consumption and more than $400 billion of capital expenditure by five technology companies create urgency around power, cooling, and construction speed. But this is top-down demand evidence, not yet a demonstrated path from Marengo’s wedge into adjacent transactions. Its rapid aerospace-to-data-center transition may reflect useful maneuverability, or it may mean the founders are searching for a buyer before discovering which knowledge is truly theirs.

The central failure mode is durability. If a competitor copies Marengo’s interface and orchestration code, the dossier identifies no asset that remains: no proprietary validation corpus, exclusive engineering data, patents, deployed hardware, or model trained on unavailable project outcomes. Entangl already occupies data-center engineering; Augmenta automates electrical design; SWAPP writes into Revit and ArchiCAD; Hypar provides general design automation. Marengo could eventually compound designs, simulation results, approval feedback, and construction outcomes into scarce data, but a possible feedback loop is not a present moat. Today the product appears to require founder-led enterprise sales and project-specific work, with no evidence that one customer improves the product or distribution for another.

The strongest bull case is that public evidence lags private deployment. Technical founders have moved from aerospace simulation into a $21.93 billion market, claim a twofold speed improvement, and report access to major builders. If those engagements generate verified design-to-construction outcome data, Marengo could become less like a software wrapper and more like a proprietary engineering model embedded in capital projects. That would fit the pattern of fragmented expert knowledge becoming scalable infrastructure. None of the known-exception logic overrides the missing proof today, however: there is no demonstrated user network, unusually validated prototype adoption, or founder evidence so overwhelming that market and moat ambiguity should be ignored.

I would pass now. I see no integrity or artificial-deadline red flag, but I do see the subtler risk of calling a recent repositioning conviction before the environment has validated it. My flip condition is verifiable: two named data-center builders must deploy Marengo on completed design packages, publish independently measured error or cycle-time improvements, and contract for repeat projects while granting Marengo rights to retain outcome data. That would convert the founders’ adjacent specific knowledge into a compounding asset. Until then, this is capable engineering labor wrapped in copyable software—useful, but not yet leverage.
