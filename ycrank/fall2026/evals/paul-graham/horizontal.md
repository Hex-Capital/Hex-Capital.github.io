# horizontal. -- Paul Graham Evaluation

What happened at the first factory where horizontal.’s agent could not complete a job? The dossier does not say. That is the fact I most want. A production system will present plenty of resistance: inconsistent records, undocumented procedures, someone who refuses to give the software permission. Watching these founders get through one such obstacle would tell me more than their plan to automate the whole factory. Without that episode, resourcefulness receives the missing-evidence default, not a judgment that the founders lack it.

The strongest argument for horizontal. is that Niklas Marxer may already know where the bodies are buried. He previously worked at askLio and reports bringing agentic AI into more than **150 industrial companies**. Jakob Hermeler brings mechanical engineering and Stanford robotics research; the founders met through that research ([founder profile](https://www.ycombinator.com/founders/niklas-marxer), [launch](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/W8z-horizontal-ai-agents-that-run-factory-operations-end-to-end)). This is meaningful industrial exposure. It gives the origin criterion modest credit, discounted because the deployment claim is self-reported and no particular encounter explains horizontal.’s starting point. The attractive possibility is that an apparently broad idea conceals a very specific problem they have seen repeatedly.

Starting with one process inside existing factory systems is a sensible way to discover that problem. It puts the founders close to the integration work that a polished demonstration can conceal. But the product description does not establish a functioning production deployment. Hermeler’s coauthored collision-recovery research is evidence of technical engagement, without a reported result here that establishes horizontal.’s execution capability ([research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15867)). My pattern of backing technical teams in unfamiliar sectors depends on concrete proof. The dossier supplies neither a measured factory result nor demonstrated developer appropriation of a new interface. Technical proof stays at its default. So does adaptability: incremental deployment is a plan, while a rejected hypothesis followed by a shipped revision would be evidence.

The demand question is equally specific: which production planner would complain if horizontal. disappeared tomorrow? The company says it works with international manufacturers, but identifies no customer, repeated workflow, or payment. Its launch was seven days old on **October 27, 2026**; that short history is ordinary for this stage. The **485 LinkedIn followers** and incubator participation do not answer the usage question. Nor does the adjacent **$20.7 billion MES market** answer whether a plant will pay enough to support the integration work. Pelico already connects ERP and MES data, and Siemens offers industrial agent orchestration. Those competitors make the first indispensable workflow more consequential than the breadth of horizontal.’s promise.

I would pass on the evidence available. The bull case could hurt: founders with this much industrial exposure might recruit a handful of plants personally and learn faster than larger competitors. There is no documented dependence on a major distribution partner, premature delegation, or refusal to change course. The normal red flags are largely unproven, rather than present. But the known exceptions require a working prototype, unmistakable user pull, or validated technical proof; none is established here strongly enough to replace the missing founder evidence. My operational gate therefore fails. **One verifiable flip condition:** a referenceable plant case documenting a specific deployment blocker, the founders’ workaround and elapsed time, measured successful production actions, and repeated operator use afterward. That would connect the promising background to observable behavior.
