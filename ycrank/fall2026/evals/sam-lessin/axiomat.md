# Axiomat -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Axiomat wants me to underwrite “AI that never hallucinates.” What would I actually own, and at what price? The dossier gives no proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or evidence that this is the first scarce check. YC Fall 2026 participation establishes none of those things. I cannot call the round an auction, but I cannot price the uncertainty either. There is also no documented capital plan identifying one experiment, its deadline, and the result that changes the company’s value. Both financing dimensions receive their missing-evidence defaults.

The product contains a potentially valuable distinction: verify a query against an explicit business model before somebody acts on its answer. Better language models would not automatically eliminate demand for that check. But the business model itself comes from documentation and schemas. A mathematically valid query can still answer the wrong business question if “revenue” was encoded incorrectly. Axiomat therefore needs to demonstrate the boundary of its guarantee, including when it refuses to answer. The absolute correctness claim raises that burden; the dossier supplies no independent benchmark establishing that the boundary holds.

The strongest argument against passing is the team’s proximity to the problem. Ethan Kharitonov researched formal methods and AI verification at AWS; Elad Perlman developed utility control software at Survalent; Jack Naimer brings BioReason and robotic semantic-safety research. That combination fits my recurring interest in people translating between technical systems and consequential operating environments. It earns partial founder credit. It does not yet establish that the founders have shipped this bridge into enterprise analytics: the dossier names no customer deployment, operating result, or proprietary access to a buyer’s workflow.

That missing bridge matters commercially. Customer-specific logical models could become a valuable pool of business definitions, but switching costs are only a hypothesis until customers entrust workflows to them. Axiomat currently documents demo booking and email/LinkedIn outreach, with no observed purchase or retained usage attributable to owned access. Meanwhile, AWS already commercializes automated reasoning checks, although equivalence to Axiomat’s SQL coverage is unestablished. The defensible asset would have to emerge from implementing and maintaining customer semantics. Whether that work compounds into software or remains expensive customization is unresolved.

My AI exceptions require operating evidence strong enough to outweigh the category risk: measured reliability, paid conversion, and retention. Axiomat has no such documented override. Nor does the dossier establish the profitable base business that would let me treat its broader AI ambition as an inexpensive option. Revenue, margins, burn, and runway are undisclosed, so economics receives the stated default. These are missing underwriting inputs, not evidence of failed execution. I also would not manufacture a founder-governance objection: Ethan is identified as CEO, and nothing establishes an equal-founder decision committee.

I pass on the evidence available. The technical approach deserves a real experiment, but the dossier does not establish what my money would prove or why equity is attractively priced for that risk. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an executable financing proposal below $10 million post-money that funds a single, dated paid production trial, with customer-approved correctness tests and a costed route from that deployment to break-even. That would connect price, technical truth, and cash in one underwritable proposition.
