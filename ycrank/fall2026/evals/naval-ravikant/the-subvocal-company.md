# The Subvocal Company -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have done something more informative than choosing a fashionable category: they built a silent-speech prototype together at TreeHacks in February 2026 and continued developing it. Yadav works on sensing hardware and embedded firmware; Kumar builds the software. That is specific knowledge acquired through action. GreenSat’s reported $2M annual revenue and Kumar’s previous 80K-user app strengthen the evidence that they execute, but neither proves mastery of physiological speech decoding. I give the founders substantial credit for the current artifact, with its performance still company-reported.

The decisive question is what remains when someone copies the software. The Subvocal Company has a physical sensing problem, which fits my recent pattern of backing embodied computing bottlenecks. But hardware is a substrate for defensibility, not automatic defensibility. The sensing architecture is undisclosed; exclusive training data and a model advantage are unestablished. AlterEgo independently pursues the same interface. Secrecy does not establish that a competitor cannot reproduce the result.

The reported speed above 200 words per minute makes this worth investigating. It does not establish usable throughput without accuracy measured under the same conditions. The earlier 45% accuracy result cannot be combined with the later speed claim into a single performance benchmark. More importantly, the company’s own hiring materials identify cross-user, cross-session, and placement generalization as unresolved. Calibration could turn a remarkable demonstration into a useful instrument—or become a recurring tax that sends users back to their keyboards. Whisper dictation from Subtle also gives some buyers a simpler substitute.

The strongest argument against waiting is that a strange, working interface can precede both its category and its distribution. My exception for exceptional prototypes applies here; lack of revenue at this stage does not invalidate it. Silent text and command entry could extend from shared-office knowledge workers into broader hands-free computing. But the dossier does not establish those adjacent buyers or their spending. Nor does it establish the capital efficiency or persistent user behavior that would make the prototype exception decisive. The reported $1.3M–$1.5M financing provides development resources, not customer validation.

The Subvocal Company could give individuals more output without an employer’s permission, another pattern I favor. Yet no demonstrated mechanism lets one customer improve another customer’s experience. A shared decoding model might eventually do that; the dossier labels the necessary data advantage as hypothetical. Planned pre-orders likewise establish neither pricing nor completed transactions. I see no documented integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-instability red flag. My hesitation concerns the asset being built, not an adverse judgment about the people.

I am neutral today. The upside is a new input channel; the present evidence supports a promising research instrument. One result would flip my willingness to invest: an independently documented paid pilot in which 20 previously unseen users repeatedly achieve at least 95% word accuracy at 100 words per minute on wearable hardware, after no more than five minutes of calibration, across 30 days. That would connect the founders’ specific knowledge to a functioning physical product that customers value enough to buy.
