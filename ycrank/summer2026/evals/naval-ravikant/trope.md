# Trope -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have acted inside the problem, but not yet deeply enough to prove specific knowledge of ERP implementation. Matthew Chow and Victor Vannara built a workflow-automation product that reached paid pilots, contracted with an organization of more than 400 staff, and reportedly completed work two to three times faster with 90% fewer errors. That is stronger than credentials from Tesla, Zipline, or Amazon. The limitation is the product transition: those results preceded Trope’s July 2026 launch as a Business Central integrator, while the claimed 200-plus ERP implementations belong to unnamed principal consultants rather than demonstrably to the founders.

The market gives them room to maneuver. ERP implementation services represented an estimated $29.52 billion in 2025, and Microsoft’s 2028–2029 NAV and Dynamics GP support deadlines create a concrete migration wedge. Fixed-fee implementations can then lead into managed agents for reconciliation, invoicing, collections, and other recurring processes. This is a coherent expansion path from migration labor into the operating layer of finance, although dependence on Business Central makes Trope a tenant in Microsoft’s market before it becomes a platform of its own.

The product converts expensive human coordination into software leverage: meeting extraction, configuration inspection, data migration, testing, documentation, and post-launch operations share one agent harness. But if a competitor copies the code, the dossier does not identify what Trope keeps. Field-level APIs, side panels, release-wave tests, and an open-source computer-use layer with 28 GitHub stars demonstrate implementation, not durability. Microsoft and established Dynamics partners possess the APIs, customer relationships, and distribution needed to reproduce the visible system. Run logs and exception histories could become scarce data, but possibility is not an asset.

The strongest bull case is that Trope is not really selling code. It is compressing a six-to-eighteen-month, hourly services business into fixed-price delivery, using experienced consultants as approval gates while agents accumulate operational traces. White-label distribution through systems integrators and VARs could turn incumbents into channels rather than competitors. Yet this does not currently match the permissionless pattern I favor: partners do not demonstrably contribute reusable supply, and one customer’s deployment has not been shown to improve another’s. Each implementation may still require proportional scoping, consulting, and monitoring.

Economics are legible but unproven. There is a named payer and a direct fixed-fee transaction model, plus separately priced managed agents, but no disclosed current-product customer, completed implementation, price, revenue, or gross margin. The earlier paid pilots prevent me from treating monetization as imaginary; they do not establish value capture for the ERP offering. I also find no founder-integrity, artificial-deadline, or reassurance-seeking red flag. The issue is structural, not behavioral.

I would remain neutral rather than write a check today. The known exception for a capital-efficient product before observable distribution does not override the missing substrate here: Trope has evidence of execution, but not yet of a product language or asset competitors cannot copy. My base case is a useful, services-heavy Business Central firm reaching roughly $2–5 million in annual revenue within 36 months, with much of the gross margin still tied to expert labor. I would flip to invest if at least ten independent partners deployed Trope across fifty paying customers and the resulting shared evaluation corpus measurably improved accuracy or deployment time across customers without proportional consultant hours.
