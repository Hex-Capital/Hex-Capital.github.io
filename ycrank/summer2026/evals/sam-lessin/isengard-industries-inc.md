# Isengard Industries Inc -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Before underwriting the drones, I need three numbers the dossier does not supply: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and check size. Isengard lists YC and four other backers but discloses neither amounts nor whether this is genuinely scarce first capital. That matters because a 30-person defense company with two production cells and six announced systems may be presented as pre-seed while carrying a consensus-priced capital plan. I cannot treat financing silence as cheapness.

The operational story is much stronger than the deal story. Four systems are described as operational, one is in rate production, and Ukrainian units including Alpha Group SBU and the 3rd Assault Brigade reportedly field the products; the U.S. Army is evaluating them. Those are meaningful pre-seed actions, not followers or integration theater. But the causal dark pool remains incomplete: the battlefield access and shared autonomy architecture could generate proprietary data, yet performance is company-reported, no repeat purchasing is documented, and the advertised 3,000-unit monthly capacity is a target rather than output.

Serra-Martins is the best evidence here. He has served as a combat engineer and previously led Terminal Autonomy, which reportedly produced $60 million in revenue. Tennant adds military service, defense investing, and financing experience. Together they have shipped from the weapons-production side and operated inside the military and procurement ecosystems they must bridge. The missing piece is organizational accountability: three co-founders are named, Skokowski’s role is opaque, and the dossier does not identify who has final authority beyond Serra-Martins’s CEO title.

The capital plan is not yet a venture moment. Isengard names a broad destination—mass-produced strike and counter-UAS systems—but not the one finite experiment this round buys, its deadline, or its acceptance metric. Is the card being flipped a verified intercept rate, a 1,000-unit production run, DDTC-approved sovereign manufacturing, or a paid deployment? Six products, two factories, AI targeting, visual navigation, foreign licensing, and government sales are too many simultaneous uncertainties unless the financing isolates one truth that changes the company’s value.

The bull case stings: named frontline deployments, a prior operator with reported eight-figure revenue, and actual production facilities could mean public revenue data simply trails a classified procurement pipeline. This is also the kind of hard operating evidence that can override an unattractive seed price, as NanoCo’s external adoption and acquisition offer did. The material difference is verification. Isengard supplies no priced order, independent performance result, unit output, or customer retention evidence that would justify overriding the missing deal terms.

My 36-month base case is that Isengard reaches $10 million–$30 million in annual revenue from allied procurement by August 2029 but remains loss-making and dependent on another institutional round because multi-year sales cycles, localization approvals, and six-system manufacturing consume cash faster than contracts convert. That can still become an important defense company; it is not yet a one-and-done personal angel check. I pass today. One verifiable flip condition is a signed, priced 12-month procurement contract whose positive unit margin covers the company’s current operating burn.
