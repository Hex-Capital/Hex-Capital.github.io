# Nxtcure Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Hammond’s cancer experience and oncology research give Nxtcure Labs something more valuable than an attractive market: a reason these founders encountered the problem. Screening a patient for trial eligibility reportedly takes several hours. That is a concrete annoyance with serious consequences. The work around it—clinical records, eligibility rules, consent, and coordination—is exactly the sort of unpleasant work that can conceal a startup opportunity. This looks like an organically discovered problem.

But I cannot reconstruct the most important sequence: what stopped the founders, what they built to get around it, and how quickly the situation changed. The dossier establishes that they shipped products. It does not establish a particular act of resourcefulness under resistance. Harvard, Yale, and MIT cannot supply that missing observation. I give the founders the absent-evidence default here, without interpreting silence as passivity. This matters because founder behavior is my principal reason for investing before the business is obvious.

The earlier matching app is real evidence of building. Its private-practice pilots and reported 93% matching accuracy deserve some credit. But the accuracy claim has no disclosed validation cohort, and Galen’s demonstration uses synthetic and CDISC pilot datasets. A downloadable app establishes more than an architecture diagram; it establishes less than a clinical workflow that works reliably for a customer. Likewise, private-practice pilots identify a plausible user niche, but do not tell me whether those users returned or demanded more. Neither the two app ratings nor the $2.99 Luna purchase option establishes recurring demand or revenue.

The strongest argument for Nxtcure is that operating the service could teach the founders things a software vendor never learns. Qualified human review could be how they discover what to automate. Medable’s overlapping agents therefore do not settle the question. A small team willing to do the clinical work personally might deliver a result that configurable software leaves the customer to assemble. Hammond’s direct exposure and Clark’s computational biology background make that possibility credible. But Galen currently describes thirteen agents spanning much of a CRO, while the evidence of customer contact belongs principally to the earlier matching product. The narrow opening is clearer than the proposed full-stack business.

Nxtcure’s move from matching in April 2026 to the current CRO offering could represent useful learning. The dossier does not identify the observation that caused it, so I cannot score it as an evidence-driven change of route. Nor do the listed relationships with NCI, Anthropic, OpenAI, and NVIDIA establish distribution. There is no evidence that the founders are waiting on those organizations, so the partnership red flag is unproven. The short disclosed cofounder history also remains an uncertainty. A compelling working prototype or independently validated scientific result could override such uncertainty; the app and unvalidated matching claim do not yet provide that strength of proof. Nxtcure has shipped, but it has not demonstrated the concrete proof spike that would change my decision.

I would pass on investing today. The decisive gap is the missing connection between founder action and a user’s improved situation, rather than the absence of a long revenue history. My flip condition is one documented deployment at a named oncology practice: a specific enrollment blocker, a founder-built workaround shipped within weeks, and that practice subsequently paying to use it again. That would connect the founders’ unusually relevant experience to observable resourcefulness and real pull. It would tell me considerably more than expanding the agent roster.
