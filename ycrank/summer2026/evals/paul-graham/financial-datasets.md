# Financial Datasets -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that Financial Datasets has an MCP server. It is that Virat Singh spent two years manually mapping more than 17,000 issuer-specific XBRL tags. The obstacle was concrete: public filings are full of inconsistent company-specific labels, restatements, and identifier changes. His workaround produced a functioning data layer rather than a diagram. But the dossier does not show what first broke, how quickly he responded, or a dated before-and-after result, so this is strong—not conclusive—evidence of resourcefulness.

The technical proof is clearer. Financial Datasets serves filings, fundamentals, ownership data, prices, and operating KPIs through APIs and a hosted MCP server. Its audit cycle reportedly checks 20,000 data points across 1,000 companies and 75 sectors against original filings. This is precisely the sort of tedious substrate people claim they will build after funding. Singh built it before permission. His investment-banking experience, engineering work at Airbnb, Acorns, and Faire, and the 26,600-star Dexter repository also make this look like a problem he encountered through work rather than an “AI for finance” idea manufactured for an accelerator.

There appears to be a narrow well of users. The founder reports one million daily API requests and thousands of agents, while Julius and OpenBB have integrated the product. Engineers and researchers at OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Jane Street, Citadel, Stanford, and MIT reportedly use the data. These claims identify the niche and demonstrate a functioning product, but they do not reveal retention, payment, or whether a few integrations generate most of the traffic. Fourteen thousand company followers and Singh’s 50,000-person audience help distribution, but attention is not pull. I therefore give the usage substantial credit with a certainty discount.

The bear case is unpleasantly plausible. Financial data APIs are substitutable, public SEC data is free, price data comes from a licensed provider, and Massive, Intrinio, Alpha Vantage, and Finnhub already cover overlapping endpoints. At $200 per month for 100,000 requests, a million daily calls could represent a valuable utility or an expensive free feature; the dossier provides no revenue result that distinguishes the two. The Julius partnership and OpenBB integration would be dangerous if “partner” were the whole distribution strategy. They are not: self-service signup, open-source projects, and Singh’s developer audience provide independent routes to users. Still, the company has not yet shown switching costs or exclusive data.

The 2024 synthetic-question dataset and the current primary-source API may represent an intelligent change of route, but there is no evidence about why the product changed. I cannot award adaptability merely because two products differ. Nor is there evidence Singh ignored contrary feedback, so this dimension receives its missing-evidence default. The larger schlep is real—maintaining issuer mappings through restatements, splits, acquisitions, and ticker changes—but the specific autobiographical collision that caused Singh to begin remains undocumented.

I would invest, though not at a price that assumes the reported request volume has already become durable revenue. The company matches two important revealed patterns: a technical founder has assembled a difficult programmable substrate, and developers are already invoking it before the category has settled. My 36-month expectation is that Financial Datasets becomes a specialized agent-native filings layer with $3–8 million in ARR, rather than a broad Bloomberg replacement. The verifiable flip condition is retention: if fewer than 10% of the August 2026 agent cohort remains active after 90 days, I would pass, because the apparent narrow well would actually be launch traffic.
