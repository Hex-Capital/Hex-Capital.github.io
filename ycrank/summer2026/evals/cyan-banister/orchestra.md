# Orchestra -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: companies no longer rent the same general intelligence for every repetitive task. Frontier models teach and audit fleets of cheaper specialists; production traces become training material; customers retain the resulting weights. Orchestra has a small leak from that future: it processed 39,962 comments for $2.82 and now shows a task costing $0.000074 against a $0.000444 frontier baseline. But the dossier does not map the intervening transitions—how evaluation quality compounds, when workloads graduate to specialists, or why Orchestra owns that transition. “100x” is also far ahead of the current 83.4% reduction.

The broken rule is much clearer. A company corrects a frontier model repeatedly, yet the provider captures another inference payment while the customer retains none of the learned behavior. Orchestra converts those corrections into evaluations, routing rules, and customer-owned weights, then keeps the frontier model as a quality backstop. That returns both economic and technical agency to the customer. It is the strongest part of the company because the rent is measurable rather than rhetorical.

There is a working artifact, but not yet strange customer behavior. The disclosed 8B model reached 0.9630 against Sonnet’s 1.0000, and the team acknowledges repeated holdout use, missing confidence intervals, and a fully loaded Fireworks cost of roughly $1.56 where modeled tokens cost $0.006617. I like the honesty; it identifies exactly where low-volume economics break. But these are internal experiments. No named customer, revenue, repeat deployment, or operator has entrusted a consequential workflow to Orchestra. Processing 39,962 comments proves machinery, not demand.

Luis Manrique and Aamir Poonawalla have unusually relevant operating tuition: Gumloop and Instacart AI product work on one side, ten years of ads-serving, auctions, experimentation, and infrastructure on the other. They have worked together, and Luis is plainly identified as CEO, so I do not see a leadership shell game in the available evidence. What I cannot find is the first-believer act: no dated pre-company experiment, customer wound that became the specification, or recruiting achievement beyond six role pages. Their résumés explain capability; they do not yet demonstrate obsession.

Orchestra sits below the application wave, but it does not yet control a scarce layer. Customers own the evaluations and weights, the repository has 18 stars, and Fireworks can both serve the models and offer overlapping continuous specialization. That makes Orchestra simultaneously dependent on and threatened by a supplier with more than $1 billion in annualized revenue. The privacy architecture is more thoughtful than alarming—gateway payloads are not stored by default and local artifacts remain local unless shared—but uploaded traces can still pass through Orchestra and subprocessors. Data custody therefore remains an enterprise gate, not a moat.

The strongest bull case is that a one-URL substitution lets this deeply qualified team enter through cost savings, quietly accumulate evaluation infrastructure, and become the control plane for customer-owned intelligence. That could make the absence of revenue irrelevant at pre-seed. My pre-revenue exception, however, requires more than a functioning prototype: it requires external proof that institutions will cross the operational trust boundary. I am neutral today. One condition flips me to Invest: three named customers each routing at least 10 million production calls per month for six consecutive months while specialist models preserve at least 90% of the frontier baseline’s quality at one-tenth the fully loaded cost.
