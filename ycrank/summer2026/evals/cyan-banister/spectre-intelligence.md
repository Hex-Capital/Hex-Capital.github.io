# Spectre Intelligence -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A trading firm can create a new sector pod by instantiating agents, feeding them expert knowledge, and letting live P&L select which reasoning survives. Spectre Intelligence has a small leak from that world today: two semiconductor and biotech pods are already risking real capital and recording decisions, traces, and outcomes. The intervening chain is still incomplete—better memory, longer-running reliability, continuous learning, and resistance to alpha decay—but at least the future is attached to a working experiment rather than an AI-market slide.

Michelle Li and Joshua Zyzak have credible ingredients, not yet a demonstrated obsession. Li brings biology research, personal trading experience, and a prior patent-pending sanitation startup; Zyzak brings reinforcement-learning robotics, AI engineering, and Optiver’s trading program. Those paths explain the chosen sectors and technical approach. They do not yet show years of pre-company trading artifacts, independently verified returns, or the recruiting gravity required to build both an investment organization and an expert network. Two Harvard founders taking evolutionary biology together is an origin story; it is not first-believer proof.

The strongest bull case is the data loop. Every live decision can become a labeled example connecting information, judgment, execution, and P&L. If Spectre is genuinely profitable, it can finance the difficult training asset internally while competitors burn outside capital. That would be wonderfully strange: the fund is simultaneously the product, customer, laboratory, and dataset generator. The missing number matters, though. “Already profitable” without AUM, return history, benchmark, drawdown, or dollar profit prevents me from distinguishing learned judgment from a favorable handful of trades.

I also cannot find the broken rule whose rent Spectre removes. Human analyst pods are expensive and proprietary judgment is fragmented, but the dossier does not quantify that cost, identify who captures an artificial rent, or show whose occupational control is returned. Spectre may simply replace one opaque allocator with another. If it later manages outside money, adviser obligations, conflicts, custody, and recordkeeping become part of the machine—not administrative debris to wave away.

My larger concern is stack position. Spectre is not yet the enabling layer beneath AI trading; it is an AI trading application competing with Abundance, Nof1, Numerai, and established quantitative funds using accessible models, market data, and execution infrastructure. The company admits that successful strategies will be copied and early alpha will decay. Its proprietary traces could become infrastructure, but resemblance is not control: no dataset scale, exclusive information source, model advantage, or demonstrated dependency by downstream firms has been established.

I would not write the check today, but this is not a dismissal. The live-capital artifact is materially better than a simulated leaderboard, and verified compounding could overwhelm several missing signals. My flip condition is precise: show an independently verified twelve-month return series, net of costs, that beats an appropriate semiconductor-and-biotech benchmark with controlled drawdown, plus ablation evidence that performance improves because of the accumulated decision-and-P&L dataset. That would convert “profitable AI fund” from a fashionable claim into a controlled learning system.
