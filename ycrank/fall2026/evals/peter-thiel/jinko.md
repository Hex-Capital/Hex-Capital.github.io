# Jinko -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Jinko’s possible secret is that agents need an independent travel operator to complete and service transactions, even when inventory suppliers offer their own interfaces. That proposition would be falsified if developers reliably booked and serviced trips through suppliers without needing Jinko. The dossier establishes the operational problem, but identifies neither a prevailing belief the founders uniquely reject nor an experiment establishing their answer. Agentic travel is an opening. Whether it contains a secret remains unresolved.

The more immediate problem is ownership of that opening. Etraveli launched its Flight MCP on October 28, 2026, covering payment, ticketing, and servicing; Nuitée also offers MCP access. Competition has already reached the transactional layer. Jinko’s combination of flights, hotels, and cars may be useful, but broader coverage does not establish a narrow market it can dominate. Its advertised 800 airlines and 2.6 million hotels describe inventory access, not exclusive control. Contracting-operator responsibilities and liability insurance demonstrate operational substance; they do not yet demonstrate a monopoly mechanism.

The founders are the strongest reason to resist an easy dismissal. Gauthier, Texier, and Zhang previously built Winglet and Mowgli together and subsequently worked at Hopper. This is an operating team with shared history, not strangers assembled around the latest financing category. Their repeated work in travel fits my preference for companies formed through durable operator relationships. The CTO’s engineering background also makes the team more substantial than a commercial partnership outsourcing its central product. Nevertheless, current ownership, full-time status, and allocation of core engineering are not established. The evidence supports founding continuity, without proving the entire alignment gate.

The strongest bull case is that Jinko absorbs responsibilities developers cannot solve by adding another tool connection: checkout, payments, supplier coordination, and post-booking service. Its documented product and [Exa Connect listing](https://exa.ai/connect) make that case more credible than a presentation about future agents. Perhaps operational completeness becomes the scarce resource while inventory interfaces proliferate. But Etraveli’s servicing capability directly challenges that mechanism, and the dossier supplies no customer behavior showing why developers retain Jinko when a supplier offers overlapping functionality. My exceptions for demonstrated technical breakthroughs or persistent transaction demand do not apply on the evidence available. The familiar AI-interface competition flag does apply; alleged founder misalignment or regulatory misconduct does not.

The economics sharpen the distinction between creating convenience and capturing value. At the published 1% flight-booking fee, $10 billion of annual booking value would generate $100 million in fees before costs. That is a coherent revenue engine, but servicing obligations make the missing margin structure consequential. The $0.005 excess-search charge above a 1,500:1 search-to-book ratio addresses exploratory usage; it does not establish profitable delivery. Exa provides a named acquisition route, while documentation and the July 2026 builder event support developer access. None establishes repeatable conversion or acquisition economics.

I would pass with my own money. Jinko has unusually relevant founders, but its strongest evidence concerns the team’s ability to operate a travel business, while its weakest concerns the company’s ability to own one. I would reopen the decision upon one verifiable result: a paid agent-developer cohort that retains Jinko for six months at its published booking fee after testing comparable direct-supplier booking and servicing. That would begin to distinguish an indispensable operating layer from an intermediary customers can bypass.
