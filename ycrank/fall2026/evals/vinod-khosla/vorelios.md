# Vorelios -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Hours of computation collapsing into milliseconds could change how engineers search for better aircraft. Vorelios has evidence for fast predictions; it has not yet established how much engineering those predictions replace. That distinction determines whether I am buying an option on radically cheaper engineering or another simulation tool. The $14.2 billion broader CAE market establishes an existing spending pool. It does not quantify the aircraft development cost, fuel consumption, or engineering capacity Vorelios could change.

The technical work deserves attention. Vorelios reports inference times of 1.1–31 milliseconds on an H100, with NASA Common Research Model errors of 6.37% for pressure and 3.81% for skin friction against solver outputs. Its published comparisons name Transolver-3 and AB-UPT. This is substantially more informative than an AI claim attached to a contact form. But lower errors against research alternatives do not establish acceptable aircraft design decisions. Small field-prediction errors can matter greatly when integrated into drag estimates or used to select between closely competing designs. The decisive question is how much conventional computation survives after engineers account for those errors.

The strongest argument for investing is that Vorelios fits an important pattern: turn an expensive scientific workflow into repeatable model inference, using internally generated simulations to supply training data. Physics study and autonomy research give Daniel McNeil and Krish Kapadia relevant starting points. Two founders publishing concrete results are worth taking seriously. If their predictions reliably guide exploration across unfamiliar geometries, the company could expand the number of designs engineers can economically test. That possibility is substantial. However, the dossier establishes neither an exclusive learning loop nor the transfer from benchmark performance to that customer outcome. Luminary already generates simulation data and offers aircraft models, so producing more training simulations alone does not resolve competitive differentiation.

I would spend the next dollars on that transfer question. The dossier supplies no founder-owned experiment with a cost, numerical acceptance threshold, deadline, and stop-or-pivot response. That earns the missing-evidence default for risk retirement; I cannot award the published benchmark again under a second heading. Likewise, McNeil’s physics background and Kapadia’s University of Florida TEA Lab research do not document rapid belief revision or recruiting someone to own a missing capability. Those behaviors remain unknown. Revenue absence, follower counts, and zero job listings do not change my decision at this stage.

The economic uncertainty is equally specific. Millisecond inference excludes the cost of generating simulations, training models, adapting them to customers, and checking their answers. Vorelios discloses no pricing or complete cost comparison. My exceptions for exceptionally consequential missions or unusually compelling instigators do not override that gap here: the dossier does not quantify the downstream consequence or document the founder behavior needed for such an override. Nor does it establish the red flags of generic model replication, premature sales expansion, or refusal to revise a plan. This is a technically interesting company with an incomplete investment case.

I pass with my own money today. My verifiable flip condition is an independently documented aerospace customer trial showing at least a 10x reduction in total computational and engineering cost for a completed design-optimization task on a held-out geometry family, while meeting the customer’s predeclared accuracy and design-acceptance thresholds. Include training allocation, adaptation, and required solver verification in that accounting. That result would connect Vorelios’s strongest evidence—fast physical prediction—to the economic consequence I need to underwrite.
