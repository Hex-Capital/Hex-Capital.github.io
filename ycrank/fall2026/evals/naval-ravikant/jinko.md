# Jinko -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Jinko’s founders have earned specific knowledge through action. They built Mowgli, sold it to Hopper, and subsequently worked inside travel distribution and booking engineering. That is substantially better evidence than three impressive biographies. The consumer-to-infrastructure pivot documented by August 2026 also puts their learning into a concrete product: discovery, live pricing, booking, payments, and servicing through API, MCP, and CLI. These founders understand what happens after someone clicks “book.”

The decisive question is what Jinko owns after a competitor copies its interface. Its contracting travel-operator role and liability coverage give the business substance beyond software. But responsibility is valuable only if accepting it creates an advantage competitors cannot economically reproduce. The dossier establishes operating obligations; it does not establish exclusive inventory, proprietary models, or accumulated resolution data unavailable elsewhere. Etraveli’s October 28 flight-MCP launch makes supplier substitution a present competitive mechanism. Jinko must become more valuable than the underlying providers’ direct access.

The strongest opposing argument is that this team can make that happen. Developers may prefer one accountable counterparty spanning flights, hotels, cars, and post-booking problems over assembling several suppliers. The advertised 800 airlines, 2.6 million hotels, and 28,000 rental locations make the product’s intended breadth concrete, although inventory coverage is not adoption. Personal-agent developers provide an initial buyer; work agents, banks, and existing consumer platforms offer adjacent distribution for the same transaction capabilities. Exa Connect supplies an actual distribution foothold. This fits my pattern of backing infrastructure that lets outsiders build, but a listing does not yet demonstrate proprietary distribution.

Jinko also names a payer and a price: 1% of flight-booking value, with $0.005 excess searches above a 1,500:1 search-to-book ratio. That is useful business-model evidence at this stage. It does not establish the contribution remaining after supplier costs, payment processing, and servicing. More importantly, the dossier shows no mechanism through which one customer improves another customer’s experience. Shared access to inventory is not itself a network effect. The danger is that each additional agent brings more transactions and more exceptions requiring company labor.

I would hold my money for now: **Neutral**. There is no documented integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-behavior red flag, and the pivot alone supplies no evidence of weak conviction. My exceptions for unresolved monetization or absent distribution depend on demonstrated self-reinforcing behavior or an exceptional product advantage; neither is established here. Founder-specific knowledge is Jinko’s strongest argument for an exception, but it does not settle whether the business compounds. My flip condition is one six-month production cohort demonstrating that exclusive supplier-resolution data generated by earlier customers measurably improves booking success for later, unrelated customers without increasing servicing labor per booking. That would turn operational experience into a durable asset.
