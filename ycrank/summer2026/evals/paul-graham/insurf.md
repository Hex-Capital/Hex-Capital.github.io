# Insurf -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Only 11.5% of denied Medicare Advantage requests were appealed in 2024, while 80.7% of appealed denials were overturned. Insurf is a schlep-rich, origin-poor startup: the work is wonderfully unpleasant, but the dossier never shows that Bryan Chung or Chang Lu personally suffered the problem. Insurf has found a swamp full of payer rules, physician attestations, deadlines, BAAs, and posted-payment reconciliation. I like swamps. I am less certain these founders are animals who discovered the swamp by falling into it themselves.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness because compliance was not incidental to the product; enduring it became the product. Insurf has the same promising mechanism. Insurf retains versioned payer policies, clinical sources, attestations, submissions, and outcomes while confronting a market in which only 3% of examined billing codes are shared across all payers. But Brian Armstrong built the Bitcoin wallet after rejection and then ran toward the regulatory grind. Insurf keeps production PHI disabled pending BAAs, vendor coverage, technical gates, and founder approval. That is sensible, but it means the hardest part of Insurf remains ahead rather than behind.

**Loopt** (2005) exposed the low-rung failure mechanism for organic ideas: formidable founder force can temporarily disguise that the product is not yet rooted in a working necessity. Chang Lu’s Brown BS/MD background and cancer-modeling research put Insurf near healthcare, while Bryan Chung’s applied mathematics, computer science, and ML work put Insurf near the machinery. Near is not inside. Insurf provides no evidence that either founder processed a denial, appealed a claim, or built the first version to solve his own daily problem. I am applying nearly the absent-evidence default here because genuine organic ideas usually produce a very specific story.

Insurf’s public live demo is an artifact, which puts Insurf above the deck-first founders I normally pass on. But animal resourcefulness compounds only when the artifact collides with reality: a founder manually obtains records, persuades a physician to attest, submits through a hostile payer channel, and chases the posted payment. Over 18 months, each ugly exception could become another edge in Insurf’s decision graph. The dossier instead says private customer evidence exists in controlled channels without publishing a customer count, outcome series, revenue, or even one named pilot. A demo is evidence that the founders can build. It is not yet evidence that they will bite through walls.

Bryan Chung has two IEEE publications, a reported 4.0 GPA, and responsibility for Insurf’s decision graph, while Insurf also has a functioning public demo. That is substantially better than technical credentials alone. The hacker score would flip upward if Bryan Chung had a pre-Insurf open-source artifact, a public repository, or a technically strange system already processing real payer-policy versions and outcomes. No public repositories were found for either founder, so Insurf currently looks like two unusually capable students rather than hackers whose trails existed before the startup.

**Stripe** (2010) is the useful co-founder parallel: two young technical founders with complementary abilities can be enough, especially when the work joins software to a horrible institutional interface. Insurf has the correct count, and Chang Lu’s clinical education complements Bryan Chung’s technical work. But Insurf establishes only that both attended Brown, not that they built together for years or survived an earlier failure together. The Collisons had sibling history and Auctomatic. Insurf has a promising pair whose chemistry remains an interview question.

Amperos has raised $20.2 million to automate overlapping portal, call, collection, and appeal workflows. By July 2029, I expect Insurf to have pivoted from end-to-end hospital denial appeals into payer-policy and clinical-evidence infrastructure sold to revenue-cycle vendors, with less than $3 million in ARR. Insurf’s strongest asset is likely to be the versioned evidence graph created by the schlep; Insurf’s weakness is that founders without demonstrated personal roots in hospital revenue-cycle work may retreat from owning the entire operational loop once integrations, PHI controls, and physician review become the daily job.

CMS prior-authorization requirements began taking effect on January 1, 2026, which is the strongest case against that forecast. Insurf could be early to a newly standardized data stream, and an 80.7% overturn rate gives Insurf an unusually legible customer outcome. The **Coinbase** exception stings here: outsiders can become the right founders precisely because everyone with experience has learned to avoid the grind. But Insurf has no named customer, no graph size, no production outcome count, and production PHI is still disabled, so the dossier does not yet show that exception operating. One named hospital reference documenting at least 100 completed appeals through Insurf, with Insurf founders personally running the workflow and payments actually posted, would flip my verdict to Invest.
