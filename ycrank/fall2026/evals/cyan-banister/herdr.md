# herdr -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

The little detail that catches me is the Stream Deck client. Someone has taken a physical button on their desk and connected it to an agent runtime. Alongside Raycast and iOS interfaces, that suggests developers are beginning to supervise work that continues somewhere else. Move the clock to 2032: people assign jobs across machines, leave, and return when a decision needs them. Herdr’s path into that world has identifiable steps—persistent execution, meaningful agent states, then coordination across customer-owned machines. The background server and socket API are present-day pieces of that path. Reliable recovery remains unfinished: restarting the server still destroys the original processes.

Users are doing some of the imagining themselves. The company reported more than 500 plugins within the marketplace’s first month in August 2026; its October homepage reports 1,789. Building an extension costs someone time and creates a dependency they must maintain. DigitalOcean’s plugin demonstration and Fly.io’s Sprites integration add concrete examples beyond the counters. I give that labor more weight than 46,194 GitHub stars or 1,644,092 cumulative installs, neither of which establishes sustained use. There is a working artifact here that people are rearranging their tools around.

Can Celik also encountered the problem through his own multi-agent workflow. His AI-assisted ultrasound work, fintech AI leadership, and separate pi-extensions project give me more substance than a newly adopted founder biography. He has built something that attracts other builders. But the dossier does not establish years of independent experimentation before herdr, complementary team recruitment, or command of the eventual paid business. The $6 million September seed provides resources; it does not answer those questions. A single named founder and CEO supplies clearer accountability than an ambiguous founding partnership, although ownership and control terms remain unreported.

Now ask why the developer keeps checking the terminal. Execution outlives the interface, sessions become disconnected, and nobody reliably tells the human when intervention is needed. Herdr repairs that coordination failure and returns attention to the user. That fits my interest in occupational control, but the economic mechanism is less developed: there is no quantified toll, artificial restriction, or identifiable gatekeeper whose rent disappears. The runtime is free under Apache-2.0, while the forthcoming Cloud service proposes to remove SSH configuration. People loving a free tool and people paying to connect their machines are separate propositions.

The strongest objection to investing is already shipping: Herdr Extended distributes upstream-based binaries with additional functionality. Herdr could cultivate the ecosystem while another distributor captures its commercial demand. Warp and Paradev have adjacent routes into the same workflow. This tests my preference for concealed infrastructure beneath a fashionable application: herdr owns runtime implementation and an integration surface, but the dossier demonstrates neither a scarce bottleneck nor exclusive control over distribution. Still, the existing extension labor gives the original project a credible coordination advantage. Forkability weakens capture without making that accumulated participation worthless.

Plugin permissions are the more immediate concern. Unreviewed, unsandboxed extensions inherit the user’s environment and permissions; an agent developer’s environment can contain valuable credentials. Installation previews and revision pinning provide explicit user controls, and pane-history persistence is disabled by default. Those choices matter, but they do not contain malicious execution. The dossier establishes exposure without establishing a severe privacy incident or concealed collection architecture. I would not invoke a past exception to excuse it. My one verifiable flip condition is evidence that an automatically indexed plugin exfiltrates credentials despite a user following the documented installation controls; that would turn my investment lean into a pass until containment is demonstrated.

I lean **Invest** with my own money because herdr has unusually tangible evidence of developers building a new working habit around its runtime. My expectation is that the open project becomes more widely used than the paid business over the next three years. That can still produce an attractive company if machine connectivity becomes a service users willingly purchase. The current evidence earns an early bet on that transition, with limited conviction about commercial capture and no justification for treating popularity as ownership of the future.
