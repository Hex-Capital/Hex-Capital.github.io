# Cyclon -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine asking my phone to get me home and having transportation arranged without opening an app. The interesting change is who controls the interaction: can I make the computer work on my terms? Cyclon’s proposed user-created apps and permissioned agents point toward that world. But I am supplying the connecting story. Wider smartphone NPU deployment is an industry catalyst; Cyclon’s detachable-camera concept is not yet an artifact demonstrating that people have started living differently. I need something from that future I can touch.

The founders give me a reason to keep looking. Thomas Kiefer worked on Android at Google; Adam Zinebi combines microtechnology training with Berkeley research on LLMs and robotic agents, and previously sold models through a music-generation business. Those are relevant experiences, including some contact with commercial reality. They make this more interesting than two people discovering that AI phones are fashionable. They do not establish a longstanding obsession with this particular problem or a record of recruiting others into it. The strongest founder evidence is practical preparation, with the first-believer story still missing.

Why does ordering a ride require navigating an app? Which part is necessary, and which part exists because someone else controls the doorway? Cyclon identifies the navigation burden, but the dossier does not establish a particular gatekeeper’s rent, a quantified delay, or how its replacement phone returns durable control to the owner. That distinction matters when agents operate through personal accounts. User-defined rules are a starting point; the dossier does not establish how permissions, credentials, and unintended actions are contained. I cannot call this a demonstrated severe privacy violation, but I also cannot underwrite user agency from the promise of permission alone.

The strongest case against passing is that this team may understand the whole device well enough to make an interaction that software layered onto existing phones cannot deliver. Brain Technologies’ April 2026 SoftBank launch establishes that intention-based phones can reach retail distribution, and Nothing’s generated apps show that the behavior is becoming technically approachable. Neither proves Cyclon’s implementation, but both make the ambition less fanciful. My pre-revenue exception could apply here: revenue is unnecessary when a working prototype and unusually compelling founder evidence carry the bet. Cyclon’s public evidence does not yet clear that alternative route. An undisclosed waitlist and ten public repositories, including LineageOS and microG forks, do not demonstrate repeated completed tasks or people trusting this as their everyday phone.

Cyclon fits the speculative side of my interests, but I cannot find the concealed enabling asset beneath the consumer object. The forks show where the team is working; they do not establish control of a scarce dependency. Nor does the dossier show the unusual user behavior that sometimes reveals an opportunity before a formal pitch can explain it. I pass with my own money today. The condition that would reopen the decision is an independently observed 30-day trial in which ten users voluntarily make Cyclon their primary phone, repeatedly complete consequential tasks through its agents, and can inspect and revoke those agents’ permissions. That would test whether the proposed interface actually returns control—and whether anyone wants to live inside it.
