# Agentcard -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The crucial missing fact is what broke. Karen Serfaty has unusually relevant history: she built the expense-card company Atlas, sold it to Remote in January 2026, and then ran Cards there. But a résumé is not a resourcefulness test. The dossier never names an Agentcard obstacle, the workaround Serfaty or Felipe Abello shipped, or how quickly that action changed a user or technical outcome. I cannot infer running-back behavior from prior success, so the people-first gate remains unproven.

The product itself is more convincing. Agentcard has built OAuth, MCP, CLI, and API surfaces around amount- and merchant-restricted virtual Visa credentials, including single-use cards, a credential vault, and hosted KYC, fraud, dispute, and chargeback handling through Third National. This is real schlep, not an invented “AI-for-X” noun. Serfaty encountered adjacent card infrastructure through Atlas and Remote, so the origin looks organic. But the dossier supplies no transaction count, authorization rate, fraud result, or independent benchmark. It proves a deployed system, not yet a measured technical spike.

The narrow well is still shallow. Ten named organizations appear under “Trusted by,” and an unnamed platform serving 1.5 million users reportedly began integrating in July 2026. Those are better signals than the 560 Product Hunt followers or three launches, but integration is not pull. We do not know whether agents completed repeated purchases, whether users returned, or whether anyone pays the $5,000 monthly company subscription. Pricing is not revenue. The simple question remains unanswered: who needs this crude product every week?

The hidden positive pattern is that Agentcard could become a programmable primitive for developers, while the founders have already assembled the ugly issuing and compliance substrate. The counterargument stings because Stripe, Crossmint, and Alchemy can assemble much of the same stack, and Alchemy has even created an identically named product. Agentcard also calls the product a debit card while its terms describe a collateralized credit account; SOC 2 and PCI DSS are pending; and core flows depend on Third National, Sumsub, Stripe, Visa, and several funding providers. Those red flags make precise execution unusually important, yet the dossier shows no evidence-driven product revision. Three launches are not three pivots.

The strongest bull case is that a repeat card founder has already shipped the difficult product and gathered ten logos before meaningful public traction should be expected. The **Dropbox** exception is relevant only in mechanism: a functioning product can override missing conventional evidence in a crowded market. The material difference is that Dropbox also supplied unusually rapid founder correction and unmistakable product behavior. Agentcard has not yet shown either. Its launches, logos, and large-platform evaluation do not replace the missing obstacle-action sequence or repeated usage.

My 36-month expectation is that Agentcard pivots from a generic “card for agents” into B2B agent-spend controls and reaches $1–3 million ARR rather than becoming the default agent-payments network. I would not write the check today. One verifiable condition would flip me: a named customer paying $5,000 per month for eight consecutive weeks of repeated agent-initiated purchases, accompanied by a dated account of which failed authorization or integration forced which shipped product change.
