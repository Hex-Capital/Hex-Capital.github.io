# Boat -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Boat has built something other developers can build on. On October 23, OpenInspect announced support for its sandboxes, including tunnels, prebuilt images, and filesystem snapshots. That matters more than the Product Hunt finish. An integration gives us an observable consequence of the software working. But my first question for these founders remains unanswered: what broke while they were building, what did they do about it, and how long did that take? The dossier documents a functioning product without documenting the particular encounter with resistance that would make me confident about the people.

The technical evidence is substantial for this stage. Boat exposes Ubuntu machines through a CLI, API, and SDKs, with separate kernels and nested virtualization. OpenInspect supplies some outside corroboration that the interface is usable. This fits my preference for programmable tools that let developers assemble their own systems. I give the shipped product substantial credit. I give the architecture claims less certainty: documentation establishes what Boat offers, but supplies neither an independently measured performance advantage nor evidence that competitors cannot reproduce it.

The strongest reason to invest is the reported demand. More than 1,000 active paying users as of October 24, alongside reported six-figure ARR, would be unusually useful evidence for a company this young. The niche is intelligible: developers whose agents need persistent computers with browsers and development tools. These users have a job to finish, and Boat removes infrastructure work from that job. But both commercial figures come from the founders. They establish a promising claim about payment, without showing how many customers return, expand their workloads, or recommend the product. I would keep the user count and ARR separate until their definitions reconcile.

Nougaret says the infrastructure emerged from building cloud coding agents. That is a better origin than deciding that agents are fashionable and searching for something to sell them. Maintaining machines, images, and snapshots is precisely the tedious work that can conceal an opportunity. Still, this is a retrospective account without a dated development history. Extracting the infrastructure also does not, by itself, prove an evidence-driven pivot: the dossier never identifies the customer observation that changed the route. The founders’ reported five-year acquaintance reduces the concern about an application assembled around strangers, but does not establish how they handle disagreement or setbacks.

The uncomfortable possibility is that Boat has found customers for cheap compute rather than discovered a product they particularly need from Boat. Its advertised $0.036 hourly price compares with $0.331 for E2B and Daytona, but that is a vendor comparison, not evidence of sustainable margins on equivalent workloads. Google and Modal already offer overlapping capabilities. A programmable interface fits one of my recurring investment patterns; interchangeable capacity weakens the mechanism behind that pattern. There is no documented dependence on a major partner, and customer outreach accompanies the launch activity, so I would not infer those distribution red flags. The unresolved issue is whether usage creates attachment faster than competitors erase the price advantage.

I would hold off on a personal check. Boat deserves consideration under the **Dropbox** working-product exception: both offer functioning software in a crowded category. The material difference is that Boat’s dossier does not establish the rapid founder correction that helped resolve uncertainty in that exception. Reported payments make this worth investigating, but do not yet replace the missing founder evidence. My flip condition is one documented customer-blocker episode: a dated failure, a founder-built fix shipped within two weeks, and that customer’s subsequent paid reuse. That would connect the working machines to the resourcefulness I am actually trying to buy.
