# Marble -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the appealing picture is obvious: a restaurant manager photographs a shelf, speaks one correction, and the back-of-house quietly orders ingredients, schedules labor, reconciles invoices, and adjusts tomorrow’s prep. Marble has a leak from that world—live software operating across six named U.S. and Canadian restaurant groups—but the dossier does not map the intervening transitions. It tells me what the product does today, not which institutional or technical changes make Marble the inevitable control layer six years from now.

The founders are the strongest part of this bet. Aakar Khanna managed his family’s multi-unit restaurants, covered the sector at Goldman Sachs, and then worked in startup operations. Arjun Chaliha led AI/ML engineering for electronic trading at Bloomberg and previously co-owned a tutoring marketplace. That is a credible Rizz–Tizz–Biz pairing: lived operational pain, technical fluency, and a decade-long relationship. What is missing is dated evidence of obsession expressed through multiple pre-Marble artifacts; the biographies establish preparedness more clearly than compulsion.

The mundane economics are wonderfully concrete. Restaurants still count inventory with spreadsheets, clipboards, and human memory; one customer reports 40% lower food waste, eliminated stockouts, and more than 30 manager hours saved each week. Marble returns time and operating control to managers, but it has not yet identified the deeper rent: who profits from the fragmentation, why the integrations remain broken, or which rule prevents incumbents from repairing them. This is a painful workflow, not yet a decoded system.

The bull case stings because these are not waitlist metrics. A&W Restaurants, The Kati Roll Company, Sophie’s Cuban, Kokomo NYC, HolyShred, and Dead Letter No. 9 are allowing a two-person pre-seed company into inventory and ordering workflows. Every count, correction, and order can create location-specific context, so repeated use may compound product quality. The exception path—working artifact, institutional proof, and unusually deep founder preparation—is therefore partially present even without disclosed revenue.

But Marble currently sits on top of the wave rather than beneath it. Nory already offers autonomous forecasting, inventory, ordering, workforce, payroll, and BI; Restaurant365 operates across more than 50,000 locations with over 100 POS integrations. Marble’s CV and voice capture are a sharper wedge, but the dossier identifies no controlled technical bottleneck, proprietary integration network, or cross-customer dataset that those incumbents cannot reproduce. The stated 99% counting and 97% forecasting accuracy lack sample sizes and error distributions. Location memory is useful context; it is not yet infrastructure.

This fits my unglamorous-workflow-repair pattern, and I see no disclosed leadership shell game, equal-control arrangement, or severe surveillance architecture. Those are not affirmative findings—the governance terms and custody of employee, purchasing, and scheduling data still require diligence. My base case is a useful vertical SaaS company reaching roughly $3–7 million ARR within 36 months, not yet a defining autonomous-restaurant platform. I am not writing my own check at 52. I flip to Invest if Marble produces one verifiable 12-month cohort of at least 20 locations where its agents execute a majority of recurring orders with fewer than 10% human overrides while preserving the reported waste and labor savings.
