# Oasive -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, a municipal analyst should be able to ask what changed at a school district and trace the answer through its finances, board decisions, and local reporting. Oasive’s source-linked borrower pages point toward that experience. Getting there requires reliable document reconciliation, continuing coverage, and analysts trusting the results enough to change their research habits. The dossier establishes the proposed interface; it does not yet establish those transitions. I can imagine the future, but my imagination cannot count as company evidence.

Anaïs Howland gives me a reason to keep looking. Wells Fargo MBS analysis, Google Treasury portfolio management, and Google AI strategy put her inside both the investment workflow and the technology attempting to improve it. Her earlier computer-use venture and public Captr_Windows recording tool add evidence that she builds independently. This is more substance than a résumé decorated with AI. What remains unestablished is the particular obsession: a documented sequence of experiments showing why municipal research became the problem she could not leave alone.

Why does an analyst still have to hunt through board minutes? Because relevant borrower information lives across documents and institutions that were never organized around the analyst’s question. Collecting it could return meaningful time and judgment to that person. That fits my attraction to unglamorous workflow repair. But Oasive has identified scattered information, without yet identifying a quantified toll or artificial restriction it removes. The $200 monthly Macro & Rates price establishes an offer, not the municipal customer’s savings or willingness to pay.

The strongest argument against passing is that Howland may already have found her first believers. Oasive reported use across **15 institutional desks on September 24, 2026**, including substantial asset managers and corporate treasury teams. Institutional professionals trying a product from a solo founder deserves attention. Combined with her domain experience, that could become a narrow, valuable business before any grand infrastructure story appears. Yet the reported use concerns MBS and rates; municipal research remains advertised through early access. There is no disclosed behavioral detail showing repeated reliance, and the paid-pilot offering does not establish completed paid pilots. I would fund before revenue if the working artifact and founder evidence carried the conviction. Here, they establish enough to investigate, but not enough for my check.

The question underneath Oasive is what it gets to own as usage grows. Its proprietary MBS models are advertised, but their advantage is not independently demonstrated, and the municipal product has no documented exclusive dataset. DPC DATA already supplies standardized financials and AI-ready inputs; MuniPro already turns debt records into research memos. Meanwhile, Oasive’s data terms make access contingent on upstream licenses and restrict certain exports. That dependence matters to my preference for concealed enabling infrastructure: the dossier currently shows a customer of important inputs more clearly than an owner of a scarce constraint. Source links help users inspect answers, but the terms also leave them responsible for evaluating unreviewed AI output.

I pass with my own money today. A sole founder and identified CEO provide clear accountability; the dossier supplies no affirmative evidence of the governance deception or severe privacy abuse that would make this an active rejection. The limiting issue is narrower: strong occupational context has not yet become demonstrated municipal user dependence or a controlled enabling asset. My one verifiable flip condition is a documented cohort of five municipal desks renewing paid contracts after six months, with usage records showing recurring reliance on Oasive’s source-linked borrower histories. That would turn a plausible time-saving tool into evidence of a changed working habit.
