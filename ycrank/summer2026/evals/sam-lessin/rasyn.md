# Rasyn -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I cannot underwrite the deal before knowing the deal. Rasyn discloses no proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, round size, or evidence that this is a genuinely scarce first check. YC participation is a stage fact, not proof of an auction, but financing silence earns only the low prior: I do not know whether I am buying an unpopular technical belief cheaply or paying a Demo Day price for “general intelligence for chemistry.”

The plausible venture moment is narrow and testable even though Rasyn has not actually specified it as the use of proceeds. Marigold integrates 54 executable tools—40 on user-connected hardware and 14 on hosted endpoints—and checks intermediate results rather than treating chemistry as unverified scripting. The experiment should be whether that orchestration can complete a defined class of real laboratory workflows accurately enough to displace the chemist’s current tool chain. But there is no named workflow, success metric, deadline, or capital plan. “Launch an agent and sell subscriptions” is a product plan; it is not yet a truth-revealing financing event.

The strongest bull case is that the team may already possess the beginnings of a proprietary workflow layer. Marigold launched in August 2026, and the preceding Larmor product reportedly reached seven medicinal-chemistry teams. Hashmi has direct computational-chemistry exposure through Caltech and the Stoltz Group, while the product’s connected-hardware architecture crosses scientific software and laboratory execution. If those seven teams repeatedly entrusted actual work to Rasyn, this could be much more informative than the company’s 58 LinkedIn followers. The problem is that the supporting page now returns 404, no retention or payment is reported, and Chauhan’s relevant operating history is unverified. This is suggestive usage, not evidence that owned access already causes purchases.

The technical facts also cut against mistaking integration count for defensibility. Rasyn’s own retrosynthesis benchmark reports a 30.3% Top-1 exact-match failure rate, its Terms require experimental verification, and the system is neither GLP/GMP validated nor automatically accumulating customer training data without written consent. Meanwhile Rowan already offers more than 15 computational workflows, and many of Marigold’s components are third-party or open source. The company may become a valuable translation layer, but today the durable asset is neither proprietary chemistry data nor proven trust inside a lab workflow.

The pricing page—$89 per researcher, at least $747 monthly for a three-seat Lab plan, and Institution contracts starting at $2,000—is refreshingly legible. It still does not establish a one-and-done business: there is no paid-customer count, gross margin, compute cost, burn, runway, or route to break-even without another round. The product-transition evidence compounds that concern. Rasyn moved from public descriptions involving retrosynthesis and 500-plus tools to Marigold and 54 tools within roughly a week, while earlier Marcus materials remained labeled “Empirical evaluation in progress.” Rapid iteration is healthy; simultaneous scope changes make it difficult to identify the one controllable cash driver.

I am passing today. The verifiable flip condition is three named laboratories converting from trial to paid contracts and remaining active for six months on one repeated workflow, with Rasyn reporting workflow-level gross margin and error rates. That would show that chemistry-specific trust, not a broad agent story, causes a purchase—and would justify revisiting missing deal terms as a possible exception rather than pretending they do not matter.
