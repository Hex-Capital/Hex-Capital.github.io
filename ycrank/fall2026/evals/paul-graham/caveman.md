# Caveman -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Julius Brussee has shipped enough that I want to meet him. But the question I most want answered is missing: what went wrong, and what did he do about it? Caveman progressed from a skill to a browser extension to infrastructure, with Cloud and version 4.0 announced on October 13. That establishes output. It does not establish which obstacle he overcame or which observation caused him to change direction. I give resourcefulness and adaptability their missing-evidence defaults. Neither is a judgment that he lacks those qualities.

The strongest evidence is that there is something to test. JetBrains measured the earlier skill and found 8.5% fewer output tokens. That is more useful than an architectural diagram. But aggregate billed cost increased 11.6% because of an outlier. The distinction matters enormously for a company selling lower bills. Caveman’s current approach—record workloads, replay proposed changes, then use shadow tests and canaries—addresses the right problem: an optimization must survive the customer’s actual tasks. The software earns substantial technical credit; the company-reported 94% evidence retention with 89% token removal earns less certainty than an independently reproduced result.

Who wants this enough to keep using it? The earlier extension’s 20,000 users and 67 ratings averaging 4.6/5 provide some evidence of usefulness. The 115.5K stars and 6.7K forks provide extraordinary attention. But these figures span earlier versions and cannot establish recurring demand for the new Cloud product. GreenPT’s integration is another foothold, though its production savings were explicitly unmeasured. Consumption prices and $250/$750 governance packages tell me how Caveman intends to charge, not whether customers have bought. I would give demand intermediate credit without treating the entire repository audience as prospective paying users.

The strongest argument for investing is that one founder has already put working tools in front of a large technical audience. Brussee’s experience with agent runtimes and retrieval systems makes that more persuasive. Caveman fits my unstated preference for technical interfaces with a concrete proof spike. If developers begin using its workload evaluations to decide which changes are safe, the crude cost-saving tool could become something they depend on. But his CV does not establish that the idea came from a problem he personally encountered. Nor does the dossier yet show customers enduring the integration work because they urgently need the result.

The opposing possibility is uncomfortable: Caveman has discovered how to attract developers before discovering what they will repeatedly use and pay for. LiteLLM and Edgee already sit near the relevant traffic, and Apache-2.0 code makes overlapping features available to them. That makes returning users particularly important. I see no evidence that Brussee is waiting for a partner, delegating the product, or relying on a ceremonial launch; those red flags are unproven. The known exceptions do not resolve the decision either. Working software is present, but its measured result is not yet strong enough to substitute for the missing founder evidence. The youth and nonprofit exceptions address different objections.

I would pass on writing a personal check today. Caveman clears my technical threshold but misses the required 18/31 resourcefulness gate. My one flip condition is a documented customer episode: a named team rejects Caveman over cost or fidelity, Brussee ships a remedy within weeks, and that team then pays and returns while provider bills fall at unchanged task performance. That would connect the founder, the technical work, and the need. Today those are promising pieces whose connection remains unproved.
