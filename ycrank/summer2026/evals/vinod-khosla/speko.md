# Speko -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Speko does not yet offer an option on an orders-of-magnitude consequence. The dossier gives a $2.5 billion voice-agent market growing toward $35.2 billion, but no quantified expert-service cost pool, population unlocked, or 10x reduction in the cost of voice automation. The stated problem is narrower: developers repeatedly compare vendors and deployed stacks become stale. That is useful infrastructure. It is not yet world-changing infrastructure.

The strongest evidence is technical but remains bounded. Speko evaluates 23 speech-to-text models across nine languages, finds four different winners, and routes STT, LLM, and TTS requests with failover. Yet there is no measured 5x advantage in accuracy-adjusted cost, latency, reliability, or task completion against LiveKit or OpenRouter. The company orchestrates other providers’ models; it does not own a new model, network, or proprietary learning loop. Publishing the benchmark data while declining to train on customer audio makes adoption easier but defensibility harder.

The bull case stings. Beknazar Abdikamalov has worked in voice technology since 2019 and spent four years living the exact provider-comparison problem. Speko already has initial partners, a commercial pilot, open SDKs, adapters, and roughly $1.6 million including YC. An incremental wedge can become a large control plane, as Block’s simple first product expanded into financial infrastructure. The material difference is evidence of expansion capacity: Speko has two stars on each principal SDK, 12 Launch YC votes, no demonstrated workload data advantage, and competitors already bundling its core function. The known-exception override is possible, but not earned.

The first dollars should attack benchmark generalization, because Speko’s own terms concede that results change with datasets, model versions, provider availability, methods, and network conditions. The cheapest kill test is obvious: run real customer workloads against Speko, LiveKit, OpenRouter, and direct provider selection, then measure failure-adjusted cost and latency. Instead, the dossier supplies no pass/fail threshold, experiment budget, or stop-or-pivot branch. A public-preview product without an SLA is acceptable at pre-seed; failing to name what must be disproven is not.

Founder-market fit earns credit, but founder adaptability and recruiting remain unproven. The Amazon and Hupo experience establishes competence, not a documented belief revision or the recruitment of complementary owners for benchmarking science, real-time infrastructure, security, or enterprise compliance. Economically, provider cost plus 5% and managed infrastructure at $0.09 per minute are refreshingly concrete and apparently unsubsidized. They still do not show contribution margin, utilization assumptions, or parity against customers buying directly or receiving routing inside larger platforms.

My 36-month base case is that Speko pivots from routing into voice-agent evaluation and observability and remains below $5 million ARR. The execution-heavy infrastructure pattern could still work, but today the product resembles a replaceable feature inside better-funded platforms more than a platform-shaped community wedge. I pass. One result flips me: a blinded production study across at least ten enterprise workloads showing Speko delivers a sustained 5x reduction in failure-adjusted cost at equivalent latency versus both LiveKit and OpenRouter.
