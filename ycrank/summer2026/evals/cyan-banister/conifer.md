# Conifer -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the attractive version is obvious: AI workloads behave like electricity dispatch, running on the cheapest capable machine and reaching an expensive frontier model only when necessary. Conifer has one artifact leaking from that world—18 of its 60 compared models already run locally, and its Rust engine reportedly decodes up to 60% faster than llama.cpp on Apple Silicon. But the dossier does not map the intervening transitions: how routing quality compounds, how heterogeneous enterprise hardware joins the network, or how Conifer retains control when Ollama adds automatic routing. I can daydream the future for them; I cannot count my daydream as their evidence.

The founders are the strongest part of this company. Charles Muehlberger researched multimodal inference on edge devices and built edge-AI hardware for RF brain-injury modeling before Conifer. Michael Jeffords built an applied computer-vision pipeline and helped expand HeartCheck screenings across more than 20 barbershops and 10,000 people. Those are shipped artifacts and institutional contact, not degrees pasted onto a fashionable category. The missing piece is a longer record of obsession with inference economics or evidence that this team can recruit and sell an infrastructure standard, but the technical direction did not arrive from nowhere.

The broken system is also real, although not yet fully exposed. Cloud providers collect usage charges even when a request could run adequately on hardware the customer already owns; teams then inherit multiple subscriptions, keys, and dashboards. Conifer proposes to return execution and purchasing control to the customer by trying local inference first and consolidating cloud escalation into one bill. The claimed reduction of more than 80% in token spend would make that rent legible, but it remains company-reported. I want an actual workload trace showing what was routed locally, what failed, and what the customer paid before and after.

Here is the bull case that stings: a three-person pre-seed team has already produced signed desktop and CLI installers, a proprietary inference engine, a repository with 248 stars, and a predecessor launch reaching more than 100 developers. This could be the concealed infrastructure beneath the agent boom, with routing outcomes becoming a proprietary dataset while applications multiply above it. Pre-revenue does not bother me when the artifact works and the founders have paid technical tuition. What stops me from underwriting that case today is that the stars span Sage, Juniper, and Conifer, while the dossier supplies no current-product cohort, repeat usage, routing volume, or paid bill. The names changed more visibly than user behavior did.

The moat is therefore balanced on a narrow ledge. Conifer controls meaningful code and may possess a faster local engine, but it sits between OpenRouter’s hosted routing and Ollama’s local runtime, both of which can move into its lane. Apple-only full-engine support further constrains the lower layer. There is also a privacy paradox: local execution could materially improve data custody, yet proprietary binaries prevent customers from verifying the local-processing and security claims. That is not proof of misconduct, but for a system touching prompts and customer data, the absence of an explicit, auditable custody architecture blocks conviction.

My 36-month base case is that Conifer pivots from a general-purpose router into an Apple-fleet enterprise inference and governance product and remains below $3 million ARR; the general routing layer otherwise gets absorbed into better-funded runtimes and gateways. One result would flip me: a verifiable cohort of at least ten companies each routing more than one billion tokens per month, retaining for six months, with independently measured savings above 50% and no material quality regression. That would prove both costly user behavior and a routing asset competitors cannot reproduce merely by adding a toggle.
