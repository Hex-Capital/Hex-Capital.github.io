# Skymerse -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Damian Szumski is a licensed flight dispatcher who owned Flightradar24’s API product, yet Skymerse publishes no committed two-to-five-year technical roadmap. That pairing is Skymerse’s signature: unusually strong founder lineage attached to an unusually indefinite plan. Skymerse understands the aviation stack from inside, but expertise without a definite destination can become sophisticated iteration. The founder is the signal; the missing plan is the anomaly.

**Anduril** is the high rung on founder lineage because Palmer Luckey had already shipped Oculus to a billion-dollar exit before attacking defense. Skymerse shares the rarer mechanism: Damian Szumski worked across LOT Polish Airlines, Lufthansa Systems, FLYR, and Flightradar24 before processing more than three million NOTAMs through Notamify. Skymerse diverges where it matters: Szumski owned a consequential API product and built a production aviation service, but the dossier does not establish that he previously created or scaled the underlying institution. This is closer to verified domain operator than canonical repeat founder—strong enough to matter, not strong enough to override every missing moat.

**Quora** is the low rung on definite planning because consumer Q&A offered no fixed technical destination; Adam D’Angelo’s Facebook pedigree supplied the exception. The same fracture appears in Skymerse: the product spans planning, dispatch, monitoring, and cockpit support, but Skymerse names no certification sequence, architectural commitment, or two-to-five-year milestone. A pilot configured around each customer’s airports, feeds, volumes, and workflows is not yet a definite plan. Skymerse could be building aviation’s operational reasoning layer, or merely discovering which workflow airlines will buy. Those are different companies.

Jeppesen’s Airflow launch identifies Skymerse’s contrarian test. Over the next 18 months, Skymerse’s secret compounds only if the custom aeronautical model, AIP extraction agents, and client-specific manuals produce decisions that Jeppesen cannot reproduce from the same public data. Skymerse names the incumbent and observes the fragmented source-oriented workflow, but it never states the institutional reason Jeppesen must remain a data supplier rather than become the decision layer. “Autopilot for flight operations” therefore sits above the generic “AI for X” red flag, yet below Stripe’s developer-first secret: a differentiated observation without a fully named incumbent failure mode.

LOT Polish Airlines already uses Notamify for network-airport monitoring, but Skymerse has not demonstrated monopoly pricing power. The score flips into Invest territory if Skymerse can show that operator manuals, minimum-equipment lists, corrections, and dispatch decisions create proprietary state that survives model commoditization and produces multi-year enterprise contracts. Without that evidence, Skymerse faces Jeppesen’s integrations, Flightkeys’ planning platform, and Air Space Intelligence’s $34 million war chest while relying on third-party aeronautical data. Three million processed NOTAMs demonstrate competence; they do not yet demonstrate captivity. Monopoly is the condition of success, and Skymerse currently has usage before it has monopoly.

Palantir is the relevant single-anchor parallel for Skymerse’s regulated-sector structure. Palantir converted high-consequence government workflows into integration depth, trust, and switching costs; Skymerse could do the same inside airline operations, where errors encounter dispatch rules rather than consumer inconvenience. But Skymerse’s terms prohibit its outputs from serving as the sole or authoritative basis for safety-critical decisions. Skymerse therefore receives the regulated-sector premium without the full regulatory moat: aviation makes entry difficult, while the disclaimer prevents the product from owning the final decision.

Skymerse will, within 36 months, reach $2 million to $5 million in ARR from airlines, cargo operators, and medevac providers while remaining an advisory decision-support layer rather than an authoritative flight-operations autopilot. Skymerse’s 3,500 registered Notamify users, LOT deployment, and founder’s aviation lineage make niche enterprise adoption more likely than death; the verification clause and Jeppesen overlap make category monopoly less likely than a valuable vertical product. One year of workflow advantage is a feature. Ten years of authority would be a company.

Notamify’s thousands of daily users form the strongest opposing case: Skymerse may already possess the distribution wedge, proprietary corrections, and operator trust needed to become the system of record before Jeppesen recognizes that interpretation—not data—is the scarce layer. The known-exception argument is Quora or Asana: exceptional operator pedigree can precede a visible monopoly. But Skymerse’s public traction is still attributed primarily to Notamify, its terms disclaim decision authority, and no definite certification or pricing-power plan closes that gap. A signed airline contract granting Skymerse authoritative dispatch-workflow status, priced above $250,000 annually and renewed after twelve months, would flip my verdict to Invest.
