# Prized -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have built together, which matters more than their credentials. Hudson Griffith was Gander’s founding engineer, Marinos Eliades also engineered there, and the company was acquired by Archer Aviation. They have shipped operational software inside the same organization and survived the compression of a startup into an outcome. That is real specific knowledge. But the dossier does not show that either founder previously owned enterprise permissions, security deployment, or internal-tool procurement—the exact constraints now governing Prized’s sale.

The product is more serious than a generic AI wrapper. Per-tool database roles, brokered credentials, outbound-host allowlists, read-only integrations, and audit logs address the dangerous part of letting non-engineers write against production systems. These mechanisms prove engineering judgment. They do not yet prove a durable asset. Superblocks already combines natural-language app generation with permissions, auditing, policy enforcement, and private-cloud deployment. If a competitor copies Prized’s code and controls, no customer-created network, scarce dataset, proprietary model, or hardware advantage remains.

The market is large enough to permit movement. Prized can enter through ops, support, finance, and RevOps tools, then expand across the $58.2 billion low-code market using the same governed connector layer. The 2026 shadow-IT problem is also real: employees can now create software faster than administrators can understand its permissions. But a large parent market is not distribution. Requiring both an enthusiastic builder and an approving security owner creates a two-principal sale, while Retool, Superblocks, Appsmith, and Lovable already own relationships on one or both sides.

The strongest bull case is that the founders understand this constraint and are building the permission system before chasing breadth. Approved connectors could be reused, tools could be forked, and accumulated configurations could eventually make a workspace increasingly valuable. Their ten hand-delivered, target-specific toolboxes also show unusual energy. Yet none of those ten deliveries is tied to adoption, repeat usage, an LOI, or payment. There is no observed loop in which one user improves acquisition or utility for another. The platform presently compounds company labor, not user contribution.

No known exception overrides that evidence. There is a working product and a compact two-person team, but the category is already legible and directly occupied; strangeness is not hiding beneath missing metrics. Nor does the dossier reveal an integrity, morale, or artificial-deadline red flag. The problem is structural rather than personal: Prized has demonstrated implementation in a market where implementation is rapidly becoming abundant.

I expect Prized either to pivot within 36 months toward a narrower governance layer for AI-generated enterprise software or remain below $2 million ARR as a differentiated internal-tool vendor. I would pass today. One result would flip the decision: three named enterprises repeatedly deploying employee-built tools while producing a proprietary permission graph that measurably shortens deployment or improves policy decisions with every new tool.
