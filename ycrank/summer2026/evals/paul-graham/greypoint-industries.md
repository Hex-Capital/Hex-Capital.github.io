# Greypoint Industries -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important missing fact is not LEGION’s range. It is what broke. The dossier shows that the founders built an aerial emitter-geolocation system, demonstrated it to the Canadian Armed Forces, and signed a government contract. It does not name an obstacle, the workaround they built, or how quickly that action changed the result. Those outputs suggest resourcefulness, but they do not prove it. On my controlling founder test, Greypoint therefore remains at the missing-evidence default.

The technical evidence is better. One successful target-geolocation test and an integrated path from LEGION sensing to ARROW interception make this more than a defense presentation assembled around a large TAM. Jordan Khoo and Steven Xu also have relevant controls, embedded-systems, and uncrewed-aircraft experience. But “successful” is underspecified. There is no disclosed range, accuracy, contested-spectrum result, or swarm-scale interception threshold. Greypoint has shipped a working proof before receiving broad institutional permission; it has not yet produced the numerical proof spike that would make the technical claim hard to dismiss.

The Canadian government contract is real user evidence. It is more informative than the company’s 1,107 LinkedIn followers, press listings, or competition prizes, and the CAF demonstration identifies a narrow initial user: Canadian forces trying to find drone operators and other emitters hidden by terrain. Yet contract value, deployment status, repeat use, and operator behavior are unknown. A buyer has pulled the crude product far enough to pay or commit, but the dossier does not show soldiers repeatedly using it or demanding that Greypoint expand an unplanned workflow.

The origin is plausible rather than demonstrated. Darcy Zhang served as a Canadian Armed Forces infanteer, while the rest of the team worked around defense systems, drones, controls, and embedded software. They did not discover counter-UAS by reading a market report. They are also entering an unusually ugly combination of RF engineering, interceptor manufacturing, military procurement, export controls, and field integration. That schlep is attractive. But the dossier never identifies the founders’ original encounter with the specific failure LEGION solves, and it contains no evidence that they changed product routes after a hypothesis failed.

The strongest bull case is that these student founders have already crossed the trust barrier that normally makes young defense teams implausible: a government signed a contract and the CAF permitted a demonstration. Canada’s July 2026 Defence Drone Initiative could turn that foothold into procurement while larger competitors remain optimized for radar, nets, or high-power microwaves. The relevant exception is Bindwell: young technical founders can overcome the age prior by producing concrete proof in a hard field. The material difference is that Bindwell disclosed a numerical benchmark and laboratory validation; Greypoint currently discloses one geolocation success without the conditions needed to judge it. Its website relationships also cannot substitute for distribution, though the signed contract keeps this from triggering the pure “partner” red flag.

My 36-month base case is that Greypoint reaches $2 million to $6 million in annualized Canadian defense contract value as a counter-UAS subsystem supplier, but has not validated an autonomous swarm-interception platform in contested conditions. I would not write the check today because the people-first gate is still unproven, not because the market is small or the product unserious. One result would flip me: a dated account of a failed contested-spectrum CAF test, the founders’ shipped workaround within weeks, and a successful rerun geolocating and intercepting at least ten mixed RF-emitting and RF-silent targets. That single sequence would prove both what the machine can do and what the founders do when it cannot.
