# Whitespace -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Alex Tung and Leon Yao grew up inside wholesale-distribution families, yet Whitespace packages that unusually authentic origin inside a fairly conventional AI-agent SaaS product. That is Whitespace’s signature: high founder-problem intimacy, low frontier or regulatory difficulty. The founders are *of* the place, not visiting it—Tung later advised distributors at BCG and Yao built logistics-distribution AI at InstaDeep—but Whitespace still risks becoming two talented people attaching agents to a $60 trillion slide rather than discovering a magically weird machine only they could build.

Niantic is the high rung because John Hanke’s decade inside mapping made location intelligence autobiographical before Pokémon Go existed. Whitespace shares that mechanism: both founders’ families operated wholesale businesses, Tung helped Ankar reach seven-figure ARR, and Yao has both an IROS paper and experience serving logistics-distribution customers. Whitespace diverges because its April 2026 AI slide-maker beta preceded the wholesale launch by roughly three months. Whitespace has the right childhood story, but the recent pivot leaves me unsure whether wholesale was the persistent obsession or simply the better market discovered during YC.

The consensus AI-agent darling fails when elegant software floats above a problem its founders never touched. Whitespace does not repeat that founder failure, but Whitespace does repeat part of the product pattern: ERP-connected agents, customer-specific knowledge bases, and correction histories are increasingly standard architecture, while Proton.ai, Distro, Stockline, and Fresho already occupy adjacent territory. “AI operating system” is not magically weird by itself. Wholesale back offices are wonderfully unsexy, but four named competitors mean the obscurity discount may already be disappearing.

Alex Tung is explicitly Co-Founder and CEO, which puts Whitespace structurally above HQ Trivia’s unresolved partnership problem. Whitespace’s governance could compound cleanly over 18 months if Tung owns customer and operating decisions while Yao owns technical execution; a 12-year friendship and experience living together may make disagreement survivable. The dossier provides no equity split, vesting protocol, or documented tiebreaker, however. Whitespace has named the captain but has not shown me the ship’s constitution, so the GameCrush lesson remains a rebuttal rather than a hard no.

Kingfisher Direct and Warwick would flip Whitespace’s non-consensus score upward if either customer documented repeat expansion from one workflow into replenishment, quoting, procurement, and accounting while sophisticated investors still dismissed the category as grim ERP plumbing. Whitespace instead reports 80%+ productivity gains, two-to-four-week implementations, and three-month payback without disclosing revenue or customer expansion. Those are meaningful pre-seed signals, but Whitespace becomes truly magical only when the ugly integration surface—not the underlying model—turns into the moat.

SpaceX required hardware, manufacturing, regulation, and capital to compound into barriers that software tourists could not cross. Whitespace has the opposite profile on this axis: SAP, NetSuite, Epicor, Infor, Dynamics, and Sage integrations are unpleasant engineering, but Whitespace remains a software layer exposed to incumbent ERP vendors and vertical competitors. I like unsexy work, yet inconvenience is not frontier risk. The tactile evidence is somewhat better: Kingfisher Direct and Warwick can observe orders entered and inventory replenished, but a normal human cannot install Whitespace, ride it, scan it, or photograph it within 48 hours.

Kingfisher Direct and Warwick make my 36-month forecast concrete: Whitespace reaches roughly $6 million to $12 million ARR by July 2029 through multi-workflow expansion, then becomes a credible acquisition target for Epicor rather than an independent category-defining platform. Whitespace’s lived founder origin should support unusually good workflow discovery and founder-led sales; Whitespace’s low frontier score should keep the destination closer to valuable vertical software than to a SpaceX, Anduril, or Crusoe-shaped outcome.

Leon Yao’s reinforcement-learning research, Tung’s seven-figure-ARR operating experience, two named customers, and claimed 80%+ productivity gains form the strongest bull case against that ceiling: Whitespace could use customer corrections and ERP integrations to become the default intelligence layer across wholesale operations. The sting is real because HQ Trivia shows that observable product pull can override a structural weakness. Whitespace has not yet shown that pull independently, however, and the three-month slide-maker pivot makes the correction-history moat look proposed rather than accumulated. I would flip to Strong Invest if Kingfisher Direct or Warwick verifies paid expansion into at least three workflows with net retention above 130% while Whitespace demonstrates materially improving automation accuracy.
