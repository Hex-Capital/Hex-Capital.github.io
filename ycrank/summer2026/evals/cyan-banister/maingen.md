# Maingen -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Power plants and factories have agents rehearsing equipment failures, inventory shortages, and vendor delays before touching a live operation. Getting there requires extracting tacit operator knowledge, encoding it as state and action spaces, teaching models against expert judgment, and proving that simulated competence survives contact with machinery. Maingen has built the first two links. SolarBench contains eight tasks, 11 models, and 880 graded simulated weeks; the best model passed only 53.8% of its runs while the authored oracle passed 100%. That gap is useful. It is a small, concrete leak from the future and an honest measurement of how far away the future remains.

The hidden asset is not “RL environments.” It is the hundreds of hours spent turning telemetry, work orders, SOPs, inventory constraints, and vendor behavior into expert-authored grading rules. That is concealed infrastructure beneath the visible agent wave, and it could compound into a proprietary corpus of industrial failure modes. But today Maingen controls one documented solar environment, not a general industrial substrate. Factory environments are still in progress, and there is no evidence that SolarBench performance transfers to live control.

Phillip Yan and David Yang fit the two sides of the problem unusually well for a two-person pre-seed team. Phillip worked on energy-trading systems and exchange infrastructure associated with more than $150 million in volume; David worked on model training and co-authored an ICLR workshop paper. Their Department of Energy science-bowl rivalry predates the company, which is a charming early artifact, but the biography does not yet show a lifelong industrial obsession, several independently shipped systems, or the commercial command to turn operator access into repeatable margins. The CosmicBrain exception does not automatically apply: both companies pair industrial AI with a working artifact, but Maingen lacks its comparison point’s documented lifelong build history and signed industrial proofs of concept.

The broken system is visible but its economics are still blurry. Operational knowledge is trapped in people, CMMS records, and vendor portals, forcing labs to build environments internally or purchase them from vendors. Maingen has identified the hiding places, but not the rent: there is no quantified cost of environment production, no named buyer budget, and no evidence that operators gain ownership, income, or control. This may make models more capable without returning agency to the people whose expertise supplies the product. That distinction matters to me.

The sharp bear case is that Maingen becomes a two-founder research consultancy wedged between industrial operators who retain the valuable data and frontier labs capable of internalizing the work. Bespoke Labs, Mechanize, Prime Intellect, AfterQuery, and in-house lab teams all have routes into adjacent territory. The reported billion-dollar environment-spending wave makes the category attractive, but also dangerously fashionable. Unnamed lab engagements do not prove recurring demand, and the dossier describes no custody architecture for sensitive operational data. CEO and CTO roles identify nominal accountability, although ownership and a contractual tiebreaker remain unverified rather than presumed problematic.

I would write a small personal check because the operator-authored rubric is a scarce lower-stack input and SolarBench is more persuasive than a deck or waitlist. My 36-month base case is $3 million to $7 million in ARR from environments spanning two or three industrial verticals, with Maingen remaining an independent specialist rather than controlling live operations. One condition flips me to a pass: if the existing lab engagements cannot produce a paid recurring contract whose IP terms let Maingen reuse its environment architecture, then the supposed corpus moat is bespoke labor wearing an infrastructure costume.
