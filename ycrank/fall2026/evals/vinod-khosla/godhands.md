# GodHands -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Preserving an employee’s procedures is useful. Making that employee’s expertise executable could change the economics of operations. GodHands sits between those propositions, and the distance matters. Its desktop application observes work across applications and captures decisions and exceptions without integrations. But the dossier supplies no baseline for labor displaced, population served, or cost reduction. I cannot price an extraordinary consequence from an unquantified promise. My downside would be one check; the evidence still has to establish what extraordinary outcome that check purchases.

The technical question is whether observing actions reveals enough hidden judgment to handle an unfamiliar exception. A recording can show what someone clicked while missing why they rejected three alternatives. GodHands emphasizes precisely this distinction, but supplies no comparative test against Scribe or Mimica. Scribe’s company-reported $100 million ARR establishes spending on the underlying need, not GodHands’s technical superiority. Moreover, the discrepancy between execution marketing and security language describing read-only agents leaves the capability boundary unresolved. I would not label this a generic model wrapper without evidence. I also would not award a technical discontinuity for describing one.

The strongest evidence against passing is the combination of relevant builders and an actual customer outcome. Sai Vamshi Batchu worked in quantitative research at Graviton; Aditya Bommakanti built low-latency systems there and published NeurIPS research. They encountered a departure that reportedly disrupted their employer for months. An anonymous support leader says the product documented previously undocumented escalations within two weeks. That could be the beginning of capturing scarce operational expertise through normal work. But the testimonial establishes documentation, not reliable execution, and the customer is unnamed. The change from August’s engineering-memory positioning to broader operations could reflect fast learning; the dossier does not identify the evidence that caused it. Technical credentials and a positioning change do not establish belief revision or risk-specific recruiting.

GodHands fits my broader willingness to fund execution-heavy enterprise businesses, so the absence of a scientific breakthrough is not an automatic veto. However, its proposed accumulation of customer playbooks has not demonstrated the proprietary experimental learning loop I particularly value. Nor does the record establish the exceptional instigator and quantified consequence that could justify overriding missing benchmarks and milestones. There is no documented exit-first behavior, refusal to learn, or acquisition spending ahead of technical validation. Those red flags are absent. The central problem remains an unproven mechanism in a market where competitors already offer workflow context and agents.

I would pass at 25/100. The dossier contains neither a costed experiment against the largest kill risk nor operating economics covering capture, inference, human correction, and deployment. Missing prices do not imply bad economics; they leave parity unestablished. One result would change my decision: an independently auditable customer deployment showing GodHands completing previously unseen support escalations at comparable quality and at least 10x lower fully loaded cost than the customer’s existing human-and-software process, including correction and deployment costs. That would connect observed expertise to executable expertise and establish a consequential economic mechanism. Two weeks to produce documentation does not yet make that connection.
