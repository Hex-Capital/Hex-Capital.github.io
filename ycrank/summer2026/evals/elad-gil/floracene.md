# Floracene -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: CMS added 271 ASC-covered procedure codes in 2026, physician-owned surgery centers now bear more implant economics, and Floracene becomes large only if surgeons repeatedly switch from familiar devices to cheaper clinically equivalent alternatives. Implant prices matter—implants represent an estimated 40–60% of orthopedic and spine procedure costs—but the controlling variable is switching behavior, not the $22.98 billion orthopedic-implant TAM.

Floracene deserves credit for isolating that variable. It uses a center’s actual manufacturer contracts to present at least three procedure-level alternatives rather than generic price benchmarks. But the claim that more than 90% of expensive implants are interchangeable comes from surgeon conversations, with no disclosed clinical methodology, adoption threshold, or observed switching rate. If surgeons reject the recommendation because of training, familiarity, or representative relationships, the rest of the product becomes reporting software.

The economic timing is plausible but not yet a technology discontinuity. The 271 new codes may increase the number of procedures for which ASCs care about implant margins, and physician ownership aligns the decision-maker with savings. Floracene has not quantified savings per procedure, implementation cost, or its effect on center margins. It is therefore unclear whether this is a market-expanding change or a better interface applied to an existing procurement problem.

The strongest bull case is the team. Jonah Kaye led hospital operations work at Palantir and was a founding engineer at Metriport; Asaph Kupferman worked on Palantir’s hospitals team and was an early Garner Health product leader. They should understand fragmented healthcare data and institutional purchasing better than a generic software team. That experience could let them normalize contracts and clinical equivalence faster than incumbents. But it is not the rare technical-team exception: no proprietary dataset, validation system, customer deployment, or costly founder commitment is documented.

The product could eventually own the loop from procedure planning through contract comparison, clinical validation, ordering, and realized-savings measurement. Today it appears to stop at recommendation. Curvo already claims coverage of 2.3 million supplies and roughly $110 billion in weekly clinical spending, while Libertas, SupplyLasso, and SurgiSmartIQ occupy adjacent procurement and implant-analysis surfaces. A future normalized dataset is not yet a moat, and a well-funded competitor could reproduce an undisclosed equivalence table unless Floracene captures decisions, outcomes, and integrations that improve with every procedure.

My 36-month base case is that Floracene pivots toward broader ASC contract-compliance and ordering software and remains below $1 million ARR because implant recommendations alone do not overcome surgeon switching friction. I would reverse that view if five independent ASCs documented at least 20% implant-cost savings and accepted more than half of Floracene’s recommended substitutions across 100 completed procedures over six months. That single result would validate the controlling variable, establish product pull, and show that the recommendation layer can become a durable workflow. Until then, I pass.
