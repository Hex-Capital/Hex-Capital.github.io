# Kairox Systems -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Kairox Systems built its first prototype with a $14,000 grant. That is the most interesting fact here. Three people have turned a difficult idea into hardware on a small budget. But I want to know what failed during the build and what they did next. The dossier gives no particular obstacle, workaround, or elapsed time. Cheap construction is encouraging; it does not yet establish how these founders behave when their original plan stops working.

The team has relevant technical depth. Joel studied mechanical engineering, Alex electrical engineering and computer science, and Jorge has worked on spacecraft controls and hardware qualification. Tempest gives those credentials something concrete to attach to: an assembled prototype, pressure-vessel architecture, and ongoing testing. This fits my pattern of backing technical founders tackling unfamiliar industrial problems. The missing distinction is between assembling the vehicle and demonstrating its difficult claim. Company-reported TRL 4, without published transition results, depth, range, or endurance, earns partial technical credit. It does not establish a reliable flight–submersion–flight system.

The strongest case for investing is that Kairox has already compressed a substantial engineering project into a tiny team and budget, while reaching Marine Corps and allied military users. A successful demonstration could make the company look much less speculative very quickly. My exceptions allow concrete technical proof or unmistakable user pull to outweigh an incomplete founder history. Neither override is established here. Military engagement identifies people worth learning from, but the dossier records no operator repeatedly using Tempest or requesting another mission. QLab acceptance and showcase selection cannot answer that question.

The commercial hinge is whether one particular unit needs both modes badly enough to accept the compromises of combining them. A coastal reconnaissance operator might. But SubUAS already demonstrates that flying and swimming can coexist, so that combination alone does not explain why someone will choose Tempest. Kairox must discover the mission where its fixed-wing configuration changes what an operator can accomplish. Procurement and payload integration could hide a valuable, unpleasant wedge. There is no documented founder encounter with that problem, however, and no evidence-driven product revision yet. Those are unanswered questions, not evidence of rigidity or a manufactured idea. Likewise, government outreach does not establish the red flag of depending on a large partner; the danger would be letting briefings substitute for learning through use.

I would pass today. The absence of revenue at this stage does not trouble me. The unresolved link between founder action, demonstrated performance, and a user who comes back does. One verifiable event could change my decision: a documented military field trial where the founders fix a named failure within weeks and the same operators subsequently use Tempest for repeated end-to-end missions. That would reveal both the people and the need far more clearly than another selection announcement.
