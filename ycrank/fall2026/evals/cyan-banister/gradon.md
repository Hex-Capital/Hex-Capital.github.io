# Gradon -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a small engineering practice accepting work it once had to refuse: its engineers specify intent, software produces coordinated designs, and licensed professionals spend their time checking consequential decisions. Gradon would need to move from executing tasks to reliably checking work across disciplines, then make each completed project improve the next. That is a future I can draw. The dossier establishes conversational execution inside existing applications; it does not establish those subsequent transitions. I cannot give Gradon credit for the company I have daydreamed around it.

The most interesting object here is the civil firm’s project: 180 hours against a 750-hour manual estimate. Someone apparently let the product into an actual engineering workflow. That deserves attention. But the comparison is company-reported, the customer is unnamed, and the baseline is an estimate rather than an observed control. The broader claim of more than 75% lower project times does not independently validate it. I see an early working artifact, with insufficient behavioral detail to establish repeated dependence.

Why does that firm need 750 hours? The dossier points to manual CAD production, calculations, and plan sets. Removing that labor could give a principal room to accept another project and an engineer time to exercise judgment. That activates my preference for returning occupational control. Yet Gradon has identified a production burden without exposing a particular gatekeeper, fee, or artificial restriction. Named-seat subscriptions with unlimited usage could make delegation habitual, but prices and delivery costs are private. On-site implementation also leaves open how much expert labor moves from the customer onto Gradon’s payroll.

Nour Zahzah has performed mechanical design; Yousef Helal brings processor-design and FPGA-infrastructure experience. Their Vendo repository’s 633 stars show a prior artifact attracted developer attention, not that engineering firms want Gradon. Knowing each other since kindergarten supplies relationship history, while the CEO and CTO titles identify operating roles. None of that establishes years of obsession with AEC production. The July–August Vendo material followed by an October Gradon announcement warrants understanding the discovery that caused the change; it does not establish opportunism.

The strongest argument against my pass is that Gradon fits my less glamorous pattern: repair an operating wound, then become the infrastructure beneath it. Firm Brain could turn historical projects and standards into something a practice entrusts with increasingly consequential work. But account-specific configuration is the demonstrated property today; a scarce, controlled dependency is not. SWAPP already advertises workflow memory and firm-standard validation. My willingness to back pre-revenue technical founders does not resolve this: the dossier supplies neither a comparable record of longstanding domain obsession nor named industrial commitments. Missing revenue is not the deciding issue.

I would pass with my own money today. Gradon’s preliminary-output designation and licensed-review requirement acknowledge the work’s consequences. Its contractual prohibition on training with customer content is useful, but a draft privacy policy leaves custody and consent insufficiently clear; that is an unresolved gate, not proof of severe misuse. My verifiable flip condition is one independently referenceable, six-month paid deployment showing that accumulated firm knowledge improves performance on subsequent projects while completed, professionally approved output per engineer rises. That would turn the central infrastructure hypothesis into observable behavior.
