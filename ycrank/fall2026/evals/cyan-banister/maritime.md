# Maritime -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a personal agent waking up in its own computer, finishing yesterday’s work, and going back to sleep without losing its belongings. Maritime has built some of that plumbing: persistent storage, encrypted secrets, and snapshot-based sleep/wake. But a computer that remembers is only one step toward an agent people entrust with their lives. Reliable delegated work and durable customer trust still have to arrive. The dossier identifies personal-agent products as a catalyst; it does not yet establish that longer chain. I see a plausible piece of the future, with limited evidence that Maritime captures it.

The founders’ operating wound is more convincing than the market forecast. Maria Gorskikh and Aryaman Shrivastava researched agent infrastructure at MIT NANDA and report encountering this hosting problem while running hundreds of research agents. That is useful tuition. Their shared research background and Aryaman’s developer-tooling work support a team building from experience. What remains unestablished is the depth and duration of their independent obsession: the dossier supplies neither a dated sequence of earlier experiments nor substantial recruiting proof. I give them credit for relevant work, without turning affiliations into a lifelong founder story.

Why should every startup become a tiny cloud operator just to give customers persistent agents? Provisioning, maintenance, and debugging consume engineering time that Maritime could return. Its $20 monthly plan includes 20 agents, although concurrency limits mean those are not 20 simultaneously working computers. This repairs an identifiable workflow; it does not yet expose a quantified institutional rent or artificial restriction. The infrastructure position fits my preference for the machinery beneath a visible application wave. However, persistent volumes and snapshots establish implementation, while Docker’s September 24 managed-sandbox launch makes ownership of a scarce bottleneck harder to claim.

The strongest case against passing is that the founders have already encountered tomorrow’s operating problem at research scale and shipped a usable answer. Open Instinct, Maria’s agent project built on Maritime, makes the product more tangible than a narrated demo. Its 416 GitHub stars indicate attention, however, rather than repeated customer sacrifice. Product Hunt launches on March 25 and August 30 establish public shipping; neither those launches nor Two Lanterns’ investment establishes that developers repeatedly entrust production workloads to the service. Pre-revenue exceptions can make sense when technical artifacts and unusually strong founder evidence compensate. Here, the evidence supports taking the builders seriously, but does not yet supply that override.

Then there is the detail that stops my check: Maritime’s privacy policy permits indefinite retention of proxy request and response contents, even after account deletion. Supplying your own API key bypasses that storage, which is a meaningful limiting path, but does not resolve custody for customers using the proxy. Persistent agents could accumulate extraordinarily intimate working histories. Technical usefulness cannot erase that concern. **I pass today.** One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an independently audited paying production deployment showing repeated use and effective customer-controlled deletion of all retained prompt and response content. That would test whether Maritime returns control to its users as convincingly as it removes their server chores.
