# Caveman -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Julius Brussee has earned the right to be taken seriously. He shipped token-efficiency tooling that JetBrains independently tested in July, attracted users to a browser product, and launched Caveman Cloud on October 13. This is specific knowledge acquired through action. His university status tells me little; working artifacts in the exact problem domain tell me much more. The unresolved question is whether that knowledge becomes an asset Caveman owns as the market learns the same lessons.

Imagine a competing gateway adopting Caveman’s optimization techniques tomorrow. Apache-2.0 permits reuse, and LiteLLM and Edgee already sit where model traffic flows. What remains exclusive? The dossier establishes no proprietary model, exclusive workload corpus, or demonstrated switching cost. Customer-specific evaluation histories could become valuable, but their usefulness to one customer does not establish an advantage across customers. Caveman currently teaches the market faster than it proves it can retain the resulting value.

The strongest objection to withholding a check is substantial: approximately 115,500 GitHub stars, 6,700 forks, and 20,000 users of the earlier extension give this founder repeated opportunities to discover what developers actually need. That fits my preference for permissionless tools that turn individual knowledge into scalable output. My exceptions for exceptional builders and working prototypes apply here; neither early revenue nor an established network is compulsory. But the adoption largely predates Cloud. Forks do not establish useful contributions, and extension users do not establish a distribution loop for enterprise infrastructure. There is no measured evidence that adding one customer improves the product or acquisition for another.

Caveman does have room to maneuver. Its initial buyer wants cheaper agent execution; recording, replaying, and controlling those workloads creates a plausible path into broader runtime governance. Edgee’s documented governance deployment at Qonto supplies evidence of adjacent buyer demand, while Caveman already offers $250 and $750 monthly governance packages. That is more useful than the inconsistent $863.7 million market estimate. Still, gateways already occupy this adjacent territory. Expansion is plausible; ownership of it remains contested.

The economics demand unusual honesty. JetBrains measured 8.5% fewer output tokens from the earlier skill while aggregate billed cost increased 11.6% because of an outlier. That finding concerns the earlier product, but it demonstrates why token reduction alone cannot prove customer value. Caveman’s provider-billed comparison guidance, replay evaluations, and deployment gates address the right failure mode. Publishing unfavorable benchmark details is encouraging; I see no documented integrity or financing-pressure red flag. Consumption prices—such as $0.012 per agent minute—identify a direct charging mechanism, but the dossier identifies no realized paid usage or contribution margin.

I would hold my check today. Caveman combines a strong builder with an asset whose defensibility remains weak; that is a mixed investment case despite impressive attention. The strongest future version pools deployment learning into optimization quality competitors cannot reproduce. My one verifiable flip condition is a controlled cross-customer test showing that learning from prior deployments materially lowers provider-billed costs on new customers’ workloads, at unchanged task success, against the same Caveman code without that accumulated learning. That would demonstrate something that compounds beyond the founder’s output.
