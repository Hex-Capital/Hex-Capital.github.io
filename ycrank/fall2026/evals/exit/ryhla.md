# Ryhla -- Exit Evaluation

**Action:** Watch  
**Evidence confidence:** Low

### Company case

**Strategic acquisition** is the strongest path. A utility or utility software vendor could buy Ryhla to integrate feeder screening and application management into its connection workflow, potentially reducing work on applications for sites that lack capacity. Ownership could be worth more than a license if it secured transferable rights to feeder models and utility-study data that measurably improve screening across territories. The memo records E.ON’s majority acquisition of envelio, which establishes precedent for a utility buying adjacent technology. Ryhla reports a live Midwest solar deployment, models across 11 utility territories, and about $160,000 in avoided application fees for one battery developer. Those claims support a customer use case. The acquisition price and the value of ownership to a buyer remain unknown. (The Idea, Defensibility, Market & Traction)

A paid rollout across multiple utility territories, benchmarked against existing screening tools and checked against utility decisions for both accepted and rejected sites, would test whether the benefit repeats. Ryhla’s reported deployment gives it a starting point, and the memo confirms YC backing. Territory expansion may require new feeder data and backtesting, with costs and runway unverified. Model and data transfer rights are also unverified. The memo supplies no usable evidence of an independent route to holder cash. (Defensibility, Market & Traction, Key Risks, Key Facts)

**Proceeds bridge.** The terms of the check under evaluation are unspecified, so use the illustrative $500,000 check at a $10 million post-money valuation. Assume 5% initial ownership falls to 3%, with no follow-on checks. A 5× gross cash return within eight years requires $2.5 million to the investor, or $83.3 million in distributable equity at 3% ownership. The hurdle is $125 million at 2% and $62.5 million at 4%. The sale scenarios below are illustrative. They assume zero net debt and transaction deductions, so enterprise value equals distributable equity. They also assume a simple pro rata waterfall with no senior claims and all consideration paid in cash at closing. Stock, escrow, earnouts, rollover, and compensation are each zero.

| Illustrative outcome | Timing | Enterprise value → distributable equity | Investor cash at 3% | Gross multiple |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Modest strategic sale | Year 5 | $40M → $40M | $1.2M | 2.4× |
| Strong strategic sale | Year 7 | $100M → $100M | $3.0M | 6.0× |
| Prolonged private ownership | Through year 8 | No sale | $0 | 0× |

At 2% ownership, even the modeled $100 million sale returns $2 million, or 4×. The memo supplies no buyer economics or transaction price to support either sale value. In the prolonged-private scenario, growth plateaus, buyers stay away, public scale remains out of reach, and the company reinvests its cash. The investor still holds the shares at year eight. The $0 scenario alone does not trigger the Pass override. (Market & Traction, Key Facts)

### Countercase

A buyer could get enough of the benefit from public hosting-capacity maps, PowerClerk with envelio, or a license to Ryhla’s product. The reported 91% approval rate covers only sites Ryhla recommended, with no disclosed sample size or outcomes for rejected sites. A buyer therefore has little basis for judging how many viable projects Ryhla misses or how consistently it improves on existing tools. Utilities also retain control over approvals and required upgrades. The evidence supports Watch. A structural Pass would require evidence that available substitutes cap the price below the proceeds hurdle. (Defensibility, Key Risks)

### Decisive diligence question

Would owning Ryhla’s model and data rights create enough value over licensing to support a sale at the investor’s proceeds hurdle? A paid, multi-territory rollout that measures review costs and missed viable sites against existing tools, and that a prospective buyer uses to value the transferable rights, would answer it. Buyer economics supporting at least $83.3 million in distributable equity under the 3% ownership scenario would move the case to Reach out.
