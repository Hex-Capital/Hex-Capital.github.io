# 83 Sciences -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Eric Riesel’s MIT PhD and materials-ML research give 83 Sciences rare specific knowledge, while 83 Sciences has no mechanism by which the next laboratory automatically benefits the previous one. That high-founder-knowledge, low-network pairing defines the bet: a deep-science intelligence service wearing the future shape of a data platform. Expertise creates the wedge. Architecture determines whether the wedge compounds.

Stack Overflow is the high rung for specific knowledge: developers building for developers, with shipped work preceding the equity story. Eric Riesel developed ML systems for materials-data analysis and a generative method for chemical experimental data; Ian Naccarella brings Stanford chemical engineering and Sila commercialization experience; Yankang Yang deployed AI programs to more than 30,000 users. 83 Sciences therefore sits close to Stack Overflow on founder-product-market fit. 83 Sciences diverges where it matters: no public repository, benchmark, patent, or independently verified discovery proves that the founders’ knowledge has crystallized into a difficult-to-copy artifact. Specific knowledge is present. Productized specific knowledge remains unproven.

Perplexity’s low-rung weakness was single-sided value: one user did not directly improve the next user’s product, so exceptional technical knowledge and permissionless distribution had to override the missing network. The same weakness is stronger at 83 Sciences. Each laboratory contributes proprietary data under unresolved IP terms, and confidentiality may prevent 83 Sciences from pooling failures across customers. 83 Sciences could accumulate a valuable central corpus, but a proprietary database is not automatically a network. Unless one laboratory’s participation measurably improves recommendations for another, the Nth user adds revenue and integration work—not compounding user value.

Grants of up to $50,000 make the leverage architecture run backward over the next 18 months: 83 Sciences pays to acquire supply, negotiates research partnerships, integrates voice notes and instrument outputs, and co-authors manuscripts. Code may accelerate the work, but 83 Sciences currently distributes through capital and scientific labor rather than through permissionless code or media. The unstated pattern across Twitter, Notion, and Replit was organic replication before institutional selling; 83 Sciences shows no open-source pull, self-service adoption, public technical community, or founder-media channel. The Founding AI Engineer search adds another labor dependency before unprompted adoption is visible.

The $170.4 million materials-informatics market, projected to reach only $410.4 million by 2030, gives 83 Sciences insufficient documented pivot-room. The market score flips a tier if 83 Sciences is really the record-and-reasoning layer for chemistry, biotechnology, batteries, semiconductors, and industrial process development—not merely materials informatics. 83 Sciences has ingredients for that expansion, but the dossier describes partner laboratories, manuscripts, patents, and joint discoveries rather than two concrete adjacent wedges. The first idea rarely works. A narrow market makes learning expensive because every pivot becomes another financing event.

Notion is the single-anchor comparison for the 10-year-partner test: Ivan Zhao’s long obsession produced repeated product rebuilds, while clean execution made patience rational. 83 Sciences has intelligence and energy in abundance—an MIT chemistry PhD, Stanford chemical engineering training, Sila operating experience, and AI deployment to 30,000 users. 83 Sciences has no adverse integrity evidence, but also no public history showing how Ian Naccarella, Eric Riesel, and Yankang Yang allocate authority, resolve IP conflicts, or treat long-term collaborators. I would take the meeting. I would not infer a decade-long partnership from credentials.

Citrine’s 550 deployed models, 380 projects, and 70,000 monthly experiment suggestions predict where 83 Sciences will be forced to compete. By July 2029, 83 Sciences will have pivoted from grant-funded joint discovery into enterprise laboratory data and workflow software, producing $1 million–$3 million ARR from fewer than 15 customers while retaining material integration and scientific-services work. 83 Sciences has enough specific knowledge to build something useful, but the absent network and permissioned distribution constrain the upper-bound geometry. Useful software is not yet permissionless leverage.

Eric Riesel’s generative experimental-data work supplies the strongest bull case: failed experiments could become a unique training corpus, and 83 Sciences could resemble the Perplexity exception—single-sided, but rescued by rare technical knowledge and category timing. The case stings because laboratories discard approximately 90% of experimental data; recovering even a fraction could create an information advantage incumbents cannot reconstruct. But Perplexity paired specific knowledge with global, self-service code distribution, while 83 Sciences offers $50,000 grants, solicits laboratories directly, discloses no dataset scale, and leaves cross-partner IP unresolved. I would flip to Invest if three unaffiliated laboratories onboard without grants and publish evidence that a shared 83 Sciences model improves prospective experimental recommendations using cross-laboratory data. Until then, 83 Sciences is knowledge-rich but leverage-poor.
