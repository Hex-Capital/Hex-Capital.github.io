# Faraday -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Faraday could make manufacturing coordination radically cheaper: orders, inventory, engineering changes, and production would move through executable workflows instead of repeated human handoffs. That is a consequential direction. But the dossier does not quantify the labor consumed today, the production capacity lost, or how much either changes if Faraday works. Manufacturing’s importance cannot substitute for the consequence calculation. I pass on writing a personal check today.

The strongest evidence is the harness: **96.2 versus 71.7 for the GPT-5.5 baseline, at 2.6 times lower run cost**, across 74 synthetic episodes and 33 scenario families. Faraday has built something testable beyond an interface. Yet the comparison establishes performance within its own development environment; it does not establish a 5–10x improvement over a manufacturer’s existing operation. Didero already executes procurement workflows, while Tulip addresses frontline operations. Faraday’s broader scope becomes valuable only if coordination across those boundaries produces an advantage that specialized products cannot match. Optional robot controllers do not establish that advantage.

The strongest argument against passing is the team’s combination of enterprise-agent experience and physical manufacturing experience. Datta Kaligotla worked at Microsoft, Boeing, and Handshake AI; Oscar Rangel built factory systems at Worthington Steel and shipped medical devices at Stryker. Their workflow mappings, permissions, and approval gates also accommodate installed infrastructure, which fits my preference for industrial technologies customers can actually adopt. This could become a repeatable industrial learning platform. But customer-specific reinforcement-learning environments are not yet evidence of an accumulated proprietary learning loop, and relevant biographies do not demonstrate rapid belief revision or recruiting against a specific weakness. The exception for an extraordinary instigator attacking a giant existing value pool remains possible; this dossier does not establish it.

The next dollar should attack transfer from synthetic workflows to live operations. Faraday publishes a benchmark, but the dossier supplies no costed experiment with a live-factory pass/fail threshold or a response to failure. My concern is that company-specific mappings require enough continuing engineering and human intervention to consume the automation savings. The 2.6x inference-cost improvement does not answer that question. Subscription and pilot agreements establish a charging mechanism, while deployment cost, supervision burden, and customer savings remain undisclosed. Those are missing economics, not evidence of permanent subsidy dependence. Likewise, absent revenue and a two-person team are ordinary pre-seed conditions.

Faraday avoids the clearest active red flags: the dossier shows no acquisition-first ambition, permanent subsidy requirement, or demonstrated refusal to learn. Its technical benchmark deserves attention. Nevertheless, the evidence currently supports a promising industrial harness more strongly than a fundamental manufacturing cost collapse. **My flip condition is a customer-audited deployment study across three unaffiliated manufacturers demonstrating a repeatable tenfold reduction in total order-to-production coordination cost, counting integration, human supervision, and error remediation.** That would turn the claimed breadth into an economic mechanism worth purchasing.
