# Instaplay -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Instaplay’s 5,567-play *Scrub Club* makes Instaplay a permissionless-media bet with a founder-partnership discount. Instaplay’s highest dimension is leverage architecture: a prompt becomes code, art, sound, and a browser-playable multiplayer game in two minutes, then travels through links at near-zero marginal cost. Instaplay’s lowest dimension is the 10-year-partner test: Gary Wu, Frank Liu, and Akhil Aggarwal have credible technical pedigrees, but the dossier does not document how Gary joined the NYU pair, how authority is divided, or whether the three have survived anything difficult together. Code compounds quickly. Trust compounds slowly.

Twitter is the high rung because every new post created permissionless media and every share distributed the network without asking a gatekeeper. Instaplay shares the load-bearing mechanism: free creation, browser links, public discovery, and remixing turn users into both producers and distributors. Instaplay diverges from Twitter where the code still does too much of the work and the users too little—four public games around 4,488 to 5,567 plays show motion, but Instaplay has not shown that creators repeatedly publish, remix, and bring their own audiences. Permissionless leverage exists. Compounding distribution remains unproven.

Clearview AI is the low rung on the 10-year-partner dimension because extreme technical capability could not erase privacy controversy and reputation risk. Instaplay shows none of that failure mechanism: Instaplay has no contested exits, partner litigation, volunteered claims of honesty, or other integrity warning. The weakness is uncertainty rather than adverse evidence. Gary Wu previously co-founded Verita, Frank Liu worked at Instagram, and Akhil Aggarwal worked at AWS, but Instaplay provides no record of the founders building together or treating former partners well. For my own money, three résumés are not yet one long-term partnership.

Instaplay’s public catalog turns the network dimension on a simple flywheel: more creators produce more games, more games attract more players, play data improves discovery, and remixing converts players back into creators. Instaplay compounds over the next 18 months only if *Scrub Club*’s 5,567 plays and *Swordling*’s 4,840 plays generate second-order creation rather than passive consumption. Without repeat creators and remix ancestry, Instaplay is a single-sided AI tool wearing a social interface. With them, Instaplay becomes a user-built entertainment network where the Nth creator increases the option set for everyone before them.

The $4.36 billion AI-in-gaming market gives Instaplay room, but the narrower $1.315 billion game-development-tools market could become a cage. Instaplay’s market score flips upward if “starting with games” genuinely means a wedge into interactive media—social worlds, educational simulations, playable stories, and creator entertainment—without rebuilding the generation stack. Instaplay’s score flips downward if multiplayer browser games remain the whole product while Astrocade, Rosebud AI, and FRVR absorb distribution. The first idea rarely works. A large label is not pivot-room; reusable primitives are pivot-room.

Stack Overflow is the single-anchor parallel for specific knowledge: developers who lived the problem built a product for developers, so the founder and user were the same person. Instaplay has technical adjacency rather than equivalent obsession. Gary Wu’s reinforcement-learning and Agentforce work, Frank Liu’s Instagram engineering, and Akhil Aggarwal’s AWS experience support the AI-web stack, but Instaplay names no prior commercial game, creator community, or multi-year game-development artifact; Gary’s and Frank’s top owned GitHub repositories each show one star. Instaplay can acquire gaming knowledge, but acquired knowledge is not specific knowledge.

Instaplay’s two-minute prompt-to-browser loop produces my falsifiable forecast: by July 2029, Instaplay will have repositioned from an AI game generator into a browser-native social creation network, surpassed 10 million monthly plays, and reached $2 million to $5 million ARR through creator subscriptions or generation credits. Instaplay gets there because creation cost collapses, links remove installation friction, and remixing turns finished games into raw material. The product is not the generated game. The product is the compounding graph of creators, prompts, games, remixes, and players.

Astrocade’s reported 20 million engaged users and hundreds of millions of monthly plays are the strongest case against the forecast. Instaplay faces a competitor with $56 million in new funding, general-purpose coding models can reproduce the generation layer, and prompts invoking GTA or Rocket League add IP and moderation liabilities before Instaplay has demonstrated proprietary models or retention. That bear case stings because permissionless code without proprietary distribution becomes a commodity. The evidence keeping me in is that several Instaplay titles independently reached roughly 4,500–5,500 plays while the product already supports multiplayer links and remixing. I would write a small wide-funnel check, not double down. If fewer than 20% of new creators publish a second game within 30 days once cohort data is available, Instaplay is a novelty generator rather than a network, and I pass.
