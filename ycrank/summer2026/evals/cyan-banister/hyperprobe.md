# HyperProbe -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Coding agents do not stop at opening pull requests; they watch running systems, interrogate failures, and repair production under explicit human permissions. The missing transition is safe access to runtime truth. HyperProbe’s present-day leak from that future is not the consensus statistic that 80% of developers use AI tools. It is a coding agent placing a line-specific probe, observing live variables without redeployment, and using that evidence to diagnose a real incident. The dossier shows the beginning of the behavior, although it does not yet map the full path from supervised probes to trusted autonomous remediation.

The strongest evidence is wonderfully concrete. CheQ Digital used the product to catch on the first attempt a synchronization problem that had taken days to reproduce. Housing.com exposed live memory during a traffic spike and fixed a race condition within the hour. Installing an in-process SDK in production and letting it inspect local variables is costly behavior: customers are spending engineering time, operational trust, and security-review capital. Those are functioning-product signals, not a waitlist wearing a costume. The absence of dated cohorts, repeat-use figures, and paid conversions keeps this below the top anchor.

The founders have paid relevant operating tuition. Raina and Singh previously built HyperTest around SDKs, application traces, and integration testing; its reported use by more than 100 product teams is not HyperProbe traction, but it is credible evidence that this pivot emerged from years inside the same technical wound. Raina supplies the technical continuity, while Singh brings product and prior startup experience. The pivot after YC acceptance could look like a fashionable agent repositioning, but it is materially different from two newcomers wrapping a model: the team already built the instrumentation substrate. I still need to know who has final authority. Two people labeled only “Founder” is an unanswered governance question, not proof of the 50/50-control red flag.

Why does debugging have its current shape? Because engineers can observe what they anticipated through logs, while an unanticipated variable state often requires new instrumentation, a deployment, and another occurrence of the failure. HyperProbe removes that wait and returns investigative control to the engineer and agent. But no external gatekeeper captures the rent; the cost is mostly internal engineering time. That makes the pain real but less structurally powerful than a regulated or artificially scarce system. The free service and $99-per-service monthly pricing, with a three-service Professional minimum, create a legible adoption path, yet no disclosed revenue proves that the saved hours support an independent platform rather than a useful observability feature.

The hidden-infrastructure pattern fits: coding agents are the visible wave, and runtime evidence is a layer they will require underneath it. The bear case stings because HyperProbe does not yet control that layer. Lightrun has raised $110 million, Dynatrace already absorbed Rookout, and Multiplayer offers a self-hosted path; the dossier identifies no proprietary corpus, exclusive integration, or structural switching cost. More seriously, local variables can contain credentials, health data, or customer secrets. Default PII redaction, probe expiry, approval gates, immutable audit logs, and private-VPC deployment are the right architecture, but vendor documentation is not independent proof. I checked the exception path and find no extraordinary scale or technical result that would justify relaxing the privacy gate.

I would write a small pre-seed check. The reason is narrow: two named customers entrusted a working artifact with production state and received unusually fast diagnoses, while the founders arrived through an adjacent product rather than a category brainstorm. The check is for HyperProbe becoming the governed runtime-evidence layer for agents, not merely selling prettier breakpoints. My one flip condition is verifiable: if an independent enterprise security test shows that default capture can export unredacted secrets or PII beyond the customer-controlled boundary, I pass.
