# Kairox Systems -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Kairox Systems could eliminate the handoff between aerial reconnaissance and underwater operations: fly to the coast, submerge, complete a mission, and leave without a separate underwater deployment platform. That could change logistics and personnel exposure. But how much? The dossier supplies no baseline for deployment cost, vessel support, or missions enabled. The publisher’s internally inconsistent $3 billion category estimate does not answer that question. I see a potentially valuable capability; I cannot yet price an option on an orders-of-magnitude consequence.

The strongest evidence is that the founders built their first prototype with a **$14,000 grant** and are testing an improved version. That is useful capital discipline. It moves Kairox beyond a rendering. However, its reported **TRL 4** does not establish repeated air–water transitions under operational conditions, and the dossier provides no test threshold connecting the next dollars spent to mission reliability. The decisive engineering question is whether combining two vehicles removes more operational burden than the combined vehicle introduces through payload, energy, sealing, and recovery compromises. A cheap prototype begins that investigation; it does not settle it ([Tempest](https://www.kairoxsys.com/tempest)).

The strongest argument against passing is the team’s unusually relevant combination of mechanical engineering, EECS, aerospace, and controls experience. Jorge’s control-law and hardware-qualification work is particularly pertinent. Reported Marine Corps engagement gives these engineers access to people who can identify a useful mission before they optimize the wrong machine. This fits my willingness to fund difficult physical systems through long qualification cycles. Yet complementary credentials do not establish rapid belief revision or difficult recruiting, and engagement is not a purchase commitment. I would not penalize a Fall 2026 company for lacking revenue; I also would not convert conversations into validation ([founder profile](https://www.ycombinator.com/founders/jorge-cavazos), [YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/kairox-systems)).

Competition makes the required proof sharper. **SubUAS already demonstrated fly-and-swim capability and reported more than $4 million in Navy Phase III contracts by 2020.** Those awards establish spending on the underlying need, not Kairox’s traction. Fixed-wing transit may produce an important advantage over another configuration, but no published range, endurance, depth, or mission benchmark demonstrates it. Nor does the dossier establish a proprietary learning loop from routine operations. My exceptions for an extraordinary instigator or overwhelming consequence do not override these gaps: the record contains neither a documented mission-driven commitment of that strength nor a quantified consequence large enough to underwrite the missing proof. There is also no evidence of exit-first behavior or premature organizational expansion; this is insufficient investment evidence, not founder misconduct ([SubUAS record](https://www.sbir.gov/sites/default/files/documents/SubUAS-LLC-FINAL-508.pdf)).

I pass with my own money today. Public pricing, manufacturing cost, service burden, and vehicle lifetime are undisclosed, so unsubsidized mission economics remain unknown; the prototype grant does not imply permanent subsidy dependence. My **one flip condition** is a customer-witnessed comparative trial showing Tempest completes a representative coastal mission at least **5x cheaper per successful mission** than the customer’s existing aerial-plus-underwater arrangement, at the customer’s required reliability, with support and expected vehicle losses included. That would connect the engineering to an operational cost collapse. Until then, Kairox has demonstrated economical prototype construction, while the transformative advantage remains unproven.
