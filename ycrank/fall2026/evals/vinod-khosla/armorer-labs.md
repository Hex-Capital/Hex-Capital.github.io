# Armorer Labs -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Armorer Labs could remove a bottleneck to deploying AI in regulated work: allowing agents to act without giving them uncontrolled authority. That consequence interests me more than another security dashboard. But the dossier quantifies a $1.8 billion security market, not the expert labor, deployment delays, or losses its technology could eliminate. Security spending is a revenue opportunity. Establishing an orders-of-magnitude expansion in trustworthy automation requires another step of evidence.

The strongest technical signal is the company-reported JEV comparison: 1.9 milliseconds versus approximately 168 milliseconds, with 98% versus 80% accuracy. Roughly 88x lower latency could make enforcement practical on every tool call. There is a concrete implementation behind the claim: integration SDKs, a proprietary runtime, and one-use authorization tokens. But 50 documents cannot establish performance against adaptive attackers or across regulated workflows. Nor does this comparison establish superiority over HiddenLayer, Zenity, or Noma. I give Armorer meaningful technical credit, bounded by what was actually tested.

The existential question is whether Armorer can control every consequential action while preserving legitimate work. Its documentation acknowledges that tool calls must be wrapped and direct credential paths removed. One unguarded path can defeat an otherwise excellent detector. The four-hour fix claim and workflow-verification approach address the right problem, but the dossier supplies no completed experiment with a bypass threshold, false-block threshold, cost, and stop condition. My next dollar would fund an independent attempt to break the complete authorization boundary. Faster classification does not answer that question.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders. Begimher and Leo spent three years building AI security systems together at AWS; their work includes ASH, ThreatForest, and SIR-Bench. Paul Vixie’s investor/advisor involvement adds relevant technical judgment. A Just Eat Takeaway engineering director’s endorsement gives the remediation proposition some external support, although it establishes neither paid adoption nor measured savings. This resembles a pattern I favor: scarce expertise converted into repeatable infrastructure. Yet the dossier does not establish that customer usage produces a proprietary learning advantage. It also documents expertise more clearly than belief revision or recruiting against a specific weakness; those are different founder signals.

I would not dismiss Armorer as a generic model wrapper. Its enforcement architecture provides evidence against that red flag. Equally, keeping runtime assets private does not prove competitors cannot reproduce the capability. My exceptions for exceptional technical founders attacking enormous existing value pools deserve consideration here, but the quantified consequence needed to invoke them is missing. CPU execution suggests economical inference; it does not establish economical deployment once integration, policy maintenance, and remediation review enter the calculation. Public pricing and a bottom-up comparison with current security labor are absent. These are evidence limits, not penalties for having two founders or undisclosed revenue.

I pass with my own money today. Armorer has a promising technical wedge, but the evidence does not yet connect that wedge to a consequential cost collapse. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently audited regulated-customer deployment demonstrating at least a 10x reduction in total security-verification cost at matched attack coverage and preserved legitimate workflows. That would connect technical performance to economic consequence and justify reopening the investment decision.
