# Whitespace -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: capable agents now let wholesale distributors automate work inside existing ERPs and inboxes, and the controlling variable is whether Whitespace can deploy reliable, expanding workflows quickly enough that labor savings repay implementation before a funded competitor captures the account. The evidence is promising but does not yet isolate a threshold. Whitespace reports two-to-four-week implementations, 80%+ productivity gains in selected workflows, and three-month payback, but it does not disclose revenue, retention, error rates, or whether customers expand after the first deployment.

The economic mechanism is stronger than a generic AI story. Distributors reportedly allocate 30–50% of staff capacity to back-office work, and Whitespace is acting on orders, replenishment, and customer queries rather than merely generating text. Two named customers—Kingfisher Direct and Warwick—have active deployments across order intake, customer service, and inventory planning. If the reported payback holds across ordinary implementations, adoption can be financed from operating savings rather than an experimental software budget. That is a real market change, although all performance figures remain company-reported and limited to selected workflows.

The product also has the right architectural shape. It connects ERP, email, finance, and business data; retains customer-specific knowledge; routes exceptions to humans; and incorporates corrections into subsequent work. Stronger models should improve task completion while Whitespace continues to own permissions, integrations, workflow state, and responsibility for the action. The weakness is that this is not yet demonstrated as a durable moat. Plato, Proton.ai, Canals, and Conexiom are already pursuing overlapping workflows with disclosed funding ranging from $14.5 million to $40 million. Customer-specific knowledge bases are switching costs only if they produce measurably better outcomes or make replacement operationally painful.

The strongest bear case is therefore market crystallization, not product usefulness. Whitespace spans inventory, procurement, sales, service, finance, and planning before proving which entry workflow creates repeatable distribution. Better-capitalized competitors can concentrate on order entry or sales, establish the system relationship, and cross-sell outward. The team’s broad “AI operating system” positioning risks funding multiple integration-heavy products simultaneously when one wedge should be doing the distribution work. Private correction histories do not solve this unless their reproduction cost or performance advantage becomes measurable.

The founders partially offset that risk. Both grew up in wholesale-distribution families; Alex Tung advised manufacturers and distributors at BCG and helped Ankar reach seven-figure ARR as its first non-engineering hire, while Leon Yao built logistics optimization products at InstaDeep and previously served as CTO of a product used by more than 1,000 users and three schools. That is credible domain context and some prior operating learning. It does not establish the costly commitment or second-time-founder history required for top-quartile founder evidence, and this is not a case where rare frontier research justifies overlooking market proof.

I would invest a small personal check, contingent on focus. The verifiable flip condition is whether, within six months, at least three additional distributors deploy the same initial workflow in four weeks or less and at least half expand into a second workflow within 90 days while maintaining payback below six months. Failure would indicate a services-heavy integration business in a crowded category; success would show that the first product is becoming a reusable distribution channel.
