# Pennant -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The strongest fact is not that Pennant uses AI. It is that Ryan spent more than ten years inside BlackRock, State Street, and PJT watching institutions make proxy decisions with filings, spreadsheets, and notes. Tomas worked on the same BlackRock Aladdin desk, and the founders have known one another for 15 years. This is an organic startup idea hidden inside an unusually dense schlep: policy interpretation, data integrations, approvals, overrides, and auditability. That earns 11/12 for founder-originated need. But the dossier never names the moment something broke, what the founders built around it, or how quickly the situation changed. Résumés prove exposure, not resourcefulness. I therefore have to leave the most important criterion at its missing-evidence default of 8/31.

There is plainly a product rather than a diagram. Pennant aggregates meetings and proposals, applies client-specific voting policies, links recommendations to sources, and records human approvals and overrides. Those are sensible artifacts for a domain where an incorrect recommendation can affect an institutional vote. But there is no disclosed accuracy result, processing threshold, corpus size, or comparison with the manual workflow. Paying customers imply that the system functions, but payment belongs mainly under demand; it cannot by itself turn an unspecified architecture into measured technical proof. This is intermediate evidence, worth 14/23.

The demand evidence is much stronger. A large U.S. public pension plan, a Top AmLaw 100 firm, and governance advisors are already paying. A global asset manager is helping design the product, and Pennant reports engagements with S&P 500 companies. These are narrow users with an expensive recurring job, not people who joined a waitlist because “AI governance” sounded fashionable. The missing facts are usage frequency, renewals, expansion, and revenue growth, so this is not full credit. It is nevertheless high-band pre-seed pull: 16/18.

The bull case stings. Selling any unfinished product to a pension plan and a major law firm is difficult, and doing it in a market where ISS and Glass Lewis reportedly control 97% suggests the founders know how to enter institutions. The schlep may itself become the moat as Pennant accumulates policy mappings, reviewed recommendations, and precedent. This resembles the concrete pre-round proof pattern I often like. But the proof spike remains commercial rather than technical: Pennant discloses neither the scale of its proprietary substrate nor evidence that its recommendations outperform an internal system assembled from SEC data, general-purpose models, and policy rules. JPMorgan is already building internally. The known-exception logic—working product plus unmistakable pull replacing a missing prior—nearly applies, but unnamed customer categories do not reveal what these founders do when an incumbent, integration, or compliance obstacle actually blocks deployment.

I see no launch-day theater, premature hiring, application-assembled cofounder relationship, or decisive dependence on the asset-manager design partner. Those red flags are absent. I also see no documented rejected hypothesis or evidence-driven product change, so route flexibility receives exactly its 6/16 default. My base case is that Pennant becomes a valuable specialist workflow company, reaching roughly $3–8 million ARR within 36 months without displacing the proxy-advisory incumbents. I would not write the check yet. One verifiable fact would flip me: a dated enterprise deployment where a named data, policy, or incumbent-integration blocker appeared, the two founders shipped a workaround within weeks, and the customer subsequently renewed or expanded paid usage.
