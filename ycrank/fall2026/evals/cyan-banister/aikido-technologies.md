# Aikido Technologies -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a coastal country putting inference capacity beside its offshore electricity generation rather than bringing every megawatt onto crowded land. Aikido Technologies offers a plausible path: qualify the floating platform, integrate compute and cooling, then persuade customers that offshore capacity is dependable. The identified UK site targeting 2028 gives that future a date. The February 2025 ABS-witnessed quarter-scale upending demonstration gives it a physical breadcrumb. But that breadcrumb proves something about deploying a wind platform; it does not yet prove that computers should live aboard it.

The enabling layer is what attracts me. Aikido owns a granted floating-wind patent and proposes combining a 15MW-plus turbine, batteries, accessible data halls, and hull-based heat rejection. That is substantial engineering beneath the fashionable application. Yet why does putting everything on one platform improve the customer’s economics? Four-plus hours of batteries do not establish continuous power through an extended wind lull. Subsea connections, diesel backup, and vessel servicing could reintroduce the infrastructure burden the platform promises to escape. The projected PUE below 1.08 cannot answer that question by itself. These are physical resource constraints; the dossier has not identified a quantified institutional rent or shown whose economic independence Aikido restores.

Kanner and Yu previously worked together at Principle Power, leading R&D and hydrodynamics analysis respectively; Reeves brings turbine engineering and helicopter-design experience. Those are relevant operating disciplines, not decorative degrees. The $4 million floating-wind financing in June 2024 also predates the March 2026 datacenter announcement. That chronology matters: the team was pursuing the underlying machine before attaching an AI business to it. I see credible technical commitment, although the dossier supplies less evidence of independently originated experiments or command of datacenter customer economics.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that useful infrastructure often finds its first commercial destination after the hard engineering begins. Aikido fits my pattern of looking beneath a visible wave for an enabling asset. Waiting for substantial revenue could mean waiting past the best investment moment. I can accept that exception when a functioning prototype and signed industrial commitments connect engineering to demand. Here, however, the technology page describes a non-operational demonstration and a planned compute retrofit. Customer interest and commercial discussions establish neither a signed deployment nor costly user behavior. The announced Norway proof of concept remains a plan in this dossier. Panthalassa’s financing cannot supply Aikido’s missing validation.

I am **Neutral**. This is a credible offshore-engineering team with a potentially valuable next destination, and I would not dismiss the expansion as opportunistic merely because AI customers are interested. But the original platform’s progress cannot stand in for integrated datacenter performance. My one flip condition is an independently verified customer-acceptance report from an offshore pilot completing 90 consecutive days of contracted inference service within its promised availability and delivered-cost limits. That would show whether Aikido Technologies has found a useful place for computers to live—and whether customers agree strongly enough to depend on it.
