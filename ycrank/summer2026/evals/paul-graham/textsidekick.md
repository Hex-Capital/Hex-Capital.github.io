# Sidekick -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that Justin So comes from a third-generation manufacturing family. It is that he spent summers on the factory floor and built a product that removes a piece of software from the worker’s path. A machine is down; the answer is buried in a binder or in a veteran’s head; the worker sends a text. That is a plausible founder-originated need, and SMS is a clever response to the practical ugliness of frontline adoption.

But the dossier omits the episode I most want to see. What broke at the first plant? Did workers ignore an app, did retrieval give a dangerous answer, or did an integration fail? What did Justin change, and how quickly? Reaching more than 20 plants and 600 frontline workers by July 2026 implies that Sidekick has shipped more than a demo, but it does not show the founder turning a named blocker into a workaround. I cannot infer resourcefulness merely from deployment count.

There is a real artifact here. Sidekick accepts text, photos, and voice, retrieves from company documents, routes work orders, escalates unanswered questions, and records resolutions. That is more than an AI-for-manufacturing slide. Yet the strongest technical claims remain unmeasured: no retrieval accuracy, response-time improvement, supported integration catalog, uptime record, or production PLC/SCADA example is public. The terms explicitly require workers to verify generated answers, and SOC 2 Type II is still in progress. Sidekick has built the machine, but has not yet shown me the gauge reading.

The 20 plants and 600 workers are the most promising evidence and also the easiest to overread. They identify a narrow user niche willing to encounter the crude product, which matters far more than the $9.73 billion connected-worker TAM or 644 LinkedIn followers. But they do not establish repeated use, payment, retention, referrals, or reduced downtime. Twenty installations that managers bought and workers abandoned would mean something very different from twenty plants where operators text Sidekick every shift. The disclosed evidence does not distinguish those cases.

The bear case is sharp. MaintainX already has work orders, AI search, voice memos, asset history, enterprise integrations, and an expected $135 million-plus in 2026 ARR; adding an SMS channel may be easier than Sidekick building the rest of a CMMS. The customer-owned plant history could create switching costs, but today that is a prospective moat rather than a demonstrated proprietary substrate. Sidekick also lacks the proof spike I see in my strongest recent bets: the 600 workers are a population count, not a measured operational result. Still, the usual red flags are mostly absent. Justin is selling through founder-led demos and referrals, not waiting for a large partner, a press launch, or a sales hire.

I would not write the check yet because the people-first gate remains unproven, and the available traction is not unmistakable enough to override it as a known exception. My verifiable flip condition is simple: show one named plant where workers used Sidekick repeatedly for at least eight weeks, alongside a dated example of a failed deployment assumption that Justin personally corrected and a measured reduction in downtime or resolution time. That would turn the current story from a sensible industrial interface into a logical proof.
