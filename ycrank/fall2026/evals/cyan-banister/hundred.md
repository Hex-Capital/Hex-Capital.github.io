# Hundred -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

A tray of tiny components is an interesting place to look for the future. By 2032, I can imagine an engineer tracing a defective finished device back to photographs taken before its parts entered assembly. Getting there requires reliable inspection at production speed, then records that remain useful across suppliers and factories. Hundred’s proposed station addresses the first step. The dossier does not establish the second, or show a present-day artifact connecting the two. That larger future is my hypothesis; I cannot give the company credit for having reached it.

The immediate problem is concrete enough to hold my attention. Sampling leaves some incoming components unexamined, and missing photographs leave engineers without evidence when something fails downstream. Why should an engineer have to reconstruct the condition of a part after it has disappeared into a device? Hundred could return investigative control to that person. But the dossier identifies neither a quantified cost of this information gap nor a gatekeeper collecting a rent from it. This is a plausible repair to an operating failure, with the economics still unmeasured.

Erica Diaz gives the repair credibility. More than five-and-a-half years designing hardware at Meta, Google, and Magic Leap, including supplier troubleshooting in China and Mexico, is relevant operating experience. Gosserand’s five years at Twitch and Liao’s quantitative and machine-learning background supply complementary skills. Diaz and Gosserand have known each other since 2017. Those facts give me reasons to take the team seriously. They do not yet show the independently pursued experiments, earlier inspection builds, or first-believer acts that would establish an obsession predating Hundred. The distinction matters when I am backing the people to traverse that imagined future.

The strongest argument against passing is sitting inside the box: exclude ambient light, control illumination, image the entire tray, and compare parts through several methods. Physical control over image capture could make this substantially more useful than adding another model to inconsistent photographs. It fits my pattern of finding opportunity in unglamorous workflow repair and, potentially, the infrastructure beneath more automated manufacturing. But Elementary already offers inspection through installed cameras. Hundred must demonstrate when its dedicated station earns the additional installation work. Its claimed sub-100-micrometer detection and single-acceptable-part calibration describe an approach; they do not establish reliable acceptance decisions across legitimate material variation.

The October 2026 report of deployments underway to three founding partners is encouraging, but it supplies no completed installation or repeated customer action to underwrite. Preferential pricing is an offer, not evidence of payment. My willingness to back pre-revenue industrial companies applies here: revenue need not come first. That exception becomes persuasive with a demonstrated prototype, signed industrial commitments, and unusually deep founder proof; this dossier does not establish that combination. Conversely, I see no affirmative evidence of a leadership shell game, severe privacy problem, or dependence on one closed model supplier. Unspecified photograph ownership deserves diligence, not an invented red flag.

I pass with my own money today. Hundred has a credible team and a useful physical wedge, while the enduring enabling layer remains unproven. My one verifiable condition for reopening the decision is a completed, paid 90-day factory trial in which the customer replaces incoming sampling with Hundred after it meets agreed false-accept, false-reject, and throughput thresholds. That would show a manufacturer trusting the station with production consequences—the behavior that would make this little box much more interesting.
