# Prescience, Inc. -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The **$1.41 trillion U.S. group-health-insurance market** makes Prescience, Inc. a regime-change ambition attached to an unproven learning loop. Prescience, Inc. is strongest on consequence: if patient-trajectory prediction lets employers bypass carrier margins, broker commissions, PBM spreads, and preventable care, several entrenched economic mechanisms break. Prescience, Inc. is weakest on demonstrated learning rate: Rishab Jain exited Qurios, but the dossier contains no specific pivot, rejected thesis, or “we were wrong” moment. Large consequence with untested adaptation is a dangerous signature. It is also an interesting one.

**OpenAI (2019)** is the high rung because generalized intelligence threatened whole categories of labor before revenue or moat clarity existed. Prescience, Inc. shares the load-bearing mechanism: using intelligence to replace allocation decisions made by expensive institutions, here insurers and fragmented care networks. Prescience, Inc. diverges where the evidence becomes clinical rather than rhetorical. The claimed approximately 20% premium reduction is meaningful, but no prospective validation, clinical outcomes, employer count, or realized claims analysis is public. Twenty percent savings can become system collapse, but today it still looks uncomfortably like the optimization play I warn against.

**Range Fuels** is the low rung on learning because plant economics kept contradicting the thesis without a sufficiently fast redesign before closure. Prescience, Inc. has no equivalent negative evidence, but Prescience, Inc. also offers no documented thesis update showing how Rishab or Aditya changed Time Machine, underwriting, or physician contracting after contact with claims reality. Qurios provides evidence that Rishab can build and exit; it does not establish learning through a specific pivot. No evidence — applying the absent-evidence default. Credentials measure distance traveled. Learning rate measures how quickly direction changes when the map is wrong.

**Time Machine’s reinforcement learning over medical histories** creates a real 18-month technical mechanism: better longitudinal data could improve routing, lower claims, strengthen employer retention, and produce still better data. Prescience, Inc. could compound through that loop. The inverse loop is equally powerful: claims mature slowly, adverse selection corrupts early cohorts, and savings-linked compensation deprives Prescience, Inc. of revenue precisely when model errors are most expensive. Angle Health and Collective Health can copy navigation features; they cannot easily copy validated counterfactual patient trajectories. The problem is that Prescience, Inc. has not yet shown those trajectories are more than an API-deep claim.

**Rishab Jain’s 10-plus ML and health papers and Aditya Jain’s Harvard MD** show intellectual capacity, but expertise is not defiance. Prescience, Inc. claims that millions of simulated patient paths can direct preventive care better than conventional actuarial and clinical workflows; that is contrarian in ambition, though not yet documented as a position the medical or insurance establishment rejected. Prescience, Inc. would flip a full tier on founder defiance if a prospective cohort showed Time Machine beating incumbent risk models while physicians followed materially different interventions. Experts predicted fewer than one million U.S. cellphones by 2000; reality delivered 109 million. I reward the person disproving the forecast, not the person merely announcing one.

**Commonwealth Fusion’s $115 million Series A** matched a roadmap whose first commercial milestone could not be reached with ordinary venture economics. Prescience, Inc. may eventually need substantial risk capital, multistate licensing, reserves, claims infrastructure, and a contracted physician network, but no disclosed capital plan establishes more than $50 million to a falsifiable milestone. Prescience, Inc. may also be shifting much of that burden to unnamed banking, risk, provider-network, and compliance partners. Capital intensity caused by insurance regulation is less attractive than capital intensity buying a scientific breakthrough. I am over-resourced until Prescience, Inc. shows where concentrated capital creates an unfair technical advantage.

**Angle Health’s more than 2,600 employers across 44 states** anchors my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Prescience, Inc. will have narrowed from an integrated payer-provider vision into a technology-enabled TPA and clinical-navigation product for self-funded employers, producing **$3 million to $8 million in annualized revenue** while no longer carrying the full insurance-risk proposition. Prescience, Inc. will discover that distribution, regulatory coverage, claims latency, and reserve economics move faster than its patient-trajectory data advantage. The ambition survives. The architecture shrinks.

**Prescience, Inc.’s reported 20% premium reduction** is the strongest objection to that forecast. The bull case stings: Prescience, Inc. already has design partners, a savings-aligned model, a California administrator license, Rishab’s prior exit and 160,000-plus YouTube audience, and Aditya’s clinical credibility. The **Glean** exception shows that domain expertise is acceptable when intelligence reorganizes a sufficiently large economic system; my unstated preference for prior exits, intellectual reputation, and YC pedigree also favors Prescience, Inc. But an undisclosed employer count, unpublished prospective validation, one listed state license, and competitors with hundreds of millions raised leave the central miracle unverified. One condition flips my verdict to Invest: independent actuarial validation across at least ten named employers and two renewal cycles showing at least 15% net claims savings attributable to Time Machine-directed interventions.
