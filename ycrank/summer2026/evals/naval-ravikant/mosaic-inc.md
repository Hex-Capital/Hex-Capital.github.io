# Mosaic -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Mosaic has shipped the right demo into the wrong competitive geometry. Dorsa Rohani brings distributed-inference work from Tenstorrent and ML research across NVIDIA, Vector Institute, and Deep Genomics; Shubham Patil reports building collaboration interfaces for developer teams. Their working multiplayer terminal, covered by The Neuron and analyzed by Oflight in July 2026, is action rather than résumé signaling. But the dossier does not show that either founder previously sold this workflow, operated it inside a real engineering organization, or produced repeat-user outcomes. The team knows the technical neighborhood; it has not yet proved ownership of the exact problem.

The product insight is real: when humans and agents share terminals, session state, and decision history, coordination becomes part of the development environment rather than an external management task. Claude Code reached a $1 billion run rate by November 2025, so more engineering work will pass through agents. Mosaic could become the shared memory layer for that work. Yet market growth is not a wedge. The cited $29.47 billion AI-code-tools market and $7.71 billion multi-agent market do not identify which initial buyer urgently adopts multiplayer control or how that capability expands into an adjacent transaction layer.

The fatal question is what survives after the interface is copied. Today the answer is almost nothing. Conductor, Superset, and Pentagon already cover parallel sessions, persistent context, or human-agent collaboration, and the dossier explicitly says they can extend toward Mosaic’s shared-workspace features. Organizational history, permissions, and workflow state could eventually compound into switching costs, but “could” is not an asset. No exclusive data, proprietary model, hardware boundary, retention evidence, or user-created network exists publicly. Rapidly reproducible code turns Mosaic’s demo into evidence of execution, not defensibility.

The strongest bull case is that the collaboration itself becomes the network: each engineer and agent contributes reusable context, making the environment more valuable to every teammate. The free Hobby tier and $200-per-seat Team plan could seed developer adoption and capture substantial value from twenty-seat teams. This is also the prototype-before-distribution exception—a strange product can deserve backing before conventional metrics when the founders have already developed a distinctive product language. But Mosaic has disclosed neither a paying seat nor repeat usage, and press coverage of a demonstration is not persistent behavior. A multiplayer feature is not yet a network effect.

I find no evidence of founder dishonesty, artificial financing pressure, reassurance-seeking, or other relationship vetoes. The concern is the subtler red flag: a fashionable AI product using category momentum as a proxy for adoption. Nor does Mosaic yet match the permissionless pattern I prefer. Users can collaborate inside the product, but there is no evidence that outsiders contribute supply, distribution, or proprietary value without proportional company labor. The likely 36-month outcome is a pivot from multiplayer coding workspace to an enterprise agent-security and control plane, where shared permissions and audit history solve a harder problem than shared terminals.

I would pass today. The founders may be good enough to find the durable company after this product, but I am investing my own money in the asset, not merely the motion. One condition flips the decision: a documented cohort of at least 20 independent teams paying $200 per seat, retaining for six months, and producing shared organizational context that measurably reduces onboarding or handoff time. That would prove direct value capture and show that usage creates a compounding substrate competitors cannot reproduce by copying the interface.
