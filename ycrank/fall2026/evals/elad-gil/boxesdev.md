# Boxes.dev -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Coding agents move more development work off the laptop; developers then buy persistent, isolated execution environments. The controlling variable for Boxes.dev is whether those environments become a recurring paid destination independent of the agent provider. Parallel processes and shared environment state create a concrete problem. The dossier establishes that problem and a working approach, but it does not establish the customer behavior that would make this a large standalone business.

Boxes.dev has built something more substantial than an output formatter. A prepared Template box can be snapshotted into isolated Linux environments where native Claude Code and Codex edit files, execute commands, and test applications. Persistent environments and credentials give the product operational state. Better agents could make that infrastructure more useful by increasing the amount of work delegated. However, Conductor already offers cloud microVMs, native agents, customer subscriptions, and mobile access. The market is crystallizing around overlapping products, and Boxes.dev uses E2B for its underlying sandbox infrastructure. Owning the development environment creates utility; the dossier does not yet demonstrate durable control over that workflow.

The economic question is unusually important here because increased usage also consumes compute. The homepage offers $19 for 50 hours, $99 for 250 hours, and $249 for 600 hours, with $0.60 hourly overages and model subscriptions charged separately. These are prices, not evidence of gross margins or improved customer economics. There is no measured setup-time reduction, increase in completed tasks, or infrastructure cost advantage. The shift toward parallel agents supplies a credible adoption mechanism, but it does not automatically give the orchestration layer attractive economics.

The strongest argument against passing is the team. Nick Bushak co-founded Gem and served as CTO; Drew Regitsky was its first engineer. They have built together, and their reported near-exclusive use of Claude Code and Codex exposed the problem personally. That is meaningful prior startup learning attached to a product they need. It fits my pattern of backing experienced operators building infrastructure around a technology transition. But buying exposure across a transition does not establish why this particular layer captures value. The scarce-research-team exception is also unsupported: the documented advantage is product-building experience, without frontier research or a scarce underlying technical asset that would justify overriding absent demand evidence.

I would pass on a personal check today. Product Hunt’s 113 launch points and the companion apps’ small public footprints do not establish repeat use, but neither do they establish rejection; paid demand remains unknown. The relevant red flag is competitive reproducibility, rather than any documented founder misconduct or vanity-project behavior. Persistent environments partly answer the model-absorption concern, while direct feature overlap leaves the distribution question open. One verifiable flip condition would be **90-day paid-team retention above 80% across at least 20 teams paying $99 or more per user per month**. That would show developers repeatedly choosing Boxes.dev as their working environment despite available substitutes.
