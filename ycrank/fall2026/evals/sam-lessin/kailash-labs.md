# Kailash Labs -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Kailash Labs offers manufacturers robot stations without upfront capex. That makes the financing question part of the product: somebody still pays for the arm, installation, maintenance, and mistakes. The reported $500K YC investment tells me capital entered the company; it tells me neither my entry price nor whether the next check is scarce. Post-money valuation and competing-term-sheet count are undisclosed. I cannot call this an auction, but I also cannot underwrite cheap uncertainty from the YC check size.

What does my money prove? Kailash’s seven-day pilot offer could become a useful experiment around one factory task. It is currently an offer, with no disclosed executed pilot, acceptance threshold, or connection between round proceeds and a dated result. The apparent move from specialized video models to managed robot stations changes the experiment considerably. Producing useful video intelligence and delivering dependable factory labor have different failure modes. I want one task whose successful deployment changes the business’s value, rather than capital funding the discovery of which business this is.

The founders give me a reason to take that discovery seriously. Sharma has released Marlin and documents robot-learning research and precision-welding motion planning; that is more substance than an AI résumé. His technical work supports a plausible bridge between perception software and physical operations. But the dossier does not establish that he has operated the customer side of that bridge, and Mete’s current involvement remains unresolved. Kailash fits my pattern of software reorganizing difficult physical infrastructure. The missing piece is evidence that the team can absorb the operational complexity economically. Formic already bundles equipment, deployment, maintenance, and financing, so integration alone does not establish an unusual advantage.

The strongest argument against passing is that Kailash has already shipped something people use: Marlin recorded 934 monthly downloads and appeared in nine Hugging Face Spaces. Specialized models could make narrowly defined factory tasks practical without requiring frontier-model economics. That possibility deserves attention. But those are earlier-product adoption signals, not evidence that proprietary factory access causes purchases. An Apache-2.0, Qwen-derived model does not itself create an owned data advantage. My exceptions to strict financing gates require unusually concrete conversion, operating economics, or proprietary access; none is documented here. Equally, I see no evidence of manufactured fundraising urgency or an equal-founder decision committee. Those red flags should not be invented from sparse disclosure.

I pass at **30/100**. Revenue, margin, burn, and equipment-financing terms are missing, so I use the stated economics default rather than assume the robot service loses money. The concern is the unresolved link between technical capability, customer payment, and capital recovery. My one verifiable condition for reopening diligence is a paid factory station operating for 90 consecutive days with collected revenue covering equipment amortization, installation, maintenance, and human intervention. That would show Kailash can sell productive capacity without quietly financing every customer’s labor savings from its next round.
