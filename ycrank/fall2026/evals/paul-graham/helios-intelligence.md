# Helios Intelligence -- Paul Graham Evaluation

What did these founders do when something broke? Helios Intelligence’s dossier gives me considerable evidence that Omar Abudayyeh and Jonathan Gootenberg understand molecular biology. It gives me no specific episode in which an obstacle forced them to invent and ship a workaround. Their prior companies and shared laboratory make them worth meeting. But acquired ventures, without the underlying stories, cannot establish resourcefulness. I assign the missing-evidence default here. Nothing suggests passivity; the decisive founder evidence simply hasn’t been supplied.

The strongest evidence is the May 2026 mAge preprint. A reported 21% reduction in baseline mortality-prediction error is a concrete research result, and both founders helped produce it. That earns intermediate technical credit. But there are two separate experiments involved in Helios’s proposition: predicting something useful from molecular measurements, and reliably obtaining those measurements from a small home-collected sample. The preprint supports the first. It does not establish that Helios can measure 1,000+ biomarkers reproducibly after collection and transport. Its acknowledged population limitations also matter when moving from a studied cohort to individual consumers.

The strongest argument against passing is that these founders have already spent years doing difficult biological work together. Their diagnostics experience and quantified research fit my pattern of backing technical teams whose experiments make unfamiliar sectors investable. The Bindwell exception is relevant: numerical technical proof, laboratory validation, and direct problem exposure can justify overriding weak conventional signals. Helios supplies genuine scientific accomplishment, but the evidence stops before validating the sampling system its commercial proposition requires. That difference prevents the exception from carrying the decision.

Who needs the first crude version badly enough to return? Roon is available for purchase, with subscriptions advertised from $9.16 per tin, but the dossier reports neither purchases nor repeat usage. Supplement availability cannot establish demand for molecular testing, whose pricing is undisclosed. SiPhox already offers home collection covering up to 60 biomarkers; Helios needs to show what its additional measurements let a particular user do. The founders’ diagnostics background makes that question credible, but there is no documented personal collision with this particular problem to establish an organic origin. Nor does launching Roon establish an evidence-driven route change: there is no rejected hypothesis, customer observation, or dated revision. Those dimensions receive their absent-evidence defaults.

I would pass on Helios Intelligence today. The dossier establishes scientific ability more clearly than founder behavior under resistance or a narrow well of urgent demand. I see no demonstrated dependence on a large partner, ceremonial launch, or newly assembled cofounder relationship; hiring growth and design staff alone does not establish premature delegation either. The concern is the missing bridge between the research and a product people repeatedly need. My verifiable condition for reconsideration is one documented pilot in which the founders fix a named home-collection failure within weeks and the same paying users return for a second test whose measurements agree with a laboratory reference.
