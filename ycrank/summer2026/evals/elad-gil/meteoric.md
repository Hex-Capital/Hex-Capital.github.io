# Meteoric -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Electric drones and faster weather models may let utility-scale solar operators manipulate local cloud cover economically; the controlling variable is incremental, saleable MWh recovered per all-in drone-hour under an approvable flight plan. Meteoric has identified the right measurable system, but it has not yet measured it. The claimed 10–30% maximum annual generation uplift is modeled, while the only physical result is 13% dissipation of an artificial cloud in an unspecified chamber. Without field-measured irradiance, drone-hours per MW, or a customer price, the governing belief remains plausible but below my primary gate.

The why-now is stronger. Electric-drone operation at $30–60 per hour versus more than $2,000 for conventional aircraft is a potentially material change in delivery cost, and rapid 15-day AI forecasts could improve mission timing. This is the most compelling part of the company: technology may convert weather modification from occasional aircraft campaigns into repeatable site-level operations. But fleets of tens to hundreds of drones could consume that advantage through fleet capital, maintenance, supervision, and regulatory overhead. Meteoric has not yet shown that its cost reduction survives at system scale or that recovered electricity is worth more than the treatment.

There is no product-market pull yet. No solar operator is named, and there is no paid pilot, LOI, repeat use, revenue, or independently observed capacity constraint. Press coverage and three Hacker News points are not demand. The chamber prototype is legitimate technical evidence, but it cannot also count as customer evidence. My 36-month base case is that Meteoric remains below $1 million in solar-farm ARR and pivots toward a more localized fog-clearing market, such as airports or industrial sites, where treatment boundaries and customer value may be easier to measure.

The strongest bull case is real. Mete Karslioglu worked on cloud-reflectivity nozzles and atmospheric instruments; Eric Nilsson worked on microfluidics, droplet removal, controls, and atmospheric equipment. They are unusually aligned with the physics and have already produced a measurable effect. The direct competitive set is also sparse: Rainmaker targets precipitation, Reflect Orbital supplies sunlight from orbit, and conventional operators use chemical seeding. If the $30–60 operating-cost claim holds in the field, Meteoric could be reconstructing physical-market economics rather than merely attaching software to an incumbent service.

I cannot grant that exception yet. There is no natural-cloud validation, published repeatability, proprietary dataset, permit position, or demonstrated closed-loop fleet. U.S. operations at 1–5 km with many drones run directly into visual-line-of-sight, one-drone-per-pilot, altitude, and waiver constraints. Rainmaker already possesses drones, radar, forecasting, and mission-validation infrastructure, so an undisclosed treatment mechanism without protected evidence is not yet a structural moat. The hurricane ambition makes the underwriting less credible rather than larger because the World Meteorological Organization reports no generally accepted evidence that tropical cyclones can be modified.

I would pass today and reconsider on one verifiable flip condition: a paid, independently measured solar-farm deployment showing at least 5% net generation uplift with all-in treatment cost below 50% of the recovered electricity’s value under an approved operating plan. That result would simultaneously validate the controlling variable, the economic discontinuity, customer pull, and regulatory feasibility. Until then, this is a domain-credible scientific prototype searching for proof that the market will amplify it.
