# Daqstra -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Physical R&D teams should describe a test objective, let software configure heterogeneous equipment, execute guarded sequences, and preserve every calibration, command, and result as reusable institutional memory. Daqstra has found a credible leak from that future: its software already issues redlines, aborts, and valve commands across NI, Siemens, Modbus, OPC-UA, and custom-TCP systems during live rocket-engine testing. What remains underdeveloped is the causal bridge from today’s operator-authored orchestration to genuinely autonomous experimentation. I can see the destination, but the dossier does not yet name the intervening technical unlocks.

The founders are the strongest part of this bet. Darell Chua and Anchit Kumar met while building liquid rocket engines, co-authored a 2025 AIAA paper on distributed data acquisition, and accumulated more than ten Mojave test campaigns before packaging the pain into a company. That is not résumé-shaped founder-market fit. It is operating scar tissue converted into a product specification. Chua’s systems work at Rivian and Kumar’s autonomy and robot-safety research also cover both sides of the problem: unreliable physical interfaces and the software intelligence operating above them.

The broken rule is vendor captivity. Engineers repeatedly reconnect hardware, scripts, controllers, and files because each supplier owns one island of the test stack. Daqstra returns control to the test team through a common API and version-controlled procedures. The signed energy pilot’s target of more than $100,000 in annual customer savings begins to price that rent, although it does not reveal how much Daqstra can capture or whether the savings repeat across installations.

The behavior is unusually costly for a pre-seed product. Active aerospace programs reportedly use it day to day, multiple rocket-engine stands have deployed it, and the March 2026 SEDS UCSD–Dispatch campaign put it inside a live heat-shield static fire. A team allowing new software to participate in abort logic is risking equipment, schedules, and reputation—not clicking through a narrated demo. Public verification remains narrow, but this is real product immersion in an environment where failure is loud.

The bear case stings. Nominal has raised $182.5 million, Sift $67 million, and instrumentation vendors can move upward from hardware into orchestration. Supporting every bespoke controller may turn Daqstra into a three-person integration consultancy, while a safety incident could stop adoption before accumulated test history creates switching costs. There is no disclosed structural barrier, revenue, retention, or clear accountable leader between the two named founders. Those facts keep this from being a check-without-questions company.

Still, “AI-native” is not the reason I would invest. The controlled bidirectional integrations, live procedures, and calibration lineage sit beneath the fashionable autonomy story and can serve aerospace, energy, robotics, and drones. This matches my concealed-infrastructure pattern more than a consensus AI wrapper. I would write a measured angel check, with one verifiable flip condition: pass if, within twelve months, three paying customers cannot independently run and renew multi-vendor closed-loop deployments without routine founder intervention.
