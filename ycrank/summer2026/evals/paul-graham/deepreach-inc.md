# DeepReach Inc. -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that DeepReach calls itself a real-world data network. Customers rejected controlled, repetitive environments, so the founders built a different collection machine: wearable stereo devices operated by local data businesses with workplace access. In three months, that workaround produced 475 deployed devices, more than 100 partners across seven countries, and hundreds of thousands of clips. The dossier does not give the exact day each rejection caused each product change, so the causal history is incomplete. But this is still unusually concrete evidence that Tim Li and Chris Liu respond to an obstacle by building around it.

The artifact is real, though its quality is less proven than its scale. DeepReach manufactured hardware, deployed it across a distributed network, and assembled software, payments, support, and quality control sufficient to collect 150,000 professional-skill demonstrations. That is a substantial technical and operational proof before permission. Yet all performance figures are company-reported. There is no independent benchmark showing that the stereo data improves a robot policy, no disclosed customer acceptance rate, and no evidence that compliance verification survives inspection. The system works as a collection engine; whether it produces uniquely valuable training data remains open.

The narrow well is frontier AI laboratories and robotics companies that need varied workplace demonstrations now. Several million dollars in signed contracts is far stronger than a waitlist, and collection volume roughly doubling month over month suggests the machines are being used rather than displayed. But the customers are unnamed, contract concentration is undisclosed, and signed value is not recognized revenue or repeat purchasing. I believe the pull is real. I do not yet know whether customers are pulling DeepReach’s particular data substrate back repeatedly or merely buying scarce data from any available supplier.

The strongest part of the case is the schlep. Li previously built a cross-border annotation network reaching roughly 70,000 people, while Liu worked on computer vision and core ML systems. DeepReach therefore looks less like an “AI for robotics” brainstorm than a recombination of problems the founders already know: recruiting distributed operators, manufacturing capture devices, obtaining workplace access, enforcing heterogeneous customer specifications, and moving payments across seven countries. These are ugly burdens, and the 10-year founder relationship makes them more credible as a team undertaking than six impressive résumés assembled for an application.

The bear case stings. Scale AI already collects more than 1,000 demonstration hours per day, several smaller companies offer overlapping egocentric data, and synthetic trajectory systems could reduce the value of human collection. DeepReach has also described itself as an evaluation platform, deployment company, data refinery, and collection network within one year. That could be running-back behavior, but the dossier never identifies the falsifying observation behind any change, so I give no extra credit for adaptability. Nor does the current network establish a moat without data exclusivity, partner retention, repeat purchases, or auditable governance. A large competitor could subsidize the same supply, and a few frontier labs could account for nearly all the contracts.

I would nevertheless invest. The operational gate is passed: the founders have shipped an unusually large workaround, built a measured system, and found buyers with real budgets. The company also matches the revealed pattern I care about most—an impressive substrate assembled before the round—without triggering the usual launch-day, premature-hiring, or single-partner red flags. My verifiable flip condition is straightforward: if, within twelve months, DeepReach cannot show at least three customers renewing paid data orders after measuring delivered-data acceptance or downstream model improvement, I would reverse the decision. Without renewal, this is a temporary labor market. With it, the network could become infrastructure.
