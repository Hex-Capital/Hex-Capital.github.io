# Whitespace -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have earned the right to work on this problem. Both grew up in wholesale-distribution families; Alex Tung later advised manufacturers and distributors on AI and helped Ankar reach seven-figure ARR within a year, while Leon Yao built logistics-optimization products at InstaDeep. Two named deployments—Kingfisher Direct and Warwick—turn that history into action rather than résumé signaling. The reported two-to-four-week implementations and live order-intake, customer-service, and inventory workflows suggest the team can cross the difficult boundary from demo to operating system.

The weakness is what survives after a competitor copies the product. Customer-specific knowledge bases and correction histories can create switching costs, but Whitespace explicitly keeps learning inside each customer rather than compounding it across the network. Plato, Proton.ai, Canals, and Conexiom already cover overlapping ERP, order, inventory, sales, and accounting workflows. If every deployment begins as another bespoke integration project, the knowledge compounds for the customer while labor compounds for Whitespace. That is a useful software business, not yet permissionless leverage.

The bull case stings because the product appears to create direct economic value. Whitespace reports productivity gains above 80%, three-month payback, and two named customers using agents in production. Usage- and workflow-based pricing aligns revenue with work automated, while expansion from order intake into procurement, finance, planning, and replenishment gives the company room to grow inside each distributor. At pre-seed, this is materially stronger than an unpaid prototype or an LOI.

But breadth is not the same as a large adjacent market, and customer value is not a network effect. The dossier supplies no market sizing, transaction volume, realized revenue, retention, or evidence that one distributor improves the product or distribution for another. The company resembles my unstated pattern of moving inefficient offline activity into software, but it does not yet make fragmented participants discoverable, transactable, or mutually useful. No known exception rescues that missing mechanism: the product has adoption, but no demonstrated self-reinforcing behavior or unusually durable technical substrate. I also see no integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-stability red flag; the concern is structural, not personal.

My 36-month base case is a credible vertical-AI company reaching roughly $3–7 million ARR, but with implementation and integration work growing alongside revenue and preventing venture-scale compounding. The single condition that flips me to invest is evidence that reusable learning from earlier deployments—without exposing customer data—measurably reduces implementation time and error rates across a later cohort. Then the company would be building an asset competitors cannot reproduce merely by copying the interface.

I would not write the personal check today. The founders and early deployments keep Whitespace out of the pass category, but the current advantage belongs mainly to execution speed in a crowded market. Code can be copied. Compounding knowledge cannot. Whitespace has shown the first and only hypothesized the second.
