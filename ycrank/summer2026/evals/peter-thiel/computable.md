# Computable -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Yuan Song’s Jump Trading history gives **Computable** a finance-native founder profile, while the July 31 H100 auction reveals no definite plan beyond the first market event. That pairing is **Computable’s** signature: unusually relevant market-design pedigree attached to an indefinite roadmap. The founders may understand how exchanges work; the dossier does not show that they know why this exchange must win.

**Affirm** is the high rung on founder lineage: Max Levchin had already built financial infrastructure to global scale before attacking credit. **Computable** shares the mechanism only weakly—Jump Trading and Coinbase backgrounds plausibly confer knowledge of auctions, market making, and settlement. Yet Yuan Song’s registration at Jump ended in August 2023, the second founder remains unnamed, and neither founder has a verified shipped-to-scale product. The unstated PayPal Mafia pattern matters here: **Computable** has institutional adjacency, not the operator re-up that made Affirm decisive.

The generic “AI for X” pass is the low rung on definite planning because customer iteration substitutes for an architectural destination. **Computable** avoids that exact cliché, but the dossier offers one H100 cluster, one auction closing July 31, and no two-to-five-year milestones for supplier depth, standardized contracts, clearing infrastructure, or regulatory authorization. **Computable** has designed an experiment, not disclosed a definite future. One auction is an event; a monopoly requires a plan.

SF Compute’s resale orderbook and Ornn’s planned exchange-listed futures expose the weakness in **Computable’s** secret. Over the next 18 months, **Computable’s** sealed weekly auctions and standing buyback quotes could compound into price history and liquidity, but only if each new supplier improves execution for every buyer. Without that flywheel, the mechanism is merely a feature that SF Compute can copy—and already previews. The secret is potentially that GPU capacity should trade as dated inventory rather than rent as cloud service, but Computable has not named why SF Compute, Ornn, or Compute Exchange is structurally unable to reach the same conclusion.

Computable’s standing buyback quote would justify a monopoly score one tier higher if three conditions proved true: independent suppliers deposited meaningful capacity, repeat buyers treated its clearing price as the reference price, and third-party market makers—not **Computable’s** balance sheet—funded liquidity. The dossier instead identifies one cluster, no transaction volume, and no disclosed reserves. Network effects can create monopoly pricing power, as Facebook demonstrated, but subsidized liquidity can also counterfeit a network until the subsidy ends. **Computable** has the outline of an exchange moat and none of the evidence that the outline is becoming a market.

**Palantir** turned regulation into a moat because government integration, trust, and procurement complexity excluded ordinary software competitors. **Computable** faces commodities or derivatives questions, but regulatory exposure is not yet regulatory advantage: Ornn is pursuing exchange-listed futures through ICE while Computable discloses no classification, license, or regulated partner. **Computable** touches atoms through scarce H100 capacity, yet owns no hardware and names no exclusive supply. The company inherits the liabilities of both finance and infrastructure before demonstrating the barriers of either.

Computable’s first auction covers August 2026 through January 2027; by July 2029, **Computable** will have pivoted from a liquid GPU-hours exchange into software-assisted bilateral capacity brokerage with under $3 million ARR, or will be inactive. Compute contracts vary by chip, networking, location, availability, and service terms, so **Computable’s** standardized liquidity thesis will yield to negotiated procurement unless supplier density becomes much greater than the dossier suggests.

Ornn’s $33 million financing and Computable’s Jump-and-Coinbase lineage form the strongest opposing case: financialization is arriving, specialist founders may build the reference price before clouds recognize its value, and the **Lyft** exception shows that a network market can deserve investment without immediate monopoly. The case stings because **Computable’s** standing buyback quote directly attacks the cold-start problem. It still loses today: SF Compute already operates thousands of GPUs and supports resale, Ornn has the regulatory route, and **Computable** discloses neither completed volume nor multi-supplier depth. I would reverse the verdict if Computable clears at least $10 million of annualized gross capacity across three independent suppliers, with more than half of buyers repeating and third-party capital funding the buyback book.
