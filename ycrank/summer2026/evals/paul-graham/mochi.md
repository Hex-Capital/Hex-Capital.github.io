# Mochi -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important fact is the missing one: what broke, what the founders did next, and how quickly it improved. Users report failed generations consuming gems and roughly $5 buying only two or three short scenes. Offering 100 introductory gems softens the damage, but it is not evidence that Robert Qiu and Michael Ninh diagnosed the failure and shipped a workaround. The dossier shows impressive outcomes without the causal episode I use to distinguish resourcefulness from favorable conditions. Founder credentials cannot fill that gap.

The product evidence is much stronger. Mochi is a functioning creation and viewing system with more than 100 series, 3,000 creators, and over 500 episodes produced daily. The team claims it can turn a concept into a 30-episode series in one day. App-store evidence—10,000-plus Android downloads, roughly 1,420 Google Play reviews, and 479 iOS ratings—makes this more than a demonstration assembled for investors. It is a measured technical artifact in ordinary users’ hands.

There is also a narrow well of demand. Anime fans are not merely joining a waitlist: 100,000-plus users are watching, creators are publishing daily, and the product has accumulated 300 million organic views and 900,000 social followers. This matches one of my quieter patterns: technical users adopting a programmable interface before its category has settled. But views are the loosest form of pull. Mochi provides no viewer retention, creator-cohort retention, paid conversion, or revenue despite selling $4.99–$99.99 gem packs and subscriptions costing as much as $19.99 weekly. The difference between a company and an attention event is whether the same people keep coming back.

The bull case is that organic distribution and abundant creator supply form a loop before competitors can buy one. That case could be much larger than it first appears. The bear case stings because AniShort already combines vertical anime with AI creation, YouArt reports more than $120,000 in monthly revenue, and Mochi has disclosed neither proprietary technology nor switching costs. Generation failures make each creator experiment expensive, while copyright moderation adds a schlep the terms of service do not solve. A market where established microdrama companies spend up to 90% of their budgets on marketing can turn 300 million views into an acquisition anecdote unless retention carries the load.

The founders are plausible for the problem. Qiu previously built a game reaching $200,000 in monthly revenue and lofi.town to 30,000 users; Ninh managed consumer growth and hundreds of video creatives while watching more than 400 anime series. This is relevant experience, but the dossier does not document the personal collision that produced Mochi or an evidence-driven route change. Missing cofounder history is not itself disqualifying because the working product supplies the kind of concrete exception that can override relationship uncertainty. I also see none of the usual launch-day, large-partner, or premature-sales-hiring red flags. Zero open jobs is mildly reassuring evidence that the founders remain inside the product.

My 36-month base case is that Mochi reaches $3–8 million in annualized revenue from gems and subscriptions but remains a volatile anime niche rather than becoming the dominant creator network. I am not writing the check yet because the people-first gate remains unproven, not because the market looks too small. One result would flip me: a dated account showing the founders reduced failed-generation rates below 2% and that the affected creator cohort subsequently retained at least 25% of creators through week eight. That would reveal both what they do when blocked and whether the product pulls users back after the novelty wears off.
