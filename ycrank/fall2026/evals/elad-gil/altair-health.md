# Altair Health -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Altair Health’s opportunity is to convert medical billing labor into a scalable product for independent practices. The controlling variable is the human labor required per successfully collected claim, including policy maintenance and exception handling. If that falls as claim volume grows, Altair can capture an existing services budget with better economics. If it remains roughly constant, the company becomes a billing operation with useful automation. The dossier establishes the proposed mechanism but provides no operating evidence that distinguishes those outcomes. I would pass today.

The strongest bull case is that Altair already takes responsibility for the work. Agents propose billing actions, a theorem prover checks formalized insurer policies, and human billers handle exceptions ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/altair-health)). This architecture fits my preference for owning institutional workflows: better models could complete more actions while verification and escalation remain part of the service. But proving compliance with an encoded policy does not prove that the policy was translated correctly or remains current. The decisive engineering burden may sit upstream of the prover. Altair has described a credible architecture; it has not demonstrated the coverage or maintenance economics that would make that architecture commercially decisive.

There is meaningful early pull. In October 2026, Jad reported that an unnamed substance-use-treatment practice uses Altair to collect several hundred thousand dollars monthly from insurers. These are client collections, not Altair revenue, but responsibility for recurring cash flow is more informative than a waitlist. The reported year from initial conversation to full deployment also suggests a substantial implementation burden, although one deployment cannot establish the normal sales or onboarding cycle. The counterargument to my pass is strong: a practice has apparently entrusted the team with a consequential operating function, and that deployment could supply the feedback needed to automate progressively more work.

Market structure makes the economics especially important. The broader outsourcing market is estimated at $7.73 billion in 2026, but that does not establish Altair’s reachable independent-practice market. Candid Health has raised over $219 million, Adonis over $95 million, and Anomaly already supplies payer intelligence. These are overlapping competitors with resources to pursue automation. Altair’s free EHR could eventually create reusable distribution, but a waitlist does not establish that channel. Similarly, Medicare’s January 2026 WISeR launch supplies a concrete authorization use case without proving lower delivery costs. The service-heavy margin red flag remains unresolved; accumulated payer histories have no demonstrated reproduction-cost advantage.

Aouss’s enterprise-sales experience and Jad’s healthcare-ML background support execution, but neither establishes prior founder learning or costly personal commitment. Their credentials also do not justify the rare-research-team exception to weak commercial evidence. Altair fits the pattern of using technology to perform institutional labor, yet the evidence does not establish the economic transformation that makes that pattern investable. My single flip condition is an independently auditable, 90-day cohort across at least three practices showing contribution margins above 50% after human billing, policy maintenance, and amortized onboarding costs, while maintaining or improving denial-adjusted collections against each practice’s prior baseline. That result would directly test whether Altair is building a scalable product inside this services market.
