# Adalat AI -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Adalat AI has secured something genuinely scarce: permission to operate inside courts. But what would my money own? The dossier describes a nonprofit delivering software free under documented pro bono agreements. The $500,000 Agency Fund grant is philanthropic funding, not evidence of an equity price or a claim on future profits. Proposed post-money valuation, competing term sheets, and availability of a first scarce investment check are all unknown. I cannot underwrite a return merely because the institution being built is valuable.

The strongest case against passing is that Adalat has already crossed an institutional boundary most software companies never cross. Oxford documents more than 4,000 courts across nine states as of March 2026; Kerala directed courts to use the product primarily for witness depositions effective November 1, 2025. A magistrate reports handling five to six witnesses daily versus two to three previously. Those are meaningful operating signals. Court agreements, training, and embedded procedure fit my preference for proprietary access between difficult systems. I give that access substantial credit even though adoption of free software does not establish willingness to pay. The 19,326 LinkedIn followers add little to this argument.

The founders also have credible access to both sides of the bridge. Saxena has practiced law and clerked at the Supreme Court; Bhattacharya brings multilingual NLP research and founding engineering experience at Enterpret. Together they developed an MVP for court demonstrations. That is a stronger combination than credentials alone. The question is who captures the economics of their work. Courts currently receive the software without commercial fees, while TERES sells managed hearing services and jhana offers commercial court workflows. An adjacent paid market exists; Adalat has not demonstrated that its own institutional relationships can support one. I cannot assume court agreements confer exclusive access or ownership of sensitive judicial data.

This is where the exception case breaks. Demonstrated adoption can justify overlooking missing financing information, and a working AI business can deserve credit despite commoditizing models. Here, however, the receipts establish institutional acceptance rather than a functioning commercial base. An unconventional security would still need a lawful claim on economic proceeds; changing the paperwork cannot manufacture those proceeds. The nonprofit mission is explicit, so I would not accuse the founders of hiding incompatible objectives. I simply lack an investable return mechanism. My 36-month prediction is that Adalat remains a grant-supported nonprofit providing its Indian court software without recurring court-license revenue.

There is also no documented capital-funded experiment with a metric and deadline, so I use the missing-evidence default rather than inventing a venture moment from nationwide expansion. The September 2026 production-administration compromise adds a concrete operating burden, although the reported remediation and absence of reported confidential court-user data access matter. Likewise, claimed 90%+ recognition accuracy does not establish economical handling of evidentiary errors; signed deposition review remains part of the workflow. These risks make delivery economics consequential, but the decisive issue is still the absent financial claim. I pass on a personal angel investment. One verifiable development would reopen it: a legally investable commercial subsidiary with signed, positive-contribution paid contracts. That would give me an actual business to price.
