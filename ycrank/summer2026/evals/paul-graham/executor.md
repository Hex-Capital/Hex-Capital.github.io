# Executor -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The strongest fact is not that Executor sits in a fashionable market. It is that one founder has already built a substantial working system before asking permission. The repository has 2,580 commits, 3,000 stars, 230 forks, about 14,000 npm downloads a week, and roughly 3,000 local users. The software ingests MCP servers, OpenAPI specifications, GraphQL APIs, and custom integrations, then resolves credentials outside the sandbox and applies allow, approval, or block policies. This is a measured technical proof, not a diagram.

But the dossier omits the fact I would ordinarily examine first. What specifically broke for Rhys Sullivan? What did he try, how quickly did he change it, and what happened afterward? His experience at Vercel and Microsoft and his previous work growing Answer Overflow to 1.5 million monthly users make it plausible that he encountered integration problems directly. Plausibility is not evidence of resourcefulness under resistance. The 2026 MCP protocol change creates a real obstacle, but the dossier does not show Executor encountering it and shipping a workaround on a dated timetable.

The users are real enough to distinguish this from an invented “AI infrastructure” pitch, but their pull is still ambiguous. Three thousand local users and 14,000 weekly downloads demonstrate adoption of the crude product. They do not identify a narrow group returning repeatedly, entrusting production credentials to it, or paying $150 per organization per month. Rhys also has 57,245 X followers, so distribution can produce an impressive initial spike without proving retention. The important question is not who installed Executor, but who would complain if it disappeared.

The bull case is quite strong. Executor matches an unstated pattern I repeatedly like: a programmable interface that technical users can appropriate across systems, accompanied by a concrete pre-round proof spike. Its local CLI, desktop application, self-hosted server, and cloud service could become the common execution layer between many agents and many tools. The ugly work—OAuth, credential isolation, protocol compatibility, approvals, and audit controls—is exactly where an infrastructure company can acquire depth.

The same mechanism creates the bear case. Microsoft offers an MIT-licensed MCP gateway, Zapier exposes more than 9,000 applications and 30,000 actions, and funded competitors already own large catalogs or authentication infrastructure. Executor’s own MIT-licensed core may satisfy developers while leaving little reason to buy the hosted product. There is no revenue, retention, migration cost, or moat evidence yet. The public launch is therefore not distribution, and the absence of a named customer workflow means no known exception overrides the failed resourcefulness gate. A working product is necessary here, but crowded developer infrastructure rewards the founder who learns fastest from resistance, not merely the founder who writes the most code.

I would not pass, because the artifact and adoption are too substantial. I would not invest yet, because I cannot tell whether this is a tool developers try or infrastructure organizations depend on. The verifiable flip condition is ten paying organizations using Executor weekly for eight consecutive weeks, plus one dated account of a customer or protocol blocker that Sullivan personally converted into a shipped change within days. That would turn the download spike into a narrow well and reveal the running back behind the repository.
