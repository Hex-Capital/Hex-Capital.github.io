# Lark -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI agents can now connect distributor communications to ERP actions, making purchasing departments a buyer for automated work; the economic variable is how much reliable purchasing activity Lark can deliver per hour of implementation and supervision. The company identifies a concrete mechanism: capture the operating rules held by experienced employees, combine them with inventory data, and execute approved purchases. That is a credible market thesis. The dossier does not yet establish the reliability or deployment economics needed to make it repeatable across distributors.

Interline Hardware’s approximately 60 hours saved monthly across 60,000-plus inventory items gives the technology shift an economic consequence. I give that more weight than the separate, unnamed customer’s claimed 50 hours saved weekly, although both appear through company-controlled publications. The savings establish useful work being removed. They do not establish Lark’s share of the value: subscription prices, implementation costs, and ongoing support requirements remain undisclosed. A distributor can receive an attractive return while its software vendor builds a labor-intensive business.

The strongest evidence is what Lark already does inside the workflow. Customer-specific ERP integration, approved writes, purchasing rules, logs, and pause controls put the product beyond generating recommendations. Better models should improve its ability to interpret communications and handle exceptions while the integration retains responsibility for execution. This fits my preference for owning operational work. It does not yet establish a moat: the dossier documents neither exclusive access nor measured switching costs, and a competitor could potentially reproduce the mappings with sufficient implementation effort.

There is also a meaningful early customer signal. Interline is named and reportedly reached first use less than a month after kickoff. That is more informative than registrations or an undated pilot announcement, although the dossier provides no paid expansion or sustained usage history. Jason’s prior Datost experience, including the attributed 400K ARR within eight weeks, supplies evidence of previous startup execution; it is not Lark revenue. Michael leaving NYU supplies a concrete commitment signal. Neither fact substitutes for evidence that distributor adoption can compound.

The strongest objection is that Lark becomes an implementation company competing against better-funded procurement platforms. Its FAQ describes setup generally taking one to two months, and Didero already executes overlapping procurement workflows with $37 million raised. This is precisely where my willingness to fund software entering physical industries needs discipline: automating a distributor’s labor does not automatically give the vendor software margins. Lark’s live ERP actions answer the thin-feature objection, but the five advertised agent categories do not prove reusable distribution or inexpensive expansion into adjacent work. I would concentrate on purchasing until that mechanism is demonstrated.

I would monitor Lark rather than write a personal check today. The workflow evidence is stronger than the evidence for repeatable delivery economics, and that distinction controls my decision. My flip condition is a five-distributor paying cohort demonstrating at least 70% gross margin over its first six paid months, including all attributable onboarding, model, and support costs. That is my underwriting threshold, not a reported result. Meeting it would show that Lark can retain the economic benefit of automation as customers multiply.
