# Elimentary -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Elimentary has evidence of a substantial labor multiplier. TresVista’s October 19 deployment reduced roughly 20 hours of filing checks to under 30 minutes of agent work plus an hour of human sign-off: approximately 13x faster end to end. That deserves attention. But the investment question is how much expensive expertise this mechanism can replace across private capital. The dossier establishes a valuable workflow improvement; it does not quantify the broader spending pool or population served. I cannot yet price an enormous consequence against the possibility of losing my entire check.

The strongest case for investing is that Elimentary already operates inside the workflow. TresVista signed a multi-year partnership, more than 25 agents are reportedly in production, and an unnamed $8B-plus fund reports covenant testing falling from four hours to 15 minutes per portfolio company. A prepared semantic layer, financial tools, and clause-level provenance give the agents more structure than an open-ended assistant. These are meaningful application-level results. However, elapsed time measures only part of the technical discontinuity. Elimentary has not established comparable correctness against human analysts or superior performance against Hypercore, Finley, and Cascade. Those competitors already combine financial infrastructure, automation, and oversight.

The largest kill risk is whether review can remain cheap while catching financially material mistakes. Elimentary’s own benchmark reports 36 filing-contradicting statements despite its prepared data layer. Publishing that result is useful evidence of testing; without a denominator, severity breakdown, or post-review escape rate, it does not establish operational reliability. Human sign-off could be an economical control, as TresVista’s reported timing suggests. It could also become expensive exception handling when agreements change. The dossier supplies no costed experiment with a numerical acceptance threshold and a stop-or-change response. I would spend the next dollars resolving that uncertainty.

Gorjilla, Saini, and Sarda bring relevant credit, engineering, and automation experience. Their prior products’ $1B loan books establish exposure to financial operations, not Elimentary’s scale. Their backgrounds are complementary, but the record contains no specific instance of revising a material belief or recruiting an exceptional person to own a missing capability. Sarda’s debt-collection background does not make this business debt collection, a category I avoid. Nor does customized tooling alone prove the integration-services red flag. The unresolved question is whether deployments become repeatable software while institutional knowledge remains with clients. The reported $150,000 annual customer savings demonstrates value, but undisclosed pricing, implementation effort, and support costs leave the supplier’s economics unproven.

I pass with my own money. Execution-heavy fintech is an actual investment pattern I must take seriously, and the TresVista relationship makes the bull case uncomfortable to dismiss: distribution and repeatable deployments could build a substantial company without novel foundational models. My exceptions permit backing an extraordinary instigator against a giant demonstrated value pool, or buying an option on an overwhelming societal consequence before the business model exists. Elimentary has not supplied that evidence yet. One verifiable result would reopen my decision: an independently audited deployment across three unrelated funds showing at least 10x lower total operating cost—including implementation amortization, review, and corrections—with no material covenant errors escaping review over a full reporting cycle. That would establish that the multiplier survives the work customers actually pay to get right.
