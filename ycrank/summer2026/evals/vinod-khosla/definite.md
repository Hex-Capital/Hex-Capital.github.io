# Definite -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Definite can plausibly turn weeks of regulatory reporting into hours, but it has not shown that this changes a consequential cost pool by orders of magnitude. The dossier identifies a $928.9 million financial-close software market, not the total labor, error, or regulatory-capital burden that its system could eliminate. This is useful enterprise software. It is not yet a 100x consequence case.

The strongest evidence is technical. Language models interpret documents; deterministic code performs calculations and validation; outputs retain evidence lineage for human approval. Definite also reports that its verification engine paired with DeepSeek V3 beats GPT-5 on FinBalance at roughly 50% lower cost and exceeds published FinAuditing results. That is more substantive than another accounting chat interface. But the benchmark is company-reported, independently unreplicated, and supplies neither the accuracy delta nor a production error rate. Numeric, Stacks, and Basis can attack the same workflow with $38 million, $35 million, and $137.6 million of disclosed financing. A laboratory benchmark without a deployment benchmark is an invitation, not a moat.

The founders have legitimate proximity to the problem. Gurshabd Singh Varaich handled wallet-policy and approval controls at BitGo; Mazin Al-Ani saw regulatory-number rechecking at Optiver and built finance agents at Boosted.ai; Farhan Ur Rehman brings production machine-learning experience from Meta. The movement from Ledgix to Bylaw, then bank reporting, and now broader reconciliations could demonstrate tactical flexibility. It could equally mean they have not found the narrow workflow whose repeated use compounds into proprietary mappings, policies, and evidence relationships. The dossier documents the changes, not the customer evidence that caused them.

The largest kill risk is whether messy institutional data can be normalized accurately enough for finance teams to trust recurring controls. Definite’s own terms say stale or missing feeds produce “Cannot verify” results and halt dependent lines. Yet there is no named experiment, pass/fail threshold, deployment count, or budget for resolving that risk. Encryption, MFA, access logging, and read-only connections are table stakes; unnamed certifications and assessments available only under NDA do not clear regulated procurement. Capital should first prove that one control survives real data across several institutions, not finance a broader catalogue of agents.

The bull case stings. This is an execution-heavy fintech team that lived inside expensive workflows, built a technically sensible boundary between probabilistic interpretation and deterministic math, and may accumulate customer-specific control graphs that become difficult to replace. That is the known exception to my science-first bias. But the exception requires evidence of an expansive financial platform or unusually strong adoption. Definite currently offers 170 LinkedIn followers, 11 YC Launch votes, undisclosed order-form pricing, and no identified production customer. The pivot history also activates my red flag: expanding product scope before publicly resolving the central reliability and procurement risks.

I pass today. My one verifiable flip condition is three named regulated institutions running the same control in production for two consecutive quarters, accompanied by an independently reproducible result showing at least a 5x reduction in analyst hours without a higher material-error rate. That would convert the reported benchmark into risk removal, validate the learning-loop moat, and demonstrate economics that can beat human delivery rather than merely underprice GPT-5.
