# Aerogen Systems -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The dossier names difficult engineering, but not the obstacle I care about most. Aerogen Systems has built multiple Talos generations and moved through three applications, yet it never says what broke, what Vogel and Lavery did next, or how quickly their workaround changed a result. Two years of research and $500,000 of R&D prove sustained effort. They do not by themselves prove relentless resourcefulness. On my controlling founder test, the evidence is missing rather than bad.

The strongest fact is the machine. Talos reportedly reached 7N purity in 16 minutes and reduced VOC concentration below 1 ppb. The founders built it before semiconductor qualification, after co-authoring NASA’s 2024 VOC-removal report, and retained the photocatalytic reactor through multiple hardware generations. This is fierce-nerd behavior: difficult hardware reduced to a numerical threshold. But it remains a company-reported 30-minute PID run, not an independently reproduced result inside a named fab process. The proof is strong enough to distinguish Aerogen from a pre-product hardware proposal, but not strong enough to establish semiconductor yield impact.

This matches an important unstated pattern in my investments: young technical founders producing a concrete proof spike before the market has granted them permission. The tempting exception is Bindwell, where youth and commercial uncertainty were overridden by numerical performance and laboratory validation. The shared mechanism is hard technical work made inspectable. The material difference is that Aerogen’s result has not been independently validated for the application it now sells, and its semiconductor customer remains unnamed. So the exception does not yet clear the gate.

Who wants the crude product now? The answer is still “perhaps a semiconductor manufacturer.” Talos is reportedly being piloted inside semiconductor tooling, which deserves slightly more credit than a waitlist, but there is no named fab, repeated use, payment, expansion, or measured reduction in defects. Entegris already generated $3.197 billion in 2025 sales and supplies tool-level AMC products; Camfil offers qualified tool filters. Aerogen therefore must prove not merely cleaner air, but enough yield or consumables savings to justify qualification and integration. I see no launch-theater, premature-delegation, or hired-sales red flag. The emerging danger is the partnership red flag: if progress depends on an unnamed OEM or fab moving on its own timetable, the pilot can remain “in progress” indefinitely.

The route changes are promising but under-explained. Vogel and Lavery kept the underlying VOC-destruction technology while moving from spacecraft air purification to industrial ducts and then semiconductor tools. That looks like a running back preserving the goal while changing fields. Yet the dossier supplies no customer observation, failed hypothesis, or technical discovery that caused either turn, so I cannot tell whether this was compressed learning or successive searches for a legible market. Their NASA work gives the idea an authentic founder origin, and semiconductor integration and qualification are genuine schlep-heavy burdens. But they encountered air purification directly, not yet the fab’s purchasing and yield problem.

My 36-month forecast is that Aerogen will be selling enclosed-air purification into aerospace or general industrial systems rather than exceeding $1 million in semiconductor ARR. The bull case stings: this team has unusually relevant hardware experience, a working reactor, a measured threshold, and an enormous cost-sensitive industry where a tiny yield improvement matters. But those facts establish technical possibility, not the founder behavior or user pull required to survive qualification against entrenched suppliers. I would not write the check today. One verifiable event would flip me: a named fab encounters a qualification blocker, Aerogen ships the workaround within six weeks, independently sustains sub-1-ppb performance, and converts that pilot into a paid multi-tool deployment.
