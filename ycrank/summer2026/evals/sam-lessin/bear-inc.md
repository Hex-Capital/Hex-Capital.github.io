# Bear -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Bear has customers buying accounting work, which gives me something real to underwrite. But what am I paying, and what does my check prove? The dossier supplies no proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, or round-funded experiment. YC membership does not establish an auction; financing silence does not establish scarce capital either. I cannot turn a promising service into an attractive personal investment without knowing the price or the truth this round is supposed to reveal.

The strongest case is that Bear owns the ledger and delivers the accounting service. It can potentially capture the economic benefit when automation reduces work, rather than negotiating another software subscription with an accountant. More than 10 paying customers, according to Sami’s website, give that integrated workflow some purchase validation. The reported $80 million processed across dozens of Finnish companies establishes operating exposure, although it is accounting volume, not revenue. This is meaningful evidence for a pre-seed company. It does not yet establish that proprietary access caused those purchases, that customers stay, or that reviewed accounting data creates an advantage competitors cannot reproduce.

The founders make the operating thesis more credible. Riki built petabyte-scale infrastructure at Token Terminal; Sami also built financial-data infrastructure there and reports an earlier project acquisition. Those are shipped receipts beyond academic credentials. Their current accounting operation supplies direct exposure to the other side of the bridge. The remaining gap is demonstrated responsibility for reliable accounting delivery: the dossier identifies human accountant validation but does not establish the founders’ own history running that function. The finance and law backgrounds of the original founders help less when their current operating roles are unconfirmed.

Here is the objection to passing: Bear could become a perfectly good accounting business while investors argue about whether it is sufficiently novel. Fixed-price service, control of the ledger, and recurring customer work could let the team retain efficiency gains and grow from cash. That fits my preference for software reorganizing neglected operations. Human signoff also addresses the agent-error problem more seriously than assuming errors disappear. But deleting the next financing from Bear’s model leaves the decisive arithmetic blank: no service price, accountant review cost, gross margin, burn, or runway. The existing customers earn commercial credit; they cannot also stand in for evidence of profitability.

The exception case does not rescue the check today. Bear has actual purchases and application-specific evaluations, but no measured conversion funnel, retention, or cash economics strong enough to override the missing financing gates. Meanwhile, Balance and Skalar already combine agents with human professionals. Bear’s proprietary ledger provides control and introduces migration friction; its value depends on whether that control produces better service economics. Neither a crowded auction nor an equal-founder decision committee is established here, so I would not invent those red flags. The unresolved issue is paying for an AI efficiency option before seeing its underlying cash engine.

I pass at the documented terms—which are unknown. My one verifiable flip condition is a written, sub-$10 million post-money financing offer funding a six-month test of whether Bear can deliver three consecutive months of positive operating cash flow, including accountant review and normal team compensation, from its paying accounting service. That would connect price, a finite experiment, and survival without another round. The current dossier connects product to purchases; it has not yet connected my capital to an underwritable payoff.
