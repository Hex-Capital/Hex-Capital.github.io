# OpenRelay -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Prashant Patel helped build Amazon Bedrock and later orchestrated clusters exceeding 10,000 GPUs, yet OpenRelay discloses no customer usage or revenue. That pairing is OpenRelay’s signature: unusually credible shipping capacity attached to an unproven demand curve. OpenRelay has built substantially more than a pre-seed infrastructure mockup, but the distinction between “just working” and a false signal appears in workloads and dollars, not 89 documented API operations.

**Perplexity** is the high rung for founder shipping because Aravind Srinivas repeatedly turned conversations into working product within a week. OpenRelay shares the load-bearing mechanism: Patel productionized inference at AWS and Voltage Park, while Jaden Wang built HPC infrastructure at Voltage Park, TensorDock, and Heaviside Compute; OpenRelay then launched its dashboard, control-plane API, Stripe billing, and extensive documentation by June 2026. OpenRelay diverges from Perplexity because the dossier identifies no customer-feedback loop, public repository, or artifact shipped between investor conversations. The founders have demonstrated relevant construction, but not yet iterative velocity around market pull.

**Mistral** carried a low-rung revenue profile when I invested, but post-ChatGPT demand and founders who had shipped Llama at Meta created a legitimate Known Exception. OpenRelay also has no disclosed revenue or usage curve: $0.29-per-hour RTX 4090 pricing and live Stripe billing show the ability to charge, not evidence that anyone is buying. OpenRelay does not yet earn the Mistral override because production inference capacity is already served by RunPod at $120 million ARR, Lambda with more than $2 billion raised, and Vast.ai. For an AI infrastructure business, silence on workloads after launch remains the weakest part of the case.

The $4.37 billion GPU-as-a-service market is projected to grow 16% annually, but OpenRelay’s claimed catalyst—2025–2026 inference demand colliding with constrained data-center capacity—is inferred rather than demonstrated through a specific customer event or platform discontinuity. Over the next 18 months, the mechanism can compound if capacity scarcity pushes inference buyers toward heterogeneous hardware; each new OpenRelay provider would then improve availability and attract more demand. OpenRelay’s own FAQ undercuts that loop today because failed VM capacity is not automatically migrated, turning the supposed re-opening into a cheaper-capacity pitch with operational fragility. That places OpenRelay above a steady-state GPU reseller, but below the post-Ukraine discontinuity that made Anduril investable.

RunPod’s 500,000 developers and $120 million ARR define the counterfactual for OpenRelay’s index position. OpenRelay moves into the Stripe-like tier only if developers treat its scheduler as the default access layer across independent GPU suppliers—supported by published provider counts, repeat workloads, and automatic cross-provider failover—rather than selecting OpenRelay because an H100 happens to cost $2.60 per hour. With no node, customer, or utilization counts and no structural barrier identified, OpenRelay currently must win share against scaled infrastructure vendors instead of automatically compounding with inference demand.

**Color Genomics** worked because the business placed workflow infrastructure on top of commodity sequencing rather than betting on new chemistry. OpenRelay has the analogous shape: OpenRelay uses established GPUs, QEMU, VFIO, and IOMMU rather than requiring new semiconductor physics. The architecture still contains more than one execution miracle, however—reliable third-party supply, secure handling of customer workloads, and compatibility across NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, and CPU environments must all hold simultaneously. Those are known engineering problems rather than scientific breakthroughs, but OpenRelay has not yet isolated them into the single-miracle architecture I prefer.

The $0.18-per-hour entry price points toward my 36-month forecast: OpenRelay pivots from a general distributed GPU cloud into managed orchestration software for regional data centers and reaches $3 million–$8 million ARR by July 2029, without becoming a broad RunPod competitor. OpenRelay’s founders are equipped to build the control plane, while OpenRelay’s missing demand curve and unreliable cross-provider migration make owning a two-sided compute marketplace the weaker business. Selling scheduling, isolation, and utilization software to existing capacity owners better matches the founders’ demonstrated advantage.

RunPod’s $120 million ARR is the strongest objection because RunPod proves that developer-led GPU infrastructure can begin as fragmented capacity and become a substantial cloud; OpenRelay adds lower pricing, hardware agnosticism, included failover, and founders who operated 10,000-plus-GPU clusters. The Mistral exception also bites: capital intensity and absent early revenue can be overridden when domain-native founders skate for the puck after a category catalyst. OpenRelay still lacks the evidence that wins that argument—no disclosed customers, utilization, provider count, or revenue, while RunPod already spans 31 regions—and OpenRelay’s FAQ contradicts the promise of seamless failover. I would not write the angel check today; verified $1 million ARR growing above 20% month over month, with automatic migration across at least three independent providers, would flip the verdict.
