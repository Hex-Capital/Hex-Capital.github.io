# Tilion -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Tilion has shipped something technically credible. I still cannot price the investment: proposed post-money, competing term sheets, and whether my check supplies genuinely scarce capital are all unknown. The reported $500,000 YC investment answers none of those questions. I give financing silence the missing-evidence default; I do not turn YC membership into evidence of an auction. The other primary gate is also unresolved: the dossier names no finite experiment, deadline, or pass/fail result that this round buys.

The business question is who captures the value when Fortress gets a browser through a block. Tilion owns native Chromium patches and identity-generation code, but its customers supply network egress, competitors also patch browser engines, and earlier BSD releases remain available for forks. Maintaining access could become a valuable operating business. It could also become an endless maintenance obligation whose benefits flow to customers while competitors constrain the price. Public detection reports are useful engineering inputs; they do not establish the proprietary feedback pool I would want to own.

The strongest objection to passing is that these founders have already cleared a substantial execution hurdle. Fortress reports 100,000-plus installs and use by engineers at 170-plus companies. BrowserArena recorded 156 ms median lifecycle latency and 99 successful runs out of 100 on October 22. Those results make the possibility of a useful infrastructure supplier concrete. Arham maintains Fortress releases, and Luthra previously shipped Scribia, reportedly reaching 5,000 installs in two weeks. That earns founder credit beyond résumés. AWS employment and shared AI research add context, but do not yet establish operating depth on both sides of the browser-access problem. Tilion fits my translation-layer pattern technically; the commercial bridge remains unproven.

Those facts make the bull case sting: existing developer adoption could turn the September licensing restrictions into paid production demand with relatively little selling. But installs do not show that Tilion controls access that causes purchases, and a [$2,000/month license](https://github.com/tiliondev/fortress/blob/main/SUBSCRIPTION-TERMS.md) is an offer rather than a disclosed sale. My exceptions for technical adoption require harder external receipts, such as enterprise purchases or an acquisition offer; my AI exception requires demonstrated conversion and retention. Neither is documented here. Nor is there a prior exit and alternative financing structure that would justify bypassing the finite-experiment gate. This is genuine browser engineering, so I would not dismiss it as a generic AI wrapper. The relevant red flag is mistaking downloads and operations for an economic engine.

Delete the next financing from Tilion’s plan. At the announced $0.03/browser-hour, what remains after compute, support, and maintaining compatibility against changing defenses? The dossier supplies no margins, burn, or break-even plan, so economics receives its exact default rather than an assumed penalty for being early. Company testing showing 86.4% successful loads across 92 protected sites also makes maintenance cost a real underwriting question; the narrower BrowserArena result does not settle it. I would propose testing whether production renewals can fund that maintenance, but Tilion has not documented that as its capital-funded venture moment. **I pass today.** One verifiable flip condition would reopen the decision: a six-month production cohort showing renewed $2,000/month licenses whose collected cash covers the cohort’s compute, support, and attributable browser-maintenance costs. That would turn developer interest into evidence of a business capable of supporting itself.
