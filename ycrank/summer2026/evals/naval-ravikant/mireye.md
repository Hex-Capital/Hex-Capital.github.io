# Mireye -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Ansh Chokshi has shipped, sold, and exited before: Poker Pit reached $250K ARR in four months and was acquired. At Mireye, he manually identified 50 prospects, iterated outbound to a 10% response rate, and helped build a catalog of more than 300 fields. That is action-revealed knowledge. But it is knowledge of building AI software and finding customers, not yet deep, corroborated knowledge of GIS, land development, or physical infrastructure. The founder signal is real but only partially specific to this problem.

The product turns 85 fragmented sources into typed, provenance-linked fields with timestamps and confidence levels. More importantly, accepted field requests become available to later callers. That is the right compounding mechanism: one customer can subsidize the next customer’s answer. The catalog growing 20% week over week and a customer reporting 100-times-faster off-market land sourcing show useful output. They do not yet show a durable asset. The government data remains available to competitors, and Mireye supplies no request volume, repeat usage, retention, or evidence that its normalized catalog is costly to reproduce.

The strongest bull case is that Mireye converts fragmented offline information into permissionless infrastructure. Data-center developers are a sharp initial buyer, while the same location-resolution capability could extend into real estate, energy, insurance, and industrial workflows inside a $117 billion geospatial market. This matches an unstated pattern I value: making opaque physical-world supply searchable and price-discoverable. The material question is whether the expanding catalog creates leverage or merely creates an expanding maintenance obligation.

The bear case stings because Mapbox already provides global location data through hosted and self-hosted MCP access, while CARTO and Fused cover adjacent agentic workflows. If a competitor copies Mireye’s interface, the remaining advantage is a US-only catalog dependent on FEMA, USDA, counties, and other upstream refresh schedules. Without exclusive data or demonstrated cross-customer reuse, every new field may require proportional integration and support. That is a data-services company wearing an infrastructure multiple.

Mireye has explicit prices—$19 and $99 monthly plans plus paid overages—but no disclosed paid customer, realized revenue, or unit economics. The 100-times-faster testimonial proves utility, not value capture. Likewise, manual outbound proves founder energy, not proprietary distribution. I see no founder-integrity, artificial-deadline, or reassurance-seeking red flag in the dossier. But none of the known-exception mechanisms is established either: there is no observed self-reinforcing network, exceptional domain-specific recruiting evidence, or unusually capital-efficient product adoption that justifies overlooking the missing moat.

My base case is that Mireye narrows toward data-center site intelligence and reaches modest seven-figure ARR within 36 months, rather than becoming the horizontal infrastructure layer for physical-world agents. I would not invest yet. One verifiable flip condition would change that: evidence that at least 30 paying customers retain for six months while more than 25% of their answered queries use fields first requested by other customers. That would show the catalog compounding across principals instead of merely accumulating integrations.
