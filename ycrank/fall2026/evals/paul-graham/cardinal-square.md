# Cardinal Square -- Paul Graham Evaluation

What happened when Cardinal Square’s founders first tried to teach someone? That is the question I would start with. The dossier cannot answer it. It identifies a two-person team, but neither founder’s identity, prior work, nor response to a concrete obstacle. I cannot infer resourcefulness from admission to YC. Equally, inaccessible research URLs do not prove the founders are doing nothing. The founder score belongs at the missing-evidence default.

The proposed sequence makes sense: begin with online supplemental instruction, then attempt accredited schools. Supplementation could let the founders teach individual students, discover where automated instruction breaks, and build around those failures. But a planned sequence is not evidence of learning. Cardinal Square has no documented rejected hypothesis or product revision. Accreditation introduces substantial operational work; the dossier does not establish that the founders encountered this problem themselves or have begun doing that work.

The strongest case for investing is the combination of AI and educators. A teacher could notice when a student is merely obtaining answers and intervene before apparent progress becomes false confidence. The June 2025 mathematics study makes that distinction consequential: unrestricted AI assistance reduced subsequent unaided performance, while teacher-designed hints mitigated the harm. That gives Cardinal Square a useful problem to solve. It supplies no evidence that its implementation solves it. There is no inspectable product, measured learning result, or demonstrated technical substrate of the kind that would activate my preference for concrete proof before a financing round.

Who wants this badly enough to keep using a crude version? “Families buying supplemental education” is still too broad. The dossier names no returning students, paying parents, or particular learning difficulty around which demand has concentrated. Synthesis advertises $29 monthly or $119 annually for its family plan. Those prices make the educator component an important economic question: does human involvement produce enough additional learning for families to pay for it? Cardinal Square’s pricing, educator workload, and retention are unknown. The $12.61 billion online-tutoring estimate cannot answer that question.

I would pass on the evidence available. This is an unestablished founder-and-user loop, not an affirmative case that the founders are unsuitable. None of the documented exception mechanisms applies: there is no working prototype, unmistakable user pull, or validated result strong enough to replace the missing founder evidence. Nor does the dossier establish active red flags such as waiting for a partner or dismissing user feedback. My one condition for reconsideration is a documented founder-run pilot showing a specific teaching obstacle, a shipped correction within weeks, and families paying to return after students demonstrate improved unaided mastery. That would reveal whether the proposed school is growing out of something people need.
