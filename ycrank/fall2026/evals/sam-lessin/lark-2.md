# Lark -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Interline Hardware’s reported 60 hours saved monthly gives Lark something worth underwriting: a distributor letting a young company into its purchasing workflow across 60,000-plus inventory items. But what am I paying for that foothold, and what does my check prove? The dossier supplies neither proposed post-money valuation nor competing-term-sheet count, and no capital-funded experiment with a metric and deadline. Those are missing facts, not evidence of an auction. They still leave my two primary investment gates unresolved.

The strongest case for writing the check is that Lark is already crossing the boundary between software and how a distributor actually operates. Interline reports first use within a month of kickoff; the product incorporates purchasing rules and executes approved ERP actions. That fits my preference for translation layers inside neglected physical businesses. The embedded workflow earns meaningful credit for observed use. It does not yet establish that Lark owns proprietary access that causes subsequent purchases or referrals. Michael’s industry-conference outreach is sensible prospecting, but it supplies no evidence of an owned distribution channel.

Jason brings more than a résumé: his biography attributes 400K ARR in eight weeks to Datost, and his industrial supply-chain agent work at Traba is directly relevant. That revenue belongs to his previous company. It supports his ability to ship and sell; it tells me nothing about Lark’s cash generation. Michael’s reported organization of more than 10,000 volunteers adds operating experience, although neither biography establishes deep distributor purchasing ownership. Jason is identified as CEO, so I would not manufacture an equal-founder governance problem simply because the founders are brothers.

Here is the economic fork. Customer-specific mappings and operating rules could make each deployment more useful and harder to replace. They could also create an implementation business whose subscription revenue subsidizes perpetual integration and supervision. Lark’s FAQ says setup generally takes one to two months. Approval limits, logs, and pause controls acknowledge execution risk, but the support cost remains unknown. Subscription terms establish a charging mechanism; they establish neither paid conversion nor margin, burn, or a route to break-even. Didero already executes overlapping procurement workflows. Lark therefore needs evidence that its customer knowledge compounds faster than its service obligations.

The objection to passing is substantial: a named customer reports concrete utility from a functioning deployment, so dismissing this as an interchangeable AI application would miss the evidence. I checked the exception case: demonstrated workflow use warrants further attention, but the dossier lacks the paid conversion, retention, cash economics, or exceptional external validation that could override the unresolved financing gates. I pass on the current evidence. My verifiable flip condition is a documented financing offer below $10 million post-money whose proceeds fund a dated replication test, backed by paid distributor contracts showing that implementation and ongoing support can lead to operating break-even without another raise. That would connect the customer foothold to an investable use of my money.
