# Vestris -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Vestris has demonstrated an 8–10x reduction in processing time: eight to ten hours becomes one hour per closing file. That is real leverage, not a polite 20% efficiency claim. But the consequence remains bounded. The dossier offers an $18.5 billion title-premium pool, not the labor cost Vestris can eliminate, the number of processors affected, or a path to making home transactions radically cheaper. Doubling one agency’s capacity is a useful company; it is not yet a 100x societal consequence.

The technical evidence is promising but incomplete. A live pilot at a title shop settling millions of dollars monthly is more informative than a demo, and human approval is sensible while errors can corrupt legal ownership records. Yet Vestris has not benchmarked accuracy, exception rates, or cost against Qualia, Flueid, or an experienced processor. “Trained on decades of approved and rejected titles” could be the beginning of a proprietary learning loop, but dataset size, ownership, exclusivity, and measured lift are undisclosed. Today I see workflow orchestration using newly capable computer-use models, not a proven architectural discontinuity.

The founders have earned the right to attack this problem. Both processed actual closings; Aahil Valliani has built software and appears on three patents, while Joshua Tang built live telemetry systems. This is better than generic AI résumés searching for an industry. The missing founder evidence is behavioral: no material belief revision, no difficult complementary recruit, and no example of changing the product after disconfirming data. Two former coworkers can understand the workflow and still reproduce each other’s blind spots.

The largest kill risk is title-output accuracy across jurisdictions, followed closely by transaction security. Vestris is testing beside one operating title shop and limiting final outputs to human approval, so capital is touching the right risk. But there is no pass/fail threshold: no audited false-negative rate, no comparison with human examiners, no security milestone, and no evidence that performance transfers beyond Washington, D.C., and Montgomery County. Expanding sales before proving those numbers would be precisely the wrong use of pre-seed capital.

The strongest bull case is the exception to my science-first bias: an execution-heavy software wedge can become important when a tiny, domain-native team enters a large existing value pool and expands into infrastructure. Vestris could own the operating layer between inboxes, county portals, underwriters, and closing systems, with every reviewed exception improving the product. But incumbents already possess integrations and underwriting relationships, while Vestris has not shown that its data loop compounds faster than Qualia or Flueid can add similar agents. This is uncomfortably close to the red-flag pattern of prevailing models plus implementation labor unless the learning loop proves proprietary.

I pass today. The one verifiable flip condition is a blinded audit across at least five materially different jurisdictions showing at least a 5x reduction in processor time, no worse title-defect rate than experienced humans, and repeat paid usage by three independent agencies. That would convert an impressive workflow claim into evidence that Vestris can become title infrastructure rather than an automation feature.
