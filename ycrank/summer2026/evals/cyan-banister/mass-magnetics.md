# Mass Magnetics -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Aerospace, defense, and robotics manufacturers need qualified magnets made from a domestic loop rather than materials that disappear when another country changes an export rule. Mass Magnetics offers a plausible chain into that world: recover motors and hard drives, classify their chemistry with machine vision, mill them, then turn the material into dense magnets through 60-second sintering. China’s April 2025 export controls and the January 1, 2027 DFARS restriction are real leaks from that future. What is missing is the middle of the movie—repeatable feedstock control, application qualification, production yield, and scaled delivery.

The broken rule is unusually concrete. Applicable defense procurements cannot use covered-country magnets after the DFARS deadline, leaving manufacturers dependent on a concentrated foreign supply chain. Mass Magnetics could return supply control and traceability to American builders across drones, motors, aerospace, and robotics. But the regulation includes a nonavailability exception when compliant material cannot be sourced at sufficient quality, quantity, and reasonable price. That exception turns the thesis from “the government created demand” into “the company must beat a demanding qualification-and-economics test.”

Silver and Groll have relevant operating tuition. Silver worked on lithium-sulfur cathode pilot lines; Groll studied manufacturing at MIT and worked on ISS and Artemis systems at NASA before Draper. That is much better than tourists discovering industrial policy during YC. Their grade-school relationship may help them endure a difficult manufacturing ramp, but the dossier does not show a dated pre-company magnet obsession, independently shipped magnet artifacts, customer recruitment, or the contractual tiebreaker between two co-founders. I see credible materials and aerospace fluency, not yet first-believer proof.

The weakest evidence is where hardware truth becomes expensive behavior. Mass Magnetics says it can sinter magnets in 60 seconds, offers preorders for year-end 2026 delivery, and promises certificates of analysis. It publishes no independent qualification results, named purchase orders, repeat lots, revenue, or customers willing to redesign around the product. The “illustrative only” compositions matter because qualification can take months. A fast furnace cycle is not a fast customer adoption cycle.

The strongest case is that this is exactly the concealed enabling layer beneath several fashionable waves: every autonomous machine still needs physical actuators, and magnets sit inside the motors. Recycled feedstock, chemistry sorting, and rapid sintering could become a valuable process-and-data system. The objection stings because Noveon has raised approximately $315 million, Vulcan is already producing in North Carolina, and Niron could eliminate rare-earth feedstock from the architecture. Mass Magnetics controls a promising process claim, but the dossier does not yet establish a scarce asset its better-capitalized competitors cannot reproduce or route around.

I am neutral today. The company fits my infrastructure barbell beautifully, but the artifact has not elicited the costly behavior that would separate a manufacturing company from an excellent laboratory story. My flip condition is one independently verified, paid, repeat production order from a named aerospace, defense, or robotics manufacturer, including qualification data showing that multiple recycled lots meet the same magnetic and chemical specification. That would prove both the process and the customer’s willingness to cross the painful bridge.
