# Agency Tool Company -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Jack Morrison and Davis Foster spent eight years together building and deploying hundreds of Scythe robots before its 2026 acquisition, which makes Agency Tool Company a missionary-operator business wrapped in an unproven financing structure. Agency Tool Company’s highest dimension is founder conviction: Deploy is the tool Morrison and Foster needed across robots operating in more than 30 states. Agency Tool Company’s lowest dimension is round mispricing because no valuation, round size, rejected investors, or unusual instrument is disclosed—no evidence, so I apply the 9/30 absent-evidence default. Great operators can still be a bad SAFE; that distinction is the whole game.

**Drop.io** is the high rung for asymmetric conviction: years inside a stubborn infrastructure problem created operating knowledge that generic founders could not manufacture for a pitch. Agency Tool Company shares that mechanism because Morrison and Foster shipped hundreds of autonomous machines at Scythe, then built byte-level, resumable deployment around failures they personally experienced. Agency Tool Company diverges from Drop.io because neither founder has disclosed category essays, public technical work, or a non-obvious thesis beyond “robot deployments are painful.” The lived obsession is unusually strong; the visible pre-pitch intellectual artifact is not.

**Robinhood** exposes the low-rung financing failure mechanism: once consensus pricing arrives, a good business can become a bad new investment; Slow’s exception worked because entry preceded the markup. Agency Tool Company gives me no equivalent entry-price evidence. Three launch partners and two exited robotics founders could make Agency Tool Company a hot Summer 2026 infrastructure deck, while the probable fleet-priced SaaS instrument looks completely standard. “Robotics infrastructure” is illegible enough to be interesting, but without a disclosed post-money valuation or a tier-one pass, I cannot tell whether I am underwriting the illegibility or paying someone else for resolving it.

Three launch partners—Burro, Tempo Works, and Gather AI—give Agency Tool Company a useful capital-structure flywheel over the next 18 months. Each deployment can teach Agency Tool Company another failure mode, integration, rollback pattern, and device configuration without financing a robot fleet itself; that is how a software layer can become default-alive rather than founder-draining. The failure path is equally mechanical: Build is still “coming soon,” revenue is undisclosed, and Formant, InOrbit, or internal platform engineers can force Agency Tool Company into expensive enterprise integration before repeatable pricing exists. This is plausibly venture-shaped, but the regatta still has several tacks.

Jack Morrison’s approximately 6,799 LinkedIn followers and Davis Foster’s roughly 2,000 put Agency Tool Company above zero distribution but below an owned-community business. Agency Tool Company would flip this dimension into the top tier if those audiences or the ROS community produced measurable, repeat inbound adoption across dozens of fleets; the July 2026 ROS launch received one like, and no newsletter, podcast, GitHub following, or 10,000-person community is disclosed. Right now the founders have reputational reach, not John Fish-style owned distribution.

**Sublime Security** is the closest single-anchor parallel on category fit: unsexy, runtime-adjacent infrastructure bought before a consolidation wave, where operational pain matters more than a visionary deck. Agency Tool Company has the same execution-machine quality through delta updates, interrupted-download resumption, selective targeting, and rollback on physical fleets. Agency Tool Company is weaker on sleeve fit because robotics deployment is neither a demonstrated Slow concentration nor a non-standard asset class; it remains industrial SaaS with feature-level overlap from InOrbit and general-device pressure from Balena.

InOrbit’s explicit OTA capability and Formant’s broader fleet platform make my 36-month forecast concrete: Agency Tool Company reaches roughly $3 million to $6 million in ARR and is acquired by Formant for less than $100 million by July 2029. Agency Tool Company can win a valuable deployment wedge because Burro, Tempo Works, and Gather AI already validate the pain, but Agency Tool Company has not disclosed the proprietary data, protocol control, or structural barrier needed to remain a standalone platform. Strong founders plus weak distribution usually produce a useful product; they do not automatically produce an independent asset class.

Burro’s fleet of more than 750 deployed machines is the objection that stings: Agency Tool Company may be the **Pinterest** exception, where missionary founders and operator-network sourcing overwhelm my financing skepticism. Morrison and Foster have an exit, eight years of shared operating history, three named production partners, and the credibility to turn a narrow deployment tool into the control plane for physical AI. Agency Tool Company still loses that argument today because the 750 machines belong to Burro, Build is unfinished, revenue is undisclosed, and competitors already sell adjacent functionality. A disclosed SAFE at or below a $12 million post-money valuation would flip my verdict—the value-seed price would then compensate for the unfinished regatta.
