# OpenVector -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: ordinary cameras no longer produce footage for someone to watch later; they perceive an operational state, decide whether confidence is sufficient, and trigger the next action. OpenVector has two believable bridges toward that world: vision-language models turn plain English into monitoring workflows, while its NVIDIA Jetson station and foveated system move inference close enough to the camera for real-time use. The CVPR 2025 Spotlight—sampling roughly 3% of pixels with claimed 5–10× lower bandwidth—is a real leak from that future. What is missing is the rest of the causal map: which decisions become reliably autonomous first, how human review recedes, and why OpenVector owns that transition rather than merely riding it.

The founders make the technical claim more credible than the market story. Andrey Gizdov conducted multimodal research across Harvard, MIT, and the Weizmann Institute before dropping out of his PhD; Vishal Urlam worked on embedded systems, cameras, and network protocols at Tata. Their relationship began with a Google Prize-winning project at MIT Grand Hack in 2025 and continued afterward. That is dated first-believer proof and complementary recruiting, not four people assembling around a fashionable label last month. I still cannot see a long pre-company trail of deployments, customer support wounds, or commercially legible experiments showing that they understand how this research becomes an enduring enterprise.

The hidden-system question is weaker. OpenVector says humans currently monitor feeds for stalled production, tailgating, missed process steps, and waiting customers, but it does not quantify the labor, downtime, insurance loss, or queue created by that arrangement. Why is a factory paying someone to watch? Who bears the cost when nobody watches? Which employee gains control when the camera acts? A horizontal list spanning gyms, car washes, offices, warehouses, and restaurants avoids answering those questions. Without one expensive broken rule, the product risks becoming an impressive capability looking for whichever demonstration gets applause.

There is a working artifact, but not yet weird or costly user behavior. OpenVector reports live use cases across four sectors and offers a $449 monthly station or a $6,499 purchase, yet supplies no named customer, deployment count, non-refundable order, repeated workflow, or measured response. The refundable $100 reservation establishes packaging, not demand. Its strongest unstated-pattern fit is lower in the stack: existing cameras, constrained bandwidth, edge inference, event detection, and action orchestration could become enabling infrastructure across many physical businesses. But deployment data rights are only inferred, and Spot AI already supports existing footage and video agents while Ambient.ai and Voxel possess much larger financing bases and narrower operating wedges.

The surveillance exception does not rescue the current evidence. OpenVector shares with **Flock Safety** the mechanism of making existing camera pixels operationally useful; the material difference is that this dossier provides no explicit consent, custody, retention, or deletion architecture. False positives can also alter customer profiles or apply POS discounts, so configurable thresholds and human review are necessary controls, not proof that the risk is solved. This matters more than the funded competition: a self-improving vision system without clear data rights could turn the asset I want to underwrite into the gate that prevents adoption.

My 36-month base case is that OpenVector narrows into industrial process monitoring or safety and reaches $1–3 million ARR, rather than becoming a horizontal autonomous-worker layer for every camera. The technical team and bandwidth advantage make that outcome plausible; the absence of a quantified rent, named paid behavior, and a defensible data loop prevents me from investing my own money today. One condition flips me: twelve named facilities paying recurring fees for at least six months, with a documented reduction in labor or downtime and contractually explicit video-data retention and training rights.
