# Decent -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Decent has evidence of cheaper insurance purchases. It has not yet shown that it makes healthcare delivery cheaper. Serif Health reports $60,000 in annual savings; Locate reports more than $100,000 against its renewal proposal. Those are meaningful customer outcomes. But comparing carriers, PEOs, and plans can reduce an employer’s bill through purchasing decisions without removing underlying medical expense. I want the consequence of success measured in resources released or expertise made radically cheaper. The dossier supplies neither savings percentages nor evidence that continuous claims intervention produces that larger consequence.

The reported automation of 89% of brokerage work is the most interesting technical claim. If that means comparable service quality with radically fewer human hours, Decent could change the economics of serving small employers. But what counts as “work”? Tasks, time, or cost? There is no measurement method or benchmark against a named broker. Nava already offers year-round support, AI tools, and bill audits. That overlap does not establish that Decent lacks novel technology; it makes demonstrated performance essential. Claims review also excludes fully insured and PEO arrangements, so the distinctive intervention cannot follow every customer through the product’s broad shopping funnel.

The strongest argument against passing is the team. Nick Soman has operated health plans and a PEO, led growth at Gusto, and built acquired companies. Andrew Budker brings production AI experience from Meta. Their relationship began building Kindle approximately 16 years ago. These are complementary capabilities aimed at a workflow Soman understands, and 67 companies reportedly working with Decent is useful early validation. My actual investing pattern includes execution-heavy financial businesses. The Block exception is relevant: a narrow transaction product can expand into financial infrastructure without a scientific breakthrough. The material difference is that Decent has not demonstrated a mechanism for expanding beyond brokerage into control of a substantially larger economic system. Prior acquisitions also provide no evidence of an exit-first mindset; I would not manufacture that red flag.

The next dollar should test whether continuous intervention creates incremental savings beyond competent plan shopping. Claims access and actionable savings are separate risks. The dossier identifies access limitations but supplies no experiment with a budget, threshold, or stop condition. Likewise, no employer brokerage fee does not establish low delivery cost: providers pay variable commissions and sometimes bonuses. There is no disclosed model of servicing labor, inference expense, revenue per account, or renewal retention. Missing evidence earns the stated defaults here. It does not establish bad economics or subsidy dependence.

I pass with my own money. The remaining exception checks do not change that decision: the dossier establishes neither extraordinary societal consequence sufficient to override missing milestones nor a demonstrated technical trajectory sufficient to override uncertain economics. **My flip condition is an independently audited, prospective renewal cohort showing at least 10% lower employer spending than a matched conventional-broker cohort, with comparable coverage, after Decent’s servicing costs and provider compensation are counted.** That would connect the automation claim to economic consequence and distinguish intervention from ordinary shopping.
