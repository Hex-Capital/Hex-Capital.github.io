# Definite -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. The interesting future is not “accountants use AI.” It is that a bank’s books, policies, approvals, and source documents form one continuously verified operating model; regulatory reports and reconciliations become outputs rather than recurring projects. Definite has a plausible artifact leaking from that world: language models interpret messy inputs while a deterministic engine performs the financial math and preserves evidence lineage. What is missing is the path. The dossier does not identify the intervening institutional changes, how far deployments remain from that future, or why this team’s model becomes the shared substrate rather than one more close workflow.

The founders have paid some operating tuition. Gurshabd encountered manual attestations and wallet-policy controls at BitGo; Mazin watched regulatory numbers get rechecked at Optiver and built financial-analysis agents at Boosted.ai; Farhan worked on recommendation systems and machine-learning agents at Meta. Ledgix also predates the Definite launch, so this was not invented entirely inside a YC batch. That earns real founder credit. It is not yet pre-company obsession at the highest level: there is no trail of repeated shipped finance artifacts, customer-driven iteration, or recruiting evidence beyond three Waterloo classmates with complementary technical backgrounds.

The system wound is recognizable but incompletely decoded. Finance teams stitch together ERP records, spreadsheets, policies, documents, and approvals, and Definite says regulatory reports that take weeks can take hours. But which rule creates the delay? Who captures the rent? Does a controller gain authority, or does the institution merely buy a faster compliance tool? “Manual and fragmented” describes an irritating workflow; it does not yet explain the governing constraint or the agency transferred when that constraint disappears.

The bull case stings because the architecture is more thoughtful than a generic AI wrapper. Definite maps sources before automating them, separates document interpretation from deterministic calculations, and produces evidence-linked workpapers for human approval. Its reported DeepSeek V3 plus verification-engine result beat GPT-5 on FinBalance at roughly 50 times lower cost. If independently reproduced inside live customer books, that could become a useful lower-stack verification layer. The problem is that reusable mappings and policy graphs are still an inferred moat, no structural barrier has been identified, and Numeric, Stacks, and Basis already attack overlapping workflows with $38 million, $35 million, and $137.6 million in disclosed funding.

I cannot find the weird or costly user behavior that would make me underwrite the larger story. Custom order-form language does not prove a purchase; 170 LinkedIn followers and 11 Launch YC votes do not prove that a finance team entrusted Definite with a close. The movement from Ledgix to Bylaw, then bank reporting, then broad reconciliation and controls may be intelligent discovery, but without a repeated deployment it may also mean the team has not found the exact wound. The pre-revenue exception requires stronger substitute evidence—a working artifact repeatedly used in the field, signed proofs, or an unusually deep builder history. A company-reported benchmark alone does not clear it.

My 36-month forecast is that Definite narrows back to bank regulatory reporting and reaches $500,000–$2 million ARR rather than becoming the horizontal live model of enterprise books. I pass today, while recognizing that this team could build its way into a sharper company. One verifiable condition flips me: a named regulated institution pays for three consecutive reporting or close cycles and then expands onto a second workflow using the same source model and verification engine. That would demonstrate both costly behavior and the cross-workflow substrate the current dossier only implies.
