# OpenTag -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The proposed truth is that enterprises want one model-agnostic coworker living inside team chat, not separate assistants tied to particular models or employees. What would prove this wrong? If teams treat model routing as plumbing rather than a reason to choose OpenTag—and obtain the same shared-agent experience directly from Anthropic, Lindy, Dust, or Glean—the supposed secret collapses into a feature comparison. OpenTag entered roughly two months after Claude Tag with a similar mention-an-agent interface. Slack plus Teams and access to 80 models are differences, but they do not yet constitute a contrarian truth or a falsifiable technical breakthrough. This is presently a response to a trend, not a discovery the trend has missed.

The founding structure is much better than the idea’s competitive structure. Tony Kam, Shelden Shi, and Wilson Nguyen attended Berkeley and subsequently worked together at Lilac Labs before forming OpenTag. Their experience spans enterprise software, infrastructure, security, and AI products; the core engineering appears internal. This is a real team rather than three people assembled around a fashionable application. But the dossier does not establish that all three are full-time, how long they shipped together, or whether they retain meaningful ownership. Long alignment is partly demonstrated; economic exposure is not.

Ten teams in two weeks of early access is a useful test of curiosity, and six named organizations displayed as doing real work provide more information than an unsupported waitlist. The strongest bull case is that usage creates a proprietary workspace state: accumulated wiki pages, skills, permissions, memory, learned formats, and approval history could eventually make OpenTag difficult to remove. If repeated execution produces compounding institutional knowledge, the product could evolve from a chat interface into an enterprise operating layer. That possibility is the company’s best argument, but retention and switching costs have not been measured. A resemblance to a network or data moat is not yet a monopoly mechanism.

The competition is unusually adverse. Anthropic controls a foundational model and already distributes Claude Tag through Team and Enterprise plans; Dust and Lindy offer shared agents connected to company systems; Glean possesses an enterprise graph, an installed customer base, and $300 million in ARR. OpenTag depends on outside models and infrastructure while claiming model neutrality as its advantage. Suppliers can improve routing, incumbents can add models, and customers can switch interfaces. The company has neither the zero-to-one technical event that justified overlooking OpenAI’s uncertain monopoly nor the persistent hostile-environment demand that supported other known exceptions. No exception overrides the failed secret and monopoly gates.

The revenue engine is coherent but not yet power-law legible. A $50 monthly workspace plan with 5,000 shared credits, top-ups, and custom enterprise pricing is one business rather than five unrelated experiments. Yet the dossier provides no paid-customer count, gross margins, contract values, expansion behavior, or evidence that pooled usage grows into contracts capable of supporting a multibillion-dollar outcome. Distribution is similarly defined but unproved: a no-card $100 trial can acquire small teams, while demo-led sales might reach enterprises, but neither conversion rates nor sales cycles connect those routes to customer economics. Processing messages, files, and credentials while SOC 2 Type II remains in progress may also slow the higher-value channel.

My base case is that within 36 months OpenTag either shuts down or pivots from the general-purpose Slack-and-Teams coworker into a narrower workflow product after failing to exceed $1 million ARR. The objection that stings is the team: three previously aligned technical founders have already attracted ten teams in two weeks, and workspace memory could become valuable faster than public metrics reveal. I would reverse the pass if, within twelve months, OpenTag documents at least 25 paying organizations retaining the product for six months, with a majority repeatedly using one narrow workflow and that cohort expanding usage despite access to Claude Tag. That would reveal both the hidden beachhead and the beginnings of a last-mover mechanism. Today the founders are credible; the company is still commodity competition.
