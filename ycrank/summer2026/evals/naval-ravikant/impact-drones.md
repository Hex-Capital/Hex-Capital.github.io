# IMPACT Drones -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is not the aerospace résumé. Jasper Lortije already co-founded AeroIntel, built GNSS-denied visual navigation for long-range ISR drones, and helped secure the 2025 NATO Summit; Alessandro Fiume sold inside the same company. IMPACT Drones is therefore a continuation of knowledge acquired through building and selling into the exact problem. The founders are not learning autonomy, defense procurement, and operational sales simultaneously. They are moving an existing capability into an interceptor.

The product also passes the first substrate test. HUNTER and the turbine-powered SABLE are physical systems launched from sealed on-site cells, not an interface over a commodity model. Copying the navigation code does not reproduce reliable airframes, launch systems, sensor integrations, manufacturing, or deployment certification. But hardware is not automatically a moat. IMPACT has published no intercept rate, environmental-test results, proprietary dataset, or deployment-derived reliability advantage, while Fortem reports more than 5,500 captures and Origin Robotics is deployed by four European NATO states. Today the company has difficult technology, but not yet a demonstrated compounding asset.

The market is broad enough to preserve option value. Data centers, airports, energy infrastructure, and bases share an increasingly expensive need to defeat cheap hostile drones; the broader C-UAS market is estimated at $6.6 billion in 2025 and $20.3 billion by 2030. The dual-airframe architecture could extend across threat classes without changing the underlying autonomy stack. The constraint is authority rather than demand: U.S. law does not clearly permit private infrastructure owners to perform kinetic mitigation, and ramming near airports or populated campuses creates debris and false-classification risk. A large market inaccessible to its named buyer is not yet a large addressable market.

The bull case is sharp. A two-person team with exact-domain experience has selected a growing infrastructure bottleneck, built around GPS-denied autonomy, scheduled Middle East pilots for Q3 2026, and packaged expensive defense hardware as a subscription. This matches the recent shift toward embodied systems and could justify investing before conventional traction. Founder-specific knowledge can override missing revenue; it cannot override physics, authorization, and a field of deployed competitors all at once. The pilots remain prospective, and press coverage, 875 LinkedIn followers, and 27 job applicants are not product validation.

The business currently compounds through company labor, not through users. Each additional protected site needs hardware, monitoring, integrations, reloads, and procurement. One customer does not yet improve protection or distribution for the next, and the inferred possibility of accumulating flight data is not evidence of an exclusive network or model. The subscription identifies a payer and a direct value-capture mechanism, but there is no disclosed price, completed transaction, revenue, or unit economics. This is a promising hard-technology company with an unproven operating system around it.

I would not write the check today, but I would keep the option open. The single flip condition is a paid, regulator-authorized pilot at a named civilian infrastructure site that publishes at least a 95% successful-intercept rate with zero false launches under representative GPS-jamming conditions. That event would simultaneously prove technical deployment, legal access, and direct value capture—the three uncertainties that presently separate an aerospace prototype from an infrastructure company.
