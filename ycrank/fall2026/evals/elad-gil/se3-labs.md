# SE3 Labs -- Elad Gil Evaluation

API-accessible robot models let independent operators test policies on physical hardware; the prospective buyer is a robot-model developer, and the controlling economic variable is the cost of producing a verified improvement in deployment performance. SE3 Labs has demonstrated physical evaluation. It has not yet demonstrated that its failure-directed data improves policies more cheaply than a customer’s internal operation. That distinction determines whether this becomes infrastructure with expanding margins or a collection of individually scoped testing projects.

The technical work is substantive. SE3 published 100 Dream evaluation episodes across 10 tasks on October 30, 2026, with recordings, telemetry, and scoring sheets. Its workcells capture failures and operator interventions and package trajectories for training. Those are concrete components of an operational workflow. Better models could make this infrastructure more useful by expanding the tasks customers attempt, while physical execution and measurement remain necessary. But the Dream report leaves fine-tuning and comparative retesting to a subsequent round. Measuring failure is established; closing the improvement loop is not.

The strongest argument for investing now is that independent physical evaluation could become a bottleneck as robot-model development accelerates. September’s comparison—6 completed placements with code-as-policy versus 18 with agentic control, from 20 attempts each—shows that control choices can produce materially different physical outcomes. An independent operator with repeatable tasks could help developers select approaches and collect precisely the missing training data. SE3’s Python documentation and task catalog offer a plausible developer entry point. This fits my pattern of buying infrastructure around a technology transition before the final application winners emerge.

The difficulty is value capture. Poke & Wiggle already offers remote physical evaluation and targeted collection, while academic infrastructure demonstrates that laboratories can reproduce parts of the approach. SE3’s customer-data restrictions also prevent assuming that every engagement compounds into a reusable training asset. Those restrictions may help customers trust the service, but a data moat would require a separate explanation of permissions, reproduction cost, and accumulated operating advantage. Contract-specific statements of work establish a way to charge; they do not establish utilization, delivery cost, or improving margins. The physical-business red flag remains unresolved because the dossier does not show technology changing the economics of operating the stations.

I would keep the demand and team judgments narrow. The published experiments demonstrate execution, but they are not evidence of external customers returning or paying. NVIDIA Inception membership does not fill that gap. Founder identities remain unverified, so Paige Xu’s reported background cannot establish founder commitment or prior startup learning. My exceptions for research-stage investments require documented rare technical talent and a credible capital path; neither is established here. The exception for physical businesses requires demonstrated economic reconstruction, which is also absent. These are missing underwriting inputs, not evidence of a bad team.

I pass on this evidence. SE3 Labs has built enough to make the infrastructure thesis credible, but the market’s controlling mechanism remains unproven. My single flip condition is a documented repeat purchase from a named external robot developer showing that SE3’s intervention data produced a measured policy improvement at a lower fully loaded cost than that customer’s internal collection process. That would connect the operating system already demonstrated to both customer pull and a reason this market supports an independent company.
