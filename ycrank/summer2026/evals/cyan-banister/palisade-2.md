# Palisade -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Fnu Prince began red- and blue-team work at 16, and that makes Palisade a lived-problem security company whose weakest signal is physical verifiability. Palisade is not a credentialed founder visiting cybersecurity after reading a $37.2 billion TAM report; Prince left Caltech at 18 and followed operating systems into the kernel. The signature is a deeply authentic technical founder building an artifact that remains invisible until something breaks. I like the human more than I can yet touch the product.

Anduril is the high rung: Palmer Luckey built adjacent technology before defense became the pitch, so biography and product were inseparable. Palisade shares that mechanism because Prince reports professional offensive and defensive security experience beginning at 16 and identifies OS vulnerabilities as a recurring problem from that work. Palisade diverges because the dossier shows no prior product shipped at scale, public repository, or customer deployment comparable to Luckey’s Oculus history. Prince is of the place, not visiting it, but Palisade has not yet proved that Prince can carry “Tiz” into “Biz.”

DeepMind sits at the low rung of tactile verifiability: an abstract technical system could not be installed, ridden, or photographed by a normal person, and only Demis Hassabis’s extraordinary polymath compounding overrode that weakness. Palisade shows the same abstraction problem even after demonstrating three named CVEs—CVE-2024-1086, CVE-2025-6019, and CVE-2025-32463—because a security lead must trust Palisade’s account of what happened inside the kernel. Palisade has no accessible website, public benchmark, or production deployment where I can watch the mitigation work. The DeepMind exception flickers in Prince’s combination of machine learning, operating systems, security, and competitive-programming education, but the dossier does not yet establish Hassabis-level cross-domain output; the HQ Trivia tactile-virality exception plainly does not apply.

Cisco’s Tetragon already performs eBPF observability and in-kernel enforcement, which is precisely why Palisade is only moderately magically weird. Over 18 months, Palisade’s weirdness compounds if accepted and rejected remediation rules become proprietary fleet-specific policy data: every safe fix makes the next fix faster, and an apparent AI wrapper becomes operational memory. Palisade’s weirdness evaporates if AI merely translates public CVEs into rules that Tetragon, Tracee, or Falco can reproduce. Five YC launch votes and 79 LinkedIn followers suggest Palisade is not yet consensus, but silence about investor passes or round pricing prevents me from calling the neglect deliberate mispricing.

Palisade lists one founder and a team size of one, giving Fnu Prince the cleanest possible tiebreaker today. The counterfactual is simple: Palisade’s governance score falls sharply if Prince recruits an equal co-founder without preserving explicit CEO authority, while documented founder vesting and an operating protocol would push Palisade toward the top rung. No public governance documents exist, so solitude is evidence of authority, not evidence of preparedness. I have scars from HQ Trivia and GameCrush; one founder avoids the 50/50 trap but also concentrates key-person risk.

SpaceX is the single-anchor parallel for capital-intensity: the rocket was the product, the technical risk, and the moat all at once. Palisade carries a smaller version of that technical audacity into kernel-enforced remediation, where a mistaken rule could interrupt legitimate processes across an entire fleet. Palisade is nevertheless software-only, Linux-centered, and built atop eBPF and LSM-BPF primitives already available to Cisco and Aqua Security. Cybersecurity is unsexy and consequential, but Palisade is not yet the ugly physical or regulated artifact that makes other investors recoil. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing, yet here the game may still be enterprise SaaS.

Three demonstrated CVE remediations lead me to predict that within 36 months Palisade will pivot from broad “every endpoint” protection to automated Linux cloud-workload hardening and reach $1 million–$3 million ARR, without supporting Windows or macOS. Palisade’s founder-market fit should earn technically demanding design partners, while Palisade’s low tactile verifiability and remediation risk will slow fleet-wide trust. The narrow wedge can become real infrastructure; the universal endpoint promise arrives later, if ever.

Cisco acquired Isovalent after Tetragon established open-source enforcement, and that is the bear case that stings: Palisade may be a gifted solo hacker rebuilding a well-funded incumbent feature while an AI-generated rule takes down a customer fleet. Palisade has disclosed no customers, revenue, false-positive rate, rollback measurements, or defensibility, and the red flag is that the model—not Prince—could become the claimed moat. The dossier’s winning evidence is Prince’s security work since 16 plus working mitigations against three specific CVEs; that is lived technical capability, not a TAM costume, and it earns a small personal check. I flip to Pass if Palisade cannot show one paid production fleet deployment with measured rollback and workload-interruption data by January 2028.
