# Proprio Robotics -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and the compelling version is not merely a robot inside a data center. Compute infrastructure physically reconfigures itself: machines replace failed disks, recable GPUs, harvest reusable components, and rack new servers without waiting for scarce technicians. Working backward requires reliable manipulation across changing SKUs, safe operation around live workloads, and deployment data that converts brittle demonstrations into repeatable labor economics. Proprio Robotics has one credible leak from that future: custom hardware performing six named manipulation tasks and an office data center the company says it maintains continuously. But the dossier does not yet map those intervening transitions or show how far each remains from commercial reliability.

The founders fit the technical problem unusually well. Anirudh Pai researched dexterous manipulation at BAIR and worked alongside NVIDIA’s GR00T team; Raghav Punnam studies reinforcement learning and humanoid world models. That is real technical adjacency, not a fashionable AI pivot inferred from two résumés. What I cannot see is the deeper first-believer record: dated pre-company builds, years of independent obsession, recruited operators, or evidence that either founder understands how downtime risk, service contracts, and capital intensity become a business. They have strong Tizz. The dossier has not yet established Rizz or Biz.

The mundane object I keep returning to is the server SKU. Rails, cables, heatsinks, and component layouts all vary, and Proprio deliberately chose disassembly partly to collect data across that variation. That is a smart beachhead: ITAD work tolerates more latency and less operational risk than touching live GPU infrastructure, while producing the failure data needed for harder tasks. My 36-month expectation is therefore specific: Proprio reaches roughly $1–3 million in ARR through ITAD and component-harvesting deployments, but live data-center maintenance remains a supervised pilot rather than its principal revenue source.

The bull case stings because the office system already works across several tasks, Trossen Robotics publicly showed autonomous Dell PowerEdge disassembly, and unnamed enterprise circular programs are reportedly engaging. A small team could use those deployments to build the cross-SKU manipulation corpus before larger automation vendors adapt. This also matches my concealed-infrastructure pattern: Proprio sits beneath the visible AI-compute boom and could improve assembly, maintenance, and recycling economics across the whole lifecycle.

But the controlled bottleneck is not yet visible. The published 10,000–100,000-record dataset is Apache-2.0, commercial deployment counts are undisclosed, and Bright Machines, Grenzebach, Boost Robotics, and ANYOPS already cover overlapping pieces of the workflow. Proprio may have an integrated stack, but resemblance to an enabling layer is not ownership of one. GR00T lowers the development barrier for Proprio and its competitors alike.

The weakest part of the case is the institutional economics. “Hundreds of thousands” of technician vacancies and weeks of server transit identify friction, but not the rule creating it, the rent captured by an incumbent, or the individual whose agency is restored. I also find no affirmative evidence of the governance, leadership-shell-game, severe surveillance, or closed-model-dependency red flags that would force a pass. This is a Neutral for me today, not because pre-seed revenue is missing, but because the artifact has not yet elicited costly behavior from an independent operator. One result flips me: a named customer completing at least 1,000 interventions across ten server SKUs over 90 days, with under 1% human recovery and zero damaged components. Then I write the check.
