# Bear -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Bear’s candidate secret is that replacing the ledger itself lets an accounting firm automate work that firms operating inside existing software cannot. That is a proposition worth testing. Compare accountant hours and filing accuracy between Bear and firms using Procountor, Fennoa, or Netvisor. The dossier supplies no such comparison and identifies no named peer that rejects the proposition. Its July 2026 evaluation process describes how the team might discover an advantage; it does not establish that advantage already exists.

Who keeps the value if this works? Bear has an initial customer population in Finnish businesses, but Balance and Skalar already combine agents with human accounting professionals. Balance’s reliance on existing ledgers makes Bear’s architecture meaningfully different, while potentially making Balance easier to adopt. Owning a ledger gives the team control over development. Monopoly requires evidence that this control produces something competitors cannot readily reproduce. No exclusive dataset, measured technical superiority, or compounding network effect is established. The competing service bundles are affirmative competitive pressure, sufficient to trigger my presumptive-pass gate.

The founding structure is more encouraging. Sami Laakkonen and Riki Karjalainen previously worked at Token Terminal, and the original founding group reportedly knew one another since childhood. That is affirmative evidence of alignment beyond assembling a team for YC. Their engineering backgrounds also make internal product development credible. However, the discrepancy between four original founders and two currently listed active founders needs clarification. It establishes neither a damaging departure nor a clean ownership structure. Full-time status and economic exposure remain unverified; I give credit for shared history without inventing the rest.

Bear’s strongest counterargument is operational: more than 10 paying customers and over $80 million processed for dozens of Finnish companies suggest that businesses will entrust it with consequential work. Owning both software and service delivery could let the team redesign accounting around automation while incumbents preserve existing workflows. That fits my recurring interest in financial infrastructure. But transaction volume is not revenue, and the fixed-price service has no disclosed price or gross-margin structure. One coherent revenue engine exists; its power-law arithmetic remains unestablished. Similarly, startup introductions, signups, and direct email identify prospective acquisition routes, while the dossier does not connect those routes to repeatable customer economics.

I checked whether the evidence warrants an exception to the monopoly gate. My exceptions require a demonstrated technical discontinuity or customer behavior that directly resolves the challenged risk. Bear’s paying customers validate demand for its service; they do not yet establish privileged value capture. I would pass on a personal check today. The single condition that would reopen diligence is an independently verified result showing Bear’s own ledger reduces accountant hours per compliant monthly close by at least 10× against incumbent-ledger workflows, with no deterioration in accuracy, across a live paying-customer cohort. That would turn the architectural choice into evidence of proprietary technology.
