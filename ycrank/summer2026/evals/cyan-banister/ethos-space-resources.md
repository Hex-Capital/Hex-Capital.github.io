# Ethos Space Resources -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock six years. Lunar machines stop behaving like exquisitely packed camping trips from Earth: they land, turn nearby dirt into roads and pads, extract oxygen, and manufacture the silicon that expands their own power supply. Ethos Space Resources supplies an unusually coherent path into that world—melt regolith under vacuum, recrystallize it, then progressively purify the same feedstock into aluminum and solar-grade silicon. The leak is already visible in terrestrial vacuum-chamber production, rocket-plume testing, a completed NASA contract, and an Astrolab operating partnership. Artemis’s targeted 2028 surface mission gives the transitions dates rather than requiring an unnamed miracle.

The hidden economic fact is that useful payload represents roughly 2% of launch mass while landers carry return fuel from Earth. Making pads, oxygen, and solar material locally attacks that burden beneath every lunar application, from mobility to propellant to data-center power. This is much more interesting than a lunar commodity story: installed processing capacity could become the enabling supply chain that several downstream operators share. But Ethos has not yet quantified the launch-cost rent eliminated per kilogram, identified who captures the resulting savings, or demonstrated a structural barrier against ICON, Starpath, and other adjacent processors. The resource-rights regime is also unclear, so local production returns operational agency without yet securing legal control.

Ross Centers looks independently pulled into this problem. A space-resources master’s degree, the Planetary Sunshade Institute, and Hydrogen Alpha predate the current company framing; Brogan BamBrogan adds documented propulsion, spacecraft, and launch-hardware experience. That pairing is substantially stronger than two founders who discovered lunar infrastructure after Artemis became fashionable. What remains missing is dated evidence of which processing artifacts they built before Ethos, how they recruited one another, and how the founding team converted technical obsession into the completed NASA work.

The $1 billion of customer LOIs is large enough to be informative and vague enough to be dangerous. No customers, deposits, binding terms, or pricing are disclosed. I give more weight to the completed NASA contract, plume testing, and Astrolab partnership because those required institutions to spend time and technical attention on a working program. Still, nobody has repeatedly used Ethos’s output on the Moon, and the first lunar mission remains planned for 2028. This is real prototype and contracting behavior, not yet the strange, costly dependence that proves the supply chain has arrived.

The sharpest adverse artifact is organizational, not lunar. Payload described three founders and 14 employees in June 2024; YC lists two active founders and a team of two in August 2026. A hard-technology company can contract while refining its path, but losing that much organizational mass without explanation raises exactly the questions I cannot wave away: who left, who controls the company, and whether one person is unambiguously accountable. Artemis dependence and nonbinding LOIs are ordinary financing risks compared with a concealed founder or governance rupture.

I would invest because Ethos combines a dated six-year future, credible terrestrial technical leakage, domain-obsessed founders, and an enabling layer that lunar operators cannot simply replace with another software interface. The check rests on the processing system, not the headline value of the LOIs. One verifiable flip condition would change my decision: if corporate records and founder references show that the 2024-to-2026 contraction resulted from an unresolved control dispute or unclear CEO authority, I pass regardless of the lunar upside.
