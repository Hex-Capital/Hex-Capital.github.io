# Open Actuation -- AGI Evaluation

**Action:** Watch  
**Evidence confidence:** Low

### Company case

**Path A, scarce complements, is the strongest fit.** The candidate bottleneck is quick-turn manufacturing for drone OEMs that need a motor modified to their specifications. Open Actuation reports that one OEM’s incumbent quoted a $50,000 minimum order and a 13-week lead time for a shaft modification, and that Open Actuation built the modified motor in five weeks. The company separately quotes four weeks from specifications to assembled motors. The five-week build is the more relevant benchmark, and the memo lacks a like-for-like comparison of order size, price, and qualification. **Scenario assumption:** If cheaper intelligence lets OEMs iterate on more drone designs, physical fabrication and testing could become more valuable on the critical path. The memo establishes a plausible procurement bottleneck in one case, with no measured link to cheaper intelligence. (The Idea; Defensibility)

The company says it controls the design-to-test workflow at its Gardena factory through proprietary tooling and software. Reusing setups and test results across designs could protect turnaround as rivals gain access to the same models, but the memo documents only one timed build. The company separately reports fulfilling volume orders for the same unnamed OEM, with payment, order terms, and repeat purchasing unverified. Facer’s manufacturing quality software experience and reported delivery of heavy-lift motor samples for prospective customers to test support seed-stage execution. Repeatable production qualification remains open. The U.S. location and early commercial evidence make this a plausible seed-stage mandate fit. (The Idea; Defensibility; Market & Traction; Founders & Team; Key Risks)

The expansion unit is another qualified OEM design moving into recurring production. The company claims assembly capacity in the thousands of motors per month. Saleable throughput depends on setup time, testing, and yield across a mix of designs. Custom-order contribution would be revenue less materials, machining and setup, assembly labor, testing, and scrap or rework. The memo provides no custom pricing or measured production costs, so the contribution from the next design and its ability to absorb factory overhead remain unknown. Path B is less supported because the memo provides neither a comparable delivery-labor measurement nor paid use from a newly viable buyer segment. (The Idea; Market & Traction; Key Risks; Key Facts)

### Countercase

With abundant capital, another supplier could match quick-turn custom production. KDE Direct already offers motor modifications and custom builds, and funded domestic competitor Westmag operates a motor factory focused on high-volume customers. Difficult domestic sourcing of rare-earth magnets and silicon steel could leave lead times and margins exposed to upstream suppliers even when Open Actuation controls assembly. The memo gives no evidence that a rival has matched the same specification, lot size, turnaround, and qualification requirements, so the Path A override remains untriggered. Credible control and repeatable bottleneck relief are still unproven, which leaves the Reach out gate unmet. The decisive build and order evidence comes from the company, supporting low confidence. (Defensibility; Market & Traction; Key Risks)

### Decisive diligence question

Can a second independent drone OEM qualify and place a paid repeat order for a custom motor that Open Actuation supplies materially faster than a credible alternative at comparable specifications and lot size, while earning positive contribution? Buyer-confirmed qualification, a competing quote, and production records covering turnaround, yield, and fully costed repeat lots would answer it. A verified result would support Reach out if the updated score clears 65. A matched supplier offer that erases the lead-time advantage on comparable terms would support Pass if that advantage remains the only evidenced bottleneck.
