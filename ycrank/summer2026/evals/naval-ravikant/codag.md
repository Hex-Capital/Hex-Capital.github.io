# Codag -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Michael Zhou has acted inside the problem. He worked on infrastructure at Okta and Shopify, shipped a 655-star developer tool, then launched Codag’s live CLI, API, and MCP server in May 2026. The 51-incident benchmark and reproducible artifacts show technical energy, not merely credentials. But the evidence stops short of named production users, paid deployments, or proof that his prior infrastructure work involved this exact logging bottleneck. Five pivots demonstrate motion; they do not yet distinguish persistent learning from unstable direction.

The product solves a real constraint: agents consume context, and repetitive logs consume agents. Codag reports answering 98–99% of synthetic incidents while using 5.9–7.5 times fewer tokens than raw logs plus grep. That is useful technical proof. It is not durable proof. The deterministic engine adapts published Drain3 work, the engine and CLI are MIT-licensed, and Dash0, Honeycomb, Sentry, and Cribl can bundle similar compression into systems that already ingest the telemetry. If a competitor copies the code tomorrow, Codag has no demonstrated proprietary data, model improvement, workflow lock-in, or customer-created network left over.

The bull case is that this resembles the early capital-efficient product pattern behind Notion: a small team develops a strange new interface before the category becomes legible. The material difference is that Codag’s current interface compresses an input inside someone else’s observability workflow, while Notion developed a product language broad enough to become the workflow. Codag’s 45 and 20 current-product GitHub stars show discovery, but neither those stars nor seven and eight repository forks prove that one user improves the product or distribution for another.

The adjacent spend is large but the expansion path is unresolved. Cribl exceeds $100 million ARR, and the funded incumbents demonstrate that enterprises pay heavily for telemetry routing and diagnosis. Yet Codag has identified a feature wedge, not a causal route from log compression into ownership of that market. The open-source, no-account installation is permissionless distribution—one of the patterns I favor—but permissionless access without a compounding contribution loop creates adoption, not defensibility.

Pricing is concrete at $19 per month for Pro and $499 per month plus usage for Team, but no payer or completed transaction is reported. Prices prove that value can be quoted; customers prove that value can be captured. There is no disclosed integrity problem, artificial financing deadline, or other founder veto. The five pivots remain the softer red flag: by August 2029, I expect Codag to have pivoted from standalone compression into proprietary agent-observability workflows, with the present product remaining below $1 million ARR.

I pass with my own money today. The founder is the option; the current product is a copyable feature. I would reopen on one verifiable condition: a reproducible advantage of at least 20 percentage points over open Drain-based alternatives on held-out, real customer incidents, attributable to a proprietary Codag data asset. That would convert today’s implementation advantage into leverage that survives copied code.
