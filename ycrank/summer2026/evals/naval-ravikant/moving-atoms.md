# Moving Atoms -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Moving Atoms has built something, but the evidence of specific knowledge is adjacent rather than exact. Shehryar Saroya designed video-recommendation models at TikTok and built an AI game project; the brothers then shipped Atom Harness and submitted 198 Physics-IQ cases. That is real action. But generating plausible video is not yet evaluating robot policies: the benchmark measures frame prediction, includes known rotation and reflection errors, and has not demonstrated correlation with physical-robot outcomes. Mathematics credentials are potential energy. A deployed robot evaluation is kinetic energy.

The strongest bull case is the wedge. Physical-AI developers cannot wait days for physical tests, and an evaluation layer could expand from checkpoint grading into simulation, failure-data generation, and eventually policy training. The markets are large—robotics simulation was estimated at $6.88 billion in 2025, while physical AI was estimated at $81.64 billion—but these are top-down categories, not yet a causal expansion path. Moving Atoms has named the initial buyer and the adjacent workflow; it has not shown that the same capability reliably crosses from generated video into robot control.

The decisive weakness is substrate. Atom Harness depends on BytePlus Seedance 2.5 for generation and Claude Opus 5 for classification and timing. If a competitor copies the harness and buys the same models, nothing in the dossier remains scarce. Customer rollouts and failure labels could eventually compound into proprietary data, but possibility is not an asset. NVIDIA, Google, Skild AI, Physical Intelligence, Genesis AI, and World Labs already possess models, capital, or deployment access that can absorb this feature.

There is also no permissionless contribution loop. Each design partner supplies a checkpoint or inference endpoint and example episodes through founder-led onboarding; no evidence shows that one customer improves another customer’s evaluation or distribution. The design-partner model may instead require custom integration proportional to customer count. Moving Atoms could become a useful laboratory, but a laboratory is labor until its experiments create a proprietary model or shared network.

Economics remain unproven. There is no published price, revenue, completed paid engagement, or unit-economic evidence. The 56.6 Physics-IQ score is more informative than the company’s 189 X followers or the launch post’s 41,000 views, but even that result is second overall, comes from one run, and sits in an open pull request whose maintainer questioned whether the submission is a model or a composite harness. Describing it as topping the benchmark creates an avoidable credibility gap. It is not enough evidence for an integrity veto, but it raises the verification burden.

The known exceptions do not rescue the investment. A strange, capital-efficient prototype can justify missing revenue or network effects, but not when the demonstrated capability is assembled from commodity model vendors and has not crossed the robot-validity gap. I would pass today. The single flip condition is verifiable: three independent robotics customers repeatedly use Atom to predict physical-policy failures, pay for those evaluations, and contribute exclusive rollout data that measurably improves later evaluations. Then the harness begins turning service into leverage.
