# Elimentary -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, a credit analyst should be able to ask which loans breach their covenants and receive an answer that points to the exact clauses and calculations. Elimentary has built something relevant to that world: prepared financial data, specialized tools, and clause-level provenance. But getting there requires dependable execution and deployments that become easier with each customer. The dossier demonstrates useful automation today; it does not yet establish that progression. I can imagine the future. I cannot credit the company for the parts I supplied.

What catches my attention is TresVista putting the product inside actual work. Its October 19 deployment covered more than 500 BDC filing checks, compressing approximately 20 hours into an agent run under 30 minutes and another hour for human sign-off. A multi-year partnership and more than 25 reported production agents are meaningful institutional commitments. Someone has changed how work gets done. That is substantially more persuasive than a narrated demo, although these remain company-published results rather than independently measured retention or accuracy.

Why did those hours exist? Agreements change, spreadsheets diverge, and people reconcile the consequences through email. Elimentary attacks a specific operating failure. Its separate covenant case study reports four hours becoming 15 minutes per portfolio company at an unnamed $8B-plus fund. That returns time to analysts, but the dossier does not show an artificial restriction being removed or an individual gaining control over prices, customers, or employment. TresVista could capture the efficiency while leaving the underlying institutional arrangement intact. Useful savings earn credit; a transfer of agency remains unproven.

The founders have paid relevant operating tuition. Gorjilla and Saini previously shipped credit products at substantial scale, while Sarda co-founded debt-collection automation company Skit.ai. Their overlapping employment also gives this team a relationship history beyond assembling for an accelerator. Those prior loan books are founder evidence, not Elimentary revenue. What I still cannot see is the dated trail of experiments that would distinguish a longstanding obsession with this particular problem from a capable team selecting an attractive adjacent market.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that mundane workflow repair can become infrastructure almost unnoticed. Elimentary fits that unstated pattern in my investing: the interesting asset may be beneath the visible agent. Its semantic layer and financial tools are substantive assets, and leaving institutional knowledge with clients is attractive. But client ownership also limits the presumed compounding advantage. Hypercore already combines agents, loan software, and servicing; Finley has added verification and administration operations. Custom deployments could produce a valuable business without producing a scarce enabling layer.

I would keep my money in my pocket today, with a **Neutral** view. My pre-revenue exception is relevant because functioning deployments and experienced founders can outweigh absent revenue; revenue silence is not what holds me back. The unresolved issue is whether reliable infrastructure emerges from the customization. Elimentary’s benchmark still reports 36 filing-contradicting statements in open-ended answers, making human review consequential. Reported security certifications are encouraging, but do not establish consent and custody architecture; nor does naming a CEO establish governance rights. There is no documented governance violation or exclusive-model dependency to presume. My one verifiable flip condition is an independently documented deployment at a second institution that reuses the existing financial tools without substantial customization and sustains comparable time savings through a full reporting cycle, with errors and review effort disclosed.
