# Grip -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Jonas Gruetter taught a 12-ton excavator to remove boulders with 70% field-test success before founding Grip, but Grip discloses no co-founder history or tiebreaker authority. That pairing is Grip’s signature: frontier hardware built by someone who has touched the underlying manipulation problem, inside an organization whose control structure I cannot yet see. The Tiz is real; the Biz and Rizz remain behind a closed door. I am investing in the possibility that wet waste is Grip’s North Dakota gas flare—an ugly physical environment hiding a compounding data asset—but a bubbe asks who holds the steering wheel before she gets into the car.

SpaceX is the high rung because capital intensity, physical engineering, and conventional-investor discomfort were the mechanism, not incidental decoration. Grip shares that load-bearing mechanism: custom hardware must identify and grasp irregular plastic inside wet organic waste where contamination, corrosion, and variable feedstock punish laboratory demos. Grip diverges sharply because SpaceX had a visibly functioning artifact and an unmistakable founder; Grip publishes no throughput, accuracy, uptime, deployment, or dataset size. Grip sits below that frontier-hardware rung but above software pretending to be infrastructure: 13/15, because the technical risk is difficult to assess but not obviously insurmountable.

HQ Trivia is the low rung because an addictive artifact temporarily concealed unresolved founder authority until the Yusupov-Kroll fracture became part of the failure. Grip’s dossier names Jonas Gruetter as “Founder,” yet Grip has four people, refers to a co-founder relationship, and provides no evidence about CEO designation, equity control, vesting, or deadlock resolution. That is not proof of dysfunction; it is precisely the conversation founders postpone because everything feels friendly at four people. Grip receives the absent-evidence default with one extra point for the singular public founder identity. A hard no versus a rebuttal: this is a rebuttal, but it is the first document I request.

Jonas Gruetter’s work at Gravis Robotics, ETH’s Robotic Systems Lab, and Ursa Mining compounds into Grip over the next 18 months because sparse-LiDAR perception, irregular-object manipulation, and field-tested heavy machinery shorten the path from failed grasp to improved end effector. Grip is not founded by a waste-plant operator who watched plastic enter compost for ten years, so the biography is adjacent rather than Crusoe-level autobiographical. The mechanism can still become lived experience quickly: every filthy shift inside a biogas facility makes Grip less academic and Jonas less replaceable. The score stops at 24/32 because the dossier proves he lived robotics in hostile physical settings, not that he lived the customer’s waste problem before 2026.

The $962.3 million waste-sorting-equipment market is not enormous or glamorous, while ZenRobotics, Recycleye, and EverestLabs have trained investors to think robotic sorting already means dry streams. Grip becomes genuinely magically weird if named composting or biogas operators say established vendors refused wet feedstock, or if sophisticated investors pass because the machines look dirty, narrow, and service-heavy. Grip falls a tier on this dimension if its only distinction is a multimodal gripper that Recycleye can reproduce during the next product cycle. With no evidence of investor competition, public rejection, pricing, or even a working website, Grip sits on the attractive side of non-consensus but cannot claim underpricing: 18/24.

Flock Safety became legible because the product left an observable signal in the physical world before a polished pitch could explain it. Grip offers the same kind of potential truth: a robot either removes plastic from moving wet organics at facility throughput or it does not. Yet Grip currently gives me nothing equivalent to that Wi-Fi name—no public prototype video, installed cell, customer deployment, uptime figure, or accessible website in July 2026. Grip is more verifiable than an abstract AI API, but today the artifact can be imagined more easily than touched, which holds the score at 8/13.

Grip’s four-person Summer 2026 crew will, by July 2029, operate 8–15 paid sorting cells across European composting or biogas facilities and produce $1.5–3 million in annual recurring software, monitoring, and service revenue while remaining independent. Grip gets there through an unglamorous loop: failed wet-feedstock grasps generate facility-specific data, the data changes the end effector and control policy, and each installation makes the next hostile stream less novel. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing; Grip is not merely selling robot arms, but accumulating the operating knowledge required to make robot arms survive soup.

ZenRobotics has sorted waste since 2007, EverestLabs already sells monitored multi-arm cells, and Recycleye has raised $24.6 million including government funding; the strongest bear case is that Grip is an academic feature awaiting absorption by an incumbent. Grip has no disclosed customers, no performance results, no structural market barrier, and no proof that Jonas Gruetter’s excavator expertise supplies the social license to sell into compost facilities. That stings because my unstated pattern favors founders who are of the place, while Grip may still be visiting it. I nevertheless invest because wet organic manipulation is a materially different physical regime, Jonas has already moved reinforcement learning from paper onto a 12-ton machine, and upstream EU rules do not eliminate plastic contamination overnight. My verdict flips to Pass if one of the named incumbents demonstrates comparable wet-organic removal at commercial throughput before Grip secures its first paid facility deployment.
