# Glen -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Nikos Dritsakos sold SalesBop for $500,000 after 18 months, making Glen a high-learning-rate founder attached to a low-capital software problem. That pairing is Glen’s signature. Dritsakos has moved through sales software, AI product development, and agent tooling at Composio; Glen can therefore learn quickly and still produce an outcome too modest for my capital. The world-state at maximum ambition is better-informed agents, not a civilizationally distinct economy. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself.” Glen has not yet shown the great-company consequence.

**Slash** (2024) is the high rung on learning rate because a documented pivot—not credentials—revealed founders willing to discard their thesis when reality disagreed. Glen shares the entrepreneurial motion: Dritsakos built and sold SalesBop for $500,000 in 18 months, then worked inside Composio’s agent-tooling stack before founding Glen. The divergence is load-bearing. Glen documents no specific pivot, failed hypothesis, or “we were wrong” moment. Glen demonstrates velocity; Slash demonstrated epistemic flexibility. Those are related, not equivalent.

**DoorDash** (2013) exposed the low-capital mechanism: commodity technical inputs and modest initial capital can still produce a large company when distribution and ambition reshape consumer-commerce flow. Glen also appears fundable with ordinary SaaS capital—an MCP server, Slack integration, retrieval layer, and enterprise security—not the $50 million first-check profile of OpenAI or the $115 million Series A profile of Commonwealth Fusion. Glen names no model-training program, fleet, plant, or scientific milestone requiring more than $50 million. DoorDash earned its exception through a distribution machine. Glen currently has a work-email waitlist and founder-led calls.

The projected **$8.85 billion enterprise-search market by 2030** constrains Glen’s stated consequence before any probability calculation begins. Over 18 months, Glen’s mechanism could compound if each agent interaction improves a shared organizational record, raising switching costs as decisions and tool-use patterns accumulate. The same mechanism can compound errors: one stale or incorrectly reconciled fact propagates across every agent, while Glean’s $300 million ARR and Dust’s $60 million-plus funding compress Glen from both distribution and product directions. Glen may make agents materially better, but Glen has not named the economic regime that disappears. This is closer to optimizing enterprise knowledge than displacing $15 trillion of labor.

Glen’s organization-wide sharing default creates a real technical problem, but not yet a consensus-repelling technical miracle. The counterfactual that would flip this dimension is a demonstrated memory architecture that preserves source-system permissions, resolves contradictory observations, and measurably improves agent-task accuracy over long horizons where ordinary RAG degrades. Glen currently offers row-level security, OAuth 2.1, encryption, and private mode, while publishing no retrieval-accuracy benchmark. Those are necessary enterprise controls. They are not QuantumScape chemistry. Glen’s technical risk is that incumbents reproduce it, not that experts believe it cannot work.

**Glean’s $300 million ARR** is the relevant single-anchor parallel on founder defiance. I accepted Glean’s expert-continuity pattern because Arvind Jain’s search expertise was outweighed by capital scale and the consequence of reorganizing Fortune 500 information access. Dritsakos likewise continues directly from AI product work and agent tooling at Composio into Glen; Glen presents no documented rejection of expert consensus or controversial technical claim. The experts may still be wrong about shared agent memory, just as McKinsey was wrong about fewer than one million U.S. cellphones by 2000. Glen has not yet told me what the experts believe that Glen proves false.

Glen’s organization-wide permission model will force the decisive product lesson: by July 2029, Glen will have pivoted from generic shared organizational memory into permission-aware agent audit and observability software producing $1 million to $3 million ARR. Glen’s high founder learning rate makes a pivot more likely than death; Glen’s low capital intensity makes a focused SaaS outcome more likely than a civilizational platform. Sensitive-data aggregation, stale-context propagation, and auditable tool-use histories create a sharper buyer pain than “unified context.” The mechanism points toward governance-adjacent infrastructure—the category I generally avoid—not OpenAI-scale consequence.

Production deployment with teams admitted from Glen’s waitlist is the strongest bull case, and it stings because **Slash** proves that a fast-learning YC founder can override my consequence prior. Dritsakos has already sold one company, worked at Composio, shipped a Slack integration, and chosen MCP just as OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, and AWS standardized around it. Glen could become the neutral memory layer across competing agents before Glean recognizes the category. The dossier still wins the argument against that case: no revenue, retention, accuracy benchmark, structural barrier, or permission inheritance demonstrates that Glen’s timeline compounds faster than incumbents’ distribution. One condition flips my verdict: Glen documents at least $5 million ARR with net revenue retention above 120% specifically attributable to multi-agent shared-memory usage.
