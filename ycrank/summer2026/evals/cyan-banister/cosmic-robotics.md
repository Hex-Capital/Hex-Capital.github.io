# Cosmic Robotics -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Construction sites are not empty; they are choreographed by small crews supervising machines that unload, position, inspect, and document physical work across hostile terrain. The path there is legible: begin with one repetitive heavy-lift task, collect ugly field data, improve autonomy, then adapt the same manipulation system to adjacent trades. Cosmic Robotics has found a credible leak from that future. Cosmic-1A has already installed tens of thousands of panels across mud, sand, ruts, and weather, and the team adapted its system to cooling-pipe installation in ten days. That is more useful to me than a generic prediction that physical AI will be large.

Why panels? Because they weigh up to 90 pounds, must be placed with millimeter accuracy, and are installed in remote, extreme-temperature environments by a workforce projected to grow 48% by 2033. The machine does not abolish the crew: workers still spot and fasten panels. It removes the punishing lift and reportedly more than doubles labor productivity. I can see the broken system and the agency returned, although Cosmic has not yet shown who captures the resulting economic surplus, what an EPC pays, or whether workers gain safer jobs rather than simply becoming attendants to an expensive fleet.

The founders have paid unusually relevant operating tuition. James Emerick deployed autonomous construction equipment at Built Robotics. Lewis Jones worked in propulsion and space systems at Relativity Space, SpaceX, JPL, and Johnson Space Center, and now leads Cosmic’s NASA contract. They prototyped together through Autodesk Research and chose solar only after visiting a farm. That is independent discovery through contact with the physical system, not two smart people discovering “robotics for construction” from a market map. The missing piece is longer-dated proof of obsession: the dossier shows excellent preparation and a working partnership, but not a trail of self-directed artifacts stretching years before the company.

The bear case stings. Cosmic is building and servicing a custom 10,000-pound vehicle while Gritt attacks the same task with rented construction equipment and off-the-shelf arms—and reports contracts covering 2.8 GW. Cosmic’s 2025 cycle time merely matched the fastest humans, disclosed revenue is absent, and competitors have raised from $32 million to more than $200 million. If the Particle system is just a fashionable physical-AI label laid over commodity components, Cosmic could own the hardest capital and maintenance burden while a lighter competitor owns deployment. Tens of thousands of cycles matter only if they produce measurable autonomy or uptime that competitors cannot cheaply reproduce.

I still lean invest with my own money because the artifact is already enduring costly, weird reality. Panels are being moved every 30–40 seconds; crews are reorganizing work around the machine; field variation is feeding a controlled operating dataset; and NASA is paying the team to test an adjacent manipulation problem. The strongest signal is not the $4 million pre-seed or the awards. It is that one embodied system has crossed from a laboratory into repeated construction labor and then demonstrated a second task. This is a credible enabling layer beneath solar, data-center construction, and eventually off-world assembly, even though the moat is not yet proven.

My flip condition is concrete: before investing further, I want one named EPC to complete paid deployments at two distinct sites and disclose sustained productivity above 2× after charging, transport, human spotting, downtime, and field service are included. Failure to produce that evidence would tell me the apparent future leak is an impressive demonstration rather than the beginning of a construction platform.
