# Ploid -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Ploid’s candidate secret is that resolving identities across websites produces substantially better people search than querying existing contact databases. That is testable. Its founder-reported benchmark claims 81% accuracy across 1,000 queries, against Exa’s 63% and Apollo’s 23%. An independent reproduction on previously unseen customer queries could overturn that claim. This is more substantial than “agents need data,” but the dossier establishes neither which prevailing technical belief is wrong nor whether Gemini-graded results measure production usefulness. There is an experiment here; a demonstrated secret remains ahead.

The decisive problem is ownership of the advantage. Ploid claims an index exceeding two billion people and proprietary identity linking, but neither establishes exclusive inputs or a market it can dominate. Sales, recruiting, and research describe several large customer categories. They do not identify a beachhead. Apollo already offers agent access, Exa offers people search, and some contact information comes from outside vendors. Ploid could improve retrieval while capturing little of the value that improvement creates.

Monid makes that danger concrete. The September 4 integration gives Ploid a named route into agent workflows, which deserves more credit than an announced distribution strategy. But Monid also combines Ploid, Apollo, People Data Labs, and other vendors behind one connection. The intermediary can aggregate demand while making its suppliers replaceable. Documentation, CLI, and MCP access reduce integration friction; they do not establish customer acquisition economics. The claim that “thousands” of companies use Ploid supplies no paid conversion or repeat purchasing evidence.

The strongest objection to passing is that identity resolution could become the indispensable layer underneath those interchangeable interfaces. Hernandez has relevant recommendation-system and embedding experience, the product is shipped, and the reported benchmark advantage is meaningful enough to investigate. My less public interest in information systems that make uncertain reality legible gives this work particular appeal. Customer corrections might eventually compound into proprietary data. But that last mechanism is explicitly an inference. My known exceptions require independently demonstrated technical discontinuity or persistent transaction behavior sufficient to remove the relevant risk. Neither is established here, and competitive substitution is an affirmative concern.

The founding evidence is more encouraging than adverse. Karic calls Hernandez his best friend; their shared NYU affiliations suggest a relationship beyond convenience matching. They do not establish prior joint execution, current full-time status, or economic alignment, so I retain the stated founder default. A neuroscience-student biography does not prove part-time commitment. Likewise, ordinary permitted-use restrictions do not constitute an enforcement red flag. The revenue model is coherent: $200 and $600 monthly subscriptions plus consumption charges. It earns some credit for a defined engine, but vendor costs, margins, and customer expansion remain unestablished.

I pass on Ploid today. A useful supplier can become a substantial business while remaining exposed to competition at every renewal. One verifiable condition would reopen my decision: a named recruiting-agent customer independently documents that Ploid uniquely resolves at least 20% of its required identities beyond Apollo, People Data Labs, and Exa, then renews a paid production contract because of those matches. That would connect technical differentiation to purchasing behavior in a bounded market. The present dossier leaves those connections unproved.
