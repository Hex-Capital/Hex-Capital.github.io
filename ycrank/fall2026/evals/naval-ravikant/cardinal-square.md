# Cardinal Square -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Cardinal Square’s investment case turns on what its founders have learned by teaching. The dossier cannot identify them, much less show a curriculum they built, students they taught, or learning outcomes they improved. That leaves my first question unanswered: what specific knowledge lets this team combine AI and educators better than existing providers? Inaccessible research sources establish uncertainty. They supply no evidence against the founders’ competence or integrity. I assign the founder default.

The decisive question is what accumulates after each student completes a lesson. Cardinal Square proposes personalized instruction, but the dossier establishes no proprietary assessments, intervention dataset, model, or instructional method. Longitudinal learning records could become an asset if they produce demonstrably better teaching and the company can retain their value. Their hypothetical existence earns no credit. Nor can I conclude that this is a commodity wrapper: its architecture is unknown. The defensibility score stays at the absent-evidence default.

There is a credible economic destination. The proposed route runs from online supplemental instruction into accredited schools, potentially extending the same instructional capability into more of a family’s education spending. Global online tutoring is estimated at $12.61 billion in 2026, and Synthesis reported $10.98 million in FY2025 revenue. Those figures establish demand around the wedge. They do not establish Cardinal Square’s addressable segment or explain how supplemental lessons become a school operation. Accreditation, staffing, and school delivery require capabilities the initial product may never develop. I give limited market credit, capped by the broad market evidence.

The strongest bull case is that educator judgment makes the AI materially more useful. The cited 2025 mathematics experiment found that teacher-designed hints mitigated the harm associated with unrestricted AI assistance. Cardinal Square’s proposed combination therefore addresses a consequential product problem. A small team could discover an effective instructional system before public metrics appear. But the dossier contains no demonstration that this team has done so. My exceptions for an exceptional founder, a compelling prototype, or an emerging network cannot carry this investment: none of those overriding signals is documented.

My recurring preference for tools that multiply individual expertise could fit an educator creating instruction that thousands of students reuse. Cardinal Square has not demonstrated that mechanism. It also shows no permissionless contributor system, aggregation of fragmented educator supply, or evidence that one student improves another student’s experience. Educator involvement alone does not establish either scalable delivery or permanent labor dependence. Likewise, tuition and subscriptions remain inferred; there is no company price, payer, or completed transaction to credit. No documented founder conduct triggers an integrity or fundraising-pressure veto. The pass rests on insufficient affirmative fit.

I would pass on Cardinal Square today. The possibility is substantial; the evidence does not yet distinguish this team’s approach. My verifiable flip condition is a founder-run, paid repeat cohort showing improved **unaided** learning outcomes while educator minutes per student decline across successive cohorts using the accumulated intervention records. That single operating result would connect specific knowledge, instructional advantage, and economic scaling. It would give me a reason to own the option.
