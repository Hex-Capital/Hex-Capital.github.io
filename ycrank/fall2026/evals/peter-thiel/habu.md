# HABU -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

HABU’s most interesting proposition is that a developer can internalize automation and capture its benefits by delivering the entire project. That is a business hypothesis worth testing. But what important truth does HABU know that other developers reject? The dossier names no disputed belief. Its claims—five times more projects per employee, 20% faster schedules, and 10–15% lower costs—supply possible tests, but no project-level results. A matched comparison showing faster paperwork without lower delivered costs would undermine the economic premise.

The distinction matters because savings do not automatically become profits HABU can keep. Phoenix Class A multifamily is a concrete initial market, and an internal operating system could improve execution. Yet the dossier establishes no exclusive data, measured technical advantage, or compounding network effect. TestFit already sells feasibility automation; Mosaic combines software with construction operations. Neither is an identical substitute, but both demonstrate that parts of the workflow are available through other organizational forms. HABU must establish why its integration creates a durable advantage that competing developers cannot purchase or reproduce.

The strongest objection to passing is the founders’ unusually relevant combination. Bryan Tharalson has managed more than $80 million in development projects and was Mutiny’s first sales hire. Junhao Chen brings Meta AI operations and Navy logistics experience. Together, they could recognize which coordination failures actually matter and build around them. HABU also fits my revealed interest in operators using technology to change physical industries. Those facts make the operating thesis credible enough to investigate. They do not establish the separate founding requirements: prior work together, full-time commitment, ownership, and economic alignment remain undisclosed. Chen’s cached Tenur affiliation does not prove divided commitment.

Distribution is the most concrete part of the commercial plan. HABU names capital partners seeking Phoenix projects with $5 million–$20 million equity checks and solicits both investors and landowners through its launch and website. That identifies counterparties and an actual route to contact them. It establishes neither repeat acquisition nor suitable sales economics. More fundamentally, those equity checks finance properties; they are not HABU’s revenue. Without development fees, delivery margins, or evidence of repeat mandates, the $160 billion multifamily investment figure cannot support power-law arithmetic for this particular business.

The execution warning is that automating internal work leaves permits, architects, builders, financing, and construction on the critical path. Phoenix’s reported 10.7% vacancy in Q3 2026 adds underwriting exposure. This is not the full technical-and-manufacturing failure stack in my red flags, and the dossier establishes no adverse founder conduct. But the evidence that could justify an exception is also absent: independently demonstrated technical performance, persistent customer transactions, or a validated deployment path. Listed backers and automation percentages cannot substitute for those observations.

I would pass on a personal check today. HABU may become a capable developer; the dossier does not yet explain why it becomes the last mover. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: a repeat mandate from the same institutional investor following an independently audited project showing 10–15% lower total delivered cost, net of HABU’s fees, attributable to its proprietary system. That would connect automation to customer behavior and economic capture. Today, that connection remains the investment question.
