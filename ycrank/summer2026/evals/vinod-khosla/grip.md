# Grip -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence is real but not yet large enough to price as a compelling option. Plastic contamination in compost and biogas feedstock damages soil quality and preserves an ugly manual workflow, but Grip quantifies neither the affected waste volume nor the labor, disposal, or agricultural cost it can eliminate. A $3.29 billion waste-sorting-robot market growing 19.6% annually describes a category, not a 100x societal consequence. I cannot infer an orders-of-magnitude outcome from a sustainability label.

The potentially important mechanism is Grip’s combination of learning-based manipulation and custom gripper hardware. Wet, dirty, overlapping organic waste may defeat the pneumatic systems used by Glacier and Recycleye, while successful and failed grasps could create a proprietary learning loop. But “human-hand-like dexterity” is not a benchmark. Grip reports no pick rate, uptime, contaminant-removal rate, accuracy, or comparison against EverestLabs’ dataset of more than five billion objects. This may be differentiated physical AI; the dossier does not yet prove a 5–10x discontinuity.

The bull case rests on Jonas Gruetter. His reinforcement-learning work included field tests with a 12-ton excavator and irregular outdoor materials, giving him unusually relevant experience for an industrial manipulation problem. A four-person team entering the neglected organic-waste niche could turn one difficult workflow into a broader manipulation platform. That fits my preference for scarce physical workflows that produce proprietary operating data. The painful counterpoint is that the prior system achieved 70% task success, while Grip discloses no evidence that its current hardware works at all in the faster, dirtier, maintenance-sensitive conditions of a waste line.

The largest kill risk is therefore measurable facility reliability, yet no milestone attacks it. There is no paid pilot, installation footprint, retrofit plan, pass/fail threshold, experiment cost, or stop condition. Founder-led outreach is appropriate at pre-seed, and I do not penalize missing revenue, but capital should first determine whether the robot can sustain useful throughput in wet organic material—not fund generic hiring or sales before that answer exists.

Economic gravity is equally unresolved. Grip has disclosed no price, lease terms, maintenance burden, utilization model, or fully loaded cost per ton. The EU packaging rules applying from August 12, 2026 may force operators to care, but regulation cannot substitute for Chindia-price economics. The system ultimately must beat manual removal or incumbent sortation on cost per contaminant removed without permanent compliance premiums.

I pass today. This is not an AI wrapper, and none of the explicit red flags is active; the problem is insufficient affirmative evidence on the gates that matter. Nor does a known exception rescue it: the dossier shows neither a giant quantified value pool nor an all-in founder mission consequential enough to override missing product proof. One verifiable result would flip me: a paid six-month facility pilot demonstrating at least 95% target-contaminant removal, 90% uptime, and a fully loaded cost per ton below manual sorting.
