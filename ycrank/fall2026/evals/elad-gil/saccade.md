# Saccade -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Saccade’s market depends on whether eye tracking can identify pathology accurately enough to change a clinical decision at a lower total cost than existing testing. The customer is initially a longevity clinic; the proposed product is a 15-minute assessment before memory symptoms appear. But the May 2025 FDA clearance of an Alzheimer’s blood test raises the competitive bar. Being noninvasive is insufficient. Saccade needs to establish where its assessment belongs in the diagnostic pathway and why adding it improves that pathway’s economics.

The decisive variable is diagnostic performance in the intended asymptomatic population. The launch claims detection up to 15 years before symptoms, while Saccade’s homepage explicitly says diagnosis and individual risk prediction remain unvalidated. That disclosure does not establish that the technology fails. It establishes that the investment’s controlling proposition remains unproven. False positives could generate additional confirmatory testing rather than savings, particularly when screening younger people. An early risk result also does not itself establish treatment eligibility: Kisunla requires confirmed amyloid pathology and initiation at mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia. The broader $10.2 billion diagnostics estimate therefore tells me little about the market this particular assessment can capture.

The strongest bull case is that Saccade has built a real measurement system with a plausible early distribution channel. UCSB documents custom cameras, illumination, chassis, and thermal management, and the company reports deployment in San Francisco clinics in October 2026. Shreyas Bhardwaj’s medical-device and soft-robotics background bears directly on building that equipment. This fits my willingness to fund tools for a physical-industry transition before the ultimate market fully develops. However, engineering competence is not evidence of prior startup learning or costly personal commitment; those remain undocumented. The scientific-research exception also needs causal evidence, unusually strong relevant research credentials, and a credible capital path. This dossier does not establish that combination.

I give limited credit to the reported clinic deployments, but their evidentiary value is constrained by undisclosed assessment volumes and the homepage’s recruitment for future research. Those are unresolved availability disclosures, rather than evidence of failed adoption. The $7,500 competition award establishes recognition, not customer pull. On durability, controlling the sensor-to-task-to-analysis process gives Saccade more substance than a generic model interface, and better models could improve interpretation of its recordings. Yet confirmed-pathology feedback, retained longitudinal state, and clinical integrations are undocumented. ViewMind’s overlapping eye-tracking offering also means that neither custom hardware nor a patent-pending label establishes an exclusive position.

I would pass with my own money today. Saccade has demonstrated more about building the instrument than about the clinical and economic advantage that would make clinics repeatedly buy it. My physical-business concern remains unresolved: there is no documented change in incumbent unit economics, so I cannot underwrite software-like value capture. One result would reopen the investment: an independently run prospective study in the intended asymptomatic population demonstrating at least 90% sensitivity at 95% specificity against an accepted amyloid reference, with a lower total cost per correctly referred patient than a blood-test-first pathway. Those are my investment thresholds, not reported results. That study would connect the engineering achievement to a market that can carry the company.
