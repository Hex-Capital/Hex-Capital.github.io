# Zaplar -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Five hotels joined **Zaplar**’s waitlist in 48 hours, but that demand belongs to an earlier event-booking product rather than the full hotel operating system now being sold. That produces a specific signature: **Zaplar** has the outline of an index-like workflow position, yet almost no evidence that customers are buying or repeatedly using it. An operating layer spanning rooms, events, spa, food and beverage could compound with every hotel workflow; today, however, the curve is five expressions of interest, zero disclosed revenue, and no named live deployment. That is closer to a market thesis than something “just working.”

**Stripe** is the high rung on the index dimension because every incremental e-commerce transaction strengthened its position as the payments default. **Zaplar** is attempting the analogous control point: one property model through which bookings, payments, staffing, messaging, and operational agents run. The analogy is load-bearing because a hotel that truly runs through Zaplar would generate configuration, workflow, and guest-data switching costs. The divergence is distribution: Stripe entered through APIs that developers could adopt incrementally, while Zaplar asks hotels to replace PMS, POS, booking, and messaging systems already supplied by **Mews**, **Cloudbeds**, and **Oracle**. Zaplar therefore has index architecture without index adoption.

**Lyft** exposed the low-rung failure mechanism: apparent scale does not rescue a structurally weak position when the evidence cannot show improving economics or share inversion. **Zaplar** is earlier, but the false-signal problem is recognizable. SEK 3 million raised, NVIDIA Inception acceptance, 821 LinkedIn followers, and California Hotel & Lodging Association membership are financing and visibility signals, not revenue velocity. Zaplar’s five-hotel waitlist arrived before the expansion from event automation to full-stack replacement, so even that demand cannot validate the current product. For an AI company, no revenue, repeat usage, named pilot, or deployment curve keeps Zaplar below the threshold where consensus pays.

**Mews’s $300 million Series D** is the mechanism acting on Zaplar’s market-reopening score. Cheaper capable agents can now execute hospitality workflows rather than merely answer guest messages, and over 18 months that capability should expand the automatable surface area. Zaplar could benefit as hotels consolidate fragmented systems, but Mews and Canary are responding to the same catalyst with installed distribution and capital. Zaplar’s “why now” is inferred from competitor activity rather than pinned to a regulatory change, technological discontinuity, or incumbent collapse that uniquely opens a window for Zaplar. The category is moving, but the puck is already surrounded.

**Axel Andersson Lingbert** worked in hotels and restaurants, and Zaplar interviewed more than 100 hotels before writing code. Those are useful founder-market signals, while Jon Milles’s security work and Oscar Karlströmer’s A$1 million-plus B2B projects add technical and commercial range. The counterfactual that flips Zaplar’s founder score is straightforward: show that Axel, Jon, Douglas, and Oscar shipped working property deployments between successive hotel conversations, with operators returning to use newly built workflows. Zaplar currently discloses no prior independently shipped product, public artifact, live customer iteration, or repeat prototype usage. Four co-founders plus a CFO title at an eight-person pre-seed company also raises my unequal-authority concern unless Axel is demonstrably the final decision-maker.

**Color Genomics** worked because it put workflow and data infrastructure on top of commodity sequencing rather than attempting to reinvent sequencing, clinical operations, distribution, and regulation simultaneously. Zaplar begins with the attractive version of that pattern—agents on top of hotel operations—but then expands into replacing PMS, POS, booking, messaging, and payments across jurisdictions where Oracle supports fiscal requirements in more than 200 countries and territories. Zaplar therefore needs reliable agent execution, successful full-stack migration, localized compliance, incumbent displacement, and changed hotel buying behavior. That is not a single miracle. Zaplar’s staff-approval interface mitigates autonomous-action liability, but it does not collapse the dependency chain.

**Oracle Hospitality’s multinational footprint** makes my 36-month forecast concrete: Zaplar will retreat from complete hotel-OS replacement into an event-booking or operational-agent layer that integrates with incumbent PMS systems, and Zaplar will remain below €1 million ARR rather than becoming the system of record. The mechanism is the high/low signature: Zaplar’s property model is valuable enough to support a narrow workflow product, while the missing buying curve makes a simultaneous rip-and-replace strategy difficult to finance and deploy. Zaplar can create useful software without becoming an index on hospitality.

**SEK 3 million raised after more than 100 hotel interviews** supports the strongest bull case: Zaplar may be an early **Color Genomics**-style exception, using now-validated models as the commodity hard layer while four founders build the operational workflow above it. Five waitlist hotels in 48 hours and an eight-person organization could indicate unusually fast execution before YC Demo Day, and a unified architecture may ultimately be easier to automate than integrations across legacy systems. The objection stings because hotel software is genuinely fragmented. It does not yet win: Zaplar expanded scope before proving the narrow wedge, offers no live-property or revenue evidence, and faces Mews’s 15,000-customer distribution. I would flip to Invest if Zaplar migrated ten hotels onto the current full-stack product and then sustained greater than 20% month-over-month recurring-revenue growth for six months.
