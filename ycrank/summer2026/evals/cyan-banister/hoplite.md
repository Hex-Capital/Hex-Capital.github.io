# Hoplite -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Ryan Morrissey is explicitly CEO and Bence Redmond explicitly CTO, while Hoplite’s least convincing attribute is the frontier itself: Hoplite is a clearly piloted two-person vehicle driving into the most crowded software lane in Summer 2026. That pairing is Hoplite’s signature. The authority labels are visible; the magically weird artifact is not. I see Biz and Tiz in two builders who previously shipped Blueprints to 250 users, but the Rizz has to fight through a category where Cursor already exceeds $1 billion in annualized revenue.

**Anduril** had Palmer Luckey as an unmistakable center of gravity; the shared mechanism is that one founder owns the mission while technical leadership supports it. Hoplite approximates that structure with Morrissey as CEO and Redmond as CTO after building Blueprints together. Hoplite diverges where it matters: the dossier contains no equity split, operating agreement, founder-vesting protocol, or answer to who wins an irreconcilable argument. Titles are a good beginning, not a tiebreaker. I have watched that missing sentence become the whole company.

The **consensus YC AI-agent darling** fails because fashionable software abstraction is rented differentiation: the models improve, the incumbents copy the interface, and the founders discover they were wrapping somebody else’s velocity. Hoplite shows that pattern through dependencies on OpenAI and Anthropic, while Devin already hands local CLI sessions to the cloud and Factory already reaches local environments through Desktop. Hoplite has no hardware, regulatory thicket, industrial deployment, or ugly physical constraint that competitors refuse to touch. This is not three uninvestable things stapled together like Crusoe. It is three extremely investable keywords—AI, cloud, developer tools—stapled together.

Ryan Morrissey previously founded Induct, interned at Stripe, and processed more than €500,000 of prediction-market volume through Blueprints with Bence Redmond; Hoplite therefore comes from builders who have repeatedly touched software deployment, even if they have not visibly lived this exact pain. Over 18 months, Hoplite’s integrations with GitHub, Slack, Linear, MCP clients, and developer credentials could compound into hard-won operational knowledge. The July 2026 pivot from prediction markets to cloud agents cuts the other way: Hoplite looks like capable founders searching quickly, not founders pulled toward one problem by biography. That is a rebuttal, not a hard no, but it keeps Hoplite well below the Niantic standard of a decade-long relationship with the underlying terrain.

Cursor’s more than $3 billion raised and Cognition’s $73 million ARR make Hoplite’s market conspicuously consensus. Hoplite would flip this dimension if sophisticated investors rejected its credential-migration architecture as too dangerous while one business proved that the supposedly dangerous design produced uniquely persistent usage. The current evidence—one founder-reported installation turning Sentry errors into pull requests, with no quantified revenue or speed improvement—does not establish that misread. Hoplite may be early, but early inside a stampede is different from non-consensus.

**Niantic** was observable magic: I played Ingress before I needed a deck, and a normal person could walk outside and feel the product rearrange the world. Hoplite has a smaller version of that truth. A developer can send Hoplite instructions through iMessage, watch Hoplite execute inside a cloud sandbox, and inspect the resulting pull request; one business reportedly watched ignored Sentry errors become proactive fixes. Yet Hoplite remains a developer abstraction handling repositories, MCP servers, and environment variables, not an artifact a normal human can ride, photograph, or encounter at a café. It is more touchable than an API, less visceral than a camera on a neighborhood street.

Devin already supports local-to-cloud handoff, so by July 2029 I predict Hoplite will have pivoted from general cloud coding agents into enterprise credential-and-environment orchestration and will produce $1 million–$3 million ARR rather than become an independent coding-agent category leader. Hoplite’s explicit CEO/CTO split should permit a fast, coherent pivot; Hoplite’s absence of a frontier moat makes that pivot necessary. I’m never playing the game I seem to be playing: the potentially durable business is not the agent, but the unpleasant plumbing required to let agents safely inherit how a company actually works.

Blueprints reached more than 250 users and €500,000 in trading volume before Morrissey and Redmond launched Hoplite, which is the strongest case that Hoplite is really a rapid-shipping founder bet. The bull mechanism is the **HQ Trivia** exception: a working, observable product can outrun governance and defensibility concerns long enough for usage to reveal the real moat, and Hoplite already has one business installation plus prepaid Stripe credits. The evidence that wins today is that Blueprints validated an unrelated product, Hoplite’s current installation has no quantified revenue, and Devin and Factory are already converging on Hoplite’s core workflow. Ten paying businesses at $25,000 or more in ARR each, all retained for 90 days, would flip my verdict; that would show Hoplite is becoming the environment layer rather than another agent interface.
