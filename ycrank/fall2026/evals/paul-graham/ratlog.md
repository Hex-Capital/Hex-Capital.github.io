# RATLOG -- Paul Graham Evaluation

RATLOG’s most interesting number is the under-$30,000 cost of its first engine hot-fire. Four months from building to firing is a useful sign of technical impatience. But it leaves my first question unanswered: what broke, and what did the founders do next? Eliminating the turbopump is a proposed engineering solution. The dossier does not describe an encountered obstacle followed by a founder-built workaround. I give the hot-fire credit as an artifact, without counting it again as proof of resourcefulness.

The founders have relevant experience. Kunhart designed Argonaut engine nozzles, Weise worked on lunar-lander research, and Kelm led electronics at Innuendo. Their shared propulsion work is more informative than merely having studied aerospace engineering. RATLOG also fits a pattern I like: technical founders producing something concrete before asking investors to believe an enormous claim. The limitation is what this particular experiment establishes. A hot-fire establishes combustion. Without thrust, duration, efficiency, or bearing-life measurements, it does not establish that removing the turbopump produces a better launch engine. The claimed 90% reduction in parts could simplify manufacturing while moving the difficulty into the rotating assembly.

The strongest case for investing is that these founders can run cheap experiments quickly while learning directly from customers. They report more than 70 customer discussions and $400 million in signed launch reservations. Satellite operators facing reported waits of three to four years have a plausible reason to entertain a crude alternative. But reservation terms matter enormously here. An operator can reserve capacity years ahead without depending on RATLOG today. Undisclosed payments, cancellation rights, and unnamed counterparties make these agreements provisional demand evidence. They cannot yet tell me who needs this badly enough to bear some of the development risk.

RATLOG’s proposed shared-flight pricing per kilogram connects its engineering claim to a business, but no published price or demonstrated vehicle economics closes that connection. SpaceX already supplies reusable launches. The decisive question is whether this engine makes another supplier substantially easier to build. The CEO’s acknowledgment of increased bearing demands is encouraging candor, though it is not evidence of a tested design revision. Similarly, the founders’ propulsion backgrounds establish competence without establishing that this business arose from a specific problem they personally encountered. I leave both adaptability and problem origin at their missing-evidence defaults.

I would pass on the present evidence. I do not see documented passivity, premature delegation, or dependence on a promised distribution partner; this is an evidence gap rather than an active founder red flag. I also checked the exception for a working prototype strong enough to override incomplete founder evidence. RATLOG’s hot-fire makes that exception relevant, but its undisclosed performance does not yet justify it, and reservations do not supply unmistakable customer pull. One result would change my decision: a documented bearing failure followed within weeks by a founder-built revision that completes an independently witnessed, flight-relevant endurance test at specified thrust. That would connect the artifact to the founder behavior I need to see.
