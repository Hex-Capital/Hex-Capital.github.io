# Shire Intelligence -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The useful fact about Shire Intelligence is that two founders have worked restaurant shifts together. Alex Tabaku spent four years in restaurants; Cameron Kuperman also worked in them, and the two have known each other since kindergarten. They have some reason to notice what software designers miss about a busy dining room. But I want the next story: what broke during service, what they built to fix it, and whether it worked the following night. The dossier does not contain that sequence. Shared experience earns credit for choosing the problem. It cannot establish resourcefulness under resistance.

There is a plausible small beginning here. A host needs to know which tables are available and which server can take another party. Existing cameras might remove the need to keep reconstructing that information manually. Shire has built a prototype using SAM3 and DINOv3, and its founders report winning NexHacks’ general track. That is more than an architectural drawing. It remains a founder-reported demonstration, without measured accuracy or latency during actual service. Recognizing an occupied table and recognizing that its occupants need something are different technical accomplishments. I give the prototype limited credit without assuming it has solved the second problem.

The strongest argument for investing is that the founders already understand the work and may have access to restaurants willing to tolerate an imperfect product. Mathews Restaurant provides a named testimonial reporting $65,000 in processing savings. But that establishes a relationship around payments; it does not establish repeated demand for camera-driven coordination. Shire’s claims of 14% more monthly guests and seven-minute faster turns would be much more persuasive with a named deployment and measurement period. At $40 a month, the intelligence product could be easy to try. The difficult question is whether staff keep following its instructions when service gets busy. An inexpensive subscription cannot compensate for interruptions that employees learn to ignore.

Shire resembles my unstated preference for technical teams that make something concrete before asking investors to believe a large claim. The resemblance stops short of the numerical proof that makes those bets compelling. The working-prototype exception deserves consideration, but this prototype has neither independently validated performance nor unmistakable user pull strong enough to replace the missing founder evidence. I also would not manufacture red flags: a reseller option is not proof of dependence on a large partner, and the CFO title is not proof that Tabaku has stopped building or selling. The actual uncertainty is narrower. We have no documented response to a deployment obstacle and no evidence-driven revision of the product. Those are unknowns, not evidence of passivity or stubbornness.

I would pass at present. Restaurant experience and an existing prototype make Shire worth watching, but they do not clear my founder-action gate. Camera setup, staff adoption, and footage handling could become the ugly work through which these founders learn something competitors miss; today they are unresolved work, not an advantage already earned. My flip condition is one documented restaurant deployment where a specific service failure prompts a founder-built correction within a week, followed by four weeks of paid, repeated staff use. That would connect the founders’ knowledge to resourcefulness and real demand. It would change the reason to invest.
