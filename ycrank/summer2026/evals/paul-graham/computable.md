# Computable -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what broke for these founders. Computable says the team spent years building trading infrastructure at Jump Trading and Coinbase, but the dossier never identifies a GPU lease they personally could not escape, a supplier they persuaded after being refused, or a liquidity failure they repaired. Credentials explain why they could build this market. They do not show what they did when reality resisted. On the criterion I care about most, the evidence is simply absent.

The artifact is much better than the founder story. Computable has deployed a self-service market with resting bids and offers, transferable weekly reservations, resale, and physical redemption into eight-H100 nodes. Public listings span August 2026 through January 2027 at $2.56–$2.69 per GPU-hour. This is not a diagram or a plan to begin after funding. Two people have built a technically and operationally nontrivial exchange before asking permission. Their electronic-market background makes the implementation credible, though the dossier supplies no matched-trade volume, redemption record, or independently measured performance.

But an exchange is not an order book. It is trades. The Show HN launch produced 45 points and 26 comments, yet there is no named buyer niche, paid transaction, repeat trader, or user who resold capacity after a workload changed. Even the visible listings prove only that capacity can be offered, not that users pull the product back. The 2% fee on each side describes revenue mechanics rather than revenue. The standing-buyback language is particularly dangerous: the website suggests every position receives a quote, while the terms disclaim any obligation to repurchase or guarantee a price. If users discover that “instant liquidity” disappears precisely when they need it, the product’s central promise inverts.

The bull case is real. GPU procurement contains exactly the sort of ugly work startups can hide inside: fragmented supply, delivery assurance, performance disputes, forward pricing, expiring capacity, and a regulatory perimeter around resellable future-dated positions. A proprietary history of bids and trades could compound into useful price discovery, while the calendar-week grid may concentrate liquidity better than a market supporting every possible duration. Runpod’s approximately $240 million annualized revenue and Vast.ai’s more than $1 million monthly revenue show that developers already spend heavily outside the major clouds. Computable might therefore have built the right primitive before public demand becomes visible.

The objection that defeats this case is SF Compute. It already offers buy orders, sell orders, and resale while managing more than $100 million of third-party hardware. Computable’s narrower time grid is an interface choice, not yet a moat. Nor is there evidence that the founders changed routes after encountering this competitor, failed trades, or customer objections. The team may be a pair of fierce exchange builders, but the current evidence looks more like a market mechanism searching for its narrow well than users forcing a crude product into existence. Within 36 months, I would expect Computable either to become a managed GPU procurement broker rather than a liquid exchange or to remain below $1 million ARR.

I am passing today because the operational gate fails: there is no documented founder workaround and no demonstrated user pull. The one condition that would flip me is verifiable evidence that at least ten independent customers completed trades and that at least three returned to buy, resell, or redeem capacity within eight consecutive weeks. That would simultaneously prove liquidity, narrow-well demand, and that the deployed machinery survives contact with users.
