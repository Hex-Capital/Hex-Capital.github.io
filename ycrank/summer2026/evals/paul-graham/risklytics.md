# Risklytics -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Risklytics is strongest where the work is ugly and weakest where the idea touches the founders’ own lives. Risklytics must obtain producer licenses, interpret AI exclusions, and persuade admitted or E&S carriers to accept physical-world liability, yet neither Samuel Gold nor Alexander Risio has worked in insurance, robotics, underwriting, or commercial risk. This is a schlep discovered from the outside. That can produce a brokerage, but it rarely produces the obsessive insight of someone building the thing he wishes existed.

Coinbase (2012) is the high rung because compliance was not incidental to the product; Brian Armstrong ran toward the part everyone else avoided. Risklytics shares that mechanism: carriers reportedly began adding explicit AI exclusions in January 2026, and Risklytics must navigate licensing, carrier appetite, policy language, and renewals. But Risklytics has not yet obtained its producer license or placed a single policy. Armstrong had built the wallet before asking me to reconsider him. Risklytics has identified the swamp, but Risklytics has not yet shown it can cross it.

Loopt (2005) exposed the danger of funding weak product timing because one founder seemed sufficiently animal to bend reality around it. Risklytics has the weaker half of that exception: no evidence that Gold or Risio personally encountered an uninsured robot, fought an exclusion at renewal, or needed the proposed brokerage themselves. Risklytics instead pivoted from Ember’s natural-disaster scoring beta into frontier-tech insurance. That reads like moving toward an interesting market observation, not following an organic idea out of one’s own life. Unlike Loopt, Risklytics has not supplied the force-of-personality evidence that could override the missing founder-problem fit.

Samuel Gold took leave from Harvard, and Risklytics did ship Ember into beta before changing direction. Those are faint animal tracks, not an animal sighting. Over the next 18 months, resourcefulness would compound through licenses obtained unusually fast, underwriters recruited through side doors, and founders manually rewriting submissions until carriers say yes. Risklytics currently has 91 LinkedIn followers, no disclosed revenue, and no current-product placement. If Risklytics waits for permission instead of improvising around the brokerage’s constraints, the schlep will consume it rather than protect it.

Alexander Risio is pursuing a Harvard computer-science A.B./S.M., but Risklytics shows no public repository, Hacker News artifact, or non-trivial technical system attached to either founder. The counterfactual is simple: a working exclusion parser trained on real policy forms, an open dataset of carrier language, or a submission tool underwriters repeatedly use would move Risklytics up a full tier on hacker genealogy. A CS credential alone does not do this. Hackers leave artifacts, and Risklytics presently offers a risk-intake form and a prior beta whose work did not survive the pivot.

Stripe (2010) had two founders whose long working relationship and shared commerce history made the pair itself an advantage. Risklytics also has the preferred number—Gold and Risio are a two-founder Harvard pair—but Risklytics provides no evidence of when they met, what they previously built together, or how their roles divide across software and brokerage operations. Two is better than one. Two names on a YC page are not yet the Collison brothers.

Risklytics will, by July 2029, have pivoted away from a specialized physical-AI brokerage and back toward property or infrastructure risk-analytics software, with less than $1 million in recurring revenue. The mechanism is the dimensional signature: Risklytics has found a genuine insurance schlep, but without lived insurance pain or carrier relationships, Risklytics is more likely to build analytical tools around the bottleneck than to endure years of manually owning it. Ember’s property-level natural-disaster model already reveals the adjacent destination.

Newfront’s reported $3.1 billion in annual premiums placed and the January 2026 arrival of explicit AI exclusions form the strongest bull case. Risklytics could be early to a new insurance category, and the Ember pivot could be resourcefulness rather than drift—the sort of idea-level awkwardness I initially misread in Airbnb (2009). But Airbnb brought cereal-box revenue; Risklytics brings pending licensure, no placements, no founder insurance history, and no hacker trail. I would reverse the pass if Risklytics obtained its licenses and placed ten named physical-AI customers through renewal within twelve months, because that would prove Risklytics can turn the schlep into an artifact rather than merely describe it.
