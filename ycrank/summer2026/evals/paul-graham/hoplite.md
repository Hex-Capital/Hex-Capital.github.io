# Hoplite -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that cloud coding agents are fashionable. It is that laptops fail under parallel agents, local sessions do not travel cleanly, and Git worktrees, ports, dependencies, CLIs, and MCP servers become a deployment mess. Hoplite has shipped a workaround that moves this state into cloud environments and exposes the result through Slack, Linear, iMessage, and Sentry ([YC Launch](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/SAM-hoplite-effortless-cloud-agents-for-programming), Jul 2026). But the dossier never shows which blocker first hit Morrissey or Redmond, how quickly they responded, or what changed after a failed attempt. I see resourcefulness in the product and their hands-on installations, but not yet the complete obstacle-action-result sequence.

The strongest evidence is the artifact itself. Hoplite is deployed at one business, where ignored Sentry errors became proactive pull requests and lower-priority tickets were continuously merged. That is considerably better than a diagram or promised milestone. It proves the team can make several difficult systems work together before receiving institutional permission. It does not yet prove a narrow well of demand. The customer is unnamed, payment is undisclosed, and there is no retention or repeated-usage interval. One installation shows that the machine runs; it does not show that users pull it back when the founders stop pushing.

The bull case is that these founders have already behaved more like builders than applicants. They met in university, built apps, freelanced, and founded products together. Their previous CarbonCopy product reportedly reached more than 250 public-beta users and $855,000 in trading volume. They are now traveling to customers and configuring software factories manually instead of waiting for scalable onboarding. Hoplite also fits a real revealed pattern: technical users increasingly want programmable infrastructure around autonomous actors. But the current-product proof spike is still only one installation, while the impressive numbers belong to the prior product. Transferring founder credit is reasonable; transferring traction is not.

The uncomfortable red flag is a pair of young dropouts selling security-sensitive infrastructure to businesses while Cursor and Factory are already converging on local-to-cloud handoff, persistent environments, Slack triggers, and alert-driven workflows. Their Stripe and AWS internships soften the enterprise-inexperience problem, and founder-led installation is exactly the right response. The known youth exception represented by Bindwell shared young technical founders, but it also had numerical benchmarks and laboratory validation strong enough to override the risk. Hoplite has a working deployment, not comparable independent proof. There is also no moat yet, while credentials, source code, and MCP configuration make a single security failure unusually costly.

The pivot from CarbonCopy to Hoplite may reveal flexibility, but the dossier does not name the observation that falsified the old route or show that a stable goal survived the change. Likewise, internships in cloud infrastructure are not evidence that the founders personally suffered this problem. The schlep is real—environment reconstruction, credentials, integrations, security, and manual installation—but its origin in these founders’ own need is undocumented. My 36-month base case is that Hoplite pivots from a general cloud-agent layer to managed security and environment orchestration for enterprise coding agents, remaining below $3 million ARR.

I would not write the check yet. The product proof is good enough to keep watching, and the manual installations could uncover a valuable wedge, but the people-first operating gate is not met by inference. The one flip condition is a current customer renewing after at least eight weeks and documenting that a founder shipped a specific deployment-blocking fix within 14 days. That would simultaneously demonstrate pull and the response to resistance that matters most.
