# Waddle Labs -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Waddle Labs’s contested truth is precise: a reasoning agent that writes, tests, and repairs reusable robot-control code can adapt robots more efficiently than either brittle classical programming or repeated end-to-end model training. What would prove it wrong? If independently measured deployments show that the generated programs do not transfer across robots and workspaces—or require as much intervention and new data as the systems they replace—the secret disappears. Six months of internal operation and roughly 1,000 overnight LEGO repetitions establish that the product exists. They do not establish the comparative claim. This is a falsifiable, secret-shaped hypothesis, but not yet a demonstrated secret.

The more serious problem is value capture. Waddle has accumulated intervention traces and a shared skill library, yet code-as-policy research is public, CaP-X and ASPIRE already implement execution-feedback loops, and well-financed competitors can pursue similar architectures. The preview API does not identify a bounded market Waddle can dominate; “robotics developers and laboratories” is a population, not a beachhead. Nor is the library yet a network effect: no deployment count, cross-customer reuse, retention, or improvement curve shows that one robot’s work makes the next customer harder to dislodge. Proprietary technology or commodity architecture? Today the evidence points toward the latter.

Song and Ye are the best part of the case. They met at MIT’s Research Science Institute, became Harvard roommates, and bring directly relevant work in program induction, multimodal models, and robot policies. This is real prior alignment rather than a convenience pairing. But the foundation remains incompletely documented: both are listed as class of 2028, full-time status is unstated, ownership is unknown, and the 2025 teleoperation product named a third co-founder who is absent from the current YC team. A pivot is not a defect; an unexplained discontinuity in product and founding structure is a question about what company actually persists through the pivots.

The strongest bull case is that Waddle is building a neglected control layer in the emerging AI production stack. Its agent can use foundation models as interchangeable tools while retaining the programs, execution traces, and corrections produced around them. If those artifacts transfer, the dependency becomes leverage: better third-party models improve Waddle while its proprietary operating history compounds. The internal demonstrations support that possibility. But no known exception rescues the present evidence. There is no independently verified zero-to-one technical result, persistent customer behavior, or paid production deployment strong enough to override the exposed architecture and absent monopoly mechanism.

The economics are still indefinite. The dossier offers no price, paid tier, revenue, gross margin, named buyer, sales owner, or implementation burden. The cited $1.671 billion industrial robot-programming market is not fund-return arithmetic, and a possible consumption API is not a revenue engine. Distribution currently means 971 X followers, preview registration, founder email, and a YC launch. Those can recruit experiments, but they say nothing about whether a robotics lab, integrator, or factory operator will purchase autonomous code execution—or accept its safety and certification risks—at a contract value that supports the sales cycle.

I pass on a personal check. My 36-month expectation is that Waddle either pivots back toward teleoperation and robot-data infrastructure or remains below $1 million ARR as a general-purpose control-agent API. The painful counterfactual is that these two founders have discovered the right abstraction before the market recognizes it, and that six months of traces become the seed of a cross-robot skill network. One verifiable result would flip my decision: three named industrial customers paying to reuse the same agent-created skills across different robot vendors, with independently reported deployment time at least ten times lower than conventional programming. That would connect secret, monopoly, and distribution. Until then, Waddle has an ingenious experiment but not yet a great company no one else is starting.
