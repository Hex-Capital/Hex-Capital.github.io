# OpEra -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

What would OpEra know that Nextmv’s customers do not? The plausible proposition is that planners can maintain sophisticated optimization models through ordinary language, making bespoke operations research economical to deploy broadly. That is my reconstruction; the dossier supplies neither a named prevailing belief rejecting it nor a test of constraint-translation accuracy. OpEra’s retailer result—38% lower delivery costs and 10% more on-time deliveries—comes from a historical backtest against nearest-store, single-order dispatch. It demonstrates an opportunity to improve that baseline. It does not establish the proposed secret: that the expensive work of building and maintaining operational models has become substantially easier.

The founders are the strongest reason to investigate. Rossignol and Chalita met at MIT’s Operations Research Center and collaborated on retailer routing before presenting this company. Their shared project supplies affirmative evidence of working alignment, and Chalita’s published implementation shows substantive technical work. This clears the concern about founders assembled merely to apply to YC. Full-time status, ownership, compensation, and control remain undisclosed; those omissions establish no adverse founding behavior. I give credit for demonstrated collaboration without assuming the entire founding structure is sound.

The decisive commercial question is whether each deployment makes the next one easier—or merely creates another custom model to maintain. OpEra currently spans factories, warehouses, and fleets without identifying a bounded market it can dominate. Its tariff-aware routing implementation is concrete, but the dossier establishes no exclusive data, comparative technical advantage, network effect, or measured switching cost. Nextmv/FICO already supplies decision-model infrastructure, and OR-Tools supplies important solver components. Neither fact proves OpEra interchangeable with those products; together they make ownership of the customer-specific modeling layer the essential unanswered question. My observed preference for factories and the AI production stack offers little additional conviction here: OpEra has not demonstrated control of a scarce industrial bottleneck.

The strongest objection to passing is that these founders may have already discovered a repeatable way to translate messy operating rules into valuable decisions. Alongside the retailer result, OpEra reports a 10.4% higher premium for a sugar producer and 54% fewer unfulfilled marketplace orders. That breadth could indicate a reusable modeling capability. It could also reflect three successful bespoke projects; the dossier does not distinguish them. I checked the known-exception routes: there is no independently established technical breakthrough, persistent transaction behavior, or validated deployment path that directly resolves the monopoly question. The relevant red flag is substitutable optimization software, while the benchmark limitation constrains what the results establish; it does not demonstrate a collapsed technical claim.

OpEra names plausible buyers—COOs, plant managers, and supply-chain executives—and offers an initial-site deployment through requested introductions. That earns modest distribution credit. There is no demonstrated acquisition process or sales-cycle evidence, and undisclosed pricing and margins leave the economics of implementation unresolved. The adjacent $11.03 billion market cannot supply the missing arithmetic for one scalable revenue engine. I pass on investing today. My single verifiable flip condition is a replicated paid deployment in one defined customer segment showing that the same modeling core beats the incumbent optimized workflow and can be installed and maintained within the contracted software economics. That would connect the founders’ demonstrated ability to a business capable of retaining the value it creates.
