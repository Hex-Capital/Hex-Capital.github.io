# Ooak Data -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

If Ooak Data works, agents learn from actual multi-step business activity rather than artificial text tasks. That could make expert workflows reproducible at machine cost. But the dossier does not quantify that consequence. Sub-50% task success and roughly 25% eight-run consistency describe a technical deficiency; a $4.44 billion training-data market describes vendor spending. Neither shows which expert-service cost pool collapses, how many workers or companies benefit, or why the improvement becomes 10x rather than incremental.

Licensed operational data, automated anonymization, and structurally preserved digital twins are more substantive than another interface around a prevailing model. Ooak also identifies named alternatives: Mercor reconstructs applications, AfterQuery uses real APIs and expert-authored tasks, and Scale AI offers a broader platform. Yet there is no benchmark showing that agents trained on Ooak environments complete workflows more accurately, learn faster, or cost less to train. Seven-figure contracts with the two largest AI labs prove expensive demand. They do not prove a 5–10x technical discontinuity. Today this could still be scarce data procurement wrapped in bespoke implementation labor.

The strongest bull case is unusually strong for pre-seed. Five people have secured seven-figure contracts with the two largest labs, smaller-lab partnerships, and a contracted source-data pipeline. The founders also demonstrate some adaptability: they made one pivot during the first year and later added Thomas Aubry, whose ML, robotics, and financial-data experience complements Pierre-Louis Vouteau’s distribution background and Grégoire Lamy’s product-operations record. My unstated pattern permits execution-heavy platforms when a small team attacks a giant existing value pool. Ooak clears that exception test better than most fashionable AI-data startups.

But capital allocation is running ahead of falsification. The plan is to acquire and anonymize more than 300 company ecosystems in six months while filling six roles. The dossier does not name the largest kill risk, whether that is re-identification, transferable model improvement, reusable licensing rights, or customer internalization. It supplies no cheapest experiment, numerical threshold, cost, or stop branch. Customer concentration makes this omission worse: two labs can provide impressive revenue and still dictate pricing, decline renewal, or internalize production.

The economic model is equally unfinished. Ooak may pay a source company as much as $300,000, while buyer pricing, reuse rights, anonymization cost, dataset yield, and gross margins remain undisclosed. A reusable library could amortize acquisition cost across many labs and become difficult to reproduce. A customer-specific corpus with narrow licensing would instead remain a high-revenue services business. The relevant parity test is whether each acquired workflow generates repeatable training value at lower cost than expert reconstruction or synthetic environments; there is no bottom-up evidence yet.

My 36-month base case is that Ooak becomes a valuable, concentrated supplier and is acquired by Mercor for its licensed workflow corpus rather than becoming an independent foundational platform. I pass today, despite exceptional commercial validation. One verifiable result would flip me: a buyer-verified benchmark showing at least a 2x improvement in end-to-end workflow completion over Mercor or AfterQuery environments at comparable per-task data cost, reproduced across three independent lab customers.
