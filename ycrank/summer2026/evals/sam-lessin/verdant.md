# Verdant -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first three numbers are blank: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely the first scarce check. That forces the financing score to its low prior. More importantly, Verdant has not said what this round proves. “Build an AI-native permitting platform” is a company description, not a finite experiment with a metric and deadline. I cannot tell whether my money buys a truth-revealing test or merely another year of product development before the next raise.

The most credible asset is access. Aidan Ng has worked across DOJ, USDA, Capitol Hill, Deloitte’s government practice, and commercial-real-estate underwriting; Jason Yi has worked on government HR software, legislative tracking, privacy, and public-benefits automation. Their 200-plus interviews with local-government employees show they can cross the government-software boundary and identify a painful workflow. But the dossier supplies job histories and interviews, not a shipped municipal deployment or an operating result on both sides of that bridge.

The potential dark pool is jurisdiction-specific configuration: ordinances, forms, GIS data, review practices, and the institutional knowledge required to move an application through intake, hearings, and reports. If deployments compound into proprietary workflow data, Verdant could become hard to remove. Today that is an architectural possibility, not an owned advantage. No jurisdiction, paid pilot, repeat usage, or purchase is disclosed. Meanwhile GovWell already offers AI completeness and code checks, Clariti owns CivCheck, and PermitFlow reports 12 million permitting datapoints. Publicly available models plus public ordinances do not constitute scarcity.

The strongest bull case is that local permitting is exactly the sort of ugly, fragmented operating system where a focused team can outrun horizontal incumbents. Governments were already testing AI pre-checks and awarding live contracts in 2025–2026, Verdant leaves approval authority with staff, and its modular order forms could limit deployment scope. The 200 interviews may therefore represent the beginning of unusually informed distribution rather than customer discovery theater. But there is not yet an operating receipt—conversion, retention, accuracy, or revenue—strong enough to override the generic-AI risk. Human validation may be the correct safety design while still leaving Verdant with expensive implementation and weak software margins.

Delete the Series A and the model gets worse. Verdant discloses no price, gross margin, burn, runway, contract value, procurement cycle, or seed-funded route to break-even. The reported global market was only $307 million in 2025, while every jurisdiction may require ordinance, GIS, form, and workflow configuration. That can produce a durable local business, but the dossier does not show that implementation revenue finances the next deployment rather than creating a capital factory disguised as SaaS. My 36-month base case is that Verdant remains below $1 million ARR and narrows toward applicant-side pre-checks instead of replacing municipal permitting systems.

I am passing today. This is an informed team in a real problem, but I would be buying missing terms, an unnamed experiment, hypothetical proprietary data, and undisclosed economics. The one verifiable condition that flips me is three named jurisdictions converting pilots into paid annual contracts, with at least two renewing after twelve months; that would prove that Verdant’s configured workflow—not a demo, conference presence, or AI feature—actually causes durable purchasing.
