# Jinko -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Jinko could make travel executable by personal agents: discover a trip, pay, receive tickets, and resolve changes without sending the traveler through another booking interface. That is useful infrastructure. But what becomes ten or one hundred times cheaper if it works? The dossier supplies inventory breadth—800 airlines and 2.6 million hotels—without quantifying the expense or expert labor eliminated. Inventory access establishes what Jinko can sell. It does not establish the consequence of success. I cannot purchase an extraordinary outcome merely because agents become a new distribution channel.

The technical question is whether Jinko removes a binding constraint that inventory providers cannot readily remove themselves. Browser blocking and rigid search queries are real constraints identified by the company. Its API, MCP, and CLI offer a practical response. Yet Etraveli launched its own Flight MCP on October 28, 2026, covering payment, ticketing, and servicing; Nuitée also offers MCP access. Jinko has no disclosed comparative benchmark for booking reliability, search efficiency, or servicing automation. Competitive overlap does not prove its technology is ordinary. It does mean that a technical discontinuity remains unestablished.

The founders are the strongest reason to challenge my pass. They previously built Mowgli, acquired by Hopper, and bring complementary travel, distribution, and engineering experience. Their move from a consumer travel app to infrastructure, documented by August 2026, is an observed strategic revision. We do not know which evidence caused it or whether it improved the business. Still, these are people who have operated inside the problem. My actual investing pattern leaves room for execution-heavy transaction businesses; scientific novelty is not compulsory. The strongest exception case is that this team turns contracting-operator responsibility and cross-category servicing into infrastructure developers increasingly depend on. The Exa Connect listing gives that argument a concrete distribution foothold. It supplies no evidence yet that developers become dependent on Jinko or that direct supplier integrations lose.

The economics could decide this before market size does. Jinko charges 1% on confirmed flight bookings and $0.005 per search above a 1,500:1 search-to-book ratio. The excess-search charge acknowledges a genuine cost exposure. It does not establish profitable economics inside the included allowance. On a hypothetical $500 flight, the booking fee produces $5 before search, payment, supplier, and servicing costs. No disclosed cost model shows what remains. Likewise, the dossier provides no budgeted experiment with a threshold for resolving this uncertainty. I assign missing-evidence defaults here; neither absent revenue nor a six-person team is a reason to penalize a pre-seed company.

I pass at **27/100**. Jinko has credible operators and an operating product, but the evidence does not establish a consequential cost collapse or a defensible technical mechanism. Its integration layer deserves scrutiny under my concern about AI products differentiated mainly through interfaces; the contracting and servicing responsibilities make that concern a question rather than a proven indictment. One result would reopen my decision: a reproducible, matched deployment showing **10x lower fully loaded cost per completed and serviced flight booking than Etraveli**, at comparable reliability, including search and human intervention. That would turn the central claim into something I could underwrite.
