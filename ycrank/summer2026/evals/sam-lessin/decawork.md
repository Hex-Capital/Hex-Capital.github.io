# Decawork -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Sarthak Aggarwal built enterprise AI at NVIDIA and Ema, while Aman Raj built Barclays compliance tooling used by 240-plus teams. Decawork is therefore a high-conviction operator bet wearing a low-fit, consensus AI-control-plane costume: the founders have lived near the problem, but Decawork’s current category is precisely where seed capital is factory-farming interchangeable infrastructure. “AI-agent security is uninvestible” is the provocative version; the operational version is that Astrix, Oasis, Token, and Okta have already made the asset legible, crowded, and expensive before Decawork has disclosed current-product traction.

**Drop.io** is the high rung for asymmetric conviction: I built against free file-sharing incumbents because the primitive mattered before the market agreed on the product. Decawork shares part of that mechanism because Aggarwal and Raj previously operated an autonomous IT product across multiple enterprises, and Raj had already encountered compliance deployment at Barclays. Decawork diverges sharply from Drop.io because the dossier shows no three-year obsession, opinionated category writing, or evidence that the founders’ agent-control-plane belief predates the 2026 market wave; the June-to-July pivot could be a smart regatta tack, but it could equally be a hot-sector tack.

**OpenAI** is the low rung for sleeve fit—not because OpenAI is a weak business, but because foundation-model gravity turns adjacent infrastructure into consensus exposure with startup economics and platform dependency. Decawork sits in that wake: Decawork sells AI-agent infrastructure through founder-led enterprise demos, while Okta already offers discovery, registration, lifecycle management, and a kill switch across 8,200 integrations. None of my unstated sleeves rescues the fit: Decawork is not pre-consensus crypto, DTC, prescription-rail health, creator infrastructure, a holdco, or a Wizard-of-Oz service still hiding an illegible asset.

**Astrix Security’s $85 million**, Oasis Security’s $195 million, and Token Security’s $27 million make Decawork’s price test worse over the next 18 months, even though Decawork’s actual round terms are unavailable. Competition will compound into a financing problem: each incumbent integration makes Decawork spend more to establish trust, while YC and the “88% experienced an agent-security incident” statistic make the pitch more consensus, not less. With no disclosed tier-one pass, unusual pricing, or sub-batch valuation, Decawork receives below the 9/30 absent-evidence default; Decawork may be early as an operator, but the round is not demonstrably early as an asset.

Decawork’s founder-led 30-minute demo and undisclosed pricing imply a conventional enterprise-software cap table, while all disclosed production use belongs to the prior autonomous-IT product. For Decawork’s capital-structure score to flip a tier, the control plane would need to become a bootstrappable security appliance—paid pilots financing integrations and default-alive operations—rather than a land-and-expand SaaS product requiring several rounds to match Okta’s trust surface. Decawork has shown no revenue cushion, royalty structure, holdco logic, or other non-standard instrument, so the current tack looks founder-draining rather than structurally clever.

**John Fish / Bookshelved** had owned distribution before the financing instrument existed; the person and community were already investable assets. Decawork has 6,329 LinkedIn followers around Aggarwal and 4,698 around Raj, which is meaningfully better than anonymous enterprise founders but still not the same mechanism: Decawork has disclosed no newsletter, channel, customer community, or 10,000-user organic funnel. The follower base can reduce recruiting and outbound friction, but Decawork cannot yet put founder-brand cash flows on the cap table.

**Okta’s 8,200-integration catalog** points to my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Decawork will have pivoted back toward autonomous IT incident resolution, reached roughly $2 million to $5 million ARR there, and abandoned the attempt to remain a standalone agent-identity control plane. Decawork’s strongest asset is the founders’ prior operational tack across identity, endpoint, security, and collaboration systems; Decawork’s weakest asset is category ownership against converging identity incumbents. Missionary operators can survive Type 2 fun, but survival here probably means changing the boat.

Aman Raj’s Barclays deployment across 240-plus teams is the objection that stings: Decawork could use genuine compliance knowledge, prior enterprise production, and a severe 88% incident rate to become the **Sublime Security** exception—an enterprise-security seed where operator depth beats apparent crowding. Decawork could also invoke the **Pinterest** exception, where missionary temperament overrode consensus concerns, but Decawork has not shown Pinterest-like user pull, public conviction, or network-sourced pricing, and its current-product adoption remains undisclosed. I pass today; one verifiable condition would flip me to Invest: Decawork reaching $1 million ARR from the current agent control plane—not the prior autonomous-IT product—within twelve months.
