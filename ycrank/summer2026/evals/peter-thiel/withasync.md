# Async -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Ben Smith dropped out at twenty and repeatedly shipped products, while Async moved from nonprofit fundraising agents to Skope billing, legal agents, and now horizontal small-business automation. Async is therefore an outsider-founder adaptation bet whose highest relative signal is founder initiative and whose lowest is definite planning. The founders can move; Async has not shown that they know where they are going. Motion without direction is indefinite optimism.

Anduril is the high rung on founder pedigree: Palmer Luckey had already shipped Oculus to a billion-dollar exit before attacking defense. Async shares only the outsider mechanism—Smith’s college exit, resale history, and willingness to ship Skope publicly, including a 55-point Hacker News launch. Async diverges where the analogy becomes load-bearing: neither Smith nor Connor Park has a prior exit, scaled product, senior operating role, or exceptional verified technical artifact; Park’s leading public repository has one star. The unstated pattern behind Affirm, Asana, and Anduril is not merely precocity. It is proven capacity to build at scale. Async has initiative, not lineage.

Quora’s indefinite consumer roadmap is the low rung on definite planning, where founder pedigree had to override an inherently exploratory product path. Async displays the same failure mechanism without Adam D’Angelo’s Facebook CTO override: nonprofit agents became billing software, billing software became legal agents, and legal agents became a multi-industry service spanning law, healthcare, and real estate. Async describes workflow audits, one-or-two-workflow deployments, and subsequent agreements, but names no two-year architectural commitment or milestone it will refuse to pivot away from. “We will find the next workflow” is a process, not a plan.

Anthropic’s May 2026 small-business launch reveals the problem with Async’s alleged secret. Over the next eighteen months, Anthropic, Gumloop, and Relevance AI will compound model capability and reusable integrations faster than Async can compound customer-specific deployment labor. Async’s possible insight—that small businesses want completed outcomes rather than another agent-building tool—is real but not yet contrarian, because the dossier names no architectural or institutional reason Anthropic cannot add managed implementation through partners. The red flag is exact: Async currently reads as “AI for small-business workflows” with service wrapped around it. There is dissatisfaction, but no named incumbent blind spot that persists for five years.

Two named law firms, Clifton Law and the Law Office of Joshua Lowenthal, give Async the beginnings of embedded workflow depth, but no public retention, revenue, deployment-volume, or proprietary-data evidence supports monopoly pricing. The score would flip a tier if Async demonstrated that conflict checks, engagement letters, and firm memory create a legal-specific system of record that customers cannot replace without reconstructing years of approval history—and then showed expansion pricing rising while delivery labor fell. Today Async faces Gumloop with $70M raised, Relevance AI with $37M, and Anthropic selling fifteen ready-to-run workflows directly. Competition is for losers; Async has entered a market already organized around imitation.

Palantir converted regulation into a moat because government procurement, classified integration, and institutional trust excluded ordinary software vendors. Async serves law firms and mentions healthcare, but Async has not shown a comparable regulated-customer barrier: the homepage claims SOC 2 Type I while the security page says certification remains in progress. Legal workflows can become sticky, yet customer compliance is not the same as regulatory monopoly. Async is atoms-light software with bespoke implementation costs, and outcome pricing may transfer execution risk to Async without creating certification depth or capex barriers.

Two deployed law-firm stories and repeated product changes predict that by July 2029 Async will have abandoned horizontal SMB positioning, narrowed into managed legal-workflow automation, and reached roughly $1M–$3M ARR without durable monopoly pricing. Async’s relatively strong founder initiative should prevent immediate death; Async’s weak definite plan and absent structural moat should force another narrowing. The likely outcome is a respectable vertical services-software business, not a power-law company.

Clifton Law and Joshua Lowenthal supply the strongest bull case: Async may already be discovering, through real deployments, that legal work contains a hidden graph of conflicts, approvals, precedents, and communications that horizontal platforms cannot safely reproduce. Asana and Quora show that exceptional founders or network effects can override missing atoms and an indefinite early roadmap. The objection fails because Async has neither the operator pedigree that justified Asana nor demonstrated compounding data effects that justified Quora; even its SOC 2 status is internally inconsistent. I would reverse the pass if Async reaches twenty paying law firms, exceeds $2M ARR, retains at least 85% gross margins after deployment labor, and documents expansion driven by a proprietary firm-memory layer rather than additional consulting.
