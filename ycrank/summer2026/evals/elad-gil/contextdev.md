# Context.dev -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI agents increasingly need information newer than model cutoffs; AI-product developers now buy managed web context, and the controlling variable is whether recurring context consumption expands faster than proxy, rendering, and extraction costs while competing APIs commoditize retrieval.

Context.dev does not yet resolve that variable. Credit-metered pricing from $25 to $499 per month creates a measurable business, and roughly 70% cached-brand coverage could improve unit economics, but the dossier gives no revenue, gross margin, retention, or cohort credit growth. The cited web-scraping market reaches only $1.17 billion in 2026, while Firecrawl, Tavily, and Exa already cover overlapping functions with substantially more capital. The market may expand beyond conventional scraping, but neither the boundary nor Context.dev’s threshold for winning it is established.

Demand is the strongest evidence. More than 400 customers include Mintlify, Rho, Passionfroot, and Klarna; 5,000 developers, 11,018 weekly npm downloads, and 2,000 monitor runs within 24 hours show that developers will try and repeatedly invoke an unfinished product. Dench attributes more than $3 million of pipeline to its monitoring use case. Definitions remain loose—“customers,” “developers,” and “businesses” are not reconciled—and there is no paid retention data, but this is materially better than a pre-seed waitlist or collection of pilots.

The technical shift is real but the value-capture layer remains uncertain. Better models increase demand for fresh, structured inputs, so Context.dev is less exposed than a formatter that disappears with the next model release. It also operates browsers, proxies, rendering, retries, caches, and extraction across five SDKs. Yet this is infrastructure supporting other companies’ workflows, not ownership of an end-to-end workflow. Monitors move in the right direction, but brand, styleguide, screenshot, transaction, and logo endpoints currently look more like expanding surface area than proprietary workflow state.

The bull case is that a repeat founder with two acquisitions has found product pull inside a discontinuity before the market fully crystallizes. Context.dev’s breadth could become a distribution channel through which monitoring and enrichment products are cross-sold. That case stings because the usage is real. But the available exception tests do not clear: this is not documented frontier research, scarce model-building talent, or a capital-intensive technical oligopoly. The red flag is the asserted cache-and-feedback moat without a reproduction-cost estimate, particularly when funded competitors already operate the same crawling and extraction machinery. Broader coverage across an AI wave is an attractive pattern; it is not by itself a reason to own a replaceable API.

Yahia Bakour’s Amazon checkout work, Sunrun AI leadership, and two prior acquisitions provide credible second-time learning, although the dossier does not document the personal desperation behind this specific company. I would monitor rather than invest today. My base case is that Context.dev reaches $8–15 million ARR within 36 months but remains one of several overlapping web-context providers rather than becoming the category-defining layer. The single condition that flips me is six-month net gross-profit retention above 130% across at least 50 paid customers: that would show both expanding consumption and economically durable delivery, directly validating the market’s controlling variable.
