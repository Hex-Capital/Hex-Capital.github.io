# Dock -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The first question is what broke for these founders. The dossier identifies a plausible problem—agents trapped in private chats—but not a specific incident in which a customer deployment failed, the founders improvised a workaround, and the situation changed. Govind Kavaturi and Mike Molinet have worked together across Branch, Thena, and now Dock. That history is evidence that they can endure one another. It is not yet evidence of how they behave when this product meets resistance. The most important test is therefore missing.

They have nevertheless shipped something substantial. Separate agent credentials, workspace-scoped permissions, attributed event history, human approval gates, real-time synchronization, and REST/MCP access form a coherent technical artifact rather than a slide deck. This also matches an unstated pattern I like: a programmable interface on which technical users might construct workflows the founders did not anticipate. But the proof is still mostly the company’s description of its architecture. Recoverably stored webhook secrets and browser-session tokens, with encryption left on the roadmap, show that the hard security boundary has not been crossed. Dock is working software, but not yet a measured proof that competitors such as Linear, Replit, or Relevance AI would struggle to reproduce.

Who wants the crude version now? The dossier cannot name anyone. A $19 Pro plan and a $49 Scale plan demonstrate that Dock can display prices, not that customers pay them. One GitHub follower, one star on the most visible repository, and zero Toolify reviews reveal no narrow well of usage. The free tier and self-serve MCP onboarding are sensible experiments, but the agency partner program risks making “partner” the decisive distribution verb before the founders have shown they can recruit and retain the first ten organizations themselves. Conflicting invite-only and self-serve messaging makes the acquisition loop still less legible.

The bull case stings because this is not an inexperienced team chasing a fashionable noun. Kavaturi joined Branch in its first year and participated in its growth from zero to $100 million in revenue; Molinet co-founded Branch, and both later built Thena. This is their third company together. They understand B2B product and go-to-market work, and Dock’s shared human-agent substrate could become valuable as agents spread through enterprise applications. That is the strongest known-exception argument: experienced repeat collaborators have already shipped a general-purpose primitive. But the exceptions require more than a prototype—rapid correction, unmistakable user pull, or independently validated technical proof. None appears here.

The founders’ history also places them near the problem without proving that the idea originated in a problem they personally encountered. Nor does the dossier describe a rejected hypothesis and a product route changed because of evidence. Enterprise security and compliance could become the schlep-heavy wedge, but today SOC 2, penetration testing, encryption, HIPAA eligibility, and ISO certification are unfinished obligations. An unsolved burden is not a moat. My 36-month base case is that Dock pivots from a horizontal company workspace into a narrower customer-operations agent product rooted in the founders’ Thena experience, rather than reaching $1 million ARR with the current general-purpose product.

I would pass today. The technical artifact clears the secondary gate, but the people-first gate does not: there is no named blocker followed by a dated founder-built workaround. One verifiable event would flip me—a blocked customer deployment, a workaround the founders ship within two weeks, and that customer subsequently using Dock repeatedly. That sequence would reveal more than the market forecast, the founders’ résumés, or another hundred product features.
