# Autoresearch, Inc. -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Autonomous coding agents make continuous experimentation feasible; application-layer AI teams become buyers if verified production improvements cost less to purchase than to generate internally. That is the market map for Autoresearch, Inc. The controlling variable is the cost of a reproducible production improvement, including compute and human supervision. Asking customers to supply a task, baseline, and evaluation creates a measurable starting point. The dossier establishes that mechanism, but provides no customer result showing where the economics become compelling.

The technical demonstration earns some credit for timing. Autoresearch reports 4,188 experiments over two days using 50 B200 GPUs and stock coding agents. That is meaningful execution capacity. It does not establish cheaper research: there is no comparable human-led baseline, campaign cost, or quantified production benefit. The distinction matters because customers could enthusiastically want better models while purchasing very little autonomous experimentation at its actual delivery cost. I would fund proof of that economic step before funding broader capacity.

The stronger evidence concerns what the product owns. Persistent experiment memory, lineage, compute orchestration, and statistical verification surround the model with a recurring research process. Hosted and customer-infrastructure deployment also give the team a plausible place inside operational workflows. Stronger coding agents should increase the usefulness of that system. The published recipe’s 0.9016 median validation bits-per-byte across 25 confirmation runs supports verification discipline, although this remains company-produced evidence on a hardware-sensitive benchmark. It does not yet demonstrate reliable optimization across customer environments.

The strongest objection to withholding a check is that this infrastructure layer could become essential before conventional demand metrics appear. Karan Brar’s production ML background, Thomas Boser’s infrastructure experience, and Jad Ghalayini’s formal-verification expertise fit the actual execution problem. This also matches my pattern of investing across a technology discontinuity: application teams may need research orchestration regardless of which model provider wins. But the research-stage exception requires unusually strong technical evidence and a credible capital path. YC and Peak XV participation help with financing credibility; they do not establish either a scarce frontier capability or customer economics. The biographies document relevant expertise, without documenting prior founder lessons or costly personal commitment.

Autoresearch’s commercial evidence remains at the absent-evidence default. Paid-plan terms and enterprise MSAs describe how customers could transact; they do not establish payment, repeat usage, or expansion. The experiment count is technical output, not demand. Meanwhile, Karpathy’s open-source loop gives internal ML teams a substitute starting point, and well-funded adjacent companies have resources to extend into orchestration. This activates my concern about reproducible infrastructure advantages. Customer ownership and enterprise data restrictions also limit the assumed cross-customer memory moat. There is no demonstrated frozen-model dependency or inappropriate financing behavior, but there is also no evidence yet that the first engagement creates reusable distribution.

I would keep Autoresearch, Inc. under observation and withhold a personal check today. Its strongest property is the architecture around experimentation; its weakest is evidence that customers repeatedly buy the result. The market gates remain unmet because technical throughput has not been connected to an economic improvement. My flip condition is one customer-verified record covering three consecutive paid campaigns for the same application team: each must improve a previously untouched production holdout, with fees plus compute below the customer’s documented internal cost for comparable work. That would turn a plausible research system into evidence of a business.
