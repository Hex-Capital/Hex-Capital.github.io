# Edviro -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

$400,000 in reported client savings makes Edviro unusually tangible, while the absence of documented tiebreaker authority makes Edviro structurally fragile. That is the signature: an observable energy product attached to an unresolved two-founder machine. I can watch seven school sites consume less power; I cannot yet see what happens when Hursh Shah and Tanuj Siripurapu reach an irreconcilable decision. Biz and Tiz are visible. Rizz and authority remain environmental signals I have not observed.

**Flock Safety** is the high rung because a physical artifact in the wild made the product legible before a spreadsheet did. Edviro shares that mechanism: meter readings, HVAC setpoints, work orders, and $400,000 in claimed savings leave fingerprints in real buildings. Edviro diverges because a normal person cannot install or experience its product within 48 hours; access runs through facilities systems, free audits, and enterprise deployment. Seven live schools still put Edviro nearer Flock than an invisible developer API, but the artifact belongs to the facilities manager rather than the passerby.

**GameCrush** is the low rung because ambiguous control allowed investors and internal conflict to strip away the product’s DNA. Edviro names Hursh Shah as CEO and Tanuj Siripurapu as CTO, which is better than shared titles, but Edviro discloses no equity split, operating agreement, vesting protocol, or explicit tiebreaker. Joint UCSB.Network activity establishes familiarity, not authority. “CEO” on a page is a rebuttal to my concern, not a hard answer.

**Niantic** had John Hanke’s decade of Keyhole and Google Earth before the company became a location-world bet. Edviro has a real but shallower origin: Hursh Shah began after analyzing school utility data, while his prior company, Skyglass, concerned aviation smart glasses. Edviro therefore did not materialize solely from a market memo, but Edviro also lacks evidence that either founder spent years operating buildings, selling into school districts, or owning an energy budget. They have touched the problem; they are not yet demonstrably *of* the place.

$20,000 ARR within three months can compound into something magically weird if Edviro’s intervention loop becomes proprietary: observe equipment, simulate a change, execute it, verify savings, and feed the result back into the digital twin. Over 18 months, Edviro could accumulate the rare dataset connecting conditions to corrective actions rather than merely collecting telemetry. The counterforce is that BrainBox AI, GridPoint, Facilio, and 75F already occupy adjacent territory, while seven live sites are not enough to establish a unique learning advantage. Edviro is unsexy in a way I like, but no sophisticated-investor rejection, aesthetic taboo, or underpriced-round evidence makes it Crusoe-weird yet.

Trane’s acquisition of BrainBox AI shows what Edviro would need for the capital-intensive criterion to flip a tier: Edviro would have to own a hard layer—sensors, controllers, certified measurement infrastructure, or equipment-specific control technology—that incumbents cannot reproduce through software integration. Edviro currently connects existing bills, meters, BMS exports, spreadsheets, and work-order systems without replacement. That is clever capital efficiency, but Edviro is selling orchestration around physical infrastructure rather than building frontier hardware. The school-facilities wedge is wonderfully unsexy; the moat is not yet correspondingly hard.

Seven live school sites and 29 additional sites expanding lead to my 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Edviro will reach $1 million to $3 million ARR as a school-and-construction facilities product, but Edviro will not become a cross-market energy operating system. Verified savings can pull the wedge forward, while heterogeneous integrations, incumbent distribution, and a founder origin measured in a project rather than a career constrain the platform story. Useful businesses are real businesses. They are not always Long Journey businesses.

Twenty-nine expansion sites are the strongest case against my forecast: Edviro may already have found product pull before most pre-seed founders have found a buyer, and $400,000 saved against $20,000 ARR suggests extraordinary customer ROI. The **HQ Trivia** exception says tactile product love can temporarily override unresolved co-founder governance; Edviro’s closed-loop savings could become the enterprise version of that pull. The objection stings, but Edviro’s savings attribution remains company-reported, the evidence covers only seven live sites, and enterprise deployment lacks HQ’s viral self-propagation. I would flip to Invest if Edviro converts all 29 expansion sites into paid renewals with independently verified savings.
