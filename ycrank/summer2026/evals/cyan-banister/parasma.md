# Parasma -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Sean Cole trained roughly 200,000 living neurons to play Doom before founding Parasma, yet Parasma’s product remains something most people can neither touch nor independently verify. That pairing is Parasma’s signature: overwhelmingly magically weird, weakly physical despite being literally biological. Cole skipped the bachelor’s-degree conveyor belt, studied spiking neural networks, and built the strange thing before assembling the company around it. I’m never playing the game I seem to be playing: Parasma is not really an AI-infrastructure pitch yet; Parasma is a bet that Cole can turn an intimate conversation with neurons into a new computing discipline.

**Crusoe** is the high rung because burning stranded gas to mine Bitcoin joined three ugly technologies through a founder who could make the parts speak to one another. Parasma shares that load-bearing mechanism: Cole connected Cortical Labs’ CL1, electrical stimulation, spike decoding, and reinforcement learning while respectable people were still debating whether “biological compute” was even a category. Parasma diverges where Crusoe had operational ownership: Parasma depends on Cortical Labs’ hardware and API, Cole’s Doom code is open source, and R&D World could not isolate whether the neurons or surrounding software performed the learning. Magically weird earns attention; owned weirdness earns the premium.

The **consensus YC AI-agent darling** fails the physical-verifiability rung because its model is an abstraction described by founders rather than an artifact I can install, ride, scan, or watch work. Parasma partially escapes that failure: Parasma has public token-prediction and reinforcement-learning demonstrations, and Cole’s Doom experiment generated coverage in *The Guardian* and *Scientific American*. But the artifact is trapped behind a specialized CL1 wetware system. A normal human cannot reproduce Parasma within 48 hours, and the 601-run software replication makes the spectacle less conclusive. This is not **HQ Trivia**’s known exception; Parasma has no tactile viral pull strong enough to override uncertainty about what the product actually did.

Sean Cole’s 2025 MSc research in spiking neural networks compounds through Parasma because each wetware experiment can produce stimulation policies, biological-response data, and benchmarks unavailable from desk research. Over 18 months, that loop either makes Cole increasingly irreplaceable or reveals that Parasma is merely a clever interface atop Cortical Labs. The unstated pattern passes: Cole built the Doom implementation before Parasma and looks more like an obsessive outsider than a credentialed tourist who discovered a TAM slide. The red flag is equally sharp: with `doom-neuron` public and no patent portfolio, Parasma’s defensibility may reside in somebody else’s substrate rather than in Cole.

Parasma’s one-person roster gives Sean Cole unambiguous CEO authority today, avoiding the 50/50-founder pathology that poisoned **GameCrush** and later surfaced at **HQ Trivia**. Parasma would fall an entire tier on this dimension if Cole recruits the founding scientist at 1%-3% equity but later adds an equal co-founder without explicit tiebreaker authority. Parasma would reach the top only with documented founder vesting and conflict provisions. A solo founder is a clean steering wheel, but it is not yet a tested governance system.

**SpaceX** is the single-anchor parallel for capital-intensive frontier risk: the first check funded a physical substrate conventional investors thought too difficult to matter. Parasma carries a miniature version of that wager through living neurons, wet-lab variability, bioethics, and the stated intention to avoid architectures capable of consciousness. Parasma is not yet SpaceX-like in capital ownership because Cortical Labs owns the biological computer, but dependence on an $11 million-funded substrate vendor does not make the scientific swing ordinary. The consciousness-and-suffering question is aesthetically transgressive enough that many investors will back away before understanding it. That is usually where I lean forward.

Cortical Labs’ ownership of the CL1 substrate produces my 36-month forecast: Parasma will demonstrate one independently validated learning task beyond Doom, remain below $1 million ARR, and be acquired by Cortical Labs for under $40 million primarily for Cole and the training stack. Parasma’s weirdness creates strategic value faster than a standalone market; Parasma’s weak physical ownership makes integration with the substrate owner more likely than infrastructure independence. The game looks like selling algorithms, but the game underneath is making CL1 useful.

R&D World’s inability to establish that living neurons performed the learning is the bear mechanism that stings: Parasma could be an evocative demo wrapped around ordinary control software, with an open-source implementation, no revenue as of May 17, 2026, no disclosed moat, and three funded competitors. **DeepMind** supplies a known exception—an unusually cross-disciplinary founder can outrun missing conventional founder-market history—but Cole has not yet shown Hassabis-like breadth, and **HQ Trivia** cannot rescue Parasma without observable user pull. I still invest because Cole touched the problem before Parasma existed, remains the single decision-maker, and chose a category too strange for spreadsheet tourists. My verdict flips to Pass if Parasma cannot publish an independent neuron-versus-software ablation or complete a second hardware integration by July 2027.
