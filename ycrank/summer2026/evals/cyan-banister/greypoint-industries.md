# Greypoint Industries -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Darcy Zhang is a Canadian Armed Forces infanteer building autonomous counter-drone hardware with three engineering co-founders, which makes Greypoint Industries a frontier-defense company with authentic problem contact but dangerously thin authority clarity. Greypoint Industries is highest on the capital-intensive, regulated-hardware swing and lowest on the single-tiebreaker test. That pairing says “real artifact, real battlefield, unresolved cockpit.” I can see the hidden hardware pattern that produced Anduril and SpaceX; I can also see four people reaching for the controls when the aircraft stalls.

**SpaceX** is the high rung because rockets were capital-intensive, technically legible, and aesthetically uninvestable before conventional venture believed hardware could compound. Greypoint Industries shares that mechanism: ARROW is a soldier-deployable interceptor, LEGION geolocates emitting threats, and Canadian export permits plus controlled-goods registration make the category harder with every step. Greypoint Industries diverges sharply from SpaceX because no range, speed, accuracy, test volume, patent, or production-yield result is public. The signed first ARROW contract keeps the analogy load-bearing, but today I am touching a prototype-shaped promise, not watching a Falcon leave the pad.

**HQ Trivia** is the low rung because unresolved co-founder authority turned product electricity into organizational combustion. Greypoint Industries names Darcy Zhang as CEO, which is better than shared titles, but Greypoint Industries has four co-founders and discloses no majority operating authority, founder-vesting structure, or exit-conflict protocol. The HQ Trivia known exception required tactile, explosive user love powerful enough to override governance temporarily; a first defense contract and two awards are not that override. “CEO” on LinkedIn is a rebuttal to my concern, not a hard answer to who wins a 2–2 vote.

Darcy Zhang’s infantry experience, Jordan Khoo’s General Dynamics and uncrewed-aircraft work, Steven Xu’s embedded-controls background, and Nic Chu’s General Dynamics experience give Greypoint Industries more than credential theater. Over 18 months, that lived and technical contact can compound: Darcy translates soldier constraints into deployment doctrine, Jordan and Steven convert field failures into controls improvements, and Greypoint Industries accumulates qualification knowledge that a visiting software founder cannot manufacture from a memo. Biz and Tiz are visible; Rizz remains unobservable. The risk is not absent founder-market fit—it is whether four complementary biographies become one operating organism.

Anduril’s approximately $2.2 billion of 2025 revenue proves defense autonomy is no longer automatically non-consensus. Greypoint Industries therefore does not earn “magically weird” merely by saying swarm warfare: Anduril, Fortem, Airobotics, and MARSS/EOS already sell autonomous interception systems, while Canada launched an explicit low-cost interceptor procurement category in July 2026. Greypoint Industries would flip upward if two sophisticated investors passed because soldier-deployable RF geolocation looked technically impossible, and the founders then produced repeatable interception data proving those investors wrong. Without that evidence, Greypoint Industries is weird in geography and implementation, but defense technology itself is now a crowded dinner table.

**Niantic** was physically verifiable because I could play Ingress and feel the location layer before reading a metric. Greypoint Industries offers the same kind of artifact in a restricted environment: ARROW can fly, miss, collide, or fail in front of an observer, and LEGION either geolocates an emitter or it does not. Greypoint Industries scores below Niantic because a normal human cannot deploy an interceptor within 48 hours, and no public flight-test statistics or field footage establish repeat use. Still, I would rather inspect one scarred ARROW airframe than read fifty AI-agent dashboards. My unstated physical-world pattern is present, just not yet independently observable.

Greypoint Industries’ signed first ARROW contract and Canada’s July 2026 Defence Drone Initiative lead me to a specific 36-month forecast: by July 2029, Greypoint Industries will have won Canadian qualification and reached $3 million–$8 million in annualized contract value, but at least one of the four co-founders will have departed as production and command authority centralize under Darcy Zhang. Greypoint Industries can reach that range through qualification learning and low-cost interceptor demand without displacing Anduril globally. The same high-hardware, low-governance signature produces both halves of the forecast.

Fortem’s more than 4,500 captures, Airobotics’ operational airport deployment, Anduril’s integrated Lattice platform, and Greypoint Industries’ complete absence of disclosed performance or moat form the bear case that stings: incumbents could qualify faster while Greypoint Industries burns scarce capital rediscovering already-solved interception problems. Greypoint Industries survives that objection today because a named founder has infantry experience, multiple founders worked in defense or UAS engineering, and a customer has already signed the first ARROW contract before public validation exists. I will invest, but the condition is sharp: if Greypoint Industries cannot show an independently witnessed multi-target interception test and a named follow-on procurement commitment by July 2027, the contract was theater rather than compounding evidence, and I flip to Pass.
