# Limnion -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Limnion’s market turns on whether open telemetry storage can deliver a substantially lower production bill without transferring the savings into engineering work. Agents may increase demand for searchable logs and traces; platform engineers would buy the infrastructure. But machine access alone will not determine the winner: SigNoz already offers MCP access, and groundcover introduced agent-facing investigations in October 2026. My controlling variable is fully loaded cost at the customer’s required query latency and availability. The dossier provides neither a benchmark nor a demonstrated threshold at which customers switch.

Siglake has a credible mechanism for changing that equation. OpenTelemetry ingestion, Parquet files, and externally readable Iceberg tables let customers retain control of their storage and use other query engines. That is a concrete architectural choice. It does not yet establish an economic step change: there are no linked storage, compute, or operating-cost comparisons. The broader $11.91 billion observability estimate also includes functions Limnion delegates to other products. I would underwrite the storage layer it can capture, rather than assume access to the entire observability budget.

The strongest argument for investing now is Todd Persen. Co-founding InfluxData and Era Software, followed by serving as Hydrolix CTO, gives him repeated exposure to this exact market’s technical and commercial constraints. Era’s acquisition establishes prior company-building experience, although the undisclosed consideration does not establish a small-exit motivation. His team has already implemented WAL ingestion, distributed SQL, compaction, and search indexes. This is substantive infrastructure around a recurring operational workload. Better models could increase demand for that infrastructure without reproducing the storage engine themselves. However, the unsuccessful 72-hour soak attempt and outstanding tenant-isolation and failover qualification leave production responsibility unresolved.

That founder-and-product combination makes the bull case uncomfortable to dismiss: an experienced database builder could use an open storage boundary to enter accounts that resist proprietary backends, then sell managed operations. It fits my pattern of backing infrastructure across a technology transition. But the distribution loop remains hypothetical. The 577 GitHub stars observed on November 3 show attention; three forks establish little about production usage. Neither proves recurring demand or willingness to pay. Apache-2.0 licensing also gives competitors access to the implementation, so operational execution must earn the commercial advantage. The dossier does not establish that advantage yet.

I would pass on writing a personal check today. Limnion avoids the disposable AI-feature red flag, and I see no evidence of status-driven fundraising or a manufactured data moat. But neither known exception resolves the market gate: this is an established competitive category, without frontier research that justifies overriding demand evidence or documented economics that reconstruct the incumbent business. Founder experience cannot substitute for those facts. One verifiable result would change my decision: a paid, independently referenceable 90-day production deployment demonstrating at least 50% lower fully loaded cost while meeting the customer’s existing query-latency and availability targets. That would connect Limnion’s architecture to an actual buying decision.
