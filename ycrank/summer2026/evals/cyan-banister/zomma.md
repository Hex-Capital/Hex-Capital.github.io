# Zomma -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Investment operations may be staffed by people who define policy and resolve exceptions while software operates the old custodian portals, carrier sites, spreadsheets, and desktop applications that institutions never modernized. Zomma has one useful leak from that future: hedge funds, asset managers, and fintech firms are already using its agents for reconciliation, onboarding, KYC, and reporting. But the dossier does not show the path from these deployments to broad autonomy. Reviewer fatigue, credentials, 2FA, and failure handling remain unresolved, so the future still requires several unnamed reliability transitions.

The founders fit the problem better than the company’s fashionable category label suggests. Jihyun Kim worked inside Goldman Sachs, Endowus, and Arrowpoint Investment Partners; Cashel Fitzgerald built systems supporting more than $20 billion in annual transactions; Varun Arumugam worked on verification infrastructure at Apple and chip-verification agents at Bronco AI. This is credible technical and operational tuition, and the three backgrounds are complementary. What I cannot yet see is pre-company obsession: no dated experiments, shipped public artifacts, or evidence that this particular wound compelled them before computer-use agents became hot. The earlier enterprise-sales and application-support positioning makes the origin look exploratory rather than inevitable.

The broken system is real but incompletely decoded. Investment-operations teams move among 10–15 systems because vendors expose inconsistent or nonexistent APIs, forcing people to download and re-key information. Zomma bypasses that institutional neglect through the interfaces already present. Yet the dossier never quantifies the hours, error costs, reconciliation delays, or fees this fragmentation extracts. More importantly, it does not show whose agency is returned. The product currently appears to increase institutional throughput, not give an individual ownership, income, or occupational control.

There is a functioning artifact here, not merely a rendering. Zomma runs portals, desktop applications, PDFs, and spreadsheets; it records approvals in a seven-year append-only log and preserves operator corrections as firm-specific memory. Using the product in KYC or reconciliation also exposes customers to meaningful operational and compliance risk, so even unquantified adoption is informative. But “used by hedge funds, asset managers, and fintechs” supplies no named customer, repeated workflow count, deployment date, retention, or money changing hands. I cannot distinguish durable reliance from supervised pilots.

The strongest bull case is that forward deployment is how the team acquires the exception traces that later become infrastructure. A dedicated engineer maps each workflow; corrections persist; subsequent deployments allegedly get faster. If reuse compounds across firms, today’s labor-heavy implementation could conceal a valuable operating layer. The painful alternative is equally plausible: heterogeneous workflows remain heterogeneous, every customer requires another embedded engineer, and Browser Use or Asteroid commoditizes the screen-control substrate. Then Zomma becomes a capable services business sitting above someone else’s enabling layer.

The privacy and governance gates are not fatal today, but they are active diligence items. Tenant isolation, encrypted storage, SSO/SAML, isolated credentials, approval gates, and immutable logs show that the team respects hidden complexity. SOC 2 Type II is still in progress, however, and agents touching regulated accounts create serious consent, custody, and surveillance exposure. The dossier identifies Jihyun as CEO, which avoids a visible leadership shell game, but provides no founder-control or tiebreaker information. My verifiable flip condition is straightforward: show two named financial institutions each running at least five production workflows for six months, with deployment time falling by at least half on later workflows and documented human-review rates below 5%. That would turn firm-specific memory from a claim into the present-day artifact I can underwrite.
