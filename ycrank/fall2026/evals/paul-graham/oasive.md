# Oasive -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Howland has worked on both sides of the problem Oasive is trying to solve: investing in mortgage securities at Wells Fargo and Google Treasury, then working on AI strategy. That is a good place to notice something worth building. But the question I most want answered is still missing: what stopped her, what did she build to get around it, and how quickly did it work? Her [documented background](https://cfa-sf.org/about/about-us.html) establishes relevant experience. It does not establish resourcefulness under resistance. I would pass on the evidence here.

The strongest argument against passing is quite strong. Oasive reported use across **15 institutional desks on September 24, 2026**, including global banks, managers overseeing more than $100 billion, and Fortune 500 treasury teams. Experienced institutional users are a meaningful audience for a solo founder to reach. If those desks repeatedly rely on Oasive to make investment decisions, I could be overlooking precisely the founder behavior I want. But the [announcement](https://oasive.ai/news/oasive-launch-y-combinator) concerns MBS and rates. It establishes neither repeated dependence nor municipal adoption. The customers’ assets under management are not Oasive’s traction.

There is some evidence of building, too. Oasive offers research software and advertises proprietary MBS prepayment and valuation models. Howland also has a public recording tool from her earlier computer-use work. These deserve more credit than a proposal. Yet there is no measured accuracy result or independently validated technical advantage for the current product. My recurring interest in founders who produce concrete proof before raising money applies only partially here. The other pattern—technical users building unexpected things on a programmable interface—is not established by research pages.

The municipal opportunity contains work worth doing. Gathering audited financials, board minutes, debt records, and local news is tedious enough that an analyst may gladly pay to escape it. Source links help make the result inspectable. But DPC DATA already supplies overlapping inputs, and MuniPro produces source-based credit memos. Oasive would have to learn which remaining task analysts urgently need solved. Its [$200 monthly rates subscription and proposed paid MBS pilots](https://oasive.ai/join) provide ways to test willingness to pay; they are not evidence of payment. Municipal early access leaves that particular test unresolved.

I would not treat the difference between the municipal YC description and the MBS website as proof of an intelligent pivot. We lack the rejected hypothesis, the customer observation, and the resulting change. Nor would I infer obstinacy. The same restraint applies to red flags: a solo founder is not a failed cofounder relationship, and licensed data is not automatically a distribution plan dependent on a large partner. There is no documented premature delegation or refusal to hear users. The known exceptions require a convincing working prototype, unmistakable demand, or validated technical proof. Oasive’s reported desk usage is the closest candidate, but its depth is too unclear to override the missing founder evidence.

The distinction matters because I can imagine a useful specialist business here without yet seeing why I should back this founder with my own money. The founder-originated context is the strongest part of the case; the missing response to a specific obstacle is the weakest. My flip condition is one documented municipal pilot in which an analyst’s concrete objection leads Howland to ship a workaround within two weeks, followed by that desk paying and returning weekly. That single episode would connect the person, the product, and the need.
