# Deploy -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Deploy’s market opens if improving robot manipulation lets fulfillment operators automate variable work without paying for custom installations. The controlling variable is contribution profit per station at customer-required throughput: monthly revenue minus hardware, servicing, integration, and human intervention costs. Deploy’s advertised below-labor pricing and bundled installation make the purchasing proposition clear. They also put the operating risk on Deploy. The dossier identifies a plausible mechanism for replacing labor, but the advertised price alone does not establish that this becomes an attractive business.

The October 2026 demonstration—800+ pens packed using approximately 30 minutes of fine-tuning data—is meaningful technical evidence. It suggests that adapting a robot to a task may require relatively little training data. It does not establish elapsed production time, unattended completion rate, or performance across changing warehouse inventory. Reported failures make that distinction consequential. I give Deploy some credit for a visible adaptation capability, while treating Physical Intelligence’s broader research as a catalyst rather than evidence of Deploy’s economics. The exhibition establishes neither customer demand nor repeat usage; absent commercial evidence receives my pre-seed default.

The strongest part of Deploy is its proposed ownership of the operating loop: decompose the task, monitor execution, request assistance, and turn interventions into training data. Better underlying models could improve completion while Deploy retains responsibility for the station. That is a more interesting position than supplying a robot demonstration. But the feedback loop is company-described, and its accumulated scale and data rights are undisclosed. Manifold already offers overlapping robotic labor. Calling intervention data a moat would require knowing how expensive and time-consuming it is for another operator to collect comparable exceptions.

The bull case is that the founders can make those exceptions progressively cheaper to resolve. Chada’s vision-language-action and world-model research at Honda complements Polson’s operational autonomy experience at AeroVect. Research capability paired with deployment experience could turn an initially service-heavy operation into a repeatable product. This fits my willingness to invest around a technical discontinuity, including in physical markets. The exception requires technology to change delivery economics, however, and Deploy has not yet demonstrated that change. Nor does this dossier establish the frontier-research scarcity and financing path that would justify overriding weak demand evidence. The founders’ relevant experience is real; it does not establish prior startup learning or costly personal commitment, so that dimension remains at its default.

I would pass today. Deploy’s workflow architecture earns further attention, but the market and technology dimensions together do not clear my investment gate. My single flip condition would be an independently checkable, 90-day paid deployment cohort across three unrelated warehouses showing at least 40% station contribution margins while delivering equivalent accepted output at least 20% below fully loaded manual labor cost. Those are my underwriting thresholds, not reported results. Include teleoperation, servicing, hardware depreciation, and amortized installation in the calculation. That would demonstrate that Deploy is building a scalable robotic labor business.
