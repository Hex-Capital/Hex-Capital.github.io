# RealPact -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032 and I can see a brokerage where an address initiates the transaction: public records arrive, state-specific documents assemble, compliance checks run, and the agent handles the human negotiation rather than copying parcel data. RealPact has one credible leak from that future—a functioning address-to-contract product operating in 10-plus brokerages and producing a signature-ready New Hampshire agreement in under five minutes. But the dossier does not map the intervening transitions. Nationwide coverage requires state-specific legal maintenance, durable MLS access, trustworthy data reconciliation, and a reason incumbents cannot absorb the feature. “Models improved” explains present feasibility; it does not explain what becomes structurally different six years out.

The present-day wound is wonderfully concrete. Disconnected MLS portals, registries, Dotloop, SkySlope, and manual forms consume 30–40 minutes per contract and an estimated 200-plus hours per agent annually. RealPact retrieves the records and returns that time to the agent. This is the company’s strongest mechanism: an irritating institutional workflow converted into a short command. It is mundane repair, which fits my unstated barbell pattern better than the “AI-native OS” label does. The missing piece is the rent. The dossier shows wasted labor but does not identify a gatekeeper profiting from the fragmentation or demonstrate that RealPact transfers durable control rather than simply making the same system faster.

Ranvir Deshmukh and Erik Peterson have earned a meaningful founder prior. They built CourseMe together, reached 4,000-plus monthly users and 90% of Dartmouth students, secured an acquisition, then carried SignPact into RealPact and sold the current product to brokerages. That is shipped-artifact and recruiting proof, not two students discovering enterprise AI during YC. Still, their documented obsession is with building software, not with real-estate operations: no founder worked inside a brokerage, managed compliance, or lived through multistate transaction failures. The Four Seasons Sotheby’s research relationship and firm-wide pilot may become that operating tuition, but it has not yet produced public evidence of the team mastering the business levers beneath the interface.

The bull case stings because organizations are already assuming real costs to test this. Ten-plus brokerages, named customers, current-product sales, and a firm-wide Four Seasons Sotheby’s pilot are unusually tangible pre-seed signals. If brokers repeatedly entrust live purchase agreements to the system, RealPact could accumulate the templates, exception handling, and integration knowledge that makes the apparent wrapper progressively less reproducible. The founders’ CourseMe history also suggests they know how to turn a narrow institutional workflow into broad adoption.

I cannot underwrite that compounding yet. SkySlope already reaches 900,000 professionals, processes 3 million annual transactions, and markets overlapping AI automation. RealPact controls no identified lower-stack asset; it depends on regional MLS permissions, customer credentials, government records, and general language, vision, and voice models. Users remain responsible for contract accuracy, while SOC 2 Type II was still in progress in May 2026. This is not proven severe surveillance architecture, but transaction and client data make custody an active gate. Nor is there evidence of repeated contract volume, paid retention, strange user behavior, or a technical exception powerful enough to override the commodity risk.

I am neutral and would not write the check today. My 36-month base case is that RealPact reaches roughly $1–3 million ARR through regional brokerage deployments but remains a transaction-automation feature rather than the brokerage operating system, pressured by SkySlope’s distribution. One verifiable fact flips me: twelve-month cohort evidence from at least five brokerages showing that RealPact processes a majority of their purchase agreements while maintaining independently measured field-error rates below manual preparation. That would prove both costly behavior and a workflow asset the incumbents have not already swallowed.
