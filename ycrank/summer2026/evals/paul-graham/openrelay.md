# OpenRelay -- Paul Graham Evaluation

OpenRelay’s 89 documented API operations reveal a technically formidable schlep company whose weakest signal is the one I examine first: whether the founders are animals. OpenRelay has shipped a dashboard, control-plane API, and live Stripe billing, but there is no customer count, provider count, revenue, or unscalable stunt. Patel and Wang look unusually qualified to build the machine. The dossier does not yet show that they will drag people onto it.

**Coinbase** is the high rung for schlep blindness: Brian Armstrong built the wallet, then ran toward compliance while everyone else ran away. OpenRelay shares the important mechanism. OpenRelay must coordinate unreliable third-party machines, isolate tenants with QEMU, VFIO, and IOMMU, survive heterogeneous drivers, and protect customer credentials. That ugliness can become the asset. OpenRelay diverges from Coinbase because failed VM capacity still must be restarted or replaced instead of migrating automatically. The schlep has been found, but not yet conquered.

**Kiko** exposed the low-rung failure mechanism on resourcefulness: clever hackers can ship software yet direct their ingenuity toward an eBay exit instead of forcing the product to live. OpenRelay has better evidence than that low rung because OpenRelay launched billing and a production control plane before accumulating a large organization. But animals leave stranger tracks than release notes. There is no OpenRelay equivalent of Airbnb’s cereal boxes or Armstrong rebuilding after rejection. The known-exception escape hatch therefore does not apply: unlike **Loopt**, OpenRelay has not shown force of personality strong enough to override weak validation, and unlike **Airbnb**, OpenRelay has no stunt that overturns skepticism.

Prashant Patel helped orchestrate clusters exceeding 10,000 GPUs, so OpenRelay’s idea is close to the founders’ working lives even though the dossier never says Patel or Wang built it to solve their own immediate problem. Over 18 months, that domain familiarity should compound into better scheduling, failure classification, and provider onboarding. The danger is that OpenRelay becomes an expert’s catalogue of infrastructure features rather than something the founders urgently needed themselves. The explicit red flag—no founder shown personally using OpenRelay as a customer—keeps organicity below the top rung.

OpenRelay’s founders have AWS Bedrock, Voltage Park, TensorDock, and Heaviside Compute behind them, but neither founder has a public repository or Hacker News trail in the dossier. That misses my unstated pattern: hackers usually leave artifacts before they leave résumés. OpenRelay would move up a full tier on this dimension if Patel or Wang could show a pre-company scheduler, hypervisor project, or inference tool adopted by outsiders. Until then, OpenRelay has strong technical genealogy by employment and strong product evidence, but not yet the tribal marker Patrick Collison brought to **Stripe**.

**Stripe** had two brothers who had already built Auctomatic together, making the pair itself part of the evidence. OpenRelay has the right numerical shape—two founders—and Patel and Wang previously worked together at Voltage Park, which is materially better than being paired weeks before applying. OpenRelay does not disclose how long they worked together or whether they have survived failure together, so the comparison stops short of the Collisons. Still, no solo-founder or startup-dating red flag appears here.

RunPod’s $120 million annual revenue run rate shows both the demand and the danger. My 36-month forecast is that by July 2029 OpenRelay reaches **$5–10 million ARR** by evolving from cheap GPU rental into a multi-provider inference-failover control plane for smaller data centers. OpenRelay’s mechanism is the work competitors prefer to expose to customers: hardware normalization, health checking, isolation, and routing around failures. OpenRelay will not win merely by charging $0.29 per hour for an RTX 4090; it can win by making the identity of that 4090 irrelevant.

RunPod’s 500,000 developers across 31 regions make the strongest bear case: OpenRelay may be entering a network market after the network has already formed, with no public node count, customer count, utilization, moat, GitHub following, or evidence of animal resourcefulness. That objection stings because infrastructure marketplaces die from cold starts, not bad schedulers. OpenRelay’s answer is that it has already shipped the difficult substrate—tenant-isolated VMs, secure tunnels, billing, and automatic backend routing—without a large staff or premium burn plan. I would invest, but narrowly. If by July 2027 OpenRelay cannot verify at least three independent capacity providers and ten repeat production customers, I would flip to pass; that would show the founders built the schlep without forcing anyone through it.
