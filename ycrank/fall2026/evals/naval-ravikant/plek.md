# Plek -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Bouquet has done the work that Plek proposes to sell. His March 2026 task-content research and leadership of MIT’s Work Analytics Lab matter more than the PhD itself: they expose him to the problem of translating job descriptions into actual work. That is specific knowledge acquired through action. The prior Biot sale adds entrepreneurial experience, but the dossier does not establish that it produced an asset transferable to Plek.

The investment question is whether that knowledge compounds inside the company. Plek claims a proprietary work ontology, but the underlying research is published and public occupational data supplies competing starting points. If another team copied the software tomorrow, what would customers still need from Plek? Employee-validated mappings could become an answer. Permission to reuse de-identified data establishes a legal possibility; it does not establish an accumulated, exclusive asset. Workhelix already overlaps with the assessment wedge, while Skan observes workflows directly. Plek must show why its estimates become harder to replace as deployments accumulate.

The strongest bull case is that assessment buys access to implementation. A CFO who discovers expensive, automatable work has an immediate next purchase, and Plek offers to deploy the automation. The founder-reported assessment of 250 employees into 735 tasks shows an initial encounter with that workflow. Moving from diagnosis into execution could reach a substantial adjacent budget; Celonis’s reported $400 million revenue in 2021 demonstrates spending on operational improvement, although it does not establish Plek’s addressable market. The danger is that each implementation consumes another unit of expert attention. Bouquet’s knowledge could support an excellent business while remaining difficult to turn into scalable ownership.

Plek currently shows little of the customer-created value I prefer. Its founder-led outreach acquires organizations individually; the dossier supplies no evidence that one organization makes the product more useful to another. Employees contributing interviews within a customer account is not yet a network effect. Nor do the displayed examples establish commercial validation: the 13,500-plus employees and 15-company portfolio cases are expressly illustrative. Negotiated order forms describe how Plek can charge, without establishing prices, receipts, or contribution margins.

The security disclosures deserve a direct explanation. Claiming SOC 2 compliance while other pages describe an ongoing audit or deny certification creates a concrete trust problem, especially when unredacted survey text reaches AI providers. It does not establish intentional deception, so I would not manufacture an integrity veto. Plek also has no documented pressure tactics or adverse founder dealings. The exceptions that could outweigh its missing commercial evidence—an observable self-reinforcing network, exceptional recruiting evidence, or a demonstrated prototype paired with unusual capital efficiency—are not established here. Strong domain research is the affirmative signal; it cannot substitute for all three.

I would keep the relationship and withhold the check. Plek has a credible founder advantage, but the evidence does not yet show that advantage becoming a company asset. My single flip condition is an independently audited, held-out customer benchmark showing that Plek’s exclusively reusable, customer-validated task corpus materially outperforms a public occupational-data baseline. That would demonstrate that deployment produces something competitors cannot obtain simply by reproducing the code.
