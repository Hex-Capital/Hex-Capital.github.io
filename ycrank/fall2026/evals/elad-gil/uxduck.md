# UXDuck -- Elad Gil Evaluation

UXDuck connects a new AI interface to an existing drone-development market; the potential buyers are manufacturers and professional operators, and the controlling variable is how much integration work they will pay UXDuck to eliminate. MCP’s November 2024 introduction makes assistant access easier to standardize. It does not establish a purchasing reason. My falsifiable belief would be that UXDuck can reduce engineering effort enough to support a paid product despite free alternatives. The dossier establishes the technical mechanism, but provides no measured savings or commercial evidence supporting that belief.

The strongest evidence is inside the runtime. Rust-based connections over serial, UDP and TCP, ArduPilot/PX4 simulation, and MCP, RPC and plugin interfaces put UXDuck closer to operational infrastructure than an AI output formatter. Documented command approvals, including denial after 60 seconds without a response, address part of the execution workflow. Better models could make this infrastructure more useful by completing more complicated tasks through existing interfaces. That weakens my model-absorption red flag. It does not establish that UXDuck retains the customer’s essential workflow: production deployment, accumulated operational state and reliability remain unproven.

The economic question is sharper because Mission Planner and QGroundControl already provide substantial functionality, and both support extensibility. UXDuck must create enough incremental value to overcome migration and ongoing compatibility costs. The broader $11.33 billion drone-software estimate includes analytics and image processing; it cannot establish the size of this particular opportunity. Auterion’s approximately $115 million reported annual revenue in November 2025 demonstrates spending on an integrated drone platform, but does not establish demand for an independent runtime. Pricing is unverifiable. I therefore give MCP’s arrival no economic premium yet: a standardized interface can expand functionality while leaving willingness to pay unchanged.

The bull case is substantial enough to investigate. Ben Butterworth has worked directly on mission planning, MAVLink integration, controller interfaces and command-and-control systems. His early engineering roles could help UXDuck select the integration problems that actually block deployments. This fits my pattern of backing software infrastructure around a physical-industry transition without taking on manufacturing economics. But the prior employer’s 1,000-plus drones are not UXDuck deployments, and employment history does not demonstrate founder sacrifice or second-time-founder learning. The company repository’s five stars and zero forks establish neither product pull nor rejection. The browser simulation and November 14 hackathon offer acquisition experiments; neither yet demonstrates reusable distribution.

I would pass on a personal check today. My exceptions for backing frontier research before demand do not apply: the dossier shows relevant engineering experience, rather than field-defining research and a documented capital path. Nor is there evidence of an incumbent abandoning the category. UXDuck’s strongest property is its working integration architecture; its weakest investment evidence concerns the economic change that makes customers buy it. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: a paid production cohort of three independent drone manufacturers showing at least a 50% reduction in integration engineering hours against their previous tools, with support costs included. That would connect the product’s technical substance to the market variable that matters.
