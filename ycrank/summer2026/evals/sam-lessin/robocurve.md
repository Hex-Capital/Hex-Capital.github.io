# Robocurve -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Jay Chooi’s AI-evaluation research and Aris Zhu’s Amazon Robotics work make Robocurve a missionary technical project with no owned distribution engine: founder conviction is Robocurve’s highest dimension, while founder-as-brand and community are its lowest. Robocurve has one physical-robot pilot and 96 GitHub stars—enough to prove two qualified founders built something real, not enough to prove that independent robot evaluation is a venture-shaped asset rather than a technically serious public good.

**Drop.io** is the high rung for asymmetric conviction: I built against free incumbents because the product belief predated the financing narrative. Robocurve shares that mechanism—Chooi contributed to Inspect Evals and worked at the UK AI Security Institute, while Zhu built navigation and fleet-management systems before Robocurve existed. Robocurve sits below Drop.io because the dossier shows research continuity, not yet the stubborn commercial thesis or years-long public argument that would establish missionary temperament through multiple regatta tacks.

**Ro** demonstrates the low-rung distribution failure mechanism: customer acquisition depended on financed machinery rather than a founder-owned audience, with regulatory rails providing the exceptional moat that justified the burn. Robocurve has even less proprietary distribution—Chooi’s largest cited GitHub repository has 9 stars, Zhu has 410 LinkedIn followers, and Inspect Robots has 96 stars. Robocurve does not need a celebrity founder, but an MIT-licensed framework without a deeply attached evaluator community leaves the distribution asset outside the cap table and available to any lab with 13-fork evidence that copying has begun.

Inspect Robots supporting 40-plus VLAs and more than 260 language models could compound over 18 months if every physical evaluation adds proprietary traces, longitudinal comparisons, and lab relationships. Robocurve instead publishes MIT-licensed tooling while disclosing no revenue, pricing, or conflict-management policy, so the likely financing tack is bespoke evaluation services funding benchmark development. Robocurve can become default-alive that way, but services revenue, expensive physical environments, and open software do not automatically form the venture-shaped structure that converts activity into defensibility.

Robocurve’s round would flip from merely obscure to genuinely mispriced if the SAFE were below the Summer 2026 batch median because conventional funds had explicitly passed on independent physical evaluation as too niche or nonprofit-shaped. No round price, size, competing investors, or visible pass is disclosed, so Robocurve receives only limited non-consensus credit from operating between METR-style evaluation and robotics infrastructure. “Hard to explain” is not the same thing as “cheap”; value seed requires both illegibility and an entry price.

**Sublime Security** is the useful single-anchor parallel: both sell trust infrastructure before the category’s consolidation wave makes independent measurement obviously valuable. Robocurve has a credible timing claim—general-purpose robotics targeted by 2028—and physical testing is meaningfully less generic than another LLM agent shell. Robocurve still falls outside my strongest revealed-preference sleeves: it has no on-chain economics like Solana, no DTC margin structure like Allbirds, and only an early Wizard-of-Oz resemblance through one manually operated pilot.

One completed physical-robot pilot predicts that by July 2029 Robocurve will have pivoted from “independent benchmark institution” to paid robot-vendor QA and validation services producing $1 million to $3 million of annual revenue. Robocurve’s founders can deliver technically credible evaluations, but weak owned distribution and an MIT-licensed core will push Robocurve toward selling labor-intensive trust engagements rather than controlling a standard with software-like margins.

Inspect Robots v1, 40-plus VLA integrations, and Chooi’s evaluation pedigree support the strongest bull case: Robocurve could become the METR of embodied AI just as Pinterest’s missionary founder justified overriding my normal structural concerns. The bull case stings because a neutral standard can acquire authority before it acquires revenue, and 96 GitHub stars may be the first hint of that flywheel. Robocurve’s one unpublished pilot, 13 forks, absent conflict policy, and vendor-run substitution risk still win today; Pinterest’s known exception had founder-network conviction, while Ro’s exception had regulatory rails, and Robocurve has neither. I would flip to Invest if three unaffiliated robotics vendors each completed and renewed paid Robocurve evaluations within 12 months.
