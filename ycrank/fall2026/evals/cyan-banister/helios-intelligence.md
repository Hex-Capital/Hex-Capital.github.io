# Helios Intelligence -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine checking a change in my biology as casually as checking my sleep, then learning whether an intervention actually helped. Helios Intelligence has a plausible route toward that world: make molecular sampling repeatable at home, connect those measurements to daily behavior, and learn which changes predict meaningful outcomes. The small leak is the founders’ May 2026 mAge research, which combines proteomics, wearables, and mortality outcomes and reports a 21% reduction in baseline prediction error. That is something to follow. But a population-level prediction improvement still leaves considerable distance to a reliable answer about what I should do tomorrow.

The founders are the strongest reason I would keep following. Omar Abudayyeh and Jonathan Gootenberg have already built molecular-diagnostics companies, including Sherlock, and jointly run a laboratory; Abudayyeh’s biographies also report acquired ventures. This is more substantial than two impressive academic résumés attached to a newly fashionable category. They have repeatedly organized people around difficult biology. I give that history real weight, while leaving room below the highest founder scores because the dossier establishes their technical commitment more clearly than their commercial command of this particular consumer business.

Roon is the curious object here: a supplement storefront advertising subscriptions from $9.16 per tin, intended to help fund biological data collection. A mundane consumer purchase financing difficult biological infrastructure fits a pattern I like. But why does buying the tin lead someone to repeatedly collect blood, share records, or change a habit? Helios has not yet supplied that behavioral bridge. Availability for purchase establishes a storefront; it does not establish purchases, retention, or willingness to use the forthcoming molecular test. I cannot count imagined customer behavior as evidence.

I also want to know exactly which constraint disappears. “Reactive healthcare” describes a frustration, but the dossier identifies no particular reimbursement rule, gatekeeper, quantified delay, or fee that Helios removes. Home collection could return time and control to people. Whether it does so depends on sampling reliability, useful interpretation, and a path to action. Function, Superpower, and SiPhox already offer pieces of that experience. Helios’s claimed 1,000-plus biomarkers matter if they change a decision; those counts cannot establish superiority over competitors’ different panels. Nor does founder expertise establish company control over assay rights, an exclusive dataset, or another scarce enabling asset.

The strongest argument against my hesitation is that waiting for consumer proof could miss exceptional molecular inventors building the difficult layer first. **CosmicBrain AI** is a relevant exception: deep founder commitment and a manufacturing thesis justified investing before revenue. The material difference was a functioning prototype and signed industrial proofs of concept. Helios’s related research is encouraging, but its commercial assay remains “coming soon.” Its proposed combination of molecular data and medical records also makes consent and custody consequential. The engineering posting explicitly requests encryption, access controls, and audit logging—constructive intent, without proof of implementation. Likewise, the discrepancy between two publicly named founders and a posting mentioning three warrants clarification; it does not establish deceptive leadership.

I land at **Neutral**. Helios Intelligence has founders worth backing in principle and a future worth wanting, but the evidence currently connects their scientific capability to the proposed experience only partway. My verifiable flip condition is an independently assessed longitudinal pilot in which users repeatedly complete the home test and its reproducible measurements improve a prespecified clinical decision beyond existing information. That would turn the interesting research pathway into an artifact people can actually use—and give me a reason to put my own money behind this particular company.
