# Dualizer -- Paul Graham Evaluation

I would ask Nathan, Chris, and Dongha what stopped them last month and what they built to get around it. The dossier tells me where they studied and worked, but gives no such episode. Chris’s AVEVA experience and simulation teaching make him unusually relevant to this problem. They do not establish how he behaves when something breaks. Dualizer gets the absent-evidence default on resourcefulness. That is the largest obstacle to my writing a check.

The technical evidence is better. KinetIQ reportedly has a deterministic simulation engine, with Vantage proposing changes an engineer approves. Keeping the physics deterministic and making model edits inspectable is a sensible division of labor. There are calibration demonstrations, which puts Dualizer beyond an architectural diagram. But those demonstrations use synthetic histories, and the proprietary engine is company-described. There is no measured-plant validation or comparative benchmark. I give intermediate credit for the artifact, with the certainty discount intact.

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders may know precisely which difficult work everyone else avoids. Their backgrounds span process simulation, controls, chemistry, and software. The $100,000 win against 531 teams in September 2026 adds a reason to inspect what they built. This resembles my pattern of backing young technical teams after a concrete proof spike. The relevant exception is Bindwell: direct problem exposure and laboratory validation made youth a distraction. Dualizer has relevant exposure and a demonstration, but lacks the external technical validation that made that exception persuasive. Winning a competition does not fill that gap.

The proposed deployment work could reveal the actual company. Proprietary operating data, unusual equipment, and custom kinetics are exactly the sort of tedious details a founder should handle personally. They could produce something engineers cannot get from a conversational interface attached to an existing simulator. That distinction matters because natural-language control of AVEVA has already been demonstrated, and DWSIM offers an MCP server. Yet a job description describes work to be done. It does not document a founder’s own collision with this problem or a workaround already delivered. I cannot award origin or resourcefulness points for the opportunity to demonstrate them.

Who needs Dualizer badly enough to use it while it is crude? The dossier identifies process engineers, but no returning user or customer workflow. As of September, the first paid industrial pilot was still a next step. That leaves demand at its default; it does not establish rejection. Likewise, the former EntroPINN name supplies no evidence of a rejected hypothesis and an evidence-driven product change, so adaptability stays at its default. The shared Berkeley history is encouraging. The advertised hires establish neither premature delegation nor founder-led selling. I see no affirmative red flag strong enough to condemn the team, but also no user pull strong enough to override the resourcefulness gate.

I would pass on Dualizer today. A market estimated at $1.77 billion is ample room to start; the unresolved question is whether these founders can turn customer-specific engineering pain into repeatedly useful software. My single flip condition is a documented customer episode: a named process engineer encounters a specific blocker, the founders ship a workaround within weeks, and that engineer repeatedly uses the resulting model on measured operating data. That would connect the people, the artifact, and the need.
