# HABU -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

HABU’s decisive question is how much cheaper an apartment becomes when development desk work approaches zero. The company estimates more than 1,000 administrative hours per project. Eliminating that work could build a better developer. But its stated destination—10%–15% lower project costs and 20% faster schedules—does not yet describe an orders-of-magnitude change in housing access. The $160 billion invested in multifamily in 2025 establishes an existing capital pool; it does not establish the value HABU can capture or the resource constraint it can overturn. I pass at this stage because the disclosed consequence is incremental against my investment threshold.

The strongest technical claim is five times more projects per employee, alongside 95% faster processing and 90%+ task automation. Those are testable claims. The dossier supplies no benchmark method, matched projects, or observed operating results that establish them. More importantly, processing an underwriting package faster only changes apartment delivery if that work controls the critical path. Permits, architects, contractors, and construction remain external dependencies. TestFit already automates feasibility, and Mosaic combines software with construction operations. HABU has described a broader operating workflow, but has not demonstrated a technical discontinuity against either alternative. That leaves technical evidence at my missing-evidence default.

The bull case has substance. Bryan Tharalson has managed more than $80 million in development projects, and Junhao Chen combines Meta AI operations experience with Navy aviation logistics. Development, selling, and operational systems are useful complementary backgrounds for this problem. Performing development directly also gives HABU a potential way to impose its workflow and observe outcomes across the project, rather than waiting for another developer to change behavior. My actual investing patterns include execution-heavy businesses; scientific novelty is not an absolute requirement. But this team’s biographies do not establish fast belief revision or exceptional recruiting, and the potential project-data loop remains an inference. The exception for a small team entering an enormous existing value pool deserves consideration. Here, the dossier has not established the expanding economic mechanism that would justify overriding the consequence gate.

I would spend the next dollar testing whether administrative automation survives contact with project economics. HABU discloses no experiment budget, numerical acceptance threshold, or stop condition, so I cannot credit risk removal merely because the operating system spans the development lifecycle. Its anticipated engineering, construction, and civil-engineering hires could address real constraints, but the dossier does not establish which uncertainty each hire removes first. Nor does the fee/merchant model disclose fees, delivery costs, or margins. Investor-provided project equity changes who supplies capital; it does not by itself prove that HABU can deliver housing below incumbent cost while earning an adequate fee.

My objection is therefore specific: HABU may successfully automate the developer’s office while leaving most housing economics intact. There is no evidence here of prohibited categories, subsidy dependence, or founders ignoring contrary data; I would not invent those red flags. The unresolved issue is whether workflow efficiency becomes a consequential production advantage. One verifiable flip condition would reopen my decision: an independently audited, completed Phoenix project delivering comparable apartments at half the matched incumbent all-in cost, including financing and HABU’s fully loaded labor, with a positive development margin. That would demonstrate a housing cost breakthrough worth underwriting.
