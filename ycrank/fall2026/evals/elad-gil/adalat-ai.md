# Adalat AI -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Adalat AI has found a distribution channel that most court-software startups would struggle to reproduce: judicial administration can make its product the default. Kerala’s requirement, effective November 1, 2025, creates an institutional opening for multilingual courtroom AI. The controlling economic variable is whether each deployed courtroom generates recurring contribution toward a scalable business. Adalat’s documented agreements specify free, pro bono delivery. Wider adoption therefore establishes usefulness without establishing a return on my capital.

This is an explicit business-model constraint, rather than missing pre-seed revenue. Philanthropy finances delivery, courts receive the software without commercial fees, and the dossier identifies no mechanism through which an equity investor participates in the value created. The adjacent $2.17 billion case-management market does not resolve that problem. Commercial suppliers demonstrate that courts can purchase software, but their revenue cannot be assigned to Adalat. Its current model deliberately removes price as an adoption barrier.

The strongest argument against passing is that Adalat has already crossed an unusually difficult institutional threshold. Oxford’s overview documented more than 4,000 courts across nine states in March 2026; the company subsequently reported 6,300-plus courtrooms across 11 states. Those figures establish substantial deployment, although they do not establish daily activity or retention. A magistrate’s report of handling five to six witnesses daily versus two to three previously suggests meaningful productivity improvement. That is a company-published testimonial, not a controlled measurement, but the combination of deployment and operational usefulness deserves substantial credit. Free distribution could create the installed base for an adjacent commercial product. The dossier simply does not document that product or its economics.

The architecture also has more substance than transcription alone. Court APIs, document processing, and case-flow dashboards connect the product to recurring judicial work. Better speech and language models should improve that workflow. However, the dossier does not establish exclusive data rights, a proprietary correction loop, or the cost of replacing these integrations. Competing suppliers already cover transcription and registry automation. The September 2026 credential-enabled production-infrastructure compromise further raises the operating standard required here: the reported remediation matters, but administrative access to production is a serious weakness in software supporting evidentiary records. I would distinguish the company’s report of no confidential-data access from independent proof that the entire system is secure.

Saxena’s court practice and Bhattacharya’s multilingual NLP work explain why this team could build and deploy the product. They do not establish prior founder learning or costly personal commitment, so I retain the founder dimension’s missing-evidence default. Adalat fits my pattern of entering regulated markets through deep implementation expertise. It also has the beginnings of a product becoming distribution. Neither pattern supplies an investable economic structure. The known exceptions do not change that conclusion: this is an operating application with institutional adoption, rather than frontier research requiring a temporary demand override, and there is no demonstrated reconstruction of supplier margins. The listed 80-person team makes delivery economics especially relevant, although headcount alone does not establish inefficient spending.

I would pass on a personal angel investment under the documented structure. My 36-month expectation is that Adalat remains a grant-supported court platform with expanded deployment and less than $1 million in annual earned software revenue, excluding grants and donations. The strongest upside remains substantial public benefit and privileged institutional access. My single flip condition would be a legally documented, investable commercial vehicle with a paid court-workflow contract demonstrating positive contribution after inference, support, and implementation costs. That would connect its demonstrated distribution to a financial return.
