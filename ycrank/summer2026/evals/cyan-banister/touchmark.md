# Touchmark -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Ilia Bolgov built product at Revolut, and Roman Yanushevskyi carries an IOI gold medal plus Citadel Securities and Lovable experience. Touchmark is a mathematically strange pricing thesis attached to an organizationally ordinary two-founder silhouette: its highest signal is making output quality—not token consumption—the billable variable, while its lowest is that neither founder is identified as CEO or tiebreaker. Touchmark may have Tiz, and perhaps Biz, but the dossier cannot show me who holds the wheel when the quality model and the customer’s invoice disagree.

**Crusoe** was the high rung because burning stranded gas to mine Bitcoin joined three unfashionable mechanisms into one coherent machine. Touchmark has a smaller version of that magical weirdness: Touchmark wants an SDK to evaluate an AI output, wait for the score to settle, and then adjust the bill. That is not another rate card. The divergence is load-bearing, though: Crusoe’s founder belonged on the well pad, while Touchmark shows no lived pricing dispute, no sophisticated investor passing on aesthetic grounds, and no evidence that its YC round is underpriced. Metronome’s $128 million and Orb’s $44 million also make the surrounding market rather less lonely.

**GameCrush** taught me that ambiguous authority lets investors and conflict strip away the product’s DNA. Touchmark’s dossier names Ilia Bolgov and Roman Yanushevskyi only as “Founder,” supplies no co-founder history, and identifies no CEO, equity structure, or operating tiebreaker. Touchmark therefore sits near that low governance rung, not because two founders are automatically fatal, but because nobody has publicly answered the question that matters: when a customer rejects Roman’s quality score and Ilia wants to preserve the account, who decides?

Ilia Bolgov’s Revolut product work gives Touchmark adjacency to pricing systems, while Roman Yanushevskyi’s quantitative research gives Touchmark the machinery to model quality. Over 18 months, lived experience would compound through hundreds of invoice disputes, customer exceptions, and uncomfortable conversations about whether a “good” answer created value; credentials alone do not create that loop. No founder is shown operating AI billing, buying evaluation infrastructure, or personally suffering outcome-pricing failures, so the origin still reads analytical rather than autobiographical. These are impressive people, but I am looking for the Wi-Fi name they did not know I was reading.

Metronome’s $128 million of funding defines the counterfactual for Touchmark’s frontier score. Touchmark would move up a tier if quality-adjusted billing became a regulated settlement layer—auditable evaluation, contractual dispute resolution, and financial liability for incorrect scores in healthcare or finance. Touchmark currently publishes none of those artifacts: no evaluation methodology, accuracy result, security detail, dispute process, or commercial terms. Today it is a software SDK that an incumbent can ingest as another event, not yet the ugly regulated machine other investors refuse to touch.

**Flock Safety** could be encountered before it could be explained: a camera, a Wi-Fi signal, an artifact in the physical world. Touchmark is the inverse parallel. Touchmark’s website says “Coming soon,” and the reported SDK has no public documentation, customer deployment, performance measurement, or product a normal human can use within 48 hours. The abstraction may eventually become observable through invoices and dispute rates, but today I would be investing entirely through founder testimony—the opposite of how I found Flock.

Roman Yanushevskyi’s Lovable AI-engineering experience makes my 36-month forecast specific: Touchmark will pivot from quality-adjusted billing into an AI evaluation and observability SDK, remain below $1 million ARR, and stop presenting itself as an independent billing system. Touchmark’s evaluation layer is easier to adopt than its pricing doctrine because buyers can inspect scores without surrendering invoice authority, while Metronome, Orb, Stripe, or Lago can consume those scores if customers eventually want them attached to billing.

Roman Yanushevskyi’s IOI gold medal is the bull case that stings: Touchmark may be a **DeepMind**-style polymath exception, where unusual technical range overrides my preference for lived origin. Ilia Bolgov’s Revolut product experience, Zendesk’s verified-resolution pricing, and Touchmark’s proposed quality-to-billing dataset could produce a compounding calibration moat before Metronome or Orb recognizes the category. The dossier still loses that argument today because Touchmark has no disclosed dataset, customer, evaluation accuracy, revenue, or working public artifact, and unresolved CEO authority contradicts an unstated pattern I learned expensively. I am neutral and would not write the check yet; five named AI vendors each renewing a quality-priced Touchmark contract after six months with less than 2% of invoices disputed would flip me to Invest.
