# Wingman Law -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. An injured person can hand over the paperwork, understand what is happening, and recover compensation without becoming a part-time case manager. Wingman Law could help create that world. But getting there requires reliable evidence handling, dependable execution, and proof that automation improves the client’s outcome. Attorneys directing agents is a plausible starting architecture; the dossier does not yet establish those transitions or show a working artifact that makes that future feel inevitable. I can imagine the destination. Imagination alone does not earn the check.

The founders are the strongest reason to keep looking. Sun Woo Lee’s reported **$350K in legaltech sales during law school** shows she did something with the intersection of law and software before Wingman existed. Nicholas Le brings **700+ prior injury cases** and product experience at StrongSuit, where YC credits him with contributions toward **$4M ARR**. Those are prior accomplishments, not Wingman traction, but they matter as evidence of operating tuition. Recruiting Le first as an employee and subsequently making him a co-founder also supplies a concrete first-believer act. The strongest bull case is that these two know which seemingly trivial case-management failures actually cost clients money. That fits my pattern of backing mundane workflow repair. It does not yet establish an independently developed technical advantage or a long record of obsession.

Why does the injured person receive less control today? Wingman identifies overloaded staff remembering tasks, but the dossier does not establish the resulting delay, lost compensation, or who benefits economically from that failure. Its contingency model removes upfront attorney fees, yet the percentage and expense treatment are undisclosed. I cannot tell whether automation will return more money and agency to clients or principally increase the practice’s margin. Direct representation gives Wingman responsibility for the outcome; it does not, by itself, demonstrate that the underlying bargain changes.

The distinction from EvenUp and Supio is commercially meaningful: Wingman serves the client rather than selling software to the lawyer. But those suppliers also make overlapping automation available to existing practices. Internal development alone does not establish a scarce enabling layer. Wingman’s public visualization uses licensed litigation data, and the proposed advantage from linked insurer responses and recoveries remains a hypothesis. There is also no documented completed client action, repeat usage, or recovery to score as product proof. I make exceptions for pre-revenue companies when a functioning prototype and concrete commitments substantiate the founder’s vision. This dossier supplies founder substance, but not that counterweight.

Two gates deserve precise treatment. Medical records passing through agents make consent and custody central, while the public privacy policy primarily addresses SMS and website analytics. That is an unresolved architecture question, not evidence of an actual privacy violation. Likewise, Lee’s CEO designation and legal-operations role alongside Le’s Managing Attorney role could reflect sensible professional responsibilities. They do not establish deceptive leadership. I would need clear accountability and contractual control arrangements before signing; I will not invent a governance failure from different titles.

**I pass on Wingman Law today.** The evidence supports capable practitioners building a potentially useful practice more strongly than it supports the future system I would be underwriting with my own money. One verifiable flip condition would reopen the decision: an independently auditable cohort of **20 completed matters** demonstrating higher client compensation after fees than comparable conventional cases, with documented consent and medical-data custody. That would connect the agents to an actual transfer of value to injured people.
