# Ultrasonium -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have acquired specific knowledge through costly action. Chris Carter worked on additively manufactured steel at Argonne and NASA-funded melt-pool simulations. Alexander Urbanski invented a DOE-funded thermoelectric generator and worked inside manufacturing-monitoring company Phase3D. Jack Qiu and Hunter Brown bring control hardware and national-laboratory experience. These are not credentials loosely adjacent to the problem; they are evidence that the team has built around metallurgy, physical control, and difficult hardware. The founder criterion is Ultrasonium’s strongest signal.

The advantage also survives someone copying the software. A hemispherical phased array must coordinate acoustic confinement, thermal modulation, feedback, and solidification of molten metal. U.S. Patent 12,208,450, issued in January 2025, gives Ultrasonium a concrete asset rather than a claim that the algorithm is proprietary. Successful deployments could compound into metallurgical data and process parameters. But that future learning loop cannot be scored as if it already exists: the NSF project still describes submillimeter accuracy, greater than 99% density, and aluminum and copper printing as anticipated results.

The sharp bear case is that this is an unusually well-staffed research program, not yet a manufacturing company. The reported 15× speed and 75% cost improvements disclose neither geometries nor test methods. Seurat and VulcanForms have raised approximately $178 million and $575 million respectively, while Fluent Metal attacks liquid-metal deposition through a different architecture. Competitors do not need to infringe Ultrasonium’s patent; they only need to deliver acceptable parts sooner. Hardware defensibility without repeatable production is protected possibility, not protected value.

The market is broad enough, but the wedge is unfocused. Metal additive manufacturing was estimated at $7.73 billion in 2023 and projected to reach $35.33 billion by 2030. The same underlying capability could serve machinists, aerospace, defense, energy, research, dental, and medical-device customers. Yet those buyers have different qualification cycles, tolerances, and sales motions. Direct sales to small machinist shops may establish the machine, but no public price, paid pilot, completed transaction, or company-specific SAM establishes which market pulls hardest. I assign no imagined monetization credit.

There is also no customer-created network. Each machine may produce process data for Ultrasonium, but the dossier does not show that one customer improves another customer’s product, acquisition, or utility. The company fits my revealed shift toward infrastructure bottlenecks and hard technology, while missing the permissionless participation pattern that creates leverage without proportional labor. No integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-morale red flag is documented. The 17-person team and fourteen advertised openings do raise a narrower concern: the organization may be adding coordination and burn before technical truth has collapsed the search space. My known exceptions permit missing revenue or network effects when extraordinary product behavior supplies the counterweight; that counterweight is not yet independently demonstrated here.

I would write a small check as a bounded technical option. Ultrasonium has the rare combination of founder-specific knowledge, physical substrate, issued intellectual property, and a market with room to maneuver. My 36-month base case is that by August 2029 it is selling or operating qualified systems for one industrial vertical at roughly $3 million to $8 million in annualized revenue, not yet a horizontal manufacturing platform. One verifiable flip condition governs the investment: if an independent paid pilot cannot reproduce greater than 99% density together with a material cost or throughput advantage on aluminum or copper by August 2027, I reverse the decision.
