# SubVysion -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence is real but not yet enormous. Locator errors or inaccurate maps reportedly cause one-third of utility strikes, and preventing those strikes would improve worker safety, project schedules, and infrastructure reliability. But SubVysion has not quantified the annual strike-cost pool, the reduction its system can produce, or the population ultimately affected. A $1.57 billion global mapping market growing to $4.08 billion by 2034 describes an industry, not a 100x societal consequence.

The rover-to-AR workflow is technically credible: multisensor GPR and positioning data become georeferenced 3D maps that crews can inspect on phones. The founders also distinguish it from Exodigo’s scanning-and-engineering service, 4M’s records-based maps, and RodRadar’s excavator-mounted detection. But the claimed centimeter accuracy and “100% visibility” have no independent detection dataset. Wet soil, clay, and small-diameter lines are precisely where the physics becomes inconvenient. Until SubVysion measures performance against 811 markings or a named competing system, this is an integrated workflow—not a demonstrated 5–10x discontinuity.

The first dollars should attack that uncertainty. The company has sold $355,000 of SAFEs, won its first customer and investor through cold calling, secured contracts, and deployed on construction sites across the United States. Those are unusually useful pre-seed signals. Yet the dossier gives no blinded excavation test, false-negative threshold, soil-condition matrix, experiment cost, or stop condition. Customer activity does not remove the central kill risk if crews still cannot know when the map is wrong.

The strongest bull case is the team. Maxwell Stone built on-device inference software used in more than 10,000 pharmacies and worked on railyard mapping and defect detection; Wenson Tang built emergency-dispatch software and a LiDAR search-and-rescue drone. They have relevant sensing, geospatial, and safety-system instincts, plus the willingness to sell directly into job sites. An execution-heavy construction wedge can become a valuable infrastructure platform if every corrected scan compounds into a proprietary subsurface dataset. That would fit the less-publicized pattern of backing operational systems in undeniable workflows.

But this is not yet the exception that overrides weak technical proof. Block began with an incremental wedge but pointed toward an American Express-scale value pool and a product capable of expanding into financial infrastructure. SubVysion’s disclosed market is much smaller, its data flywheel remains inferred, and three well-capitalized competitors already span scanning, records aggregation, and live detection. There is no exit-first, harmful-category, or permanent-subsidy red flag. The relevant warning is subtler: financing and customer deployments can create the appearance of progress while the largest sensing risk remains unmeasured.

I pass today. The founders may build a good construction-technology company, but I do not yet see the one-sided option on a radically different cost curve. One result would flip me: an independent, blinded field trial across wet clay and small-diameter utilities showing at least a 5x reduction in missed utilities versus standard 811 workflows, at a project cost below the expected losses avoided.
