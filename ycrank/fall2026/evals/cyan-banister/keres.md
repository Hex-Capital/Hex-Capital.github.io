# Keres -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine an air-defense crew counting sorties instead of counting down its remaining missiles. Keres’s Garmur would destroy an incoming drone, land, and go back out. The consequential word is “again.” Getting there requires surviving the engagement, recovering reliably, and returning to service cheaply enough to change the defender’s arithmetic. The dossier describes that destination, but supplies no flight artifact demonstrating the complete sequence. I have a future to investigate; I do not yet have its present-day leak.

Why does the defender lose economically even after a successful interception? Because destroying an inexpensive threat can consume scarce, expensive defensive inventory. DIU’s 2025 counter-UAS program documents that problem. Keres proposes returning the aircraft to the operator after the kill, potentially restoring control over magazine depth. That is a concrete system mechanism. But the dossier identifies neither a quantified engagement-cost advantage nor an artificial gatekeeper collecting a removable rent. Inspection, damage repair, and turnaround could simply move the expense into the hangar. Recovery matters only if the recovered aircraft is economically useful.

Christopher Palazzolo gives me the strongest reason to keep looking. More than four years across Boom’s aircraft maintenance, avionics, flight testing, and XB-1 production operations, followed by H3X operations leadership, are relevant operating tuition. Those jobs put him near the unglamorous details that could determine whether reuse works. They do not establish independently pursued interceptor experiments, unusual recruiting ability, or command of military procurement economics. Parker Jamieson is identified as CEO by secondary sources; his earlier work and the founders’ decision arrangements remain unknown. Missing biographies do not establish opportunism or a governance shell game.

The strongest objection to passing is that an experienced aircraft operator may recognize a practical design opportunity before public demonstrations exist. Anduril’s Roadrunner-M can return when it is not expended; Keres proposes returning after destroying the target. That difference could be substantial. My pre-revenue hardware exception at **CosmicBrain AI** rested on a similar willingness to believe ahead of revenue, but included a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a documented lifelong building record. Keres has relevant professional experience without those additional proofs. Its October 12, 2026 media profile establishes visibility, not completed missions or costly customer commitment.

Keres also brushes against my preference for concealed physical infrastructure, but the evidence currently describes a finished interceptor. There is no demonstrated controlled bottleneck serving multiple downstream products, and the proposed proprietary mission dataset remains an inference. My pattern of discovering companies through working artifacts is unfulfilled here: no operator’s repeated behavior exposes something the pitch cannot. Equally, the dossier establishes no severe privacy violation, deceptive leadership, or dismissive claim that incumbents are trivial. This is a pass for insufficient affirmative fit, rather than an active red-flag rejection.

I would pass with my own money today. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an independently witnessed military evaluation showing the same Garmur completing repeated destroy–recover–relaunch cycles, with maintenance records demonstrating lower fully burdened cost per successful interception than the buyer’s existing alternative. That would make the central promise observable. Until then, the aircraft’s most important feature is still a claim.
