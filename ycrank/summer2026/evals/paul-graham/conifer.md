# Conifer -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when Conifer broke. The dossier shows three names—Sage, Juniper, and Conifer—but not the observation that caused any change, the obstacle the founders encountered, or the workaround they shipped. Renaming may be rapid search or mere confusion. Without the before-and-after story, it proves neither resourcefulness nor running-back flexibility. Jeffords’s earlier expansion of health screenings to 20-plus barbershops and 10,000 people shows initiative, but the dossier does not identify the resistance he overcame or connect that execution to Conifer.

The strongest evidence is the artifact. The team shipped desktop and CLI installers and built a proprietary Rust inference engine reportedly decoding up to 60% faster than llama.cpp on Apple Silicon. That is considerably better than a diagram or a plan to begin after funding. Muehlberger’s edge-inference research and edge-AI hardware work make the technical claim credible enough to investigate. But the benchmark is company-reported, the binaries prevent source-level verification, and there is no published routing volume, task-success rate, or quality-versus-cost curve. The engine is real evidence; the claimed 80%-plus savings remains a hypothesis built on it.

Who wants the crude product now? The answer is still blurry. More than 100 developers joined the prior Sage launch, and the shared repository has 248 stars, but neither number establishes repeated use of the current router. Conifer’s 461 LinkedIn followers and the Wall Street Journal coverage of Muehlberger’s Princeton gap year are attention, not pull. There are no named teams, payments, retained workloads, or users forcing the product into an unplanned workflow. “Teams with high monthly token spending” is a market description, not a narrow well.

There is a plausible founder-originated wedge. Muehlberger worked on multimodal inference at the edge, while Jeffords built an applied computer-vision pipeline. They did not discover local inference by reading a market map. Yet the dossier never shows that either founder personally suffered the specific problem of routing expensive production requests across local and cloud models. The schlep is also incomplete: full local execution is concentrated on Apple Silicon, with Windows and Linux receiving thin clients. That could become useful infrastructure, but it could also remain a Mac optimization inside a route that Ollama, OpenRouter, or another funded platform can reproduce.

The bull case is that this matches the pattern of young technical founders producing a concrete proof before the market has settled. The closest exception is Bindwell: both teams pair youth with relevant technical work and an artifact built before permission. The material difference is validation. Bindwell had laboratory results; Conifer has a proprietary, self-reported speed benchmark and no demonstrated current-product pull. None of the usual red flags is explicit—there is no dependence on a large partner, ceremonial launch, or premature sales hiring—but the favorable exception is not strong enough to replace the missing evidence.

I would pass today. My 36-month base case is that Conifer pivots from general least-cost routing into Apple Silicon inference tooling and remains below $3 million ARR, because the engine is more substantiated than either the routing advantage or the customer need. One result would flip me: five named teams each routing at least 100,000 production requests per month for eight consecutive weeks, with independently reproducible savings above 50%, less than a 2% decline in task success, and a dated account of the routing failure the founders had to fix to retain them. That would turn the engine, the users, and the founders’ response to resistance into one logical proof.
