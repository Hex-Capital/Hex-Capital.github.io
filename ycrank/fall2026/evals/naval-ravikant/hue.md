# Hue -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Kethini’s most useful credential is having built and launched Datadog’s AI SRE. He has worked on an agent whose mistakes matter in production. Hue’s public SDK, first released September 15 and updated to version 0.14.0 on October 25, 2026, adds an observable artifact to that background. This is meaningful evidence of specific knowledge expressed through action. It does not yet establish that the founders have solved the harder problem: reproducing production conditions faithfully enough that passing a simulation predicts real reliability.

Hue’s mechanism is sensible. Capture a task, reconstruct its context, simulate its tools, preserve state changes, then reset and rerun. The decisive investment question is what accumulates after the software is copied. Customer regression suites might become expensive to replace, but the dossier establishes neither that switching cost nor exclusive rights to useful production data. Uninstrumented calls remain invisible to the SDK. That makes coverage dependent on customer implementation; it supplies no evidence of a defensible asset.

The strongest argument against passing is that the founders are building exactly where agent deployment becomes difficult. Recorded outputs cannot fully represent changing APIs, mutable state, and sequences of actions. Someone who launched an AI SRE may understand those failures before a general evaluation vendor does. The release cadence makes that possibility credible. But Patronus already offers simulation environments, while Braintrust and LangChain occupy adjacent production workflows. Hue needs an advantage that strengthens through deployment. Being early to another implementation of a recognized need is insufficient.

The public SDK fits my preference for tools developers can adopt without institutional permission. The missing mechanism is customer-created value: nothing establishes that one team’s usage improves another team’s tests, onboarding, or reliability. Customer-specific environments could remain isolated islands. The $1.01 billion testing-and-validation category estimate establishes a market hypothesis, but it does not identify an adjacent buyer and a shared capability that carries Hue into substantially broader demand. Pricing and revenue are also undisclosed, so direct value capture receives the absent-evidence default.

My exceptions for an already-forming network or an unusually compelling, capital-efficient prototype deserve consideration here. Hue has shipped, and its two-person team could learn quickly. Yet the dossier shows neither a self-reinforcing network nor demonstrated simulation performance compelling enough to override the durability gap. I see no documented integrity violation, artificial financing deadline, or founder instability. The concern is structural: the product may improve while competitors inherit the same capabilities.

I would pass with my own money today. The single condition that would reopen the decision is customer-validated evidence that a contractually reusable corpus of production-derived environments improves regression detection for subsequent customers. That would turn deployment into accumulation. Today, Hue demonstrates relevant builders and an executable product idea; the compounding asset remains hypothetical.
