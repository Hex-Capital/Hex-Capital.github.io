# Ryhla -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Saving a developer $160,000 by telling it which projects to abandon is a useful beginning. Ryhla reports eliminating eight of ten planned battery projects within days. That is a concrete job for a small group of users who have an expensive reason to want it done. But it answers what the product did. It does not yet answer my first question about the founders: what happened when they were blocked, and what did they do next?

Sara El Baghdadi and Yassine Guennoun have more relevant experience than their mathematics and machine-learning credentials alone suggest. They reportedly researched grid models at Stanford and initially deployed them with utilities. That gives Ryhla a plausible origin in work they actually encountered. Interconnection also contains plenty of neglected work: utility-specific requirements, applications, upgrade estimates, and follow-through. Their friendship since middle school removes one concern about a hastily assembled founding team. None of these facts, however, supplies an observed episode of resourcefulness or an evidence-driven change of route. Those remain unknown.

The strongest reason to invest anyway is that the founders have apparently crossed from research into use. Ryhla reports utility approval for 91% of model-approved sites among its 2025 applications, alongside deployment with a Midwest solar developer. This fits my pattern of backing technical founders in unfamiliar sectors when they produce concrete proof. But the percentage lacks a denominator, utility coverage, and independent validation. A model that approves only the safest sites could achieve a high approval rate while missing most useful opportunities. The battery-project savings demonstrate practical value; they do not establish repeat purchasing, retention, or revenue. I give the technical result and customer outcome separate, provisional credit.

There is a plausible large company here if managing applications creates a growing record of utility decisions that makes subsequent predictions better. The dossier establishes neither that learning effect nor exclusive access to the underlying data. Paces already offers interconnection reports and power-flow studies, while Enverus owns simulation software. Ryhla will have to learn faster through customer work. Utility approval authority is an operating constraint, but there is no evidence that the founders are waiting for a large partner to deliver distribution. Their request for developer introductions is a reasonable starting point. I would want to see it become personal recruitment followed by customers bringing their next projects.

I would pass for now. The strongest objection is that working deployments and substantial avoided costs may already reveal unusually effective founders whose obstacle stories simply were not captured in public research. That is credible. Working products and validated results can justify exceptions to ordinary founder uncertainty, but Ryhla’s anonymous deployment and incompletely specified benchmark do not yet justify overriding my resourcefulness gate. One verifiable condition would change my decision: a dated customer account showing the founders personally overcoming a utility-specific blocker, shipping the workaround within weeks, and winning a second paid project from that customer because it worked.
