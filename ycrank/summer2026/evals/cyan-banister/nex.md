# Nex -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. GTM teams probably describe an outcome and let software inspect company context, write the workflow, request approval, and execute across the stack. Nex has a small artifact leaking from that future: WUPHF earned 1.2K GitHub stars, 94 forks, and a 260-point Show HN discussion, while the commercial product turns natural-language goals into rerunnable code across 1,000-plus integrations. But the dossier never works backward through the transitions that make Nex—not Clay, Gumloop, or Relevance AI—the owner of that future. “Models improved in the last six months” explains today’s crowded round, not what comes next.

The founders are the compelling part. Nazz Mohammad worked in product at HubSpot, built a ChatGPT plugin used by 48,000 people, and participated in VentureBasecamp through acquisition. Francisco Dias was HubSpot CRM’s fourth engineer and later worked on its AI infrastructure. Together they possess real operating tuition in CRM interfaces, integrations, and organizational data. WUPHF also shows that they can recruit developer attention around a shipped artifact. This is stronger than résumé-shaped founder-market fit, although three product identities in six months—AI-native CRM, context infrastructure, then GTM automation—make me unsure whether I am seeing independently derived obsession or fast adaptation to whichever AI surface currently attracts users.

The system wound is legible but not yet deep. GTM operators stitch together Clay, Outreach, Gong, HubSpot, Slack, and email, sometimes paying another $100–$500 monthly for each manual workflow. Nex returns some agency by letting an operator describe the desired result instead of waiting for GTM engineering. Yet no rule, artificial gate, or rent-capturing institution has been isolated. Tool fragmentation is irritating; it is not automatically structural. If an incumbent can add the same conversational layer to an existing workflow runtime, Nex has reduced configuration effort without changing who controls the system.

The bull case stings because the ingredients for a durable layer are present. A real-time context graph, SOC 2 Type 1, granular RBAC, 1,000-plus integrations, prior CRM revenue, and undisclosed enterprise proofs of concept could compound into the organizational memory every GTM agent needs. The open-source attention may be the consumer beachhead for that harder infrastructure. But the dossier calls the customer-specific graph and learned playbooks an inference, supplies no retention or workflow-volume evidence, and explicitly notes that funded competitors already control comparable integrations and runtimes. Today Nex looks more like an application riding the wave than a scarce layer underneath it.

The permissioning problem is the hardest red flag. Nex ingests CRM, email, Slack, and messaging data, while a company-reposted account acknowledged that permissioning learned knowledge was not fully solved. RBAC and SOC 2 govern access and process; they do not establish whether derived knowledge preserves consent, provenance, deletion, and boundaries between users. Because workflows can send messages and open pull requests while the terms disclaim output accuracy, a context mistake can become an external action. The product must make its custody architecture as concrete as its integration count.

I would not invest my own money today. My 36-month expectation is that Nex either pivots again toward context and permissioning infrastructure or remains a sub-$5 million ARR GTM-automation product pressured by larger platforms; the founder quality makes death less likely than repositioning. The one verifiable condition that flips me is twelve months of cohort data showing at least 20 organizations repeatedly executing 100-plus workflows per month, with expanding usage, documented permission-safe context reuse, and no dependence on one integration or model provider. That would prove the graph is becoming infrastructure rather than a fashionable interface.
