# Open Actuation -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Domestic sourcing restrictions are pushing drone OEMs toward US suppliers; whether Open Actuation becomes a large company depends on the cost of delivering each additional custom design. Four-week delivery is useful, but customization must become repeatable production rather than repeated engineering expense. The dossier establishes a delivery promise, not the relationship between customization, manufacturing cost, and gross margin. That missing relationship controls my decision. The broader $18.62 billion actuator market does not resolve it.

The strongest affirmative evidence is that Open Actuation owns meaningful parts of the production workflow. Its disclosed LA facility covers machining, winding, magnet insertion, balancing, and testing, supported by proprietary software and tooling. Improvements to that system could make the company better across successive customer designs. This is a more substantive technical position than an engineering configurator alone. However, accumulated process knowledge and customer switching costs remain hypotheses. There are no measured reductions in engineering hours, scrap, or changeover costs, and custom rotary actuators remain in development. I give credit for the disclosed manufacturing workflow without assuming it already produces exceptional economics.

The bull case is credible: an institutional change could create demand before domestic supply catches up. July 2026 reporting connects customer reshoring interest to restrictions on new foreign-made drones and certain components. Open Actuation also has initial samples receiving customer feedback and reports building motors for drone OEMs. Those are useful pre-seed signals. But samples do not establish repeat purchasing, and stated capacity—thousands monthly on YC versus thousands weekly on the product site—does not establish shipments or a production bottleneck. Atlas Motion already advertises custom prototypes within weeks, while Westmag has raised $11 million for overlapping domestic production. Reshoring can expand the category while leaving individual suppliers with ordinary margins.

Jon Facer’s five years as 1Factory’s first employee provide some evidence of learning inside an early manufacturing-software business; his Gecko Robotics experience adds relevant context. Neither establishes prior founder outcomes or costly personal commitment. The more interesting distribution possibility is the CubeMars storefront: an existing component purchase could introduce an engineering buyer to custom motors. Yet the disclosed $187 catalog price is neither realized revenue nor evidence that those buyers convert. Imported actuator resale also has different economics from proprietary manufacturing, so I would separate the two when assessing customer acquisition and margins.

I would pass today. Open Actuation fits my pattern of entering physical markets where software can change production economics, but it has established ownership of production steps more clearly than an economic advantage. The physical-business exception requires demonstrated changes to unit economics; the research-stage exception requires unusual technical evidence and a credible capital path. Neither is established here. This is missing proof, not evidence that the business cannot work. My single flip condition is a documented six-month cohort of at least three independent OEMs placing repeat custom-motor orders, delivered within four weeks at gross margins above 40% after charging design labor, scrap, and warranty costs. That would connect the manufacturing system to a scalable market proposition.
