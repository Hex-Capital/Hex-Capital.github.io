# Vorflux -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a small engineering team waking up to completed maintenance, tested changes, and a short list of decisions requiring human judgment. Vorflux’s recurring Slack AutoBots are a small artifact pointing toward that world. But getting there requires reliable verification, bounded permissions, and substantially less human supervision. The dossier establishes planning, implementation, and browser QA; it does not establish those remaining transitions. I can daydream the destination. I cannot give Vorflux credit for having mapped the road.

The most interesting object here is a $600 compute bill. Supermemory reportedly spent two days optimizing its memory graph through Vorflux. Somebody committed real time and resources to a particular engineering problem; that tells me more than the undated claim of one trillion tokens consumed. TrashLab’s implemented contract-color and logo-placement controls provide another concrete product outcome. These are useful artifacts, although company-published testimonials without repeat-use cohorts support only modest conviction. At compute prices of $0.2117–$3.3868 an hour plus model usage, I want to know whether a completed task costs less human attention. More machine activity alone does not answer that question.

The strongest argument against passing is the team’s ability to turn this into something much deeper. Prasanna co-founded Rippling and served as CTO; Anant brings Databricks staff-engineering experience; Haritha worked on cloud-native protection at Rubrik. Their overlapping employment histories give the team a basis for working together, and the reported July 2026 financing supplies $15 million to pursue the problem. These founders have operated inside complicated enterprise software. My preference for unglamorous workflow repair gives that combination real weight. However, the dossier does not connect Vorflux to a specific operating wound they previously encountered, or document experiments establishing a long-running obsession with this problem. My exception for an early prototype backed by extraordinary founder obsession therefore remains only partially supported.

Why must developers still babysit the machine? Vorflux identifies the labor involved in prompting, debugging, reviewing, and testing, but it has not demonstrated which part of that burden it removes or who previously collected an avoidable rent. Existing subscriptions and cross-lab review offer customers some choice. They also weaken the allegation that this is merely dependent on one closed supplier. Yet customer choice is different from controlling a scarce enabling asset. Cursor already supplies cloud execution and browser verification; Factory offers model routing and multiple deployment options. Persistent repository knowledge and configurable workflows might become valuable infrastructure, but the dossier supplies no measured dependency or switching effect. The concealed infrastructure pattern I look for has not emerged here.

The privacy architecture also deserves attention: account-lifetime transcript retention and inference outside the primary storage region matter when the material is proprietary code. Account-scoped controls and managed-provider zero-retention arrangements prevent me from declaring an established severe privacy violation; they do not resolve every custody question. Nor does having three co-founders establish a governance failure. My pass rests on the investment mechanism: accomplished builders and working software, with little evidence yet that Vorflux owns something the next wave must pass through. I would keep my money today. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: a documented cohort of three named engineering teams renewing after 90 days of recurring AutoBots use, with task records showing less human review time per accepted change than their previous agent workflow.
