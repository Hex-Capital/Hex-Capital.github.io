# Callbook AI -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most revealing obstacle is voicemail. It was consuming enough inference and telephony capacity to matter, so the founders trained a small detector on hundreds of Latin American recordings, deployed it in production, reached 91.4% recall and more than 96% held-out accuracy, and cut voicemail-related processing expense by 60%. That is a real blocker followed by a shipped workaround and a measured result. The dossier does not say how quickly they did it, so I would not award full resourcefulness credit. But this is stronger evidence about the founders than their résumés.

The product has crossed the line from demonstration to machinery. Callbook AI reports 23 million collection interactions, two million borrowers managed, and 100,000 people negotiated with each week. Its customers include Banco W, Banco Contactar, Sistecrédito, and Aseguradora Solidaria. These are identifiable institutions using the product for an unpleasant, consequential job. The reported $11 million recovered and perfect record in head-to-head pilots are company claims rather than independently audited figures, but they show more than polite enterprise interest. Borrowers are being contacted, commitments are being recorded, and money is moving.

The founders also fit the mechanism. Diego Avellaneda spent more than six years in embedded ML and has two peer-reviewed publications. Daniel Martinez previously helped build a conversational B2B company past $8 million ARR. More importantly, they have worked together for over nine years, beginning with an IoT and custom-hardware venture. Callbook therefore looks less like an AI-for-finance idea assembled for YC than the convergence of production voice engineering, enterprise conversational software, and a long cofounder relationship.

The route change is promising but less well proved. In 2024–25 the company sold a general AI-calling product connected to Zoho CRM; in 2026 it narrowed itself to late-stage collections. That could be a running back finding the deep part of the field. But the dossier does not identify the customer observation that caused the change, nor separate current collections volume from the earlier product’s aggregate interactions. I give credit for a material revision, not for an evidence-driven pivot whose causal chain remains undocumented.

The strongest objection is that Callbook may be presenting inherited call volume as collections pull in a market already occupied by Salient, InDebted, and Skit.ai. Revenue is undisclosed. Enterprise distribution still appears to mean asking for introductions. And autonomous negotiation creates a real regulatory trap: Regulation F limits calling frequency, while Callbook publishes neither an independent compliance audit nor a negotiation-error rate. If its advantage is merely cheaper voice infrastructure, larger competitors can reproduce the components and use their compliance operations to win the buyer.

I still invest. Collections is full of the work fashionable software companies avoid: regional speech behavior, channel orchestration, opt-outs, payment commitments, account-level context, human escalation, and creditor-specific negotiating rules. Callbook has already turned one piece of that schlep into a measured artifact and appears to have real institutions feeding the system production volume. My verifiable flip condition is simple: if a customer-level audit shows that fewer than 10% of the claimed 100,000 weekly negotiations come from repeat deployments of the current collections product, I would pass.
