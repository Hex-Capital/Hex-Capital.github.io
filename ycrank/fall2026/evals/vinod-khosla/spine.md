# Spine -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Spine makes the employer’s administration fee disappear. It has not shown that the underlying cost of delivering benefits disappears with it. Carrier commissions pay dedicated specialists to operate Rippling, Deel, Gusto, and ADP. That can be a useful business. My first question is what becomes radically cheaper if it succeeds. The reported answer is approximately 15% healthcare savings and less administrative work for founders. Against a $49.5 billion brokerage market, that is commercially meaningful, but the dossier supplies no mechanism for an order-of-magnitude expansion in access or reduction in resource consumption.

The strongest argument for investing is that Spine has already found demand and a way to collect money without charging customers an additional service fee. Thirty-plus startups use it, and its investor reports approximately 4.4x commission-revenue growth over six months through July 2026. Those are meaningful early signals. My actual investment pattern includes execution-heavy financial services and businesses that work through installed infrastructure; scientific novelty is not compulsory. The exception would require evidence that this operational wedge can expand into a much larger financial platform. Spine currently shows brokerage-funded service adoption. It does not yet show that broader mechanism.

The technical distinction matters because labor can consume the apparent advantage. Specialists execute the workflows; AI supplements plan analysis and compliance monitoring. There is no measured reduction in specialist hours, error rates, or delivery cost against an existing broker or managed HR service. The prospective claims-data learning loop remains an investor hypothesis. This triggers my concern about differentiation resting on implementation labor. It does not establish that Spine is merely a transformer wrapper, but it leaves the central question unanswered: does each additional customer improve a repeatable machine, or require more people?

The economics deserve limited credit. Carrier commissions are commercial revenue, and employer service fees are already zero; this is not a disclosed subsidy-dependent model. But the approximately 78% contribution margin is company-modeled. Without support hours, renewal retention, commission schedules, and workload variation, I cannot tell whether that margin survives demanding customers. The conflicting 15% and 25% savings claims also need one consistent measurement basis. My flip condition is one independently audited customer-cohort study demonstrating at least 10x lower delivery labor than comparable brokerage service, at equal coverage and service quality, with positive contribution after all delivery costs.

Onur’s engineering background and Ece’s legal training provide relevant complementary skills, and their third company together suggests an established working relationship. Neither fact demonstrates rapid belief revision or recruiting against a named weakness. The consumer-health history does not explain what evidence drove the current business. Likewise, the dossier provides no costed experiment or stop threshold for the largest uncertainty: whether commissions can support this service as it scales. I would pass with my own money. The known exceptions require an extraordinary instigator or a demonstrated route into an enormous consequence; neither is established here. Spine has enough customer evidence to deserve respect, but its present mechanism supports a capable brokerage more clearly than the asymmetric technical bet I want to buy.
