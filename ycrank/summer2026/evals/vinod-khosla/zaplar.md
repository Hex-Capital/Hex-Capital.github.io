# Zaplar -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

If Zaplar works exactly as described, hotels coordinate labor better and replace fragmented software. That is useful, but the dossier does not show an order-of-magnitude consequence. The relevant market is $4.2 billion, growing 7.2% annually, and the only quantified outcome is a company-reported 20% reduction in estimated housekeeping walking distance across tens of schedules. There is no baseline for hotel labor spending, operating cost per room, or population-scale benefit. This is currently a workflow improvement, not a 100x societal option.

The spatial property graph is the interesting technical claim. It could let agents optimize across rooms, staff, bookings, payments, and physical constraints instead of placing another interface over a PMS. But the benchmark is too narrow and too synthetic: 20% less estimated walking is not a measured 5–10x improvement in cost, throughput, or capability. Mews already serves 15,000 customers and has raised $811.6 million; Apaleo already connects more than 250 applications and markets agent support. Zaplar has not demonstrated that its architecture does something these incumbents cannot reproduce, only asserted that their installed architectures make reproduction difficult.

The largest kill risk is not whether an agent can generate a housekeeping schedule. It is whether a hotel will replace up to 15 systems, migrate sensitive operational data, retrain staff, and trust one pre-seed vendor across bookings, payments, and guest operations. Pilots in several countries and more than 100 hotel interviews establish genuine contact with the problem. They do not test the kill risk because the dossier gives no customer count, systems displaced, uptime, security certification, error rate, or conversion from pilot to contract. Expanding from event-booking software into a complete operating system compounds the reliability surface before the core migration thesis has been falsified.

The founders are better than the product evidence. Douglas Solberg brings mathematics and family-hotel exposure; Axel Andersson Lingbert has worked in hospitality; Jon Milles adds cybersecurity and autonomous-systems experience; Oscar Karlströmer has handled seven-figure B2B accounts. Their move from a family event-booking problem to a broader system after interviewing 100-plus hotels suggests tactical flexibility, and the team is deliberately complementary. But there is no documented instance of revising a material belief after contradictory deployment data, nor a difficult recruit demonstrably assigned to the migration or operational-safety risk.

The strongest bull case is that the $4.2 billion software estimate understates the labor and revenue pool being coordinated. Founder-led hotel visits, international pilots, and a property-specific data layer could create an execution-heavy platform with substantial switching costs. That pattern can justify an exception to my technical-risk preference when a small team attacks a giant existing value pool and keeps expanding from a narrow wedge. Here, however, neither the larger cost pool nor the wedge economics is quantified, and an eight-person team is already pursuing the whole stack without showing that one production workflow has become indispensable.

I pass today. My verifiable flip condition is a named hotel group replacing at least five incumbent systems across multiple properties and reporting a sustained 50% reduction in operating labor hours per occupied room without higher booking, payment, or service-error rates. That would convert the property graph from an architectural story into a discontinuity and show that capital is removing the migration risk rather than funding scope.
