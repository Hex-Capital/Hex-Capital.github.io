# Exosat -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A telecom operator in Central Asia does not petition an American platform or a Chinese state constellation to keep mines, farms, and remote communities online; it operates dedicated orbital capacity through standard NTN devices. Working backward, that requires inexpensive satellites, export-control-independent components, coordinated spectrum, launch capacity, local gateways, and years of network operations. Exosat names those transitions. The 11,000-satellite ITU filing, $400 million-plus of reported LOIs across six nations, and April 2027 launch booking are credible leaks from that future, although they do not yet prove it has arrived.

Edward Ge is the strongest part of this bet. He did not discover space after direct-to-device became fashionable: he co-founded Stratodyne and Aethero, launched two satellites, built millions of dollars of prior-company revenue with Blue Origin, Booz Allen Hamilton, and AFRL, and participated in a $109,740 Air Force satellite-edge-computing award. That is technical and commercial tuition earned before Exosat. The missing piece is recruitment. A one-person company proposing an 11,000-satellite system has demonstrated Tizz and Biz, but not yet the Rizz required to assemble the manufacturing, regulatory, RF, and network-operations team.

The broken rule is unusually concrete: nations can buy connectivity but cannot guarantee control over the foreign constellation delivering it. Exosat proposes to return service authority to national agencies and network operators through dedicated turnkey systems built without ITAR- or EAR-controlled content. That is real agency, not a generic claim that telecom is expensive. But the dossier does not quantify the rent paid to current providers or show that sovereign buyers will fund independent constellations rather than negotiate redundancy across existing networks.

Here is the painful counterargument: the apparent moat is presently paperwork plus promises. The first Exosat satellite is not due until April 2027; the claimed $300,000 cost, days-long production cycle, lower-orbit performance, and three-to-five-year life remain unvalidated on orbit. The $400 million pipeline consists of company-reported LOIs, while ITU filings trigger coordination and deployment milestones rather than conferring a finished spectrum fortress. Automotive-grade components may lower cost, but they also make the hardware easier to commoditize. Meanwhile, Starlink already produced $11.4 billion in 2025 revenue, and AST SpaceMobile had thirteen BlueBird satellites in orbit by August 2026. Capital can become the unnamed miracle in an otherwise coherent future chain.

The known-exception test still favors taking this seriously: pre-revenue is not disqualifying when a functioning technical artifact, industrial commitments, and an unusually prepared founder compress the remaining uncertainty. Exosat has the prepared founder and meaningful institutional commitments, but it does not yet have its own functioning orbital artifact. Early pilots and a booked launch earn more credit than a waitlist, yet they do not show users paying, integrating production traffic, or reorganizing operations around the network. I expect that within 36 months Exosat can operate an initial IoT service and reach roughly $3 million to $10 million in annualized contracted revenue, but with a constellation far smaller than the filing implies.

I would invest, but this is a disciplined early check rather than a heroic one. The company sits beneath the satellite-connectivity wave as sovereign infrastructure, controls a potentially valuable spectrum position, and is led by someone who has already converted satellite engineering into customer revenue. My flip condition is verifiable: if the April 2027 mission fails to demonstrate the claimed cost architecture and a named LOI customer does not convert into a paid deployment within twelve months of launch, I stop treating the filings as a present-day leak and treat them as expensive ambition.
