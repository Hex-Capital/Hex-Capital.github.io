# Central Blue -- Paul Graham Evaluation

What happened when Brian Wu and Jeewoo Choi first tried to make power beaming work? That is the question I would want answered before investing in Central Blue. The dossier supplies relevant backgrounds: spacecraft navigation at JPL, robotics, and aerospace thermal testing. It also supplies a prior robotics project they worked on together. Those are good reasons to take the founders seriously. But there is no documented sequence of obstacle, workaround, and result. I cannot turn their résumés into evidence of resourcefulness.

The most important technical distinction is between a specification and a measurement. Central Blue describes a nearly completed prototype, while HELION’s 300 kWh storage and 10 kW delivery for four to five hours remain targets. CORE’s Blackwing Space demonstration is scheduled for 2027 and would validate recovery software rather than power delivery. These are sensible things to build. They do not yet establish that the team has crossed a difficult technical threshold. My pattern of backing unfamiliar industrial projects after a concrete proof spike therefore does not apply yet. The missing item is a measured result from Central Blue’s own system.

The strongest argument for investing is that these two founders have unusually complementary experience for the ugly work involved. Autonomy, pointing, thermal constraints, and spacecraft integration belong in the same engineering conversation. Their dedicated receiver could give them control over an interface that existing-array approaches must accommodate. But that choice also asks a spacecraft operator to change its design. Who needs the extra power badly enough to accept that burden? Central Blue’s design-partner intake form does not answer this. There is no disclosed operator repeatedly using a receiver, committing to integration, or paying for power. Pricing is also undisclosed, so the dossier cannot yet show whether delivered power justifies the customer’s integration cost.

I would give the founders room to change the route. Earlier messaging emphasized standalone CORE recovery assurance; current positioning incorporates it into power infrastructure. But without the observation that prompted the change, that sequence earns no credit for learning from evidence. Likewise, their aerospace work establishes relevant experience without documenting the particular power problem they personally encountered. Receiver integration is a real schlep, but its existence alone does not establish a founder-originated insight. Named demonstration collaborators are not evidence of the partnership-dependent distribution red flag; the dossier does not establish that dependence. It also does not supply the working prototype, independently validated result, or unmistakable user pull that would justify an exception to my founder-evidence gate.

I would pass today. Central Blue could be much better than its public evidence, especially given this team’s technical fit. But my money would currently be underwriting several untested propositions at once, including the founders’ response when the hardware fails and the customer’s willingness to integrate it. One verifiable event would change my decision: a founder-led, paid receiver trial that a named spacecraft operator confirms met its agreed power requirements, with a documented technical blocker the founders overcame to complete it. That would connect the people, the machine, and the need.
