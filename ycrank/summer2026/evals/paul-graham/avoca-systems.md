# Avoca Systems -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when Avoca Systems first got stuck. The dossier tells me Max Shand previously built and sold Serenade and Campbell Mercer-Butcher led engineering teams, but credentials are not resourcefulness. It does not name a rejected integration, failed deployment, clinical constraint, or customer refusal and then show what the founders shipped in response. I cannot tell whether they behaved like running backs or simply executed the original plan with sufficient staff. That matters more to me than the impressive nouns around the product.

What they have shipped is impressive. This is not a diagram labeled “AI operating system.” Avoca handles voice calls, referrals, booking rules, patient communications, human escalation, and real-time RIS/EHR write-back. It claims integrations with Epic, Oracle Health, MEDITECH, Karisma, Aura, and Visage, alongside SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 certification. A deployed system resolving 86% of calls is meaningful technical proof. The evidence is still mostly company-reported, so I would not treat the architecture or safety claims as independently validated, but the functioning artifact deserves substantial credit.

The strongest signal is that a narrow user niche appears to use the product for an urgent job. Several radiology networks are live, including groups operating more than 150 clinics. Avoca reports a 90% reduction in abandoned calls, a 4.6/5 satisfaction score for Jobfit, and 30 bookings converted within half a day for another customer. Lumus Imaging, Western Radiology, ForHealth, and the other named logos make this more than anonymous “interest.” The uncertainty is repetition: there is no disclosed revenue, retention, booking volume over time, or evidence that deployments expand after the first site.

Radiology scheduling is usefully full of schleps. Protocols, referrals, preparation rules, site constraints, clinical escalation, and brittle RIS access are exactly the work generic voice-agent demos omit. But the dossier does not show that either founder encountered this problem through prior radiology work. It also exposes a concrete unresolved schlep: patient data currently stays in Australia while the company is expanding into the United States. The team has demonstrated an appetite for ugly implementation work, but not yet the founder-originated collision that would distinguish an organic idea from a well-executed market selection.

The bear case stings because the proof could be evidence of a good feature rather than a defining company. openDoctor and AbbaDox already combine radiology scheduling, referrals, and integrations; Assort Health and Prosper AI can approach the same workflows from broader healthcare-agent platforms. Avoca’s clinic rules and exception histories may eventually create switching costs, but there is no disclosed proprietary dataset or retention evidence today. Partner-led selling to radiology executives, private-equity groups, and technology vendors also risks becoming dependence on incumbents rather than direct founder-led distribution.

The concrete pre-round proof and real deployments match my preference for founders who build before permission, and they are strong enough to prevent a pass. The known-exception test nearly overrides the missing founder evidence: a working product with unmistakable user behavior can substitute for an incomplete biography. But it cannot show how the founders react when the next essential integration, regulator, or customer blocks them. I would not write the check yet. My one flip condition is a dated case study showing that a named deployment was blocked by a specific clinical or integration constraint, that the founders shipped the workaround within weeks, and that the network subsequently processed at least 10,000 successful bookings through it.
