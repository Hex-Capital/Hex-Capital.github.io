# Ploid -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Agents create a new buyer for people data: software that needs to identify someone, verify facts, and act without manually cleaning a CSV. Ploid becomes a large company if it delivers a correctly resolved, evidence-backed person record at a persistently lower total cost than competing providers. That is the controlling variable. The founder-reported benchmark—81% accuracy across 1,000 queries versus Exa’s 63% and Apollo’s 23%—addresses part of it. But Gemini-graded, company-published results do not establish the production advantage, including verification cost, that would make customers standardize on Ploid.

The market opening is credible; the economic step change is less established. Ploid already exposes its product through an API, CLI, and MCP server, so agent distribution is implemented rather than hypothetical. Yet Apollo also offers agent access, and Exa already maintains people-search capabilities. Moving from database filters to agent queries could expand consumption across the category without improving Ploid’s bargaining power. The $200 and $600 monthly plans establish packaging, but credits do not reveal cost per successful result or gross margin after live retrieval and third-party contact purchases. I give the team some credit for serving a new consumption channel, little for an unproven margin expansion.

The strongest bull case is that Ploid owns substantially more than a search response. Cross-site identity resolution, live retrieval, and field-level provenance form a useful verification layer. Better models could improve that system while leaving the identity infrastructure valuable. This fits my pattern of investing across the infrastructure surrounding a technology discontinuity. However, the claimed two-billion-person index does not establish a data moat: the dossier gives no reproduction cost, exclusive access, or demonstrated correction loop. Monid’s integration is simultaneously distribution and a warning. Its October 20 announcement describes combining Ploid with Apollo, People Data Labs, and other providers, which places the customer relationship and supplier selection above Ploid.

There is some demand evidence, but it does not yet resolve that position in the stack. The company’s October 2026 claim that “thousands” of companies use Ploid earns limited credit; it lacks a paid/free breakdown or repeat production usage. The September 4 Monid integration supplies a concrete route to consumption, not proof of customer dependence. Hernandez’s recommendation systems and embedding experience at Wander is relevant technical context. Neither that background nor Karic’s research credentials documents costly founder commitment or prior startup learning, so I keep the founder dimension at its missing-evidence default.

I would pass on investing my own money today. Ploid’s strongest evidence is an implemented research and verification product; its weakest is documented founder commitment, but the decisive investment gap is market economics. The dossier does not clear my market-driver and economic-change gates. I checked the exceptions: there is no documented frontier-research scarcity supporting a talent override, nor a demonstrated reconstruction of incumbent unit economics. My single flip condition is an independently audited, 90-day paid production deployment showing that a customer repeatedly chooses Ploid over available substitutes because its cost per correctly resolved, sourced person is lower, while Ploid earns a positive contribution margin. That would connect the technical advantage to durable purchasing behavior.
