# Computable -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders know electronic markets because they have built trading infrastructure at Jump Trading and Coinbase, and they have converted that knowledge into a live order book for forward-dated GPU capacity. That is action, not résumé theater. But the evidence stops one layer short of exact founder-market fit: nothing shows that either founder previously bought, sold, or operated GPU infrastructure, and the dossier does not identify their individual roles or prior artifacts. They understand the market mechanism better than the underlying commodity.

Computable turns inflexible GPU leases into transferable weekly reservations, with resting bids and offers, physical redemption, and a 2% fee charged to each side. This is direct value capture attached to a real transaction rather than advertising or an unspecified future business model. Yet public listings at $2.56–$2.69 per GPU-hour are supply, not commerce. No completed trade, fee revenue, repeat buyer, or redemption establishes that the exchange is clearing.

The fatal question is what remains after a competent competitor copies the calendar-week grid. Today, very little. Ownership of bid, order, price, and volume data is not a moat unless real trading produces exclusive information and better liquidity. SF Compute already supports resale, has raised $40 million, and manages more than $100 million of hardware. Runpod and Vast.ai also possess existing supply and demand relationships. Code can create an order book; only participants can create a market.

The bull case is strong enough to sting. GPU-as-a-service was estimated at $8.21 billion in 2025 and projected to reach $26.62 billion by 2030. Bilateral leases lasting 6–24 months and reported 2x H100 price dispersion are exactly the kind of fragmented, opaque supply that price discovery can reorganize. If traders bring offers, developers bring demand, and each transaction improves the forward curve, Computable becomes a permissionless coordination layer rather than another cloud interface.

But that is still a diagram of a network, not evidence of one. The Show HN launch produced 45 points and 26 comments, while the dossier supplies no participant count, trading volume, repeat activity, or liquidity independent of Computable’s standing quotes. The website promises every position a buyback quote while the terms disclaim any obligation or guaranteed price. That discrepancy is not proof of bad integrity, but it makes the apparent liquidity impossible to underwrite. None of the known-exception patterns applies: there is neither demonstrated obsessive adoption nor an already-forming network that can override the absent moat.

My base case is that by August 2029 Computable has pivoted from a general GPU exchange into a managed procurement and brokerage service for AI teams, producing $1–3 million in annualized revenue but sacrificing the software-like network economics implied by the marketplace. Skilled market builders can construct a useful operation; they have not yet shown that this operation compounds without their balance sheet or labor. I pass today. The single flip condition is three consecutive months above $10 million in monthly third-party trading volume, with at least 30% repeat counterparties and Computable acting as principal on less than 10% of volume.
