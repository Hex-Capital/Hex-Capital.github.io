# Phone Harness -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Shawn Pana has shipped something. What I cannot yet see is what happened when he got stuck. Phone Harness controls an iPhone without Xcode or a phone-side installation, which removes a concrete setup burden. But the dossier does not establish the obstacle Pana encountered, the workaround he personally built, or how quickly it changed the situation. I give the software credit as software. I cannot also count it as a demonstrated response to resistance.

The technical evidence is the strongest part. There is public code, a specific control mechanism using iPhone Mirroring and OCR, Android accessibility support, and an available cloud beta. Those are inspectable artifacts. The distinction from iphone-use’s XCTest installation is useful because every installation step loses developers. But removing setup friction does not establish reliable task completion. Multitouch, camera, and Face ID flows remain unsupported, and unlabeled controls require a vision-capable model. Phone Harness has built a working interface; the dossier supplies no measured completion rate for the jobs customers actually need.

The strongest argument for investing is that this is precisely how a new developer primitive can begin. Roughly 4,000 stars and 403 forks suggest that Pana has reached people who want to experiment with phone control. His previous growth work at Browser Use gives him relevant experience reaching those developers. This fits my recurring preference for programmable interfaces that users can turn into unexpected applications. The missing distinction is between wanting to try something and needing it every week. There is no identified user niche with repeat usage, payment, or unsolicited referrals. Forks deserve some credit, but they cannot tell me which workflow would break if Phone Harness disappeared.

The commercial experiment is sensible: free personal-device use, cloud phones at $0.05 per minute, and custom-priced dedicated devices. The question is whether developers pay to escape operating phones themselves. MIT licensing and alternatives such as Droidrun/Mobilerun make that operational service important. Pana’s prior work puts him near the problem, but it does not document his own collision with this particular need. Nor does the dossier show a customer observation that caused him to change the product’s route. Both remain unanswered questions rather than evidence of a manufactured idea or stubbornness. The September 16 telemetry issue is more concrete: a service handling logged-in phones must earn considerable trust, and current remediation has not been independently established.

I would pass today. The usual launch-and-partnership red flags are not established here, and being a solo founder is not a reason to reject working software. I also checked the exceptions: the prototype-based exception is relevant, but this dossier lacks the exceptional founder correction, unmistakable user pull, or validated technical result needed to override the resourcefulness gate. My flip condition is one documented customer episode: Pana personally resolves a named blocking workflow within two weeks, after which five teams in that same niche pay for and repeat the workflow weekly for four consecutive weeks. That would connect the code to both founder behavior and a narrow well of demand.
