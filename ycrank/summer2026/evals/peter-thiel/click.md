# Click -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Click’s implicit contested truth is that agents will pay for one neutral MCP layer spanning web, professional, social, financial, mapping, and travel data rather than assemble connectors themselves or rely on their model provider. What would prove this wrong? OpenAI or another host platform matching the useful connector breadth at lower friction. That counterfactual is already partly visible: OpenAI provides native web search and remote MCP support, while Exa, Tavily, Linkup, and Parallel sell overlapping retrieval infrastructure. Click has identified a convenience, but the dossier neither names a belief those peers reject nor reports an experiment that could establish a secret.

The aggregation is broad, not proprietary. Click routes to Exa, Crustdata, Google Maps, Reddit, X, YouTube, Apify, and others, but those suppliers control the underlying access, pricing, and availability. There is no exclusive dataset, measured routing advantage, network retention, or structural barrier. Cross-provider usage data might eventually improve selection, yet this is inferred rather than demonstrated. A company that creates a useful interface while its suppliers and host platforms retain the scarce assets creates value without capturing it: commodity, not monopoly.

Aditya Asgaonkar is the strongest evidence in the dossier. His Ethereum Foundation research, consensus work, and writing about agent context engineering are substantially more relevant than the usual résumé decorated with “AI.” A solo founder also avoids the newly assembled co-founder failure mode, and “Building Click” alongside YC suggests commitment. But the record does not establish ownership, salary, full-time status, or whether the core routing technology is built in-house. The repeated movement from assistant frontend to operations automation to research connectors could be intelligent discovery; it could also mean the company has not yet found the important truth around which to organize itself. Missing evidence is not refutation, so the founding gate remains unresolved rather than failed.

The economics expose the same problem. A public $49 monthly plan for 500 credits gives Click one intelligible revenue engine, but no paying-user count, gross margin, conversion rate, or Business price connects it to a power-law outcome. At that price, a large consumer-scale base is required, while upstream connector costs can rise with usage. The inferred self-service route fits the ticket size, but 157 LinkedIn followers and NVIDIA Inception membership do not demonstrate acquisition or retention. The $16.28 billion AI-search estimate is a category statistic, not an answer to who buys Click repeatedly.

The bull case is sharper than the current evidence: a protocol researcher could turn provider fragmentation into a routing layer whose task-completion data compounds, and one installation across multiple vertical sources may be materially better than single-provider search. MCP’s growth to more than 10,000 public servers supplies timing. But this describes a future monopoly mechanism, not one already present. The known exceptions do not rescue the bet: there is no independently verified technical discontinuity, persistent transaction behavior, or customer-qualified deployment overriding the failed secret and monopoly gates. Nor does the emerging AI infrastructure stack help if Click remains a reseller sitting above better-capitalized suppliers.

My 36-month base case is that Click pivots back toward operations automation and remains below $1 million ARR because generic connector aggregation is absorbed by model platforms or compressed by upstream economics. I would reopen the investment decision upon one verifiable milestone: a bounded cohort of 100 paying operations teams showing greater than 80% six-month retention while repeatedly using at least three distinct provider categories through Click. Until then, this is an able founder in a fashionable layer where competition has arrived before monopoly. I pass.
