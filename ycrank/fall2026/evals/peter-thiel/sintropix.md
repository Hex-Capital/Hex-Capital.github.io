# Sintropix -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Sintropix’s potentially important truth is that fragmented LATAM accounting can be standardized enough to close multinational books almost autonomously. That is my reconstruction, however: the dossier identifies neither a prevailing belief the founders reject nor a test against it. The reported move from day 20 to day 1 is worth examining, but faster closing alone cannot distinguish proprietary automation from unusually attentive accounting services. The decisive experiment would measure the human work and corrections required to produce those books.

The competitive problem is more concrete. Campfire already advertises transaction matching, account coding, and intercompany reconciliation; Rillet offers an AI ledger. Sintropix’s multi-country focus could define a legitimate beachhead, but geography does not itself produce a monopoly. Its claimed 100-plus integrations establish breadth, without demonstrating exclusive access, superior technology, or compounding network effects. Embedded account mappings might eventually make replacement expensive; the dossier labels that possibility an inference. I cannot purchase a future switching cost as though it already exists. This feature overlap activates my crowded-market red flag, although it does not establish that the founders copied anyone.

The strongest argument against passing is how the brothers reached the product. Alfonso and Borja moved from manual bookkeeping in April 2026 to a proprietary ERP described in September, after experiencing the workflow themselves. Alfonso’s Toku product background supplies relevant context; their longstanding relationship and shared operating effort provide more founding substance than a recently assembled team. Ten active clients across Chile, Mexico, Brazil, and the United States also make this more than an architectural proposal. The missing ownership, compensation, and explicit full-time commitments prevent a high founding-structure score, but they are unanswered questions rather than adverse evidence.

Distribution is Sintropix’s clearest commercial accomplishment. Referrals brought initial accounting customers, and Alfonso personally contacted over 100 accountants through LinkedIn. There is an identifiable seller doing identifiable work. The company-reported reduction in onboarding from 30 days to 24 hours could materially improve delivery economics, although a search snippet does not establish repeatability. Services starting at 20 UF monthly provide a real pricing reference; the ten clients are not disclosed as ten contracts at that price. With ERP pricing negotiated separately and no labor-adjusted margins or account-expansion evidence, I can see a coherent recurring business before I can see a power-law outcome.

Finance itself fits my revealed investment preferences; an unglamorous category is no objection. The harder question is whether Sintropix keeps the value it creates. My known exceptions permit independent technical proof or customer behavior to overcome an otherwise failed gate. These clients demonstrate demand, but neither their presence nor the reported closing improvement establishes an advantage competitors cannot reproduce. **I pass.** One verifiable flip condition would be an independently audited, six-month comparison showing that Sintropix closes comparable LATAM multi-entity books with at least tenfold fewer human accounting hours than competing systems, at equal accuracy. That would turn the proposed technical advantage into evidence capable of changing the monopoly judgment.
