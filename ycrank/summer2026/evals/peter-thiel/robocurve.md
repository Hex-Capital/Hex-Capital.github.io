# Robocurve -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Robocurve’s contested truth is that robotics progress will be constrained less by model intelligence than by the absence of independent, reproducible measurements on physical machines. The proposition is falsifiable: if evaluations fail to predict deployment performance—or laboratories continue trusting internal tests despite credible alternatives—the company has discovered no secret. The first real-robot frontier-model pilot and roughly 400 commits to Inspect Robots move this beyond a slogan, but the pilot results remain unpublished and the API is explicitly early. This is a plausible secret, not yet a demonstrated one.

The harder problem is value capture. Robocurve has selected a real function, but not a narrow market it can dominate: its public catalog spans multiple embodiments and job domains while Positronic, Instance, and ReSim already approach overlapping evaluation problems. The MIT license lets those competitors and robotics laboratories reuse the framework. Thirteen repositories, 134 stars, and 19 forks demonstrate technical output and early attention; they do not establish proprietary technology, network effects, or control of the emerging standard. Open source may distribute the measurement system while distributing away the monopoly.

The founders fit the problem unusually well. Jay Chooi worked on AI evaluations through MATS and the UK AI Security Institute; Aris Zhu brings Amazon Robotics, Amazon AGI Labs, and embodied-systems research. Their complementary knowledge explains how Robocurve could connect policy grading to physical execution. But attending Harvard together is not evidence that they previously shipped together, and the dossier does not establish full-time status, ownership, compensation, or whether all essential hardware operations remain internal. The foundation is promising but unverified.

The strongest bull case is that the apparent weakness becomes the mechanism: open-source adoption makes Inspect Robots the common interface, independently operated evaluations become authoritative, and every run adds a cross-model, cross-robot history that laboratories cannot cheaply reconstruct. The first pilot, rapid progression to version 0.21.1, and community activity support the beginning of that path. But this is still a forecast about future data, not a moat today. The simultaneous 2026 launches of PhAIL and Instance validate demand while also showing that several capable teams identified the same opportunity. A trend can be real while its obvious investments remain competitive.

Economically, Robocurve has no disclosed price, paying customer, recurring contract, margin profile, sales cycle, or dominant revenue engine. The inferred sequence—free package, community adoption, direct laboratory outreach, then paid evaluations—could produce a respected testing service. It does not yet explain how one company captures a power-law outcome rather than becoming a labor- and hardware-intensive benchmark operator. Physical resets, controlled facilities, maintained embodiments, and domain experts make each new benchmark potentially more expensive rather than automatically cheaper.

I pass today. My 36-month base case is that Robocurve becomes a credible managed robotics-evaluation provider but remains below $1 million ARR or pivots toward testing infrastructure for individual laboratories, because technical adoption outruns proprietary value capture. One result would flip the decision: three independent frontier robotics labs purchasing recurring annual contracts while using Robocurve’s benchmark history in model-release or procurement decisions. That would prove distribution, authority, and a data mechanism the MIT-licensed code alone cannot reproduce.
