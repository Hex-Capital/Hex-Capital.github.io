# hardware intelligence -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: agentic execution now lets chip-verification teams automate portions of waveform debugging, but hardware intelligence becomes large only if Wave reduces time-to-root-cause enough to overcome entrenched Synopsys, Cadence, and Siemens workflows. Debug time is the controlling variable. The dossier establishes that verification engineers spend substantial time debugging, but supplies neither a reduction target nor a customer benchmark. Multi-gigabyte demonstrations show feasibility, not that the economic threshold has been crossed.

Wave is more substantial than a waveform chatbot. It traces signals, proposes fixes, reruns the customer’s simulator, and produces an auditable diff while operating on-prem with the customer’s model key. Better models could improve that loop rather than erase it because the product performs actions and validates outcomes. This is hardware intelligence’s strongest feature. Yet the workflow remains incomplete where adoption matters most: FSDB requires customer-specific integration, while Synopsys already owns that data ecosystem and offers natural-language analysis, automated tracing, and waveform comparison.

The technology discontinuity is real but no longer unoccupied. ChipAgents released waveform root-cause analysis in November 2025 and reports deployments at more than 120 semiconductor companies plus 6× ARR growth in the first half of 2026. Synopsys introduced overlapping capabilities by April 2026. These facts validate customer interest in agentic verification, but they also indicate a crystallizing market in which hardware intelligence has neither first-mover distribution nor a demonstrated economic discontinuity. Terminal-native delivery and on-prem operation may improve adoption, but the dossier does not quantify faster debugging, lower license cost, or superior margins.

The strongest bull case is the team. Athreya Anand combines agentic-workflow experience with exposure to AWS Inferentia and Tesla chips; Rishov Sarkar built AMD simulators, worked on LLVM flows at Siemens EDA, and maintains the 77-star LightningSim project. They have also known each other for nearly a decade. This is unusually relevant technical coverage for a two-person pre-seed company. But domain depth is not the same as the rare-team exception: hardware intelligence is entering a category where funded competitors and incumbents have already shipped comparable functionality, and there is no documented frontier breakthrough that makes customer pull temporarily unnecessary.

Wave opened pilot onboarding in July 2026, but there is no disclosed usage, paid deployment, repeat debugging activity, or revenue. My base case is therefore that the team builds a credible product but is forced toward an integration layer inside existing FSDB-centered verification stacks rather than replacing the incumbent debugging environment. The moat cannot currently be proprietary customer data because on-prem deployment prevents aggregation, and no durable workflow state or distribution channel is yet demonstrated.

I would pass today. One result would flip the decision: three paying semiconductor-design teams completing 90-day FSDB-based deployments with at least a 50% measured reduction in median root-cause time. That would simultaneously validate the controlling market variable, establish product pull, and show that Wave can enter the workflow despite incumbent overlap.
