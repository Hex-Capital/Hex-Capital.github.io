# 83 Sciences -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The useful fact is not that 83 Sciences has an AI agent. It is that an anonymous battery lab had reached a capacity plateau, the team produced revised synthesis conditions, and the resulting material exceeded the lab’s previous result in under two months. That is a real obstacle followed by a shipped workaround and an observable change. But the account is company-reported and does not reveal what failed during the engagement, which founder intervened, or how the team changed Dalton when reality disagreed. It is evidence of resourcefulness, but not yet the full running-back test.

The technical proof is stronger. The company reports one novel material discovered, a completed case study, and a manuscript drafted rather than merely promising that research will begin after funding. Eric Riesel’s MIT chemistry PhD, Ian Naccarella’s work at Sila, and Yankang Yang’s enterprise-AI experience make the result more credible, though credentials are not experiments. The missing pieces are a named lab, a quantified capacity improvement, and independent reproduction. Bindwell is the tempting exception: both convert computational materials claims into physical results, but Bindwell supplied laboratory validation and a numerical benchmark. 83 Sciences currently supplies an anonymous description of having crossed a plateau.

The origin of the idea is unusually plausible. These founders worked inside experimental chemistry, battery materials, commercialization, and large-scale AI deployment. They would have encountered the mess they are organizing: notebooks, instrument exports, failed runs, provenance errors, publication restrictions, and foreground-IP negotiations. This is genuine schlep. It may also be the moat, because open models such as MatterGen can reproduce parts of the model layer but cannot automatically obtain a lab’s unpublished failures. The important contractual question is whether each engagement strengthens a shared system or merely creates another isolated customer database.

Demand remains less convincing than the science. “Dozens of partnerships in progress” is not usage, and grants of up to $50,000 can reverse the direction of payment. The battery lab is unnamed; there is no revenue, renewal, retention, or evidence that researchers repeatedly query Dalton without the founders operating a bespoke discovery service around it. The active Head of Sales search is therefore premature enough to worry me. If the founders hire someone to discover why laboratories buy before learning this personally, they will turn a difficult product-learning problem into a sales-pipeline story.

The bull case is that in-person onboarding plus discovery contracts is exactly the manual work required to structure proprietary experimental histories, and that each engagement makes Dalton more useful. The bear case is sharper: incompatible IP terms prevent cross-partner learning, grants manufacture apparent adoption, and 83 Sciences becomes a respectable contract-research business in a materials-informatics market estimated at only $170.4 million in 2025. There is also no documented route change yet, so I cannot tell whether the team will abandon the attractive “AI-native discovery platform” description when customers demand a narrower tool.

I would not write the check today. The founders have crossed the technical gate, but they have not shown that a narrow group of laboratories pulls the product back or that one customer’s schlep compounds into the next customer’s advantage. My flip condition is concrete: two named laboratories must pay, physically reproduce Dalton-recommended improvements, and renew while permitting their normalized results to improve the shared platform. That would turn the present scientific proof into evidence for both demand and a compounding company.
