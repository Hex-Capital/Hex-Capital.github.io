# Halmos Labs -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Halmos Labs has demonstrated something more useful than another agent producing a plausible research answer: an unnamed customer confirmed a missing fermentation nutrient variable and revised its experimental plan. That is a change in someone’s work. It could become a business. But proposed post-money, competing term sheets, and what this round specifically finances are all undisclosed. I cannot underwrite cheap uncertainty without the price, or a venture moment without knowing what the money proves. Those are missing inputs, not evidence that the founders are running an auction.

The strongest case for Halmos is that experimental design becomes valuable before full laboratory automation works. Its company-reported collaboration matched a bioprocessing campaign’s best performance with an average **30% fewer evaluations across repeated simulations**. Pair that with the confirmed nutrient finding and there is a credible wedge: help a process-development team decide which expensive experiment to run next. Industry-partner verification funding from LU Innovation in summer 2026 adds evidence that the team can get its work tested. I give the customer action limited credit for workflow access; it does not establish proprietary distribution, retained usage, or ownership of the resulting data.

The distinction between simulation and the laboratory matters economically. Halmos reports repeatedly recommending experiments outside a collaboration’s permitted range, with experimentalists retaining control. Human review may be entirely sensible, but its cost belongs in the product economics. Deductive consistency checks cannot by themselves establish biological usefulness. Likewise, finding a validated epitope among the top twenty predictions for each of five TESLA patients is computational evidence, not proof that a buyer saves cash prospectively. Atinary already has instrument integration and an August 2026 Bruker collaboration. Halmos needs to own a consequential decision inside the customer’s workflow; assembling publicly available models leaves that position contestable.

The founders make this worth examining. Akke’s biochemical machine-learning and drug-discovery research at MIT, AstraZeneca, and Cambridge connects to the actual problem. Löfgren’s quantitative work and LLM-security research connect to checking unreliable computational systems. That pairing fits my preference for people translating between ecosystems, and the associated LLM4BO research repository gives the technical work some substance beyond credentials. It still falls short of documented commercial deployments on both sides of the bridge. I would credit relevant research experience without inventing a history of shipping into industrial laboratories.

Delete the next financing from Halmos’s plan and the dossier cannot tell me what survives. Its terms permit scoped paid projects, which could support a focused service business, but published prices, revenue, gross margin, burn, and runway are absent. Those omissions receive the specified economics default; a three-person pre-seed team does not need mature financial history. More consequentially, no disclosed use of proceeds names a hypothesis, deadline, and pass/fail endpoint. The existing benchmarks are not evidence that this particular round funds one finite experiment. Nor do the known exceptions rescue the case: there is no documented paid conversion and retention, exceptional external acquisition evidence, or explicit alternative capital structure. The AI red flag remains an underwriting question rather than a demonstrated failure—the dossier does not show that margins assume errors disappear.

I pass on the current evidence. Halmos has the beginnings of useful scientific decision support, but neither the scarce asset nor the capital bargain is established. The strongest objection is that one correctly identified experimental variable can save enough laboratory expense to fund a modest business; the customer’s revised plan makes that possibility concrete. My verifiable condition for reopening diligence is **a paid prospective fermentation deployment that matches the customer’s target yield with 30% fewer wet-lab runs and positive contribution margin after human review**. That would turn the current simulation result into evidence of a functioning base business. Price would still determine whether I buy it.
