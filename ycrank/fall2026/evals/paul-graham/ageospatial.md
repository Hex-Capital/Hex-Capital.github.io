# Ageospatial -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Maaz Sheikh has done something useful: he has personally contacted 1,487 insurance prospects and reported 18 upcoming demos. That is better evidence of founder involvement than a distribution slide. But the question I most want answered remains open. What stopped a customer from using Ageospatial, and what did Sheikh build to get past it? The dossier contains sales effort, but no named obstacle followed by a shipped workaround and an observed result. I give that missing evidence the default score. I cannot infer resourcefulness from persistence at outbound alone.

There is evidence that Sheikh can build. The University of Twente independently described his earlier Geo+ product reaching 1,000 users within a month of its January 2024 introduction. Ageospatial also demonstrated a combination of Gemini 3, Copernicus flood data and Overture building footprints in November 2025. These support technical execution beyond credentials or diagrams. They establish less about the current insurance product: neither demonstrates how accurately it detects underwriting-relevant property conditions. I assign the historical product evidence to technical execution, rather than counting those 1,000 users again as demand for insurance software.

The strongest case for investing is that Ageospatial may already be crossing that gap commercially. It reports signing its largest insurance customer in October 2026, lists AXA and Die Mobiliar under “Trusted by,” and reports increasing revenue. For a company this early, those are meaningful signals. A founder who understands geospatial software and personally sells it to insurers could discover a valuable workflow before the public evidence catches up. But the customer announcement is anonymous, the logos disclose no engagement scope, and the revenue report comes through a search snippet. Together they earn provisional demand credit. They do not yet show a particular underwriting team repeatedly pulling the product into its work.

The problem also has an encouragingly ugly edge. Sheikh’s geomatics training and reported seven years building geospatial software for insurance, energy and government give him relevant context. Reconciling imagery, public records and insurer information could hide an opportunity inside integration work that others avoid. Engineer-led deployment could teach Ageospatial exactly what to automate. It could also produce a business that requires another engineer for every customer. Negotiated contracts provide a plausible way to charge for that work; the dossier supplies no delivery economics to distinguish those outcomes. Likewise, the progression from Geo+ through GeoForge to insurance shows continued building, but no documented customer observation that caused a specific change of route.

I would pass today. Ageospatial fits my preference for technical founders doing their own early selling, and there is no evidence of waiting for a partner or delegating customer discovery. It has not yet demonstrated the independently measured proof or unexpected user appropriation that makes my stronger technical bets compelling. I checked the exception case: a working earlier product deserves credit, but it does not supply the rapid founder correction, unmistakable current-product pull or validated technical result needed to override the missing resourcefulness evidence. Moody’s/CAPE and Betterview make the distinction consequential: producing property flags is already possible; winning requires discovering what an insurer urgently needs that its existing tools fail to provide. My flip condition is one documented deployment where a named insurer’s rejected output led Sheikh to ship a correction within weeks, after which that insurer paid to expand usage.
