# Mireye -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequential version of Mireye is not a better map API. It is a machine-readable factual layer that lets software evaluate every parcel, facility, and infrastructure constraint in the United States without armies of analysts searching county portals. The dossier gives one promising result—customers sourcing off-market data-center land 100 times faster—but does not quantify the labor pool displaced, development capital unlocked, or population affected. A $117 billion geospatial-analytics market is a category estimate, not a consequence model.

The product has useful architecture: one API and MCP server, 85 integrated sources, more than 300 typed fields, and field-level URLs, timestamps, and confidence values. Accepted requests also enlarge the catalog. That could create a proprietary learning loop if usage concentrates around scarce fields. Today, however, Mapbox already exposes location capabilities through MCP, while CARTO and Fused cover adjacent agentic workflows. Mireye’s 100-times-faster claim has no disclosed test method, baseline, or evidence that the improvement comes from technology competitors cannot reproduce. This is an integration advantage, not yet a demonstrated technical discontinuity.

The bull case stings. Chokshi previously built Poker Pit to $250,000 ARR in four months and an acquisition. He then taught himself outbound sales, tested messaging until it produced a 10% response rate, and found a sharp initial wedge in data-center development. A two-person team expanding its catalog 20% weekly could turn repeated field requests into the default provenance layer for physical-world agents. This resembles the known-exception pattern where a modest initial product grows into infrastructure for a giant existing value pool.

But the exception does not yet override the evidence. Mireye shows no repeat usage, revenue, retention, or structural barrier. The advertised $19 and $99 plans make experimentation easy but say nothing about the economics of maintaining thousands of changing county and government sources. Source freshness depends on FEMA, USDA, and other providers, while the catalog remains largely US-only. If customers need analysts to validate stale or inconsistent fields, cheap API pricing hides the labor rather than eliminating it.

Capital is also not aimed at a named kill risk. The dossier describes catalog expansion and prospecting, but no cheapest decisive test of whether provenance accuracy, source maintenance, or incumbent substitution kills the company. No error-rate threshold, comparison set, experiment cost, or stop condition is disclosed. The founder’s rapid commercialization record earns credit for execution, but there is no documented belief revision or complementary recruit assigned to the GIS-data risk.

My forecast is that within 36 months Mireye either pivots into a vertical data-center site-selection product or remains below $3 million ARR; the horizontal physical-world data layer is too reproducible without a proprietary usage loop. I pass today. The single condition that flips me is a published cohort of three named enterprise customers repeatedly using Mireye for at least 100 parcel decisions each, showing at least a 10x reduction in analyst hours versus Mapbox or manual GIS while maintaining above 80% gross margin. That would simultaneously establish discontinuity, repeat demand, and economic scalability.
