# Spine -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Spine’s interesting proposition is that insurance commissions can finance the work that HR software leaves to founders. Operating a customer’s existing Rippling, Deel, Gusto, or ADP account avoids a replacement decision and addresses an actual operational burden. But what important truth does Spine know that established brokers reject? The dossier names neither a disputed belief nor a test demonstrating that this service can be delivered on economics competitors consider impossible. There is a coherent business proposition here; a falsifiable secret remains unestablished.

The founding relationship is the strongest evidence. Acadian describes Spine as Onur Kardesler and Ece Cokar’s third company together. That matters more than their degrees: repeated collaboration supplies evidence against the convenience partnership assembled for fundraising. Their backgrounds also make the combination of software operations and compliance intelligible. However, shared history does not establish current ownership, full-time status, or whether essential engineering stays in-house. Those are unanswered questions, not evidence of the part-time founders or investor-dominated capitalization that would trigger a presumptive rejection.

Who keeps the value Spine creates? Startups using existing HR systems constitute a recognizable beachhead, but compatibility makes entry easier for Spine without necessarily making entry harder for another broker. Its 50-plus practitioner network demonstrates relationships, not exclusivity or a network effect. The proposed claims-data advantage remains an investment hypothesis. The dossier establishes overlapping alternatives and leaves monopoly mechanisms unproved; it does not establish that Spine is indistinguishable from those alternatives. I therefore retain the absent-evidence score for market ownership rather than impose a commodity-business penalty.

The strongest objection to passing is that the business may already be discovering unusually attractive service economics. Commission revenue reportedly grew approximately 4.4 times over six months through July 2026. One commission engine finances a broader operational relationship, potentially giving customers several reasons to stay. This fits my less-public preference for financial infrastructure and operating businesses; an unfashionable brokerage can be more interesting than fashionable software. But the approximately 78% contribution margin is company-modeled. Neither that model nor competitors’ billion-dollar revenue establishes Spine’s own scalable profit engine. The conflicting 15% and 25% savings claims also leave its customer proposition insufficiently measured.

Distribution is more concrete than a generic enterprise-sales plan. Spine reports more than 30 startup customers and has identified practitioner referrals, VC introductions, YC channels, and founder events. These are plausible routes to the buyer, especially when the employer pays no separate administration fee. Yet the dossier does not attribute customers to particular channels or report acquisition costs, sales cycles, or delivery workload. Free to the employer still requires someone to finance every additional hour of service. The referral network earns credit as an existing route, without being counted again as a moat.

I would pass on a personal check today. The known exceptions do not overturn that decision: there is no independently demonstrated technical discontinuity, and live customer adoption has not yet established protected value capture or realized exceptional economics. Spine also lacks the documented technical collapse or synchronized hardware-and-regulatory dependencies that would justify a stronger rejection. My one flip condition is verified, multiyear exclusive practitioner agreements accounting for a majority of new commission revenue. That would turn an accessible sales channel into evidence of a protected position and reopen the investment question.
