# Sinter -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Sinter’s consequential possibility is to make tooling cheap and fast enough that hardware teams can run many more physical experiments. That could change who can manufacture, and what they can afford to attempt. But the dossier establishes a $362.5 billion injection-molded plastics market, not the fraction constrained by tooling or the magnitude of improvement Sinter can deliver. I cannot turn total industry spending into a consequence-of-success calculation. The missing evidence is the multiplier: how much manufacturing becomes economically possible if this process works?

The critical distinction is between operating a manufacturing service and inventing a substantially better manufacturing process. Sinter offers FDM production today; polymer tooling and fill, pack, and warp simulation are labeled “coming soon.” There is no measured tool life, cycle time, repeatability, or comparison with conventional metal tooling. Mantle supplies metal tooling systems, while Xometry already offers automated manufacturing procurement. Neither comparison establishes Sinter’s technical advantage. REST/MCP ordering can reduce transaction friction, but the investment case depends on what happens inside the mold.

The strongest bull case is the founders’ practical proximity to that problem. Oliver Johnson operated a print farm and makerspace. Justin Kwon combines Shinkei mechatronics work with distributed-training software experience at Manifold. Their earlier marketplace reported onboarding more than 150 printers nationally, including over 20 around NYC. Those are supply relationships, not customers, but they demonstrate activity beyond a presentation. The move toward in-house manufacturing could reflect a useful discovery about where control matters. Without evidence explaining the change, however, I cannot credit it as demonstrated belief revision or risk-directed recruiting.

Sinter touches an unstated pattern I like: a scarce physical workflow that might become a repeatable production platform. The missing link is evidence that each tooling run improves a proprietary process. My exception for an early, unproven product requires an unusually compelling instigator and consequence case; these founders’ relevant experience and the broad plastics market do not yet establish that override. Nor does the dossier show an active red flag such as financing organizational scale before testing feasibility. This is insufficient evidence for my bet, rather than evidence of bad founders. Two people and undisclosed revenue are entirely ordinary at this stage.

My money should first buy the cheapest decisive test of whether polymer tooling survives useful production economically. Sinter discloses neither that experiment’s threshold and budget nor a bottom-up cost model including tool replacement, rejected parts, labor, and machine utilization. Cheap tooling can still produce expensive acceptable parts. **I pass.** One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independently witnessed comparative production trial showing 10x lower fully burdened cost per accepted part against conventional metal tooling for a defined application, including measured tool life and yield. That would connect Sinter’s proposed mechanism to a consequence worth underwriting.
