# Amulet -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Amulet mounts Linux workspaces in under 100 milliseconds and forks them in roughly 3 milliseconds, making Amulet a technically credible infrastructure bet whose strongest dimension is the secret and weakest is structural protection. The secret is that disposable agents need persistent data branches rather than persistent machines. The problem is that Amulet remains atoms-light, unregulated software: an elegant primitive without a reason the primitive must remain independent. One year of performance advantage is a feature; ten years of pricing power is a company.

Stripe is the high rung for a contrarian secret because developer-first payments exploited an architectural and cultural failure inside PayPal and legacy processors. Amulet offers an analogous inversion: Archil accelerates buckets, while Amulet treats workspaces as forkable, content-addressed histories with conflict-safe publishing. The analogy stops where it matters. Amulet names Archil’s missing capabilities but not an institutional reason Archil, Modal, E2B, or AWS cannot reproduce them. Amulet has found a non-obvious workload shift; Amulet has not yet found an incumbent incapable of following it.

Asana is the low rung on atoms-heavy or regulated-sector protection: pure workflow software, no capex barrier, rescued by Dustin Moskovitz’s Facebook lineage rather than by structural scarcity. That failure mechanism appears almost exactly in Amulet. Amulet has no regulated customer, hardware constraint, certification moat, or scarce physical asset; partial POSIX coverage and the exclusion of live same-file multi-writer access actually narrow its technical territory. “We wanted flying cars, instead we got 140 characters” applies here with uncomfortable precision.

Amulet charges $0.20 per GiB-month while making API calls, transfers, snapshots, and forks free, so Amulet’s monopoly trajectory depends on histories and provenance compounding into switching costs faster than storage pricing commoditizes. Over 18 months, successful deployments could embed workspace identifiers, commit graphs, credentials, and publishing semantics throughout agent pipelines. The opposite loop is equally plausible: Modal and E2B bundle comparable primitives into compute, AWS prices storage as a complement, and Amulet becomes a replaceable filesystem layer. Competition is for losers, and the present market structure contains several well-financed competitors but no protected small market Amulet can own.

Amulet’s recent pivot from customer-success agents to an AI filesystem is the clearest evidence against a definite plan. Amulet would move into an Invest tier on this dimension if Nithik Bala and Varun Puru committed to a two-to-five-year architecture—specific POSIX milestones, multi-cloud durability targets, and a permanent boundary between Amulet and agent compute—instead of presenting private-beta capabilities without a roadmap. SpaceX could name the sequence from launch to reusability. Amulet can name benchmark numbers, but benchmarks are not a definite future.

Nithik Bala’s Meta web-infrastructure and Rockset experience, paired with Varun Puru’s Meta, Amazon, and backend-systems work, places Amulet above an undifferentiated YC infrastructure founding pair. The single-anchor parallel is Anduril: Palmer Luckey had already shipped Oculus to a billion-dollar exit, while Amulet’s founders have relevant senior-company exposure but no verified product shipped to scale, public technical corpus, or prior exit. Amulet therefore receives real founder-market-fit credit, but the Known Exception does not trigger: Meta employment is not the Asana exception because neither founder is Dustin Moskovitz.

Archil’s $18 million financing and E2B’s $32 million financing make my 36-month forecast straightforward: by July 2029, Amulet will have pivoted from a standalone filesystem into an agent-sandbox storage and provenance layer, remain below $5 million ARR, and be acquired by E2B for less than $75 million. Amulet’s technical secret is useful enough to survive but its low structural-moat position makes independent monopoly unlikely. The Ring is more valuable to the platform that controls the compute than to the filesystem that carries it.

Modal’s reported annualized revenue above $300 million supplies the strongest opposing case: explosive agent compute could enlarge the adjacent storage category quickly enough for Amulet to become the neutral data plane across Modal, E2B, and hyperscalers. Amulet’s 1.9-millisecond p99 reads, 8.4 GB/s throughput, immutable commits, and zero-copy forks could then create the integration depth that the private beta has not yet demonstrated. The Asana exception also stings—pure software can win when founders and workflow lock-in overpower the missing atoms moat. But Amulet has a recent pivot, company-published benchmarks, no disclosed revenue, and no Moskovitz-level operator override. I would flip to Invest if Amulet reaches $2 million ARR from at least ten customers by July 2027; until then, the secret is sharper than the monopoly.
