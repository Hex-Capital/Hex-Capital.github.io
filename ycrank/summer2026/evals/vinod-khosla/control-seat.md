# Control Seat -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Battex Chile’s deployment makes **Control Seat** technically credible but economically modest: an industrial-software builder whose strongest dimension is stack depth and whose weakest is a capital profile too small for my advantage. **Control Seat** may replace weeks of PLC, SCADA, historian, and database integration with one tag layer, but the current success-state improves factory operations rather than breaking an economic mechanism. That is an optimization play until proven otherwise. Consequence comes before probability, and a $20.05 billion projected SCADA market is not yet a civilizational variable.

**Commonwealth Fusion** required physics milestones and a $115 million Series A when fusion was investor-toxic; the load-bearing analogy is willingness to attack infrastructure that conservative buyers consider too risky to change. **Control Seat** has a real technical wedge: proprietary SCADA, historian, analytics, and Virtual PLC software, plus a self-reported victory over CODESYS on nine of ten IEC execution workloads. **Control Seat** diverges where it matters: the benchmark excludes I/O latency, scheduler jitter, motion, and safety certification, while the documentation keeps safety functions independent. Those omissions turn a potential control-system revolution into capable software engineering. There is perhaps one falsifiable miracle—production-grade virtual control across heterogeneous brownfield plants—not the two or three miracles that repel consensus capital.

**DoorDash** had ordinary technical risk and a capital-light starting profile; that weakness was excusable only because consumer-commerce flow could compound into a massive network. **Control Seat** shows the same low-capital mismatch without the demonstrated network mechanism: a seven-day hosted trial, custom-priced integration deployments, and software running above existing installations. **Control Seat** has disclosed no plant fleet, certification program, hardware rollout, or greater-than-$50 million commercial milestone where my capital changes the probability distribution. Any competent industrial-software investor can fund the current plan. I am over-resourced for it.

The **$12.89 billion** 2025 SCADA market compounds into consequence only if Control Seat’s integration wedge becomes the default control plane across plants, not another layer beside Ignition, AVEVA, FactoryTalk, WinCC, and CODESYS. Over 18 months, every Battex-like deployment could add validated control logic, configuration data, and plant history, increasing switching costs and making **Control Seat** more valuable. The opposite compounding mechanism is equally strong: systems-integration work generates bespoke configurations, service burden, and compatibility obligations faster than reusable software. **Control Seat** currently names one deployment and no incumbent regime that disappears. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself” does not mean mistake a $20 billion software category for the transformation of industrial production.

Warren Shepard’s SpaceX Starlink and manufacturing work, combined with Jack Grodnick’s Tesla and SpaceX SCADA experience, gives **Control Seat** unusually relevant founder-market fit. It does not establish defiance of expert consensus. **Control Seat** would flip this dimension into invest territory if Shepard or Grodnick demonstrated that certified virtual PLCs can replace incumbent controllers under real latency and uptime constraints—and published evidence that Rockwell, Siemens, or CODESYS engineers said the architecture could not work. Today the founders look more like excellent domain experts applying AI-assisted software generation to a familiar stack. Experts extrapolate the past; credentials do not substitute for an instigating thesis.

**Slash** earned an exception through a documented pivot that exposed exceptional learning rate; the publicly told Kanye West pivot was evidence, not founder mythology. **Control Seat** reports that Shepard and Grodnick compared industrial-internship experiences and concluded AI had lowered the cost of building industrial software, but **Control Seat** discloses no abandoned thesis, failed deployment that changed the architecture, prior exit, or explicit “we were wrong” moment. At pre-seed I will not punish short history, so I apply the absent-evidence default. Learning rate above everything else still requires evidence of learning.

A **seven-day hosted trial** and custom integration pricing point to a falsifiable 36-month outcome: by July 2029, **Control Seat** will reach roughly $3 million to $7 million in ARR as an integration-led SCADA overlay, remain independent, and derive substantial revenue from deployment services rather than displacing Rockwell, Siemens, or CODESYS controllers. **Control Seat** gets there because brownfield compatibility lowers adoption friction, while the independent-safety requirement and excluded real-time benchmark dimensions prevent the Virtual PLC from becoming the plant’s authoritative control layer. Useful is not the same as consequential.

Battex Chile is the strongest objection to my pass: **Control Seat** has already consolidated MQTT brokers, virtual machines, databases, dashboards, and APIs for a named operator, while founders trained at SpaceX and Tesla built a proprietary stack that beat CODESYS on nine of ten measured workloads. The bull case is the **DoorDash** exception married to the **Glean** exception—domain experts use an unglamorous workflow wedge to become the universal information and control layer for factories. That case stings because industrial incumbents are slow and brownfield data creates switching costs. **Control Seat** still loses the argument on disclosed evidence: one deployment, no revenue figure, no structural barrier, and no safety-certified control path. I would flip to Invest if **Control Seat** demonstrates one verifiable condition: at least ten named multi-site production customers running its Virtual PLC for operational control—not merely monitoring—with aggregate ARR above $5 million and documented uptime.
