# Computable -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. AI companies schedule training and inference against a visible curve of future compute prices; unused capacity changes hands instead of expiring inside bilateral contracts; GPU access behaves less like a cloud subscription and more like power. Computable has found a credible leak from that world: H100 prices reportedly vary 2x, leases lock buyers in for 6–24 months, and its live market lists transferable eight-GPU nodes by calendar week from August 2026 through January 2027. The destination is legible. What remains unproven is whether weekly standardization creates liquidity or merely makes an illiquid contract easier to display.

The broken rule is Computable’s strongest evidence. Buyers currently absorb forecasting mistakes because reservations cannot follow changing workloads. Letting them redeem a position for SSH access or resell it returns real scheduling and capital control, while resting bids and offers expose a price that bilateral negotiations hide. The 2% fee on each side is economically clean. But “instant liquidity” is doing more work than the market has earned: the terms impose no obligation to repurchase and guarantee no buyback price. Without independent counterparties, a standing quote may be a company-supported promise rather than a market.

The founders plausibly know how to build the machinery. Years of collective trading-infrastructure experience at Jump Trading and Coinbase fit order matching, price formation, and transaction integrity unusually well. Yet the dossier does not name either founder, assign roles, document a pre-company experiment, or identify who ultimately runs Computable. That is not merely sparse biography. A two-person company offering something that looks financially sophisticated needs especially crisp accountability, and I cannot run the Rizz–Tizz–Biz test from two employer names. The market may be cleverer than the publicly visible founder proof.

There is a working storefront, quoted H100 capacity at $2.56–$2.69 per GPU-hour, and enough technical specificity to permit full-node redemption. The Show HN launch drew 45 points and 26 comments. Those are artifacts, not costly user behavior. The dossier supplies no matched-trade count, redeemed week, repeat buyer, seller concentration, or fee revenue. Even the listings could originate from one supplier. Market-data ownership becomes defensible only after people trade; terms assigning bid and price data to Computable do not themselves create proprietary information.

The bull case stings because SF Compute’s more than $100 million of third-party hardware validates the need, while Computable’s narrower weekly grid may concentrate fragmented demand better than a flexible hourly market. Trading specialists could deliberately begin with one fungible contract, establish a forward curve, and later become the price-discovery layer beneath AI infrastructure. The bear case wins today because SF Compute already supports buy orders, sell orders, and resale, and cloud marketplaces can copy fixed-week contracts. Computable controls neither the GPUs nor a demonstrated pool of liquidity; its most important asset is still hypothetical.

My 36-month expectation is a pivot toward brokered GPU procurement or capacity-management software, with the open weekly exchange remaining secondary and total ARR below $5 million. I am neutral, not dismissive: the system insight is sharp, but the strange behavior I need is an unaffiliated customer buying a future week and then successfully reselling it to another customer without Computable supplying the exit. Show me ten such round trips across at least three independent capacity providers, with repeat traders and durable spreads, and I would revisit the check.
