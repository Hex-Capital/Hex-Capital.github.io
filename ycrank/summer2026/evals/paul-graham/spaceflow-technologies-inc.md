# SpaceFlow Technologies, Inc. -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The important fact is not that SpaceFlow Technologies, Inc. calls itself an AI-agent runtime. It is that the founders encountered procurement split across customized ERPs, email, documents, and supplier messages, then shipped a governed context layer that works inside customer networks. Identity passthrough, approval thresholds, short-lived credentials, allowlists, and signed audit logs are concrete responses to the obstacles that normally kill enterprise agents. Hundreds of daily production users now operate through the system. This is a shipped workaround, though the dossier does not show the dated sequence—what failed in a deployment, how quickly the founders changed it, and what improved afterward—needed for a top resourcefulness score.

The technical proof is unusually strong for pre-seed. SpaceFlow runs against $400 million of annual supplier spend, has six-figure contracted annual revenue, and reports 4x faster RFQ cycles, three times as many RFQs processed, 42% less maverick spending, and $120,000 in annual savings. These are company-reported measurements, so I would not treat every number as independently established. But a national restaurant brand signed a contract, its supply-chain executive invested personally, and hundreds of employees use the product daily. That combination is much harder to fake than a benchmark or demonstration.

The narrow well is visible. The users are enterprise procurement teams forced to copy supplier information between systems, and they are paying for the crude product now. The team interviewed 200 companies, built an autonomous procurement engine with a medical manufacturer before YC, and still sells founder-to-founder rather than hiring salespeople to explain an unlearned product. Relationships with private-equity funds could become the dreaded distribution plan whose decisive verb is “partner,” but they are not the only route: SpaceFlow already has a direct enterprise contract and production use. The evidence therefore contradicts, rather than activates, that red flag.

There is also real schlep blindness here. Hakan previously sold a tender-matching engine, and the team’s pre-YC medical-manufacturer deployment connects the founders to the problem before the current pitch. Customized SAP tables, air-gapped networks, approval chains, prompt injection, and decades of business rules are precisely the work that superficially similar AI startups avoid. The product’s model-agnostic runtime and customer-specific evals fit my pattern of programmable infrastructure backed by a concrete pre-round proof spike. The unresolved question is whether these mappings accumulate into software or merely accumulate into consulting.

The strongest case against investing is that SAP, Zip, ORO, and Levelpath can bundle similar agents into systems customers already trust. SpaceFlow’s $400 million figure is customer spend, not revenue; the outcome metrics are self-reported; and one contract does not establish retention or repeatable deployment. More seriously for me, the dossier contains no rejected hypothesis or evidence-driven route change. We know the team can make this route work once, but not yet whether they behave like running backs when an incumbent, security review, or implementation failure closes it. No known exception needs to rescue the company—the working product and real user pull already satisfy the substantive gate—but neither do those exceptions excuse future obstinacy.

I would invest. By August 2029, I expect SpaceFlow Technologies, Inc. to reach $8–15 million in ARR, with most revenue coming from procurement deployments rather than a horizontal agent runtime. The clean flip condition is production replication: I would change this to a Pass if fewer than three independent customers have daily production users by August 2027. That result would mean the apparent platform is probably one successful custom installation.
