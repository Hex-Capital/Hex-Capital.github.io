# Florin -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The first obstacle is not slow underwriting. It is becoming an insurer. Florin calls itself a full-stack carrier, but the dossier identifies no license, capacity provider, reinsurance arrangement, or founder-built route around those constraints. These may simply be undisclosed, but I cannot turn an unresolved regulatory question into evidence of resourcefulness. The founders have impressive résumés and unusually durable relationships—they were middle-school friends, college roommates, and housemates—but the dossier contains no named blocker followed by a dated workaround and observable result. That criterion stays at its missing-evidence default.

The technical claim is more interesting. Florin says its system combines ACORD forms, imagery, permits, public records, and social evidence, simulates tens of thousands of policy years, and produces a bindable quote in under a minute. Shaurya built TPUs and encryption deployed across 100,000 hosts; Amol worked on autonomous systems at Tesla, Orchard Robotics, NASA, and Boeing. This is a plausible team to build the machinery. But “bindable” is doing too much work when there is no bound policy, reproduced pricing benchmark, actuarial validation, or loss data. It is a self-reported artifact with a numerical latency claim, not yet measured insurance proof.

The proposed users are specific—robotics, space, advanced-energy, and defense companies rejected by conventional classifications—but none is shown pulling the product back. Florin has disclosed no customer, policy, revenue, repeated use, or signed application. A work-email form and Shaurya’s Calendly are sensible founder-led distribution tools; they are not demand. Nor does the $911.7 billion commercial-insurance market help. Four well-financed competitors already automate parts of underwriting, and Coterie already offers sub-one-minute binding. Their funding proves that investors like the category, not that an unusual physical-technology company will trust Florin with its risk.

The banking-to-insurance change could be evidence of a running back, but the dossier supplies neither the goal that remained fixed nor the observation that falsified banking. Two nearby snapshots of different products are merely a pivot. The problem is certainly schlep-heavy, yet no founder is shown encountering commercial-insurance failure firsthand. Robotics and fleet experience make the target customers legible to the team; they do not establish why these founders discovered this insurance problem or understand the actuarial and regulatory work hiding beneath the interface.

The strongest bull case is the team itself. Three experienced technical founders with long shared history are attacking an administratively ugly market, soliciting customers personally, and refusing the conventional assumption that underwriting requires underwriters. Aydin’s reported role in taking TerraFirma from $500,000 to $30 million of realized revenue adds operating credibility. This could be exactly the sort of team that reveals its best evidence only after launch. But my unstated pattern is concrete proof before the round, and Florin has disclosed none. The known-exception route likewise requires a working product or unmistakable real-world pull strong enough to replace missing priors. Technical credentials alone do not qualify.

I am passing. My 36-month forecast is that Florin will have become an MGA or underwriting-software provider for one physical-technology niche, with less than $3 million ARR, rather than operating as a zero-underwriter carrier. One causal episode would flip the decision: document a licensing or capacity blocker, the workaround the founders shipped within weeks, and ten paid policies subsequently bound without human underwriting.
