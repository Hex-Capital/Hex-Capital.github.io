# Evergrove -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: injured-worker care does not advance because somebody finally survived an IVR queue. Software schedules treatment, retries unreachable providers, documents each attempt, updates the claim system, and hands humans the exceptions requiring judgment. Getting there requires reliable voice interaction, deep claims integrations, auditable escalation, and institutional permission to let machines handle protected information. Evergrove has a real leak from that future: 350,000-plus production calls, with customers running more than 500,000 annually. The dossier does not map the remaining six-year sequence or identify which controlled asset compounds, so the future is visible but the path is still partly my daydream.

The broken rule is telephone bureaucracy. Short-staffed contact centers coordinate care through phone, fax, email, holds, transfers, and repeated callbacks; injured workers and providers pay the rent in delayed rehabilitation and lost throughput. Evergrove reports 40% higher workflow completion, ten-times-shorter cycles, and more than 60% lower cost. Those results make the wound measurable, although the dossier never identifies a regulatory restriction or incumbent deliberately capturing the rent. This is operational waste being removed, not yet a demonstrated transfer of economic control.

Sid Unnithan and Jacob Chia did not arrive from a weekend voice-agent hackathon. At Newfront they jointly built agent-based insurance placement, while Jacob worked on authentication, authorization, and insurance-file infrastructure and Sid led insurance AI product work after Microsoft’s VS Code and Azure ML teams. That is meaningful pre-company tuition in both the visible workflow and its concealed machinery. Jacob’s 8,600-star pinned repository is additional evidence that the team can ship something technical people adopt. What I cannot yet see is a dated, self-directed obsession predating their employers or proof that they recruited an exceptional team around it; the biography supports fluency more strongly than inevitability.

The strongest bear case stings: this could be a narrow interface on rented voice intelligence while Liberate, with $72 million raised, expands horizontally across insurance. Evergrove has not disclosed revenue, retention, proprietary-data scale, or independence from a closed model provider. Its possible moat—facility contact patterns, call outcomes, workflow configurations, and integrations—is inferred rather than demonstrated. The mundane-workflow side of my portfolio pattern fits beautifully; the concealed enabling layer does not yet. Claims-system integrations, approval controls, audit trails, and three-week deployments create switching friction, but they do not prove ownership of a scarce bottleneck.

Privacy is the gate I will not wave through. Evergrove handles PHI and automated communications, and the disclosed BAAs, encryption, role-based access, retention controls, audit logs, opt-outs, supervision, and human escalation are the right architecture. They are still company claims. There is no disclosed consent failure, governance shell game, or equal-control problem—the CEO and CTO roles are clear—but founder equity and the ultimate contractual tiebreaker remain unknown. One verifiable flip condition would reverse my decision: an independent security and workflow audit showing that Evergrove cannot enforce consent, deletion, and escalation end-to-end across its model provider and claims integrations.

I would write a modest pre-seed check. The working artifact is much stronger than the future map: hundreds of thousands of real calls are costly user behavior, not a waitlist, rendering, or LOI. My 36-month bet is that Evergrove remains focused on workers’ compensation and reaches roughly $8–15 million in ARR by converting its production volume into multi-year MCO and provider-network contracts; the failure mode is commoditization before those integrations and outcome data become a controlled operating asset.
