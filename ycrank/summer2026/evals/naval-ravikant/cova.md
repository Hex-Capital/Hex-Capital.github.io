# Cova -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Cova’s strongest asset is not the founders’ credentials. It is the sequence of action behind them. Jordan Ibe and Ansh Tandon worked together on Palantir’s healthcare team, spent the following year building AI systems for home-care agencies and hospitals, converted Omnition into an internal operating system, and then became a licensed Michigan provider. That is specific knowledge made visible through deployment. They did not merely study the workflow; they crossed the boundary from selling software to owning its operational consequences.

The problem is that execution and durability are different assets. Cova’s Michigan license and direct payer billing create friction, but the dossier identifies no exclusive data, proprietary model, measured automation advantage, or structural barrier. Aidaly already employs family caregivers through software-powered agencies across five states, while Honor and Cera have much larger operating footprints. If a competitor copies Cova’s agents tomorrow, the remaining advantage is a young agency license and accumulated workflow knowledge whose transfer into a defensible asset has not yet been demonstrated.

The market supplies room to maneuver. Cova begins with relatives seeking payment for care, but the same enrollment, benefits, insurance, and billing machinery can serve a meaningful portion of the $162.35 billion U.S. home-healthcare market. The adjacent demand is real; the causal path is less proven. Each new state brings new payer relationships, reimbursement rules, forms, and licensing work. This can become a large company, but geographic replication is currently an operating hypothesis rather than leverage.

Cova captures value directly by billing Medicaid, managed-care organizations, long-term-care insurers, and private-pay customers. A prospective caregiver reported receiving a $22-per-hour Medicaid quote, which is more informative than a free-user count, but there is no disclosed revenue, reimbursement rate, payer contract, or payer mix. More importantly, one enrolled family does not yet make enrollment, care, or distribution better for the next. Referrals from hospitals, case managers, and social workers remain institution-by-institution acquisition, while caregivers remain labor rather than permissionless supply creating utility for one another.

The strongest bull case is capital efficiency: two founders claim an end-to-end agency with no non-caregiver headcount, despite applicants still completing four state forms and texting photographs of every page. A working, strange product can deserve patience before network effects or monetization become visible. But the known-exception case requires the prototype to reveal a product language or behavior competitors have not found. Here, Aidaly’s overlapping family-caregiver model and Cera’s AI-assisted delivery make the category legible; what remains unproven is Cova’s superior mechanism.

I would not reject the founders, but I would not yet write the check. In 36 months, my base case is a competent provider operating in two to four states with $5 million to $15 million in annualized revenue, still valued primarily as an efficient home-care agency rather than a compounding technology network. The verifiable flip condition is straightforward: show that every 1,000 completed enrollments produces proprietary workflow data that cuts enrollment time or back-office cost by at least 50%, while generating payer or caregiver referrals without proportional headcount. Then the agency becomes a data-producing system instead of software wrapped around regulated services.
