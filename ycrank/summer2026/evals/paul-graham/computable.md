# Computable -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Computable’s first H100 auction reveals a finance-native schlep machine with almost no visible hacker genealogy. Computable is strongest where most founders flinch: regulatory ambiguity, non-uniform contracts, concentrated supply, and a standing buyback quote that could consume real capital. Computable is weakest where I usually begin: Yuan Song has an inferred Jump Trading history, the second founder is unnamed, and neither founder has a public repository or prior technical artifact. The problem leaves tracks. The founders barely do.

**Coinbase** (2012) made compliance grind an asset because Brian Armstrong personally built the wallet and kept running toward the regulators. Computable shares the load-bearing mechanism: Computable must price calendar-specific H100 capacity, standardize imperfectly interchangeable compute, fund or match redemptions, and decide whether resalable positions are commodities or derivatives. But Coinbase had Armstrong’s artifact and unmistakable appetite for the schlep. Computable has one auction, one disclosed cluster, no transaction volume, and no identified regulatory structure. The unpleasantness is real; the evidence that Computable can metabolize it is not yet real enough.

**Loopt** (2005) exposed the danger of substituting founder force for a durable hacker artifact: Sam Altman was enough of an animal to earn an exception, but weak infrastructure timing still constrained the product. Computable shows a thinner version of that pattern. The `yuansong98` account shipped Computable on Show HN and earned 46 points, which is better than a deck, but Computable discloses no repository, technical benchmark, matching engine, settlement system, or named second founder. “Jump Trading and Coinbase” may conceal formidable builders. It may also be credential shorthand. Hackers usually leave more debris.

Computable’s sealed bids and standing buyback quotes could compound resourcefulness over 18 months if every auction forces Computable to recruit supply manually, warehouse unwanted positions, and improvise settlements when buyers exit. That would create the animal tracks I want: not merely software, but a sequence of ugly problems solved without permission. Computable has shipped the mechanism and launched it publicly, yet 46 Show HN points and a July 31 closing date are attention rather than proof of resourcefulness. No completed auction, repeat buyer, revenue, or liquidity rescue puts Computable above the default, but far below Airbnb’s cereal boxes.

Computable’s Jump Trading and Coinbase backgrounds plausibly make the idea organic: people who have operated trading and digital-asset infrastructure would naturally notice that reserved GPU capacity behaves like an illiquid position. The score flips upward only if Yuan Song or the unnamed cofounder personally held stranded compute commitments, built Computable to unwind them, and can name the first counterparty who asked to use it again. If Computable instead began from the observation that GPUs are becoming financial assets, the idea is merely clever. Organic ideas begin with “we needed this,” not “this market ought to exist.”

**Stripe** (2010) had two founders whose long shared history and commerce hacking made the pair itself evidence. Computable also has two founders with apparently complementary Jump Trading and Coinbase experience, which is the right numerical shape and potentially the right operational shape. But Computable provides no name for one founder and no evidence that the pair previously worked, lived, or built together. Two resumes are not yet a pair. Computable gets credit for avoiding the solo-founder problem, not for proving Collison-like chemistry.

**SF Compute’s** $55 million, thousands of operated GPUs, and resale orderbook make a broad liquid exchange difficult to establish from one H100 cluster. Within 36 months, Computable will pivot from instant-liquidity weekly GPU positions into an institutional brokerage and market-making service for reserved compute, reaching $1 million to $3 million in annualized revenue without becoming a broad exchange. Computable’s schlep competence should produce a useful business: manually matching heterogeneous contracts is more plausible than creating continuous liquidity across GPU model, network, location, and service terms. Computable can make money precisely where the exchange abstraction breaks.

**Jump Trading and Coinbase** form the strongest objection to that forecast. Computable’s founders may understand market structure and custody machinery better than the sparse dossier reveals; the sealed auction, published clearing prices, and standing bid could be Computable’s version of Armstrong’s wallet, while the idea’s wrong-seeming financialization could make it a Black Swan rather than a broker. Loopt proves that unusual founder force can override incomplete early evidence. But Computable has not disclosed a cleared trade, named supplier, reserve, repeat buyer, or fulfilled buyback, while SF Compute already operates resale infrastructure and Ornn has raised $33 million to pursue compute hedging. I would flip to Invest if Computable completes three monthly auctions with repeat buyers and suppliers, honors every requested buyback, and demonstrates at least $50,000 in monthly gross profit without balance-sheet subsidies.
