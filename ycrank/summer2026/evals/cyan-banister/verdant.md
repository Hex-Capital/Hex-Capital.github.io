# Verdant -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Aidan Ng is explicitly Verdant’s CEO, while Verdant has no named municipal deployment a normal applicant can touch. That pairing is Verdant’s signature: a structurally legible founder arrangement attached to an artifact-poor product. I can see Biz and some Tiz in Ng’s government exposure and Jason Yi’s work at Holly and Aidy; I cannot yet see Rizz expressed through a city, an applicant, or a permit moving differently in the world.

**Anduril** is the high rung for unambiguous authority: Palmer Luckey’s conviction, product identity, and leadership all resolve to one human. Verdant shares the named-CEO mechanism, which matters because two Georgetown co-founders otherwise invite the 50/50 question. Verdant diverges where the dossier supplies no equity split, founder-vesting arrangement, or documented tiebreaker authority. “Aidan is CEO” is a good label; it is not yet proof of who wins the argument that could kill the company.

**DeepMind** is the low-rung exception on physical verifiability: the product was inaccessible abstraction, but Demis Hassabis’s polymath compounding overrode that weakness. Verdant currently offers a July 3 marketing-site launch, an applicant-facing portal description, and an estimate that AI review may be only 80–90% accurate—but no named city where I can submit something and watch Verdant work. Verdant is closer to software testimony than to riding **Uber** or playing **Niantic**. Jason Yi’s government-automation research is interesting, but it is not yet a Hassabis-level exception.

**Niantic** had John Hanke’s decade inside Keyhole, Google Earth, and location products before the pitch existed. Verdant has a softer version of that origin: Ng worked around DOJ, USDA, Congress, Deloitte Government, and commercial-real-estate underwriting, while Yi built local-government HR software and public-benefits automation. Verdant’s 200 interviews cut both ways. They demonstrate persistence, but the March grant-management identity followed by a July permitting launch suggests the founders researched their way toward permitting rather than carrying a specific permitting wound into Verdant.

Verdant’s regulated-government character can compound over the next 18 months through ordinance mappings, security approvals, workflow configuration, and the institutional scar tissue of procurement. Verdant already contemplates property addresses, plans, zoning data, DPAs, encryption, MFA, and human decision authority; those are unsexy constraints that repel tourists. The trouble is that software complexity is not frontier risk by itself. Accela, GovWell, and Clariti can absorb the same mechanism, and Verdant has no accumulated deployment evidence proving that local configuration has become a moat rather than implementation labor.

GovWell has raised $35 million and reports more than 150 agencies, while Clariti has acquired CivCheck and Govstream already has a Bellevue deployment. Verdant therefore does not yet look “magically weird”; Verdant looks like a late entrant into a category whose procurement window has visibly opened. The score flips upward if municipalities reject the funded end-to-end vendors because ordinance-grounded applicant guidance is politically or operationally strange in a way only Verdant understands. No such rejection, contrarian investor pass, or underpriced-round evidence appears in the dossier today.

GovWell’s $35 million balance sheet makes my falsifiable forecast that, by July 2029, Verdant is acquired by GovWell before reaching $3 million in ARR, with Verdant’s ordinance-guided intake folded into AutoCheck. Verdant can build useful jurisdictional machinery without building an independent category leader: slow municipal sales, no moat today, and three deployed competitors create exactly the conditions where Verdant’s product becomes more valuable to an incumbent than Verdant becomes as a standalone company.

Verdant’s 200 local-government interviews form the strongest bull case: Ng knows government buyers, Yi has shipped government software, one CEO is named, and regulated ordinance workflows can become wonderfully sticky. Verdant could be **HandUp**-like if a deeper biographical connection emerges, **HQ Trivia**-like if applicants show undeniable product love, or **DeepMind**-like if Yi proves to be a rare polymath—but none of those Known Exceptions is evidenced today. The unstated-pattern test also hurts: Verdant was selected through interviews rather than surfaced from an unobserved lived moment, while the red-flag test finds an AI mechanism available to well-funded incumbents. Ten named municipalities renewing Verdant after twelve months would flip my verdict because that single fact would make the artifact observable, the workflow sticky, and the incumbent threat rebuttable rather than a hard no.
