# Quippy -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Jonathan Li has shipped something people can use. That matters. But the question I most want answered is what happened when building Quippy became difficult. The dossier contains no specific obstacle, founder-built workaround, or elapsed time. His product work at Duolingo and Luvu gives me a reason to investigate. It cannot supply the missing episode. I would pass on the evidence here.

Quippy’s strongest evidence is the product itself: voice and text rehearsal, feedback on individual lines, and practice that adapts to recurring difficulties, distributed on both iOS and Android. This is beyond a diagram or a promise to begin after funding. It fits my preference for founders who build before asking permission. But the stronger pattern in technical bets is an inspectable proof spike. Here there is no measured improvement in conversational ability or demonstrated technical advantage. A functioning learning app earns substantial credit; its promised learning effect remains unproved.

The August 12 review is more interesting than the follower counts. Someone moved to San Francisco, used Quippy, and reported making a new friend group. That suggests a particular person with an urgent problem. It is the beginning of a narrow well. But one reported outcome does not establish that this group repeatedly wants the product. The 4.8 rating from 24 US iOS ratings and 100+ Android installs show some exposure and favorable response. They do not tell me whether practice becomes a habit or ends after curiosity is satisfied.

The strongest argument against passing is that Li has already worked on precisely the machinery Quippy needs: consumer learning and engagement. His YC biography credits prior Duolingo work with an English course reaching 280,000 daily active users in four months. Combine that experience with a shipped app and one unusually concrete user outcome, and there is a plausible company here. I have allowed working products and unmistakable demand to override missing founder evidence before. Quippy has the working product, but the evidence of demand is not yet strong enough for that exception. The prior-employer figures cannot answer whether people return to this product.

The economic question follows from that distinction. Quippy lists a $19.99 monthly subscription, but neither paying subscribers nor realized revenue are disclosed. Personalized history could make practice progressively more useful; it could also accumulate in an app people stop opening. Yoodli already offers adaptive roleplays and personalized feedback, so Quippy needs to discover something unusually valuable about its particular users. Li’s learning-product background is relevant, but the dossier does not document his own collision with this problem or an ugly operational barrier he has chosen to overcome. Nor does it show a product route revised after contrary evidence. Those are unknowns, not evidence of a manufactured idea or stubbornness.

I would not turn seven advertised jobs into a claim that Li is prematurely delegating. The dossier does not establish that red flag, just as advertising attribution does not establish dependence on a large partner. My decision rests on the missing founder test. One verifiable event could change it: a dated account of a specific retention failure among adults who recently moved cities, the change Li personally shipped in response, and cohort data showing those users subsequently returning and paying. That would connect resourcefulness to an actual need. Today those pieces remain separate.
