# Capveon -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Capveon connects the systems used to sell and deliver infrastructure. Does that make infrastructure radically cheaper to build? The dossier establishes manual reconciliation across lead feeds, estimating systems, CRMs, ERPs, specifications, and email. It does not establish how much infrastructure spending that work consumes or how removing it changes project cost or delivery capacity. Infrastructure is consequential. Participation in its software stack does not automatically produce consequential leverage. I pass on the evidence available.

The technical question is whether Capveon eliminates work faster than customer-specific integration and human review recreate it. Entity resolution, clause extraction, and proposed record updates address concrete problems. Source citations, approval queues, and field-level audit trails are sensible controls. But there is no measured comparison against a named incumbent workflow, including implementation and correction time. Trimble’s April 2026 announcement that it would acquire Document Crunch and embed its capabilities into construction workflows makes this distinction urgent: Capveon must demonstrate an advantage that native software cannot absorb. Commercial models do not themselves establish the AI replication red flag; the missing evidence is a distinctive capability or proprietary learning loop.

The strongest argument against passing is the founders’ combination of building and selling experience. Lancaster worked on search ML at Publix and previously founded FinnedAI, with a claimed approximately $40K MRR. Moorhead held early sales roles at Bonsai and Harper. Both worked at Harper. That combination could turn an unglamorous integration wedge into control of valuable operational workflows, consistent with my broader pattern of backing execution businesses alongside scientific ones. But prior-company revenue is not Capveon revenue, and complementary résumés do not demonstrate belief revision or difficult recruiting against a named weakness. My exceptions allow an initially ordinary product when an exceptional instigator and enormous accessible value pool justify the option. This dossier does not establish that override.

I would spend the next dollar testing whether automation survives messy customer records without becoming a services business. The dossier supplies no ranked kill risk, budgeted experiment, numerical threshold, or stop condition. It also provides neither pricing nor the implementation, model, support, and approval costs needed to establish economic advantage. Those are evidence gaps, not proof of bad economics or premature scaling. Newsletter coverage and a conference tool covering 1,147 exhibitors do not resolve them; neither demonstrates repeated operational use.

My condition to reopen is one independently auditable paid deployment showing at least a 10x reduction in fully loaded human effort—including setup, approvals, and corrections—at unchanged error rates and positive contribution margin. That would establish a mechanism worth investigating for broader consequence. Until then, Capveon offers a plausible workflow business without evidence sufficient to underwrite the extreme outcome I seek.
