# Tracer -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The strongest fact is not the AI-inference market. It is that Adam Rida built an open-source router, published its method, attracted 1,000 GitHub stars and 70 forks, and then shipped Echo behind an OpenAI-compatible endpoint. Echo reports 98.6% on MATH-500 for $4.58 and 92.8% on LiveCodeBench for $5.35. This is the behavior of a fierce nerd: turn an abstract quality-cost tradeoff into working software and measured results before anyone grants permission.

But the dossier omits the fact I look for first. What broke? What did Rida do when it broke? How quickly did the workaround change the product or its users? Moving from the earlier classification router to coordinated models may have involved such a sequence, but no rejected hypothesis or dated obstacle is documented. Shipping is evidence of technical ability. It is not automatically evidence of resourcefulness under resistance. The same distinction limits credit for flexibility: Echo is a new route, but the evidence does not show what observation caused the route to change.

There may be a narrow well here. AI teams with production inference workloads really do face a quality-cost problem, and Tracer offers an unusually direct acquisition method: benchmark the team’s workload, conduct a 30-minute inference review, and privately configure the system. That is better than waiting for a ceremonial launch or a giant distribution partner. The cost-plus-25% pricing also makes the economic claim testable. Yet the dossier names no team repeatedly sending production traffic, paying the margin, or returning after the review. Five free guest messages are product access, not pull. The 1,000 stars belong to the prior router, not Echo, so they cannot be silently converted into current-product demand.

The bull case is sharp. Rida has applied-mathematics and explainable-AI experience, built a technical substrate before fundraising, and previously grew DeepRecall to about €100,000 ARR in under three months. Tracer also fits an unstated pattern I like: a programmable interface that lets technical users obtain a better primitive without rebuilding the underlying machinery. Its public endpoint and quantitative benchmarks supply more evidence than most pre-seed companies have. No major red flag is active: the founder is not delegating the product, depending on one large partner, or substituting press for customer recruitment.

The problem is that OpenRouter already offers routing and fusion across more than 400 models, reportedly at roughly $50 million in annualized revenue, while Martian and Not Diamond attack adjacent selection problems. Echo’s multi-model coordination is a real technical distinction, but without retained workloads it may be an optimization that users admire and do not adopt. Nor does a known-exception override apply yet. There is a working product, but its benchmarks are self-reported and incomplete across multilingual, repository-level, and agentic tasks; there is neither independently validated proof nor unmistakable current-product pull strong enough to replace the missing founder-response evidence.

I would not write the check today. Tracer is technically impressive but has not crossed the people-first gate: Criterion 1 remains below 18, and current-product demand remains below 11. One result would flip me: five identifiable production teams each routing at least one million tokens per week through Echo for eight consecutive weeks, with a customer-discovered failure producing a shipped revision and improved retention. That would simultaneously reveal the narrow well and show what Rida does when reality resists.
