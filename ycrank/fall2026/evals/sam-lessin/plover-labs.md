# Plover Labs -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Plover Labs has something worth underwriting: enterprises reportedly signing five-figure monthly contracts for a product used inside their engineering workflow. But what am I paying, and what does my check prove? The dossier supplies neither proposed post-money valuation nor competing-term-sheet count, and it names no funded experiment with a deadline. I cannot turn YC membership into evidence of an auction, or financing silence into evidence of a bargain. Both primary gates remain unresolved.

The strongest signal is Fieldguide’s production collaboration. Its company-specific Blueprints, regression cases, and environment definitions accumulate through actual engineering work. This fits my pattern of backing a translation layer between systems: coding agents generate changes; enterprise engineering organizations need evidence they can accept them. Fieldguide’s customer-authored account establishes embedded usage beyond a founder’s demo. The potential dark pool is the accumulated knowledge of how this particular organization’s agents fail. The ownership question matters, though: customer-specific knowledge is not automatically Plover’s proprietary asset. If the customer can retain the verifiers and replace the vendor, the workflow may compound value primarily for the buyer.

The technical evidence also puts a boundary around what has been sold. Forge produced merge-ready results on 37 of 40 historical tasks, with a 28-minute median completion time, but that evaluation omitted production review and some browser and document checks. Fieldguide still retains human approval. Its reported 40% increase in merged PR throughput is encouraging and explicitly non-causal; I would not substitute the separate company-reported tripling of PRs per engineer for proof of autonomous shipping. Plover has demonstrated useful assistance. Eliminating review remains an experiment. Sonar’s deterministic checks and Harness’s combined delivery infrastructure make that distinction economically consequential: competitors can sell verification while Plover is still establishing how much responsibility its gates can safely absorb.

Edward Ahn’s safe reinforcement-learning research, Apple agent work, and prior InPerson founding experience give the technical direction substance. They do not establish a prior commercial operating result across both enterprise delivery and agent development. I would keep that dimension at its missing-evidence default rather than manufacture founder-market fit from affiliations. The dossier identifies a CEO; it supplies no affirmative evidence of the equal-founder decision paralysis I worry about.

The reported Envoy and Fieldguide contracts earn real economic credit, especially for a two-person company. They do not establish cash generation after implementation, support, and model costs. Gross margin, burn, runway, and a seed-funded break-even budget are unavailable. Delete the next financing from the spreadsheet: I cannot yet tell whether these contracts sustain a compact software business or purchase substantial founder labor. There is also no evidence that management actually plans a financing treadmill, so that red flag remains unproven.

The objection to passing is substantial: Plover already has named enterprise buyers and production feedback that could make its verification increasingly valuable as coding agents improve. My exceptions for AI businesses with demonstrated conversion and operating evidence are relevant. But this dossier supplies neither measured retention and cash economics nor a hard external valuation signal sufficient to override the missing deal and capital plan. I pass on the present evidence. One verifiable flip condition would be an executable sub-$10-million post-money financing package funding a dated production experiment with customer-agreed defect thresholds and a documented route to cash break-even without another round. That would give me a price, a truth to reveal, and a business capable of surviving the answer.
