# Dock -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: task-specific agents are moving from under 5% of enterprise applications in 2025 toward 40% by the end of 2026, creating a buyer for shared agent state, but Dock becomes large only if cross-agent coordination becomes an independent system of record rather than a feature inside existing workspaces.

That is the governing belief, and the dossier does not yet make it falsifiable. Dock identifies fragmentation across private agent sessions and provides shared documents, tables, attribution, and permissions, but it gives no threshold for agent density, cross-functional usage, or workflow frequency at which companies adopt a separate workspace. The $19 Pro and $49 Scale plans also leave the economic model unclear: are these introductory prices for infrastructure that later supports meaningful expansion, or evidence that customers value coordination as a lightweight utility? A generic $24.5 billion agentic-AI forecast does not answer that question.

The technology shift is real but the value-capture boundary is not. REST and MCP access, individual credentials, workspace-scoped roles, synchronized state, approval gates, and replayable history form more than an output wrapper. Better models and more agents should increase the need for coordination. The problem is that Linear, Cursor, Lindy, Replit, and Hex already own artifacts or execution environments and can extend into these controls. Dock has not shown why a general-purpose horizontal workspace will outrun products that begin with an established workflow and distribution channel.

The strongest bull case is the team. Kavaturi participated in Branch’s growth from zero to $100 million in revenue; Molinet co-founded Branch and served as COO; both later built Thena, and this is their third company together. They understand B2B product development, international expansion, and go-to-market mechanics. That history makes a rapid repositioning plausible if the original abstraction is wrong. It does not, however, constitute the rare frontier-research or market-reconstruction exception that would let me ignore the missing market gate.

There is also no demonstrated product pull. One GitHub follower, one star on the strongest visible repository, zero Toolify reviews, and no reported revenue or repeat usage do not prove rejection at pre-seed, so I use the missing-evidence baseline rather than treating them as churn. But the combination matters strategically: the agency program, referrals, free tier, and free Scale month describe several possible channels without showing that one works. Dock has not yet found the equivalent of a narrow initial buyer whose adoption can compound into adjacent workflows.

The durability test is more favorable. Dock owns permissions, agent identities, shared state, attributed events, and human approvals, all of which become more useful as models improve. Yet the prospective moat—accumulated schemas, history, and audit records—is inferred, and a well-funded competitor could reproduce the current primitives. Security further constrains the enterprise wedge: no SOC 2 Type II, no third-party penetration test, recoverable webhook secrets, and plainly stored browser-session tokens are particularly consequential for a product asking customers to centralize agent credentials and approvals.

My 36-month forecast is that Dock pivots from a general-purpose “run your company” workspace into an agent identity and governance control plane and remains below $3 million ARR. The repeat founders and underlying architecture make that outcome more likely than simple failure, but it is not yet an investable horizontal-platform thesis. The verifiable flip condition is five named organizations each running at least ten production agents through Dock, retaining for six months, and paying at least $2,000 per month for permissions, audit history, and cross-application coordination. That would establish both the controlling variable and a credible economic boundary.
