# Routant -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Routant could remove a bottleneck between capable agents and useful work: permission to act inside private enterprise systems. If that unlocks reliable automation of expensive expertise, the consequence could extend well beyond authorization software. But the dossier does not quantify the labor displaced, deployment cost removed, or population served. The adjacent market estimate—$210 million in 2026, projected to reach $1.02 billion by 2031—is spending on permissions, not evidence of an orders-of-magnitude consequence. I cannot price the upside by assuming every dollar of agent productivity accrues to this access layer.

The strongest argument for buying the option is that security may control whether agents reach production at all. In the January 2026 Okta-commissioned survey, 69% of 150 decision-makers said security concerns slowed adoption. Routant’s proposed division of responsibility—vendors integrate once, customers specify resources, approvals, and audit requirements—addresses that obstruction directly. This fits my pattern of backing infrastructure beneath an existing workflow. An authorization layer could become strategically important without inventing a new model architecture. That is a serious bull case.

The technical question is whether Routant changes what customers can safely deploy. Keycard already documents policy-bound credentials and delegation chains; Arcade documents per-action authorization, approvals, and audits. Routant supplies no verified enforcement architecture or measured improvement against either. Competitor funding does not settle this question, but their documented capabilities establish the comparison Routant must win. I see overlap, not proof that the product is merely a wrapper. The red flag of competing through interfaces and implementation labor remains unresolved rather than established.

The first dollars should test whether permissions remain enforceable when agents behave unexpectedly, credentials propagate, and access is revoked. The dossier identifies bypass and delayed revocation as inferred risks; it does not show that the founders have ranked them or designed a decisive experiment. There is no disclosed test threshold, budget, or response to failure. Founder identities are also unavailable, so I cannot assess learning rate or recruiting around security weaknesses. The reported team size of zero is not evidence of an incapable team. Missing revenue is ordinary at this stage; missing pricing, support burden, and connector-maintenance costs simply leave the economic case unproven.

I pass at 25/100. The exceptions for an initially ordinary product entering a giant value pool, or an extraordinary instigator pursuing a consequential mission, require affirmative evidence that Routant’s dossier does not provide. Nor does it establish harmful-category exposure, premature scaling, or founder rigidity. This is an unproven fit, not an active contradiction. My single flip condition is an independently reproducible production comparison showing Routant reduces the engineering hours required to deploy and maintain the same private-system agent workflow by at least 10x versus Arcade, while passing identical adversarial authorization and revocation tests. That would demonstrate a mechanism for changing deployment economics. Today, the dossier supplies the bottleneck and a proposed access layer, but no measured discontinuity.
