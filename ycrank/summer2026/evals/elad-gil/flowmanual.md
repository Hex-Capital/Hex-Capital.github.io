# FlowManual -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Construction back offices now have models capable of comparing heterogeneous project documents, and general and MEP contractors will buy when those comparisons prevent enough missed scope, pricing, and deadline errors to justify recurring software spend. The controlling variable is repeated paid use across a project’s bid-to-contract and buyout-to-invoice cycles. FlowManual identifies hundreds of hours of manual review and $5K–$10K exposure from a missed clause, but it does not establish the required conversion rate, savings threshold, or frequency of use.

The technology shift is directionally real but economically unproven. Anthropic reported 21% fewer source-information errors in Opus 4.7, and FlowManual adds exact-line citations and audit logs. Yet its terms still require professional verification, while Anthropic and Azure perform important parts of the document processing. The dossier does not show that improved models reduce review labor, increase contractor margins, or make a previously impossible workflow reliable. This is model progress attached to a plausible use case, not yet a demonstrated change in construction economics.

The strongest part of the company is product architecture. FlowManual connects bids, contracts, purchasing history, invoices, takeoffs, bid leveling, and change orders while retaining customer-specific vendor prices and observations. Better models should improve these comparisons, and the accumulated workflow state could become useful distribution for adjacent purchasing products. This is meaningfully deeper than document summarization. The countervailing risk is that breadth becomes validation burden: every new document class introduces another way to make a financially consequential error, and the privacy policy prevents customer data from automatically becoming a pooled advantage.

The bull case is credible. Document Crunch serves more than 500 companies and is being acquired by Trimble; Trunk Tools, LightTable, and Primepoint have collectively raised substantial capital. Those facts validate construction-document intelligence, while FlowManual’s purchasing and invoice focus could provide a differentiated wedge and subsequent cross-sell channel. But this market is already crystallizing. Competitors have distribution, capital, and overlapping source-linked analysis, while FlowManual has disclosed no customers, revenue, repeat prototype usage, or paid conversion. Hundreds of interviews demonstrate effort, not product-market pull.

The founders moved quickly—development began April 18, 2026, YC funding followed roughly one month later, and they interviewed hundreds of construction professionals in two months. Michael Lin also participated in engineering work associated with Dijie’s growth from $3K to $336K MRR. That is useful operating exposure, but neither founder has documented construction experience, a prior startup outcome, or evidence of having personally lived this workflow. The research-stage exception does not apply: FlowManual is neither developing frontier technology nor supported by a rare technical team whose underlying research asset independently warrants the bet.

My 36-month base case is that FlowManual narrows to either bid-to-contract review or invoice reconciliation and remains below $2M ARR rather than becoming a broad construction back-office platform. The red flag is not hiring—there are zero open YC roles—but premature claims of workflow breadth and a customer-data moat whose reproduction cost has not been quantified. I pass today. The verifiable flip condition is a cohort of at least 10 contractors paying $25K or more annually and renewing after using both bid-to-contract and buyout-to-invoice workflows throughout live projects.
