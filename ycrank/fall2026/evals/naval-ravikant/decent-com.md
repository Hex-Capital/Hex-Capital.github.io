# Decent -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Nick Soman has earned knowledge that cannot be acquired by prompting a model. He operated health plans and a PEO; the earlier Decent was acquired in June 2025. Andrew Budker has built production AI systems, and the founders have known each other since working on Kindle roughly sixteen years ago. These are substantial reasons to take the team seriously. They establish knowledge of the problem and capacity to execute. The new entity, announced in September 2026, still has to establish what it owns that another capable broker cannot reproduce.

Decent’s strongest commercial evidence is specific: Serif Health reports $60,000 in annual savings, and Locate reports more than $100,000 against its renewal proposal. Comparing funding arrangements can create real customer value. The harder question is whether serving the next employer improves the result for employers already enrolled. The dossier establishes no such mechanism. Its 67 companies “working with us” indicate early engagement, without establishing completed placements or a shared learning advantage. Paid introductions could distribute the product, but a referral reward alone does not demonstrate that users generate distribution.

The reported automation of 89% of brokerage work is the strongest argument against withholding a check. If that figure survives measurement, two experienced founders could build an unusually efficient brokerage while retaining human judgment where it matters. That fits my preference for small operating teams. But efficiency available through broadly accessible AI can spread to competitors. Nava already offers year-round advice and automated bill audits. Decent’s license and quoting relationships are operating assets; the dossier establishes neither exclusivity nor a proprietary model. Claims review also excludes fully insured and PEO arrangements, limiting where its proposed learning engine can operate.

There is meaningful demand here: the cited KFF statistic puts 51% of covered workers at employers with 10–199 employees in self-funded or level-funded plans. That supports the relevance of claims review. It does not establish a path from this employer wedge into a large adjacent market. Likewise, commissions from insurers, PEOs, and administrators identify an actual payer, but undisclosed rates and realized revenue leave value capture only partly established. Variable commissions and bonuses create an incentive conflict worth examining; written compensation disclosure is a useful response. Neither fact establishes dishonest founder behavior, and the dossier supplies no evidence of coercive fundraising or the other integrity red flags that would veto the investment.

My founder-led exceptions matter here: demonstrated operating knowledge can justify investing before a network or revenue history exists. Decent deserves that consideration. Its model also touches my pattern of organizing fragmented offline supply, but carrier approval remains a gate, and no participant-created network has emerged. I am neutral and would hold my check. The verifiable condition that would change my decision is a renewal cohort showing that additional employers contribute claims data Decent has durable rights to use, measurably improving savings for existing clients. That would turn accumulated brokerage work into an asset that compounds.
