# Risklytics -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, a small robotics company should be able to put machines to work without discovering that its insurance stops precisely where autonomy begins. Risklytics is working on a necessary passage into that world: getting someone to accept the liability. The missing steps are consequential, though. Brokers must translate unfamiliar machine behavior into explicit coverage, carriers must become comfortable pricing it, and coverage must remain available as deployments grow. The dossier establishes a foothold in that transition, but not yet a mechanism for completing it.

Why can a robot operate successfully and still be commercially undeployable? Because general liability and professional liability can leave a gap, and optional ISO AI exclusions introduced in 2026 can widen it. Risklytics has identified an actual contractual obstacle rather than merely calling insurance broken. Removing that obstacle gives a robotics operator permission to serve customers. Its economics remain conventional: carriers reportedly pay commissions of 10–20% of premiums. That can support a useful business, but the founders have not shown that they reduce premiums, shorten a measured delay, or change who ultimately controls access.

The strongest argument for writing a check is wonderfully concrete: three companies reportedly obtained coverage after earlier declines, spanning robotics rental, bridge-collision technology, and teleoperation, in the week preceding the August 26 launch. Someone apparently got through a previously closed door. That matters much more to me than follower counts. Almost 50 reported application users add evidence that the workflow exists, but they are not 50 bound policies. These remain company-reported outcomes without named customers, renewal behavior, or demonstrated willingness to keep paying for this particular broker.

Gold and Risio have done more than attach AI to an insurance website. Their earlier Ember catastrophe-modeling work and Risio’s producer license show effort to learn the machinery. The pivot also stayed within insurance; it does not establish opportunistic trend chasing. What I cannot establish is an obsession predating the company or unusual recruiting ability. The pre-revenue exception behind **CosmicBrain AI** is relevant: both businesses could enable physical autonomy before substantial revenue exists, but that exception rested on a lifelong robotics record, a functioning prototype, and signed industrial proofs of concept. Risklytics has encouraging placement reports, while the founder evidence is substantially thinner. Gold is identified as CEO; the roommate relationship alone supplies no evidence of a governance problem.

The exclusion tracker is the artifact I would spend time with: 736 filings, 49 states, 33 insurers. That is an unglamorous object worth being curious about. It fits my pattern of looking beneath a visible technological wave for the operating work everyone will need. But a compiled public database does not establish control over scarce underwriting capacity. Wholesaler and MGA partnerships provide access; they do not demonstrate durable bargaining power. RiskCube already advertises exclusion reviews, and Koop serves related insurance needs. Risklytics must accumulate something those brokers cannot readily reproduce. Its customer-confirmation step is sensible, while the dossier leaves data custody unverified rather than documenting a privacy violation.

I am neutral and would keep my money in my pocket today. The team may build a valuable specialist brokerage, but the evidence currently supports repairing access more strongly than owning an enabling layer. My one verifiable flip condition is a carrier-signed delegated underwriting agreement authorizing Risklytics to bind explicitly AI-inclusive robotics coverage. That would move the company from finding someone willing to open the door toward controlling the passage itself.
