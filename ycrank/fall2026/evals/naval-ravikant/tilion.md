# Tilion -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Tilion’s founders have earned the right to be taken seriously through something they built. Arham maintains Fortress releases; the team shipped native Chromium modifications that developers can use through existing Playwright and Puppeteer code. BrowserArena recorded 156 ms median lifecycle latency and 99 successful runs out of 100 on October 22. That is specific knowledge expressed through a working artifact. The founders’ college credentials and previous employers tell me less than their ability to make this difficult infrastructure work.

The investment question begins where that accomplishment ends: what does Tilion retain when someone copies the implementation? Earlier BSD releases remain available for forks. Camoufox and other competitors already modify browser engines. New production licensing can restrict newer releases, but it cannot withdraw permissions already granted. Tilion also depends on externally supplied network egress when IP reputation causes blocking. The team has solved meaningful engineering problems, yet the dossier identifies no exclusive asset that makes the next solution progressively harder for competitors to reproduce. Maintenance could become a recurring expense without becoming a compounding advantage.

The strongest objection to my hesitation is that developers may already be choosing the team faster than competitors can copy it. Fortress reports 100,000-plus installs and use by engineers at more than 170 companies. Combined with the independent browser benchmark, that is a credible foundation for becoming the default runtime for agents. I can back a demonstrated prototype before a user loop exists; that exception deserves consideration here. But downloads do not establish repeated dependence, and the dossier does not demonstrate the unusual capital efficiency or founder recruiting evidence that would justify overriding weak durability. The bull case is substantial. Its decisive mechanism remains unproved.

Tilion does fit my preference for tools that let individuals produce useful work without an employer’s permission. Its packages, containers, and familiar browser interfaces lower adoption friction. The stronger unstated pattern—outsiders contributing work that improves the system for everyone—is absent. Public detection reports could help maintenance, but there is no measured contribution loop or proprietary feedback dataset. Another installation currently proves another distribution event, not additional utility for existing users. Nothing in the dossier establishes an integrity or artificial-deadline red flag; the licensing transition alone does not establish misconduct.

There is room beyond anti-bot handling: developers collecting data and developers executing browser workflows can use the same underlying runtime. That is a plausible expansion path, although the dossier does not establish adjacent customer spend. Browserbase and Kernel demonstrate competing suppliers, not Tilion’s addressable revenue. The $2,000 monthly organization license names a payer and a price; announced hosted pricing of $0.03 per browser-hour offers direct consumption monetization. Neither proves completed purchases or sound contribution margins, and the hosted waitlist makes current availability uncertain.

I would hold off on a personal check. Tilion has stronger evidence of founder capability than of an asset that compounds independently of founder effort. My one verifiable flip condition is a controlled deployment study showing that feedback from existing customers materially improves protected-site success for new customers over the publicly forkable Fortress baseline, using data Tilion has exclusive rights to retain. That would turn deployment volume into an accumulating advantage. Today, the evidence supports capable builders and a useful product; it leaves that investment mechanism unresolved.
