# Frontrunner -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Frontrunner’s strongest result is a claimed 15% increase in new-business win rate. That could matter enormously to a customer. It does not yet establish an orders-of-magnitude reduction in the cost of selling. The $5.37 billion sales-intelligence estimate identifies an existing spending pool; it supplies neither the labor baseline nor the mechanism for radically expanding access to expertise. I can lose my entire check. Before buying that option, I need a consequential success case beyond improving outbound conversion.

The business graph is a concrete product mechanism: connect CRM, email, calendar, product data, and calls so agents retain context across tasks. That addresses a recognizable limitation of standalone Claude sessions and earns some technical credit. But Clay’s September 2026 announcement already describes shared context, autonomous workflows, and outcome-based learning. Frontrunner supplies no comparative accuracy, throughput, or cost measurement. Clay’s reported $100 million ARR establishes customer spending on the underlying need; it is not evidence of Frontrunner’s superiority. The relevant AI red flag is reproducible orchestration without demonstrated proprietary learning. The dossier leaves that concern unresolved rather than proving the product is merely a wrapper.

The strongest objection to passing is the founders. Maya Nayyar has run growth at Console and Encord; Carl Bager combines growth engineering with a prior acquired company. Their former colleague specifically credits them with implementing account-intent routing at Encord. They have lived this workflow and built pieces of it before. Customer testimonials reporting 40–50% outbound-to-meeting conversion and 100-plus opportunities actioned daily make the possibility of useful automation worth taking seriously, although neither claim discloses its measurement window or customer identity. My broader investing pattern includes execution-heavy businesses. **Block** illustrates the exception: a relatively ordinary initial tool can open a much larger infrastructure opportunity. Frontrunner shares the practical workflow wedge, but the dossier does not establish a similarly expansive infrastructure mechanism. Relevant experience and an acquisition also leave belief revision and risk-specific recruiting unobserved.

The cheapest decisive work should address whether persistent context produces reliable economic gains against an existing alternative. Agents can write to connected systems, and Frontrunner’s terms acknowledge inaccurate outputs and require customer review. Review labor could consume the apparent automation savings. There is no disclosed experiment with a threshold, budget, or stop condition, so risk-removal process receives the missing-evidence default. Contract-specific pricing supplies no bottom-up economics either. I infer neither subsidy dependence nor attractive margins. Likewise, missing revenue and a two-person team are ordinary pre-seed conditions.

I pass on the present evidence. The other exception routes—visible exponential technical progress or a quantified societal consequence large enough to override missing milestones—are also absent. One independently measured customer deployment showing at least a 10x reduction in fully loaded cost per qualified opportunity against its prior Clay-based workflow, with qualification quality held constant and human review included, would flip this pass to active diligence. That would demonstrate a cost discontinuity worth investigating. Today, Frontrunner demonstrates workflow relevance and promising customer claims; its transformative mechanism remains unproven.
