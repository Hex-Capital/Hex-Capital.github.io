# Quippy -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Jonathan Li has put a learning product into users’ hands. Quippy’s voice rehearsal, line-level feedback, and adaptive drills are concrete evidence of execution. His Duolingo and Luvu work adds relevant experience building consumer habits, although the $50.4M ARR growth and $1.5M ARR figures belong to those employers. I give him meaningful founder credit for the shipped product; I cannot yet infer exceptional knowledge of social-skills instruction or independently attribute those earlier commercial outcomes to him.

The decisive question is what Quippy owns after another team reproduces the roleplay. Its [privacy policy](https://quippyapp.com/privacy) describes personal practice history and personalization memory, while excluding session content from shared-model training. That is a legitimate privacy choice. It also means I cannot assume that one customer’s practice improves the system for everyone else. Personal history could become valuable through accumulated understanding, but the dossier supplies no evidence that preserving it improves outcomes or makes switching costly. A remembered conversation is an asset only when remembering changes the result.

The strongest objection to passing is that curriculum and product judgment can compound without a shared model. Quippy has 4.8 stars from 24 US iOS ratings, and an August 2026 reviewer credits it with helping form a new friend group after moving to San Francisco. Those are early signs of value reaching beyond the screen. Li’s consumer-learning experience makes the possibility worth taking seriously. My exceptions allow a compelling prototype and unusually effective founders to precede observable network effects. Here, however, the evidence establishes a useful product more clearly than exceptional curriculum quality, capital efficiency, or persistent user behavior.

There is room to expand: the same rehearsal capability can serve friendship, dating, interviews, and workplace conversations. Yoodli’s enterprise offering and Vocal Image’s reported $12M ARR provide evidence of spending in adjacent communication-training markets. They also expose Quippy to established alternatives. Its [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/quippy-social-skills/id6772510214) prices—$19.99 monthly and $49.99–$69.99 annually—identify a direct payment mechanism, but establish neither completed purchases nor contribution economics. Storefront availability, advertising attribution, and social followers likewise do not demonstrate customers distributing the product to other customers.

I would pass with my own money today. Quippy fits my interest in tools that help individuals develop useful abilities; it has not demonstrated my stronger pattern of participants creating value for one another. The dossier contains no affirmative evidence of integrity problems, artificial fundraising pressure, or founder instability. The constraint is the missing compounding mechanism. My single flip condition would be a controlled 90-day cohort result showing that accumulated personal practice history materially improves paid renewal versus an otherwise identical version without that history. That would turn personalization from a plausible feature into evidence of an advantage.
