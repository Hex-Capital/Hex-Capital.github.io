# MOCHI.TV -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: anime is no longer something fans wait years to receive. A teenager describes a world, generates consistent characters, releases an episode that afternoon, and grows the story with its audience. Getting there requires reliable character continuity, legitimate rights, retained creators, and economics that reward original franchises. MOCHI.TV has one real leak from that future: a three-person team already releases episodes daily, has built more than 100 series, and reports 300M organic views. That is much more interesting than the $41.7B anime TAM. But the dossier maps only the beginning of the transition, not the remaining six-year chain.

Robert Qiu and Michael Ninh make a credible pair for this experiment. Qiu brings machine learning, games played by more than 100K people, and a prior game business reportedly reaching $200K in monthly revenue. Ninh brings consumer growth, hundreds of video creatives, and the slightly unreasonable act of watching 400-plus anime series. There is technical ability, distribution fluency, and commercial command here. What is missing is the first-believer biography: dated pre-company experiments, evidence they recruited one another around an independently derived obsession, and the history of how this team behaves together when production or growth breaks.

The strongest case for writing the check is the working artifact. MOCHI.TV reports 100K users and 900K social followers; Google Play independently shows 10K-plus downloads, 1,180 reviews, and a 4.5-star rating. People are not merely joining a waitlist. They are watching enough for the company to manufacture and distribute more than 100 original series. I will sometimes forgive an immature moat when a consumer product creates a genuinely new ritual, but 300M off-platform views do not yet prove that exception. The dossier gives no dated retention cohort, creator activity, subscription revenue, or reconciliation between 300M views, 100K users, and 10K-plus Android downloads.

The broken rule is visible but only half decoded. Traditional anime production takes years; this team says it can move from concept to a 30-episode series in one day. Mochi Lab could transfer authorship from studios to individuals, which is the important possibility—not simply cheaper content. Yet there is no evidence that creators repeatedly publish, attract followers, or earn money. Meanwhile, reviews cite incomplete series, authentication failures, and broken ads. Those complaints are operating tuition: compressing production is not useful if the audience repeatedly reaches unfinished stories.

Underneath the excitement, MOCHI.TV is currently an application on top of the AI-content wave, not its enabling layer. It controls an audience and perhaps valuable viewing feedback, but no disclosed model, exclusive rights, production primitive, or scarce distribution channel. AniShort, Dashtoon, HOLYWATER, GammaTime, and several App Store peers can attack adjacent formats with similar tools. More seriously, the product permits AI-generated and user-published anime without disclosing training-data licenses or a rights-review process. That threatens the very franchise asset the upside case requires. Robert is clearly presented as CEO, so I do not see a leadership shell game, but the co-founder history and control structure remain unknown.

I am not writing my own check today. My base case is that MOCHI.TV can become a valuable, fast-growing content asset without becoming the durable creator system implied by Mochi Lab. The verifiable flip condition is one cohort of at least 1,000 Mochi Lab creators showing 30% or better 90-day retained weekly publishing, measurable payer conversion, and documented chain-of-title for their output. That would turn enormous attention into strange, costly creator behavior—and show that the company owns more than a moment in the feed.
