# Graphide -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Dan McGee and Benjamin McIntyre have known each other since middle school and built together at four hackathons. That matters. They have some history of making things together before starting a company. But the question I most want answered about Graphide is still unanswered: what stopped them, what did they do about it, and how quickly did that work? Building Illumio’s simulation platform for 300+ virtual machines is evidence of McGee’s engineering experience. It does not supply the missing episode of resourcefulness under resistance.

The product contains a promising distinction. A graph edge inferred by a model should not have the same authority as one established by static analysis, a test, or production behavior. If Graphide preserves those distinctions through a replayable rule violation, it could help a programmer decide what to trust rather than merely generate another explanation to read. The GitHub App, documented integrations, and reported testing on fzf, Flask, Hono, and Excalidraw support credit for an implemented product. But those are company-reported demonstrations. The dossier provides no measured detection accuracy, false-positive rate, or independently verified example of a consequential bug caught.

The strongest case for investing is that these founders are building something technical users could turn into infrastructure. Graph context exposed through MCP fits my recurring interest in programmable tools whose users discover applications the founders did not anticipate. McIntyre’s editor-tool work and McGee’s simulation experience make that possibility more credible. Yet testing Graphide on Flask is different from Flask maintainers repeatedly relying on it. The reported developer use does not identify an urgent niche, returning users, or an unplanned workflow that people insist on keeping. My working-product exception deserves consideration here, but the available proof and pull are not strong enough to replace the missing founder evidence.

There may also be a useful change of direction underway. YC calls Graphide a graph IDE; the current site emphasizes GitHub checks and team sales. Moving into an existing workflow could make adoption much easier than persuading programmers to change editors. But I cannot credit an evidence-driven revision without knowing what observation caused it. Nor does related engineering experience establish that either founder personally encountered this particular problem. Both remain open questions, rather than evidence of rigidity or a manufactured idea.

The concrete danger is that Graphide asks for substantial trust before demonstrating indispensable value. Its October 16 terms permit model training on stored content by default, while its privacy draft describes cloud source storage. Those choices could obstruct the very repository access needed to prove the product. Custom team pricing offers a route to learning this directly from buyers, but no completed sale is documented. Meanwhile, CodeRabbit and Qodo already advertise overlapping graph and architectural capabilities. The founders’ opportunity is to solve one painful verification problem so well that developers demand access despite the installation burden. A broader collection of checks will be easier to substitute.

I would pass today. The cofounder relationship is encouraging, and the dossier shows neither dependence on a major partner nor premature delegation. Those red flags are absent. The decisive issue is that technical promise carries almost the entire case, while the founder-action gate remains unmet. One verifiable deployment could change my decision: a named team encounters a specific adoption blocker, the founders ship a workaround within two weeks, and that team subsequently relies on Graphide’s verified checks across successive pull requests. That would establish both how these founders respond to resistance and whether anyone urgently wants what they build.
