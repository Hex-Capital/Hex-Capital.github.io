# Decawork -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Aman Raj built compliance tooling used by 240+ Barclays teams, yet Decawork’s present product has no atoms-heavy or regulated-customer barrier. That pairing is Decawork’s signature: unusually credible operators building a structurally ordinary software business. Founder quality can rescue a weak initial market, but it cannot manufacture monopoly. Decawork has more evidence of people who can ship than of a secret worth owning.

Anduril is the high rung for founder lineage: Palmer Luckey had already shipped Oculus to a billion-dollar exit before attacking defense. Decawork shares the operator mechanism at smaller scale—Raj previously served 52,000 families, processed $1.5 million, and led a product to 100,000-plus MAU; Sarthak Aggarwal worked on AI systems at NVIDIA and Ema. Decawork diverges because none of those accomplishments established category ownership or an adjacent exit. These are strong proxy signals, but not the Luckey exception that independently justifies a check.

Asana’s pure-software workflow architecture supplied no atoms-heavy or regulatory moat; Dustin Moskovitz’s Facebook pedigree overrode that weakness. The same failure mechanism appears more starkly in Decawork: Decawork is a software gateway serving ordinary enterprise IT, with no named government customer, certification, capex barrier, or regulated deployment. Identity security is sensitive, but sensitivity is not regulation. Decawork’s credential concentration may actually invert the moat by making every outage or compromise a reason for customers to prefer Okta.

Okta’s 8,200-integration catalog exposes the missing contrarian secret. Decawork correctly sees that agents must become identity-bearing principals, but Okta, Astrix, Oasis, and Token Security all see it too. Decawork’s broader deploy-monitor-recover-retire workflow could compound over 18 months into proprietary policies and operational data, yet the dossier never names the architectural or institutional reason Okta cannot extend into those functions. Without that incumbent failure mode, Decawork is “AI infrastructure for X” with a better product boundary—not a Zero to One secret.

Astrix Security’s $85 million and Oasis Security’s $195 million make the monopoly counterfactual unusually demanding. Decawork would move into the Invest tier if its gateway became the mandatory execution layer—not merely an identity console—so that approvals, credentials, run histories, and recovery state created irreversible switching costs. Decawork has disclosed no current-product adoption, pricing power, or data-volume advantage, while Okta already owns distribution. Competition is for losers, and four overlapping vendors plus an incumbent is evidence of competition unless Decawork can define a narrow market it alone can dominate.

SpaceX committed to a multi-year reusability roadmap; Decawork moved within roughly one month from an autonomous IT administrator presented in June 2026 to an agent control plane centered in July. A pivot can be intelligent, but it is the opposite of evidence for a definite plan. Decawork names useful architectural commitments—externalized credentials, gateway-mediated tool calls, scoped permissions, approvals, and attributed logs—but no two-to-five-year milestone that the founders refuse to abandon. The dossier therefore supports implementation clarity, not definite optimism.

Decawork’s prior autonomous-IT product was live across multiple enterprises, while current-product revenue remains undisclosed. By July 2029, Decawork will have pivoted back toward autonomous IT operations and remain below $5 million ARR rather than becoming an independent agent-identity control plane. The mechanism is straightforward: strong operators will follow customer demand, but weak structural differentiation will force Decawork away from the identity layer that Okta and heavily capitalized specialists can bundle.

Aman Raj’s 240-team Barclays deployment is the strongest objection because Asana and Quora show that exceptional operators can override missing structural moats. Decawork could combine its prior production footprint with the gateway architecture and become the system of action while Okta remains merely the system of identity. The objection stings, but Raj’s achievements are not Moskovitz’s Facebook-scale lineage, and Decawork has disclosed neither a migrated enterprise nor a moat for the current product. Five named regulated enterprises routing most production-agent tool calls through Decawork under multi-year contracts would verify the system-of-action thesis and flip my verdict.
