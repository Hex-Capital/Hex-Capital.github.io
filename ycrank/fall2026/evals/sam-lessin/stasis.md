# STASIS -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

STASIS has an intelligible product hypothesis: glasses that detect ocular strain and change the conditions causing it could be more useful than glasses that merely report another wellness score. But what am I buying, and what does my check prove? The dossier supplies no post-money valuation, competing-offer count, or evidence that this is the first scarce check. I cannot call the round an auction because the company is in YC. I also cannot underwrite a cheap entry that has not been offered. The financing gate stays at its missing-evidence default.

The proposed shipping window—end-2026 to early-2027—is not yet a venture moment. Integrating inward-facing sensors, automatic tinting, and Bluetooth screen adjustments produces a device; it does not establish that the device meaningfully reduces strain. The founder’s reported 30-day wear test is useful evidence of personal experimentation, but it cannot separate an effective intervention from enthusiasm for something he built. There is no disclosed capital plan tying proceeds to one measured result by a deadline. That is missing evidence, rather than proof the founders intend to run a financing factory.

The strongest argument against passing is the team’s actual continuity in ocular hardware. Berkeley independently identifies both Soham Chakraborty and Neil Goradia on GlaucoGlasses; their combined bioengineering, EECS, and device-development experience gives this project more substance than a consumer-brand concept attached to outsourced electronics. STASIS fits my pattern of looking for people who can connect software with difficult physical systems. That earns founder credit. It does not yet establish shipped commercial operations or privileged access to buyers on both sides of that bridge. Conversations with Kaiser Optometry leadership are promising access to investigate, not a distribution asset I can own.

The decisive commercial question is whether the sensing-and-adjustment loop causes somebody else to buy and keep wearing the glasses. Website preorder solicitation does not establish completed purchases. The 348 physician intent forms and seven distributor LOIs totaling $168,000 belong to the separate glaucoma product; moving those figures onto STASIS would manufacture the strongest evidence in the investment case. My known exceptions require unusually concrete conversion, operating economics, or proprietary access to override missing financing gates. None is established here. The claimed patents have unverified scope, and a future longitudinal dataset cannot defend a business before customers generate it.

Delete the next financing and the economics remain unmodeled. Conflicting $95 and $240 preorder prices, plus an optional $15 monthly subscription after year one, do not tell me hardware contribution margin, warranty costs, burn, or cash required to deliver. I am not assuming those economics are bad. I am assigning the stated default because they are unknown. JINS MEME’s discontinued consumer offering makes durable use a particularly relevant question, although it does not establish that STASIS will suffer the same outcome.

I pass on the current evidence. The one result that would change my view is an independently documented cohort of 50 paying screen users with at least 70% still wearing STASIS weekly after 90 days, showing measured strain reduction and positive hardware contribution after returns and support. Those are proposed underwriting thresholds, not company claims. That result would establish a functioning base business worth reopening the deal discussion around; today, the strongest asset is the founders’ relevant building experience.
