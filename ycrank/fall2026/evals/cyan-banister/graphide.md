# Graphide -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine developers setting architectural boundaries while agents write most of the code. The interesting object in Graphide is the evidence label: did a model guess this relationship, did static analysis establish it, or did a test observe it? Those distinctions could become essential when nobody can read every change. But that future is my extrapolation. The dossier establishes branch-specific graphs and replayable rule violations; it does not yet establish the transitions from useful review tool to trusted control over autonomous development.

The founders give me more reason to pay attention than the category does. Dan McGee built an Illumio simulation platform handling more than 300 virtual machines and previously ran an automation business. Benjamin McIntyre built editor tools and campaign systems, then recruited more than 100 students into a Linux/FOSS club. Four shared hackathons and a relationship beginning in middle school add evidence that they can build together. There is independent technical curiosity and some ability to recruit belief here. What remains unestablished is a sustained, pre-company obsession with this particular problem and command of its commercial economics.

Why must a developer inspect every line? Because someone remains accountable when an agent quietly crosses a system boundary. Graphide could return that person time and control by making the boundary checkable. That fits my interest in repairing irritating systems, but the dossier identifies a review bottleneck rather than a quantified institutional rent. Custom team quotes establish an offer, not customers’ willingness to pay. Likewise, reported developer use and testing on fzf, Flask, Hono, and Excalidraw deserve modest credit, but they do not show teams repeatedly trusting Graphide enough to change their release behavior.

The strongest case against passing is that the team is building the concealed infrastructure beneath coding agents: a record of what software actually does, accessible through MCP and usable across development tools. That fits my pattern of backing the enabling layer beneath the visible wave. The evidence labels and whole-package test fallback also acknowledge operating complexity rather than pretending it disappears. But CodeRabbit already advertises codegraphs and architecture analysis, and Qodo offers organizational rules and a software map. Graphide has not demonstrated control over a scarce dependency. My willingness to back a pre-revenue prototype does apply; the exceptional founder obsession and specific external commitments that would overcome the remaining uncertainty are not established here.

Then I reach the source-code terms. The October 2026 documents describe cloud source storage, default session-content uploads, and permission to train models on stored content. Disabling diagnostic collection does not stop graph uploads. That creates a concrete consent and custody concern precisely where Graphide asks customers to entrust it with their architecture. The CEO and CTO roles are identified; equal-control deadlock, concealed leadership, and dependence on one closed model supplier are not established. The data permissions are an actual concern in the dossier, rather than a hypothetical red flag.

I would pass with my own money today. My single verifiable condition for reopening the decision is a paid proprietary-code deployment under a signed agreement prohibiting training and specifying source and graph retention, with a documented customer account of repeatedly using Graphide’s checks to change release decisions. That would connect permission, trust, and costly behavior in one observable artifact. It would earn another look; it would not automatically establish the larger infrastructure thesis.
