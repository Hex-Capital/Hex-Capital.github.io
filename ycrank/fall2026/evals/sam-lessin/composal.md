# Composal -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Composal has identified a potentially expensive consequence of cheap code: somebody still has to establish that the software works. But what does my check buy? The dossier gives no proposed post-money, competing-term-sheet count, or capital-funded experiment. Those are unknowns; YC membership does not establish an auction. I cannot underwrite either a cheap entry or a specific truth the round will reveal. The product hypothesis—independent browser agents catch failures that coding agents miss—is understandable. A financing plan with a measurable result and deadline is absent.

The strongest asset is the team’s experience across the relevant systems. Zembashis brings coding-agent and infrastructure work from Cosine; Davies brings product engineering and software work for Telefonica, Mitie, and Vodafone. That supports the translation-layer pattern I look for: understanding both how agents produce software and how people experience it. The 80,000 repositories cited in earlier materials belong to Cosine, however. They establish context for the founder’s experience, not Composal’s distribution. I give the founders meaningful credit without treating former employers’ scale as their new company’s operating result.

The uncomfortable competitive fact is that nine interviewed teams had already built internal QA agents. Those buyers own the application, its permissions, and its workflows. Composal needs to establish why an external provider captures durable value when customers can increasingly build the tool themselves. Persistent journey maps might become proprietary knowledge, but the dossier establishes neither exclusive access nor purchases caused by that knowledge. CLI and MCP integrations provide entry points. Ten setup calls show engagement. Neither establishes the owned distribution that would make this business unusually defensible, and mabl’s October launch puts an established vendor in the same verification workflow.

The bull case deserves attention: these founders understand the production machinery creating the problem, and they have shipped a product that returns recordings, logs, and reproduction steps. Composal also publishes unusually useful limitations. Its October 18 benchmark passed 157 of 166 attempts, recorded one false success, and explicitly disclaimed production generalization. That candor helps me assess the engineering; it does not yet establish dependable verification. Half of ten setup calls stalling on authentication or network restrictions suggests that access and deployment could determine the business before agent capability does. My exceptions for AI businesses require observed conversion, retention, cash economics, or unusually strong external validation. These receipts do not yet meet that bar.

Then delete the next financing. The $500 monthly plan includes 3,000 browser minutes—about 17 cents per included minute at full utilization—while additional prepaid minutes cost $1 each. That difference makes actual utilization and delivery cost consequential; it proves neither good nor bad margins. Browser infrastructure, inference, onboarding, and investigation of questionable results all need to fit inside the collected cash. There is no disclosed burn or contribution margin, so I use the economics default. I also see no evidence of an auction, equal-control paralysis, or a plan that assumes errors disappear. Those red flags remain unestablished. The affirmative concern is narrower: Composal has demonstrated technical activity without establishing an economic asset it owns.

I pass on the current evidence. My one verifiable reconsideration condition is a cohort of five independent teams renewing the $500 plan for three consecutive months at positive contribution profit after browser, model, and attributable support costs. That would show a repeat purchase surviving the actual delivery burden, and justify reopening the financing discussion. Today the founder fit is stronger than the evidence that this particular company captures the value being created.
