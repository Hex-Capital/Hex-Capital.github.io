# Praxis -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Praxis has shipped into law firms. That matters more than the founders’ employer names. Diana’s reported experience taking Gorgias’s AI Agent to 1,000 customers and $8 million ARR suggests she understands selling automation; those are prior-employer results, and her personal attribution remains self-reported. Maxence has built multimodal systems at SightCall. The strongest evidence is their current product operating inside U.S. firms, although the dossier supplies little detail about what those deployments have taught them. There is specific knowledge here, but exceptional knowledge of legal operations remains partly unproven.

The decisive question is what Praxis keeps when competitors reproduce the workflow. Qualifying inquiries, preparing engagement letters and chasing invoices form a useful administrative chain. Clio already advertises workflow-executing agents; Lawmatics launched native intake qualification in February 2026. Those products make the substitution threat concrete. Praxis identifies no proprietary model, exclusive data rights or demonstrated switching cost. Firm-specific configuration could eventually become an asset, but an inferred future asset earns no present credit. I would be buying exposure to the team’s execution while the durable advantage remains unidentified.

The strongest case against passing is that these founders can turn fragmented administration into a coherent operating system. An unnamed partner reports spending 1.5 hours daily on setup and engagement letters. Recovering that time across intake, matter management and collections could make Praxis central to a firm. The expansion path is credible: the same context and approval system serves successive stages of the client relationship, and Clio’s reported $500 million ARR independently establishes substantial software spending. But the testimonial measures the old burden, not Praxis’s improvement. Public pricing and payment status are undisclosed, so I cannot yet distinguish useful deployment from direct value capture.

Praxis also falls short of the compounding mechanism I look for when backing fragmented professional markets. Another law firm adds another customer; the dossier shows no corresponding improvement in utility or distribution for existing firms. Confidential, separated firm records do not establish a shared learning network. The planned October 30 event with HeyCounsel and Mission Law is sensible prospecting, but supplies no evidence of customer-created distribution. There is likewise no evidence of dishonest dealings, artificial financing pressure or dependence on investor reassurance. My objection is structural, not a judgment against the founders’ integrity.

I can make an exception for an unusually insightful prototype before revenue or a user network exists. Praxis’s live deployments make that exception worth considering. What is missing is evidence that the product embodies an insight competitors cannot readily absorb; the already overlapping incumbent workflows weaken that case. I would pass with my own money today. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: a paid customer cohort renews after receiving a comparable bundled incumbent offer, with reference calls establishing that an accumulated Praxis-specific operational asset makes replacement materially costly. That would demonstrate something customers preserve after the feature advantage disappears.
