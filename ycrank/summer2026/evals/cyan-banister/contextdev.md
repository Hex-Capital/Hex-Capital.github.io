# Context.dev -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Agents continuously negotiate purchases, monitor markets, and rebuild interfaces from live web state; they need context that is structured, current, and dependable. Context.dev could sit underneath that world, but the dossier does not work backward from it. It gives me today’s problem—developers maintaining browsers, proxies, parsers, caches, retries, and queues—and today’s explanation that agents need fresher data. I am missing the intervening transitions and the reason Context.dev, rather than a search index or commoditized crawler, becomes the durable layer. The future is plausible; the ownership of it is not yet mapped.

The small leak from that future is real usage. More than 400 customers, 5,000 developers, 11,018 weekly npm downloads, and 2,000 monitor runs in 24 hours describe a functioning artifact rather than a Demo Day rendering. Mintlify, Rho, and Klarna are named users, while Dench attributes more than $3 million in pipeline to social-platform monitoring. That is unusually strong product contact for pre-seed. Still, “customers,” “developers,” and “businesses” are used inconsistently, and no revenue, retention cohort, or workload volume tells me whether users have built operations around the API or merely tried generous free credits.

Yahia Bakour is more than a category tourist. He led checkout engineering at Amazon, managed AI work at Sunrun, and previously built two acquired products. That demonstrates technical and commercial command. What I cannot see is the pre-company wound: no dated sequence shows him fighting this infrastructure for years, building increasingly strange artifacts, or recruiting believers before web context became a crowded AI category. The discrepancy between YC’s team size of one and the launch text’s team of four also needs a boring, exact answer. One accountable founder is clean; an undefined operating team is not.

The economic mechanism is sensible but not yet sharp. Context.dev removes a bundle of operational taxes—proxy rotation, rendering failures, extraction drift, retries, and vendor coordination—and sells the reclaimed engineering time from $25 to $499 per month. Five SDKs, SOC 2 Type I, 70% cached-brand coverage, and the combined brand, transaction, screenshot, and monitoring endpoints make this a genuine enabling layer. But Firecrawl, Tavily, and Exa already attack overlapping constraints with substantially more capital, and Context.dev still depends on third-party sites whose defenses it does not control. Breadth can become infrastructure, or it can become a large maintenance surface without proprietary leverage.

The strongest bull case is that the working product itself earns an exception: hundreds of customers have already found value, a solo repeat founder has shipped through production complexity before, and the broader endpoint surface could compound into reusable source handling. That case stings. What stops me from writing the check today is the combination of an unmapped six-year advantage and a privacy boundary that is barely described. Anti-bot bypass plus social-platform monitoring can drift from useful context into surveillance; SOC 2 addresses operational controls, not consent, custody, or acceptable collection. My one flip condition is verifiable: show a six-month cohort of 100 production customers with at least 90% gross retention, each using two or more endpoint families. That would demonstrate that the bundle is becoming a system of record rather than a fashionable API sampler.
