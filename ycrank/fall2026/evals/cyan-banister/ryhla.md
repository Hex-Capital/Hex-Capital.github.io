# Ryhla -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Eight battery projects killed within days: that is the detail that makes me stop. Ryhla reports saving a developer approximately $160,000 in fees by identifying projects that should never enter the utility application process. Why must someone pay and wait before discovering whether a site can support their project? The utility controls the answer; the developer carries the uncertainty. Moving that knowledge earlier gives developers control over where they commit capital. That is a specific operating wound.

Six years forward, I can imagine developers designing solar, storage, and charging projects around available power before buying land or commissioning studies. Getting there requires reliable capacity estimates across utilities, followed by predictions that survive actual approval and upgrade decisions. Ryhla’s distribution-grid models address those steps. But that future is my hypothesis: the dossier does not establish the transition or show how far the company has traveled toward it. I will not turn a plausible destination into evidence that these founders have mapped the journey.

The founders have more substance than two impressive school names. Sara El Baghdadi and Yassine Guennoun reportedly researched grid models at Stanford and deployed them with utilities. Guennoun’s UCSF uncertainty-estimation work and public medical-imaging repository show technical work beyond a fundraising biography. Their friendship since middle school supplies relationship history. What remains missing is a dated record of independently pursuing this particular problem, recruiting others into it, and learning its commercial machinery. I see technical capability; the evidence for an enduring grid obsession is thinner.

Ryhla also reports an unnamed Midwest solar deployment and 91% utility approval among model-approved sites in its 2025 applications. That denominator matters. It does not tell me how many viable sites the model rejected, how many utilities were represented, or whether developers returned. The ten-project battery screening shows a consequential customer decision, but it does not establish repeated paid behavior. Missing revenue alone would not stop me: my willingness to back pre-revenue technical companies rests on prototypes, committed counterparties, and unusually strong founder evidence. Ryhla has pieces of that exception, without enough corroboration to invoke it confidently.

The strongest case for writing a check is that this modest screening service becomes the concealed infrastructure beneath several enormous markets. Solar, batteries, charging, and data centers all need the same scarce answer: where can power actually connect? That fits my pattern of backing an unglamorous enabling layer. The uncomfortable counterargument is that Ryhla may accumulate consulting work rather than a compounding asset. Proprietary models are claimed; reusable data rights and learning effects are unverified. Utilities retain approval authority, Paces already offers overlapping diligence, and Enverus owns interconnection simulation capabilities. A useful prediction does not automatically confer control over the bottleneck.

I am **Neutral**. Ryhla has identified a costly institutional workflow and demonstrated an early reason for developers to change decisions. I see no documented privacy violation, leadership shell game, or dismissive claim that utility operations are trivial; those red flags do not explain my hesitation. The gap is between repairing this workflow and owning the enabling layer beneath it. My flip condition is a paid repeat deployment at a second utility, with independently verified prediction results and contractual rights to reuse the resulting application data. That would make the compounding mechanism tangible enough for me to reconsider investing my own money.
