# Invertix -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Invertix has access to operating solar infrastructure. The investment question is how much additional electricity, engineering capacity, or lower-cost maintenance that access produces. The reported 1.8 GW managed is a deployment footprint, not a measured energy gain. Customers’ combined 50 GW is a larger opportunity, not capacity Invertix controls. If the agents let one engineer operate ten times as many assets safely, the consequence could matter enormously. The dossier supplies neither today’s engineering baseline nor the demonstrated multiplier. I cannot price that consequence from installed capacity alone.

The strongest argument for investing is that customers are already buying. Company-reported “multiple six figures” in ARR and TotalEnergies On participation are meaningful evidence at this stage. They suggest an expensive operational problem and a team capable of reaching the people who own it. Invertix also fits a pattern I like: working through installed industrial infrastructure rather than requiring customers to replace it. Embedded engineers may be exactly the right way to discover the operational knowledge automation needs. A potentially valuable operating layer can begin with considerable manual work.

The decisive uncertainty is whether each deployment makes the next one substantially cheaper and more capable. Organization-specific ontologies could capture reusable engineering knowledge; they could also accumulate customer-specific implementation obligations. Approximately 60 days from contract to deployment makes that distinction economically consequential. Physics-informed models and differentiable simulators are credible technical directions, but there is no measured advantage against Delfos or Raptor Maps. Delfos already combines operational data, domain rules, and machine learning. I would not dismiss Invertix as a wrapper, but I would not pay for a technical discontinuity that has yet to be demonstrated. Customer-controlled data use also leaves the proprietary learning loop unresolved.

Invertix needs to spend against that uncertainty before expanding implementation capacity. Its terms require customer-defined permissions, supervision, and professional sign-off; that is sensible, but it means autonomous task completion must be measured after including human review. Revenue establishes willingness to buy something. It does not establish reliable labor substitution. The dossier gives no costed experiment, numerical reliability threshold, or stop condition addressing this risk. Pricing, deployment labor, ongoing supervision, and compute costs are undisclosed, so the path to beating human-delivered operating costs remains unproven. There is no evidence of permanent subsidy dependence; there is also no bottom-up parity case.

Perrotta’s commercial background and Durmaz’s work in computer vision, differential privacy, and AI for chemistry provide useful complementary starting capabilities. They do not yet document belief revision or recruiting someone to own a specific energy-engineering weakness. That is missing evidence, not adverse founder behavior. My exceptions for execution-led businesses and undeveloped technical plans deserve consideration here: paid adoption could precede a much broader infrastructure platform. But those exceptions require an unusually compelling founder instigator or a demonstrated mechanism connecting the initial wedge to an enormous consequence. The current commercial signals do not establish either override. Equally, the dossier shows no exit fixation, refusal to learn, or harmful-category conflict.

I pass at present. Invertix may build a successful energy-software business, but my money would be underwriting repeatable implementation before I have evidence of an engineering multiplier. One result would flip the decision: an independently audited deployment across three unrelated operators showing at least a 5x reduction in fully loaded labor cost per correctly resolved operational incident versus their existing workflow, including onboarding and supervision, with no increase in missed critical faults. That would connect capability, reliability, and economics in the same experiment.
