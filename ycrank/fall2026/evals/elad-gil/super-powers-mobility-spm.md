# Super Powers Mobility (SPM) -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Super Powers Mobility (SPM) addresses a concrete military requirement: radios, sensors, and electronic warfare equipment need power without the acoustic signature, heat, and fuel logistics of an idling engine. The controlling variable is whether SPM can sell a repeatable, qualified power system rather than repeatedly sell engineering hours. Its standard 15 kWh modules and 12 kW continuous AC output make that proposition concrete. The dossier does not yet establish deployment endurance, qualification status, or production economics. I see a credible market mechanism, with limited evidence that SPM can capture it at scale.

The strongest evidence is the company-reported **$421,815 of realized revenue in one month following its September 2026 pivot**. That is unusually informative at pre-seed and substantially more useful than the earlier vehicle preorders. I discount it because customers, revenue composition, and repeat purchasing are undisclosed, but actual purchasing deserves considerable credit. The separate $792,000 qualified pipeline reinforces the opportunity without becoming additional revenue. My strongest objection to withholding a check is straightforward: SPM may already have discovered an urgent purchasing requirement, and waiting for a longer history could mean missing the opening.

The question is what those customers purchased economically. Standard battery products, custom development, and vehicle integration can produce very different businesses despite similar initial revenue. The June 2026 supply-chain restrictions offer a plausible procurement catalyst, but SPM’s eligibility and resulting customer savings remain unestablished. Likewise, eliminating generator use has operational value without necessarily creating superior supplier margins. The broader military-battery market is reported at $1.6 billion with 4.3% forecast growth. Bren-Tronics’ $208 million acquisition against approximately $100 million of annual sales demonstrates a strategic exit market, while giving little support to assuming exceptional valuation multiples. The physical-business exception requires technology to change those economics; SPM has not documented that change yet.

There is more substance here than an interchangeable battery reseller. SPM documents battery, power-electronics, drivetrain, and controls integration, bench and vehicle testing, and tactical demonstrations. Those are affirmative architectural capabilities. However, retained production programs and qualification-based switching costs remain prospective, and Inventus already supplies custom military systems. Jonathan Powers’ Honda, Workhorse, and Tesla experience makes the move into physical systems more credible and fits my pattern of backing domain expertise in difficult industries. It does not establish the sacrifice or prior startup learning required for additional founder-motivation credit. Nor do the demonstrations independently verify the claimed performance advantage.

I would monitor SPM and hold off on a personal check. Its demand evidence is ahead of its evidence for repeatable product economics; that is a promising supplier whose investment case remains unresolved. **My flip condition is a verified production order with scheduled repeat deliveries of the same qualified PEK design at greater than 40% delivered gross margin, including attributable integration costs.** That would connect the observed willingness to pay with a system SPM can scale, and establish the economic exception that makes this physical business compelling.
