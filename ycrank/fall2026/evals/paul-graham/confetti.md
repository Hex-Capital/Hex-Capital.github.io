# Confetti -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Confetti’s hardest problem will appear when someone tries to assemble the thing. A generated design can look convincing while specifying parts that do not fit. What did Gu and Yuan do when that first happened? The dossier does not say. That is the missing fact I care about most: a particular obstacle, a workaround the founders built, and what changed afterward. Their Penn backgrounds and research experience are encouraging, but they cannot answer that question.

The strongest case for Confetti is that these founders might understand both sides of that failure. They coauthored EngineLab, accepted at an ICML workshop, and Yuan participated in Penn Electric Racing. Their advertised workflow includes component selection and printed-part ordering, where mistakes become physical and expensive ([founders and product](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/confetti)). Helping a beginner finish one working project could teach them more than generating a thousand attractive designs. The compatibility checks, supplier coordination, and manual debugging could eventually become the valuable part of the company. But the dossier supplies neither an inspected end-to-end build nor a measured success rate. EngineLab establishes shared technical work; it does not establish that Confetti’s machinery works.

Confetti resembles a pattern I like: giving people a programmable tool that lets them make things they previously could not. The decisive evidence would be users taking that tool somewhere unexpected and returning to keep building. Here, “first-time hardware builders” remains a broad description rather than an observed narrow well. The 58 LinkedIn followers tell me nothing about completed projects or repeat use. Pricing is unverified, and transaction fees are explicitly an inference. None of this is unusual for a pre-seed company. It does mean I cannot yet distinguish an urgent tool from an entertaining demonstration.

The apparent move from Asteria’s retail sales agents to hardware could be evidence of useful flexibility. It could also be an unrelated change of direction. The dossier identifies neither the observation that prompted it nor the elapsed time, so I give it no affirmative pivot credit. Similarly, the founders’ technical backgrounds and racing exposure do not establish that Confetti arose from a problem they personally encountered. Zoo and Flux already cover portions of conversational design and sourcing. That makes successful assembly and delivery a consequential possible wedge, but entering an operationally difficult workflow earns credit only when the founders actually solve its difficulties.

I would pass on the evidence available. The resourcefulness gate is unmet, and none of the documented exceptions supplies an override: there is no demonstrated working prototype, unmistakable user pull, or independently validated technical result. I also see no evidence of founders waiting for permission, relying on a major partner, or refusing contrary feedback; missing evidence should not become a character accusation. **One verifiable condition would flip my decision:** a documented four-week cohort in one specific builder niche where the founders fix a named assembly blocker, at least five users complete functioning projects, and at least three return for a second build. That would connect founder action, technical proof, and real need—the three things Confetti’s current description leaves disconnected.
