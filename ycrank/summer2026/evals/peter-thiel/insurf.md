# Insurf -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Insurf’s implied contested truth is that providers can recover more denied care by turning payer rules, clinical evidence, physician attestation, and payment outcomes into one auditable decision record. The falsification test is simple: across comparable cases, does Inveto materially improve approval or collection rates while reducing staff hours? The selected specialty-practice pilots could answer this, but Insurf publishes neither case counts nor outcomes. The company has made the claim testable; it has not yet shown that the claim is true or that peers reject it.

The strongest part of the product is not generative drafting. It is the preservation of policy snapshots, submitted text, attestation events, payer responses, and posted payments. This makes a contested administrative process legible and could eventually produce payer-specific knowledge unavailable to a generic model. But could is not has. The pilots use selected, non-random cases; dataset sufficiency remains unresolved; and no measured advantage separates this record from an auditable feature that Waystar, Silna, or another incumbent could reproduce.

The monopoly problem is more serious. Insurf enters a market where Silna has served 50,000 patients, Anterior reaches organizations covering 50 million lives, and Cohere processes more than 12 million annual requests. Combining authorization and appeals is differentiation, but differentiation is not monopoly. There is no exclusive dataset, dominant specialty beachhead, tenfold technical result, or structural barrier. The dependence on CoverMyMeds and athenaOne interfaces further suggests that Insurf currently occupies a workflow inside other companies’ platforms rather than controlling one.

Chang Lu and Bryan Chung possess a credible division of knowledge: clinical and health-modeling context on one side, computer science, physics, and published technical work on the other. Both attended Brown, but shared attendance does not establish that they have shipped together for years. The dossier also does not establish full-time status, founder ownership, salary, or whether all core engineering is internal. Their student status is not itself adverse evidence, but the foundational alignment gate remains unproved rather than passed.

A plausible distribution route exists: direct pilots with specialty practices, initially preserving existing staff and systems. The buyer and wedge are more concrete than a generic “enterprise healthcare” strategy, yet contract value, pilot conversion, sales-cycle length, implementation cost, and pricing are absent. The inferred software-plus-service model may secure early customers, but it can also turn Insurf into labor-intensive denial management. With a cited global denial-management software market of only $1.32 billion in 2025, the company has not shown how one revenue engine reaches a power-law outcome.

The strongest bull case is that regulation forces electronic workflows just as Insurf begins accumulating the evidentiary record connecting policy language to paid outcomes. That would fit the pattern of an adversarial information system making institutional decisions legible. But none of the known-exception mechanisms is present yet: there is no independently verified technical discontinuity, persistent transaction behavior under hostility, or customer-qualified deployment that overrides the crowded market. Meanwhile, incomplete BAAs, disabled production PHI, unfinished compliance monitoring, interface dependency, and insufficient outcome data must all be resolved before the proposed data advantage compounds. My verifiable flip condition is five paying specialty groups reporting, over at least six months, a reproducible improvement of 30% or more in recovered-denial dollars per staff-hour. Until then, this is a thoughtful workflow in a competitive category, not a demonstrated secret or monopoly. I pass.
