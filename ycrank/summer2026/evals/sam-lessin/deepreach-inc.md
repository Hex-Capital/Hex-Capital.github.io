# DeepReach Inc. -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

DeepReach’s most important metric is not 500,000 clips. It is several million dollars in signed contracts. The distinction matters: clip volume can become metric theater, while contracts show that frontier-model and robotics customers will pay for access to a distributed workflow spanning 150-plus local businesses, 1,000-plus experts, and seven countries. DeepReach appears to own a real dark pool of workplace access rather than merely renting an audience.

The founders plausibly built both sides of that bridge. Tim Li previously operated HireIO to a reported $15 million in annual revenue and assembled Talex.ai’s 70,000-person annotation network across ten countries. Chris Liu brings a computer-vision PhD, Meta ML-systems experience, and model-development work at Aven. Li knows how to organize distributed labor; Liu knows what makes captured data useful. The 475 deployed devices and three months of collection receipts show more than résumé fit.

The bear case still stings. Scale AI already collects more than 1,000 demonstration hours daily, several competitors offer overlapping egocentric data, and NVIDIA’s synthetic-trajectory work could reduce the value of human demonstrations. DeepReach discloses neither data exclusivity nor partner retention, while workplace collection creates consent and confidentiality liabilities across jurisdictions. Its shifting descriptions—evaluation platform, robot deployment, data refinery, then collection network—also raise the possibility that customer demand is real but the durable company boundary remains unsettled.

What keeps me engaged is that the local-operator structure may solve the precise thing centralized competitors struggle to manufacture: diverse, permissioned access to real professional environments. Customer limits on concentration by environment type make that breadth economically relevant. But the same structure can destroy the business if quality control, compliance, and operator payments consume the contract value. Signed revenue demonstrates purchase causation; it does not establish contribution margin, repeat purchasing, customer diversification, or a path that survives without another financing.

The missing deal is therefore not paperwork around the thesis; it is part of the thesis. There is no proposed valuation, competing-term-sheet count, ownership offer, or evidence this is a scarce first check. There is also no named use of proceeds with a deadline and pass/fail result. “Deploy more devices and collect more clips” would be a capital factory. A legitimate venture moment would test whether one additional geography or occupation can produce repeat customer revenue at a specified gross margin while meeting rejection and compliance thresholds.

I am Neutral today, despite unusually strong pre-seed commercial evidence. None of the known-exception logic resolves the omission: hard traction can justify an expensive or imperfect deal, but only after the actual price and capital experiment are known. My one verifiable flip condition is a financing plan at no more than a $10 million post-money valuation that commits this round to proving at least 40% gross margin on renewed customer contracts by June 2027. Without that, I may believe DeepReach becomes a substantial data supplier while still declining to buy the security offered.
