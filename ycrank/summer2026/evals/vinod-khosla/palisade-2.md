# Palisade -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Palisade promises to compress OS-vulnerability mitigation from weeks to minutes across thousands of endpoints. That is useful leverage, but the dossier never converts it into the consequence-of-success case: breach losses avoided, security labor displaced, systems protected, or an expert workflow made 100x cheaper. A $5.74 billion cloud-workload-protection market growing toward $25.51 billion is a market proxy, not evidence of societal consequence. The upside may be large; it has not been quantified.

The technical work is the strongest evidence. A live LSM-BPF control plane making syscall-level decisions and enforcing kernel rules is more than an AI interface. Detecting three named Linux privilege-escalation vulnerabilities also establishes a working mechanism. But the claimed discontinuity remains founder-published and unbenchmarked: no detection-rate comparison, remediation-time distribution, false-positive rate, performance overhead, or independent reproduction. Oligo, Upwind, and Aqua already possess overlapping eBPF telemetry and runtime-enforcement primitives. Palisade has demonstrated differentiation in workflow, not yet a defensible 5–10x technical advantage.

The largest kill risk is not whether a model can generate a kernel rule. It is whether that rule can be pushed across a production fleet without blocking a legitimate process and causing the outage the security team was hired to prevent. The cheapest decisive experiment is straightforward: run Palisade in shadow mode against known exploits and representative benign workloads, publish the false-block and escape rates, then test automatic rollback on a production-like fleet. The dossier provides no threshold, rollback mechanism, policy-testing system, customer pilot, cost, or stop condition. Three CVE demonstrations spend evidence against detection risk while leaving remediation safety—the more consequential risk—untouched.

Fnu Prince is unusually aligned with the problem: self-reported red-team and blue-team work since 16, operating-systems experience, ML engineering, and a Caltech departure at 18. A one-person team building this control plane is a real technical signal. It is not yet evidence of rapid belief revision or risk-tuned recruiting. There is no documented instance of changing course after contrary data, and no complementary recruit owns enterprise deployment, kernel safety, or security validation.

The strongest bull case is one of the unstated patterns I will fund: a scarce technical workflow becomes a repeatable platform, and ordinary usage produces proprietary mappings between kernel behavior, vulnerabilities, and safe mitigations. The exception case would be an exceptional instigator using a narrow wedge to enter a giant existing value pool. Palisade could become that company. Today, however, it has no disclosed customer telemetry, proprietary dataset, revenue, or structural barrier, while three heavily funded vendors can reproduce much of the sensing substrate. This is still a possible learning loop, not an operating one.

I pass today. This is not the red flag of a generic transformer wrapper; it is the harder problem of insufficient proof around a dangerous control surface. The inferred per-endpoint SaaS model supplies no price, deployment cost, gross-margin evidence, or comparison with human remediation expense, so economic parity is also untested. One condition flips my decision: a paid, named-enterprise pilot covering at least 10,000 endpoints for 90 days that reduces validated vulnerability-to-mitigation time below 15 minutes, causes zero severity-one production interruptions, and demonstrates automatic rollback. That would turn an impressive kernel prototype into evidence that Palisade removes the right risk per dollar.
