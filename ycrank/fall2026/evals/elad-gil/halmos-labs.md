# Halmos Labs -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Halmos Labs sits at a useful market intersection: increasingly available biological models let biotech process-development teams buy computational experiment selection, and the economic variable is how many actual laboratory runs they can eliminate while reaching the same performance. That is a falsifiable proposition. Halmos reports matching a bioprocessing campaign’s best result with **30% fewer evaluations in repeated simulations**. The investment turns on whether that advantage survives laboratory constraints and the cost of reviewing its recommendations.

The constraint problem is consequential. Halmos repeatedly proposed experiments outside a collaboration’s permitted range. A recommendation can be scientifically interesting and operationally unusable. I would measure feasible laboratory runs saved, including experimentalist review time, rather than optimization performance alone. The simulation result provides intermediate evidence for the controlling belief; it does not establish a change in customer economics. Evo 2’s February 2025 release makes more biological modeling components available, but the dossier does not connect that development to measured delivery costs, pricing power, or margins for Halmos.

There is an early demand signal worth separating from those benchmarks. An unnamed customer reportedly confirmed a missing fermentation nutrient variable and revised its experimental plan. That is more informative than a registration or an expression of interest: the output changed a research decision. LU Innovation’s summer 2026 verification funding also created an opportunity for industry testing. Neither establishes recurring demand yet. I give the customer interaction modest credit without treating undisclosed revenue as evidence against a pre-seed company.

The strongest bull case is that Halmos discovers important omitted variables before laboratories spend money optimizing the wrong experiment. That could be more valuable than improving an existing optimization algorithm. Akke’s biochemical research at MIT, AstraZeneca, and Cambridge gives the team relevant scientific context; Löfgren’s quantitative modeling and LLM-security work fits its verification approach. This resembles my broader pattern of funding tools that enable a physical-industry transition. But the research-stage exception requires unusually strong technical evidence and a credible path to financing the remaining proof. Company-reported simulations and retrospective predictions, including the enriched single-patient demonstration, do not yet meet that standard. The biographies also establish expertise, rather than documented prior startup learning or costly personal commitment.

Value capture remains a separate problem. Automatic deductive checks, alternative-method sampling, and human review could become useful infrastructure as models improve. The dossier does not establish retained experimental state, laboratory integrations, or exclusive feedback access that would make the workflow belong to Halmos. Atinary’s August 2026 Bruker collaboration illustrates the competitive importance of connecting optimization to instruments. My red flag about software attached to unchanged physical economics therefore remains unresolved: Halmos has shown promising computational assistance, while the recurring workflow and resulting economics remain unproven.

**I would pass on a personal check today.** The market-variable score clears my first gate, but the evidence for an economic step change leaves the combined market gate short. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an independently documented, paid prospective bioprocessing deployment that reproduces roughly the reported 30% reduction in feasible wet-lab experiments at matched performance, includes review costs, and leads the customer to purchase another campaign. That would connect the technical result to customer economics and repeat demand.
