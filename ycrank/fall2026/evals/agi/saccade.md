# Saccade -- AGI Evaluation

**Action:** Pass  
**Evidence confidence:** Low

### Company case

Saccade has no supported building-blocks path. Its machine learning analyzes eye movements recorded during five guided VR tasks. The headset serves that assessment workflow, and the memo identifies no AI workload whose deployment is constrained by an input Saccade supplies. (The Idea)

The industry-enabled path also scores zero. The memo documents a gap in clinical testing options for adults without cognitive symptoms, including an indication for Roche’s blood test that covers symptomatic patients. Saccade’s terms say its investigational assessment lacks validation for individual risk prediction. Automated scoring could reduce specialist interpretation time, but the memo provides no manual labor baseline, measured savings, or evidence that delivery cost kept this group from getting a clinically useful assessment. Acuity’s listed $897 quarterly price includes a headset exam, blood work, and physician consultation. Whether machine learning makes repeat assessment economically viable remains unestablished. (The Idea, Key Risks)

The company-reported patent-pending attachment and proprietary scoring model offer limited potential protection. ViewMind’s similar use of VR, eye tracking, and machine learning weakens the case for durable control. Company-reported clinic testing and a defined research study provide an early trial signal. Completed enrollment, paid conversion, and repeat use remain unknown. A documented working headset and the founder’s medical-device design experience support prototype execution, with dependable clinical delivery still ahead. The list price gives a revenue reference, while local staffing, blood work, equipment, and clinician time leave contribution per completed visit unquantified. (Defensibility, Market & Traction, Founders & Team, The Idea, Key Facts, Key Risks)

### Countercase

If guided VR and automated scoring can produce a clinically useful result with materially less specialist time, quarterly assessment could become viable for adults who currently have few presymptomatic testing options. Even with comparable models widely available, patients would still need eye-movement capture and, for Acuity’s bundled service, blood collection and clinician follow-up. The company-reported clinic testing and documented headset make that workflow plausible. Validation and the cost per clinically useful result remain open, so this case does not clear the outreach gate. (The Idea, Defensibility, Market & Traction, Key Risks)

### Decisive diligence question

Can a prospective paid pilot show that automated scoring makes quarterly assessment viable for adults without cognitive symptoms who previously lacked an affordable option? A result that meets a prespecified clinical usefulness benchmark, shows lower fully loaded cost per useful result than a specialist-led assessment, and records repeat paid visits at a price that covers delivery costs would change the action to Reach out.
