# Superserve -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Superserve reports more than 100,000 machines running weekly. What am I being offered ownership of, at what price? The dossier discloses Open Core Ventures backing but no proposed post-money valuation, competing offers, or current financing terms. That establishes neither scarce capital nor an auction. It also names no funded experiment with a metric and deadline. These are missing underwriting inputs, not evidence against a three-person pre-seed team. But I cannot turn functioning infrastructure into an attractive personal investment by assuming the deal.

The strongest reason to keep looking is that someone already uses the product. Lindy and YC are named users; full-memory snapshots, persistent forks, and host-scoped credential brokering address concrete operating problems. This is more substantial than an AI application waiting for models to become reliable. Superserve fits my translation-layer pattern: let agent builders operate persistent computers without becoming infrastructure operators. Pavitra Bhalla’s Dosu and LTSE experience gives that bridge credible domain context. The biography, however, supplies roles rather than specific prior deployments or operating results demonstrating mastery of both sides.

The bull case stings because persistent execution state could become the asset customers cannot casually move. If Lindy builds long-running workflows around Superserve’s snapshots and credential configurations, the team might acquire durable access while competitors compete over provisioning speed. Weekly machine activity is affirmative evidence of use, so it deserves credit beyond repository stars. It does not establish that proprietary access caused purchases or that customers remain after introductory credits expire. E2B already preserves memory across pauses, and Modal offers VM sandboxes. Superserve’s unrestricted session duration is a useful distinction; the dossier does not yet demonstrate that it creates customer dependence.

Now delete the next financing. Superserve charges $0.0504 per vCPU-hour and $0.0162 per GiB-hour, with Pro starting at $1,000 monthly billed annually. Those are prices, not receipts or margins. One hundred thousand short, subsidized machine runs could produce very different economics from fewer long-running paid workloads. Without duration, realized collections, infrastructure costs, or burn, neither profitability nor a capital-intensive financing treadmill is established. The relevant red flag is feature convergence: competitors already supply overlapping capabilities. I would not additionally invent an auction, negative margins, or dependence on disappearing model errors.

I pass today. The documented product and named users make this worth tracking, but the evidence does not satisfy the exceptions that could override missing deal and capital-plan gates: demonstrated paid conversion, durable cash economics, or unusually strong proprietary access. My single condition for reopening diligence is a customer-cohort ledger showing three consecutive months of positive contribution profit from the same named customers, counting collections after credits and deducting compute, storage, networking, and direct support costs. That would establish whether Superserve’s activity is becoming a business its customers finance.
