# Vorelios -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine an aerospace engineer changing a wing and watching its aerodynamic consequences appear while her hand is still on the mouse. Vorelios offers a small glimpse of that world. But getting there requires predictions that remain trustworthy on unfamiliar geometries, followed by integration into actual engineering decisions. Expanding into heat transfer and structural mechanics adds another transition. That is my reconstruction of the opportunity; the dossier establishes an aerodynamics artifact, not yet a demonstrated path through those transitions.

The artifact deserves attention. Vorelios reports inference in **1.1–31 milliseconds on an H100**, with NASA Common Research Model errors of **6.37% for pressure and 3.81% for skin friction** against solver outputs ([results](https://vorelios.com/results/)). Internally generated simulations and a model predicting physical quantities give the team something technical to control. This fits my pattern of looking beneath a visible application for enabling infrastructure. Still, average agreement with a solver does not tell me where the model fails or whether an engineer can safely trust its advice. I credit the technical layer here; I cannot also count those benchmarks as customer devotion or proof of a six-year future.

Why does an engineer wait hours between questions? Computational expense restricts how many designs she can explore, and Vorelios could return that time and creative control ([website](https://vorelios.com)). That is a concrete operating wound. The dossier does not establish a licensing restriction, an intermediary extracting rent, or the financial value of removing the delay. Nor is speed an uncontested opening: Luminary already offers aircraft-wing models and a model-building platform ([Luminary](https://luminary.ai/resources/luminary-cloud-secures-72m-series-b-to-lead-the-physics-ai-era/)). Vorelios needs to make a particular engineering decision substantially easier, then earn the right to become infrastructure.

Daniel McNeil’s physics background and Krish Kapadia’s **2025–2026 autonomy-lab research** are relevant preparation ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/founders/daniel-mcneil); [TEA Lab](https://tea.ece.ufl.edu/team/)). They do not establish the pre-company experiments, recruiting pull, or sustained obsession that would make me believe these founders will traverse the difficult remaining distance. That evidence is missing, rather than adverse. The named CEO and CTO provide basic role clarity; there is no documented leadership shell game, equal-control deadlock, severe privacy problem, or dependence on one closed model supplier. I would not manufacture a red flag from a sparse biography.

The strongest argument against passing is that the enabling layer may already be taking shape before customers know how to buy it. Vorelios has published technical results, and generating its own training simulations could support continued improvement. My pre-revenue **CosmicBrain AI** investment is a relevant exception: a working technical artifact can justify an early check. The material difference was its founder’s lifelong building record and signed industrial proofs of concept. Vorelios has neither documented here. No revenue is required to interest me, but a benchmark cannot substitute for evidence that someone repeatedly changes their work around the product.

I pass with my own money today. The technical promise is stronger than the evidence of founder obsession or user behavior, and those are essential parts of this particular bet. My verifiable flip condition is **a named aerospace customer completing a paid design cycle in which engineers repeatedly use Vorelios to choose unfamiliar geometries, with subsequent solver checks meeting tolerances agreed before the trial**. That would show the milliseconds buying real engineering freedom.
