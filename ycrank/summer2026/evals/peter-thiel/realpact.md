# RealPact -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

RealPact’s contested truth is that brokerage contracts are not fundamentally documents; they are deterministic data-assembly workflows that modern models can compress from 30–40 minutes to under five. The falsifying event is straightforward: agents still spend comparable time verifying and correcting the output because public records, MLS data, and state forms cannot be reconciled reliably. RealPact has shipped the proposition into 10+ brokerages, including a firm-wide Four Seasons Sotheby’s pilot, but it has published neither accuracy results nor measured time savings. This is a testable secret, but not yet a proved one—and “AI can automate paperwork” is closer to consensus than contrarian truth.

The founders are the strongest evidence. Ranvir Deshmukh and Erik Peterson previously built CourseMe together, reportedly reaching 4,000 monthly users and 90% of Dartmouth students before an acquisition. They then evolved SignPact into RealPact rather than meeting merely to apply to YC. Their current-product sales and brokerage research show more commitment than a synthetic founder pairing. Yet the dossier does not establish the third team member’s role, whether every founder is full-time, or their ownership and economic exposure. The foundation looks coherent, not fully verified.

The decisive weakness is monopoly. New Hampshire is a legitimate narrow beachhead, and state-specific forms plus registry integrations could make it inconvenient to dislodge RealPact. But inconvenience is not yet a mechanism for retaining value. No proprietary data, exclusive integration, dominant local share, exceptional accuracy result, or network effect is documented. SkySlope already distributes overlapping AI automation to 900,000 professionals across 3 million annual transactions, while general language, vision, and voice models can reproduce the central workflow. The red flag is not that competition may arrive; it is that competition is already installed.

The distribution evidence is better than the technology moat. RealPact has named brokerage customers, a firm-wide pilot, and current sales rather than merely claiming “enterprise GTM.” But no buyer title, sales-cycle length, conversion rate, price, or renewal evidence connects acquisition cost to contract value. The subscription engine is intelligible—agent-count and transaction-volume pricing—but the disclosed $515.8 million global category is too small to support a power-law outcome without a credible expansion from contract preparation into a much larger brokerage operating system. Calling the product an OS does not create that expansion.

The strongest opposing case is that the team has already demonstrated unusually fast distribution learning: CourseMe achieved campus saturation, SignPact became RealPact, and 10+ brokerages adopted the new product before meaningful institutional scale. State-by-state complexity could become accumulated proprietary infrastructure, while incumbent breadth could leave a focused entrant room to dominate contract initiation. That bull case would matter. But the unstated pattern I favor is an operating thesis assembled around proprietary access or deep operator knowledge; neither founder has documented real-estate operating experience, and the integrations remain inferred rather than exclusive. The known-exception override for a verified zero-to-one technical event also fails: under-five-minute generation is a company assertion, not an independently measured breakthrough.

My 36-month base case is that RealPact reaches roughly $1–3 million in ARR from regional brokerages by August 2029 but is acquired by SkySlope rather than becoming an independent brokerage operating system. That is a respectable software outcome, not the power-law result required for my own money. I pass. The verifiable condition that would flip the decision is documented processing of more than 50% of New Hampshire brokerage purchase contracts; that would convert a convenient geographic wedge into observable market domination.
