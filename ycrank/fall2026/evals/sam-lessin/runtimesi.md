# Runtime.si -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Runtime.si has built a way to run more robot tests. The investment question is whether it can own the evidence that makes someone comfortable deploying a robot. Those are different businesses. Nomad validates actions, executes them in simulation, and records task outcomes—a concrete engineering foundation. But an announced collection of 10,000 open-source simulations does not establish scarce access, a purchasing decision, or a reason this company needs my money.

I cannot price the bet: post-money valuation, competing offers, and round size are undisclosed. YC and Pareto backing establish neither an auction nor a cheap entry. The capital plan is equally unestablished. Runtime’s October 15 research reports 152/525 successes without skills versus 283/525 for selected skill cohorts, but explicitly describes a retrospective, in-sample comparison. That is useful research disclosure. It does not tell me what finite experiment this round funds, by when, or which result changes the business’s value. Both financing dimensions receive the missing-evidence defaults.

The strongest case for the founders is that they can connect robotics research with the ugly work of making machines operate. Peng and Zhong have GRASP connections; Ying brings manipulation, manufacturing, and productization experience. The published harness gives that background more substance than academic affiliations alone. Runtime fits my pattern of funding software that reorganizes difficult physical operations. The objection that stings is straightforward: reliable evaluation could become the purchasing bottleneck for every robotics team, and these founders already have a mechanism for producing it. But the dossier does not yet document deployments demonstrating that they can bridge simulation results and physical reliability. I give the team partial credit, not an inferred operating history.

Ownership is the harder problem. NVIDIA’s open tooling puts pressure on the price of simulation capability, while SignalFlag already spans hardware, replay, and simulation. Runtime needs a proprietary relationship between its tests and failures customers actually experience. Its waitlist and research publication show an acquisition channel, without evidence that this channel caused customer action. The Lambda Robotics pilots in older job descriptions cannot supply that evidence for the current product. There is also no published pricing, margin, burn, or runway from which to underwrite a business that survives without another round. Missing economics are not evidence of bad economics; they receive the stated default.

I would pass with my own money. The documented exceptions to my price and capital-plan gates require something compensating: observed enterprise conversion, retention, cash generation, or a contractual structure that reduces repeated financing. Runtime supplies none of those yet. Nor does the dossier establish an auction, dysfunctional founder governance, or an automation margin fantasy; I am not manufacturing red flags from silence. One verifiable condition would reopen diligence: a paid, held-out hardware validation in which a named robotics customer confirms that Nomad identified consequential failures its existing evaluation process missed. That would begin establishing the scarce asset here—trusted deployment evidence—and give us an actual venture moment to price.
