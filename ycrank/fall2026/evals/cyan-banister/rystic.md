# Rystic -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I expect agents to rehearse consequential work before anyone gives them production credentials. Cancel the subscription. Change the permissions. Recover when the payment succeeds but the callback disappears. Rystic offers a small piece of that world today: stateful environments where software can make a mess safely. Getting there requires broader service coverage, faithful failure behavior, and development teams making rehearsal routine. The local binaries establish a credible starting point; the dossier does not yet establish that transition.

Why should testing a payment workflow require waiting on somebody else’s shared environment? Rystic reports that 50 parallel Stripe jobs finished locally in 19 seconds, while the shared sandbox completed four before a 40-minute cutoff. That is a company benchmark, not a universal performance claim, but it identifies a concrete constraint. The scarce resource is access to an external testing system; developers pay through waiting and constrained experimentation. Isolated instances return control over when and how they test. At $89.99 per seat monthly plus metered usage, the economic proposition is understandable if those delays recur.

The strongest fit is underneath the agent excitement. Rystic controls executable service implementations and a pipeline that checks fidelity against live APIs per commit. Payments and workplace collaboration are distinct downstream uses for that capability. I like this concealed-infrastructure pattern: repair an irritating development problem today and potentially supply environments for agent training tomorrow. But the controlled asset is implementation work, not an established exclusive resource. Veris already offers stateful enterprise simulations, including on-premises deployment. Rystic must keep earning its place through fidelity and convenience.

The founders have relevant operating tuition. Evan helped build Roblox’s service mesh; Zachary shipped AI integrations in Gmail and Google Docs. Their relationship dates to professional Magic competition in 2016, including more than a year on the same team. That gives me more substance than two impressive résumés assembled for an AI round, although it does not establish years of independently pursued simulation work or an ability to recruit first believers. Their published limitations matter, too: 99.1% agreement across 1,813 Stripe checks comes with disclosed disagreements and a recommendation to retain real-sandbox smoke tests. These founders acknowledge the hidden complexity rather than claiming they have casually eliminated it.

The case against my hesitation is quite sharp: an unnamed pilot team already found a Slack thread-reading bug its mocks missed. A useful infrastructure company can begin with exactly that sort of mundane discovery. Revenue need not precede my check when a working prototype and exceptional founder evidence already carry the bet. Here, however, the pilot establishes one useful encounter, not repeated dependence, and the biography does not yet supply the exceptional obsession that would override that gap. Missing Slack retries and interactive callbacks also sit precisely where agents need to learn recovery. Trace-derived inputs warrant an explicit custody check; local execution alone does not establish the privacy architecture.

I land at **Neutral** with my own money. Rystic has a credible enabling asset and a specific system constraint to remove, but I cannot yet tell whether users will reorganize their work around it. My verifiable flip condition is three unaffiliated customer teams paying for Rystic and using it as a required predeployment gate for 90 consecutive days. That would demonstrate costly, repeated behavior around the artifact—the evidence currently weakest in an otherwise promising infrastructure bet.
