# Lantern AI -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Thomas Chan has earned the right to have an opinion about recruiting. Lantern AI’s interviewer reached named customers including Crypto.com, Hyatt and Gammon; its interview module reports 100K+ cumulative interviews. Hyatt’s reported threefold increase in weekly screening provides evidence of a useful product. Those operating artifacts matter more than Chan’s consulting résumé or the ambiguous “2M+” sales claim from earlier ventures. Customers requesting upstream sourcing also makes this expansion intelligible: the founders learned where hiring judgment breaks by operating inside the workflow.

The reported **$1M ARR** matters separately. Employers apparently pay for this work, through subscriptions and usage allowances. That is direct value capture at an unusually meaningful scale for pre-seed. But the announcement does not allocate revenue between the interviewer and newer sourcing platform. I cannot transfer the older product’s commercial validation to every new module, or infer attractive margins without pricing and delivery costs.

My hesitation is what Lantern owns after another team reproduces the software. Hiring criteria, interview feedback and decision history are concrete inputs to its Company Hiring Model. Preserving that context could make the product better for an individual employer. The dossier does not establish exclusive rights, a proprietary trained model, measurable learning gains or costly switching. Gem already shares recruiting context across agents; Juicebox adapts sourcing to feedback. Calling the system a model does not establish that its advantage compounds.

There is room to maneuver. Interviewing opens into sourcing, application review and the broader hiring workflow, using the same employer criteria across those tasks. The estimated **$3.62B recruitment-software market in 2026** establishes an existing spending category, although it does not establish Lantern’s attainable share. What remains absent is my preferred mechanism for escaping proportional acquisition effort: one employer joining has not been shown to improve another employer’s results or bring the next employer. Lantern People’s agent connections and free trial are access mechanisms. Neither demonstrates the permissionless contribution or customer-created distribution I repeatedly seek.

The strongest objection to withholding a check is that the founders may already have the important thing: enterprise relationships through which hiring taste becomes a persistent operating asset. A useful interviewer, customer-requested expansion and reported recurring revenue could become the foundation of a much larger company. I make exceptions for capable builders before a user loop emerges. Here, however, capital efficiency is undisclosed, and the proposed durable asset remains unvalidated against competitors with overlapping workflows. The pivot looks responsive to customers; the dossier supplies no evidence of misconduct, artificial fundraising pressure or indiscriminate changes of direction. My concern is structural, rather than a founder veto.

I would hold off on investing. Lantern AI has stronger evidence of specific knowledge and payment than of compounding advantage. **My flip condition is a controlled customer study showing that accumulated hiring history produces a sustained matching-quality advantage over a fresh deployment using the same underlying models.** That would demonstrate something a competitor cannot obtain simply by copying the implementation.
