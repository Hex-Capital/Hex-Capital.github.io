# Shepherd Robotics -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a technician directing several robots through a data-center build, spending her judgment on exceptions instead of routing every wire herself. Shepherd Robotics has picked concrete pieces of that world: terminal insertion, inspection, commissioning. The important transition is from robots that require repetitive surroundings to robots that tolerate changing equipment. Then supervision must become sufficiently infrequent that one technician gains capacity. That is a future I want. The dossier identifies the work, but it does not yet contain the little artifact that tells me this transition has begun. A description of supervised manipulation cannot establish that the supervision burden is shrinking.

The founders give me the strongest reason to keep looking. Feifei Duan deployed factory robots at Chef Robotics and trained transformers at Waymo; David Fang researched robot learning at Dyna and worked on autonomous-driving ML at Cruise. Those are relevant encounters with physical reality. Someone who has deployed a factory robot should know how many things happen between a successful demonstration and a useful machine. But the record supplies little about independently pursued experiments, recruiting complementary believers, or commercial command. I credit the work described, without turning employer names into proof of lifelong obsession.

Why is a scarce technician still doing this task? Shepherd’s answer—variation defeats conventional automation—is more useful than saying manufacturing is broken. But technician scarcity alone does not identify an artificial gatekeeper, its rent, or a transfer of economic control to the worker. The company could release skilled people for better work; it could also require them to babysit another machine. Founder-reported relationships with unnamed hyperscalers and manufacturers do not distinguish those outcomes. Neither the reported $500,000 YC financing nor 425 LinkedIn followers demonstrates a customer repeatedly committing labor, money, or production access.

The strongest argument against passing is that Shepherd Robotics belongs to my recurring pattern of backing the physical infrastructure beneath a fashionable market. Its combination of hardware, models, integration, and support addresses work that additional software cannot perform by itself. My pre-revenue exception for **CosmicBrain AI** shared that physical-automation mechanism, but included a functioning prototype, signed industrial proofs of concept, and a documented long-running founder obsession. Those differences matter here. Shepherd’s dossier does not establish comparable product behavior or customer commitments. Meanwhile, Micropsi already reports manufacturing customers, and Standard Bots reports data-center deployments. The bottleneck is real; Shepherd’s control over it remains unproved.

I appreciate that the team explicitly includes human supervision and exception handling. That acknowledges operating complexity rather than waving it away. The dossier also establishes no severe privacy violation, deceptive leadership arrangement, or dependence on one closed model supplier. My pass rests on the missing affirmative connection between relevant founders and a repeatably useful machine. Modular hardware and model development offer some enabling-layer substance, but no demonstrated scarce asset yet. **My flip condition is one customer-verified, 30-day deployment showing that a technician supervising Shepherd robots completes more accepted wiring work per total labor-hour—including interventions, setup, and rework—than the customer’s manual baseline.** That would give me something small and concrete from the future to hold.
