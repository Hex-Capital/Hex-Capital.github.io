# Ooak Data -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The contested truth is precise: authentic business workflows—not reconstructed applications or expert-written tasks—are the scarce input required to make agents reliable. What would prove it wrong? Models trained on cheaper synthetic environments achieve the same performance on held-out company workflows. Ooak Data has established that laboratories will pay to test this proposition: it reports seven-figure contracts with the two largest AI labs and partnerships with several smaller ones. But purchasing is not proof. The dossier provides no controlled result showing that its licensed operational data produces better agents than Mercor’s replicas or AfterQuery’s real-API environments. This is a plausible secret with commercial validation, not yet a falsified-and-surviving technical secret.

The beachhead is appropriately narrow: frontier laboratories building tool-using agents. Ooak has also assembled the beginnings of a proprietary input—licensed communications, documents, financial data, and cross-tool workflows transformed into anonymized digital twins. Yet licensed is not exclusive, and difficult is not monopolistic. Mercor, Scale AI, and AfterQuery already possess larger networks, customer footprints, and adjacent environment products. The proposed library of more than 300 company ecosystems could become a compounding asset, but in July 2026 it remained a target rather than an observable monopoly mechanism. The difference between a valuable supplier and a last mover is whether customers can reproduce, multi-source, or internalize the input.

The founding structure is competent but insufficiently seasoned. Thomas Aubry brings ML, robotics, and financial-data experience; Grégoire Lamy brings five years of product and operations work at Epsor; Pierre-Louis Vouteau brings distribution experience. Their roles imply full-time commitment, and the core technical work appears internal. But Grégoire joined Pierre-Louis only around mid-2025, Thomas joined later, and the company has already pivoted once. There is no ownership, salary, or secondary-liquidity evidence. This is not an adverse part-time-founder case, but neither is it the long-aligned team organized around a decade-long secret.

The strongest bull case is economic. Seven-figure contracts at pre-seed are unusually informative, direct laboratory sales have already crossed the boundary from channel theory to customer behavior, and one product can serve increasingly capable models across many business functions. Ooak also fits the movement toward a complete AI production stack rather than another superficial application. If authentic workflow data remains scarce, its source-company relationships could become the production infrastructure behind agent training.

The bear case stings because the same economics may reveal a services intermediary rather than a monopoly. Two customers account for the strongest traction, source companies may receive up to $300,000, buyer pricing and gross margins are undisclosed, and acquiring 300 ecosystems in six months requires contracting, anonymization, GDPR compliance, quality control, and delivery to scale together. Six open roles before this process is shown to be repeatable increase that coordination risk. A large lab can internalize the work; a larger vendor can bundle it; a source company can license to several buyers. The known-exception override would require a verified zero-to-one technical result strong enough to outweigh competition. Ooak has not published one.

I would investigate, not invest today. The single flip condition is a controlled held-out benchmark showing that models trained on Ooak’s licensed twins achieve at least twice the successful completion rate of models trained on reconstructed environments, under multi-year exclusive data rights. That would convert contracts into proof and a library into a monopoly mechanism. Without it, Ooak is selling something scarce today without yet proving it can remain scarce tomorrow: promising infrastructure, but not yet a last mover.
