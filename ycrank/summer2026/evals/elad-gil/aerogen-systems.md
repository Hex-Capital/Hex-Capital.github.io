# Aerogen Systems -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: advanced packaging makes parts-per-billion contamination more consequential, semiconductor fabs and equipment OEMs therefore buy control closer to the wafer, and Aerogen Systems becomes large only if eliminating consumables produces a measurable yield or total-cost advantage after qualification. The dossier identifies that general mechanism but not the controlling threshold. A $6.163 billion contamination-control market growing 8.8% annually is relevant; it does not tell me whether Talos saves a fab $100,000 or $10 million per tool-year. Without quantified yield improvement, filter replacement savings, or allowable integration cost, the core belief remains only partially specified.

The technical evidence is the strongest part. Talos recirculates and chemically destroys contaminants inside equipment rather than filtering facility air, reached a company-reported sub-1-ppb VOC concentration and 7N purity in 16 minutes, and combines treatment with onboard monitoring. Multiple hardware generations, two years of NASA-backed work, and more than $500,000 of R&D make this more than a software-aware industrial product. Better sensors, catalysts, and control systems should deepen the product. But the owned workflow is still prospective: no tool-specific adapter, qualification record, automated response loop, or field-performance dataset is documented.

The opposing case is meaningful. Hybrid bonding and finer-pitch packaging could turn molecular contamination from a facilities expense into a yield-critical process variable. If Talos removes recurring media purchases while preventing even a small number of ruined high-value wafers, deployment could expand from one tool to a fleet and then give CleanPipe a reusable distribution channel. Entegris, with $3.197 billion in 2025 sales, Camfil, and Purafil also validate that customers already allocate substantial budgets to this problem. Aerogen would not need to invent the category; it would need to change its economics.

That same incumbent structure is the central bear case. Entegris and Camfil already supply qualified tool-level AMC products, while Aerogen’s only disclosed semiconductor demand signal is an unnamed pilot. The sub-1-ppb test is technical proof, not product-market pull: there is no named fab, paid deployment, repeat usage, revenue, or evidence that contamination destruction improves wafer yield relative to established filters. Tool-by-tool evaluation and qualification may turn nominally non-consumable hardware into a slow, integration-heavy business with unchanged industrial margins. Patent-pending IP and an asserted data moat do not resolve how quickly a well-funded incumbent could reproduce the configuration.

Vogel and Lavery have credible technical fit. They co-authored NASA’s 2024 VOC-removal work, retained the reactor through several applications, and bring aerospace manufacturing, detector engineering, and photocatalytic-reactor experience. That supports execution but does not establish the founder signal I weight here: the dossier documents neither prior startup learning nor a personal sacrifice or need that forced them toward semiconductor customers. The progression from spacecraft purification to industrial ducts and then semiconductor tools may reflect productive search, but it could also mean the market is still being selected around the technology.

I would pass today. The known exceptions for physical companies require an already-visible reconstruction of incumbent economics, and Aerogen has not yet shown that. My single flip condition is a paid, named fab or equipment-OEM rollout across multiple tools with independently measured data showing either a material wafer-yield gain or at least a 30% reduction in total contamination-control cost. That result would identify the controlling variable, validate demand, and show that Talos changes the margin structure rather than merely replacing one filtration component.
