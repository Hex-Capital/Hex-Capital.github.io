# Simulithic -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Simulithic’s founders have shipped something. What I cannot find is the incident that tells me how they behave when shipping becomes difficult. Ryan Ning and Satyam Singh say they encountered release-verification problems at previous employers. Their product now discovers application journeys and compares preview builds with production. But the dossier never connects a particular obstacle to a founder-built workaround and an observable result. That leaves my most important founder test unanswered. Internships at Uber, Shopify, and Ramp cannot answer it.

The software is the strongest evidence. A capture script, CLI, GitHub App, recordings, and documented browser and simulator environments make this more substantial than an application promising autonomous testing. The October 15 report of deployments at several companies supports that conclusion, though it remains a founder claim. The important technical question is whether Simulithic finds consequential bugs that the customer’s existing tests miss. There is no published detection benchmark or customer-verified example. I give credit for a functioning product, with limited confidence in its effectiveness.

The awkward part is that discovering a journey and verifying it are different achievements. Simulithic’s explorer never submits forms, and its agents avoid destructive controls. Those are understandable precautions. They also constrain testing of transactions where correctness can matter most. Repository-based mapping offers another route, but the dossier does not establish how well it closes that gap. The opportunity could lie in doing the tedious work of test accounts, safe state changes, and customer-specific setup. Simply making an agent click around leaves Simulithic exposed to QA Wolf’s overlapping exploration tools and BrowserStack’s existing infrastructure.

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders have already built together and reached developers. GitWrap reportedly attracted 100,000 users. That belongs to their previous project, but it makes the team worth taking seriously. Their application-engineering and agent-research backgrounds also connect them to the problem, and coding agents plausibly increase the burden of verification. This matches my pattern of backing useful tools built by technical founders. It has yet to match the more decisive pattern: developers using a new primitive repeatedly and pulling its development forward. “Several companies” does not establish that. Neither do 1,830 LinkedIn followers. The 2,000-, 8,000-, and 25,000-session plans describe packaging; they establish neither consumption nor payment.

There is some movement in the product direction, from experiment prediction in September-indexed text to verification in the October 2 blog. I cannot tell whether that reflects learning from users or a change in description, so it earns no flexibility premium. Equally, I see no evidence of obstinacy, dependence on a major partner, or premature delegation. The founders’ account of encountering the problem at work deserves partial credit for an organic origin, though it lacks a dated example. The closest known exception is Dropbox: working software can overcome doubts about a crowded category. The material difference is that Simulithic’s documentation does not yet demonstrate an unusually impressive result sufficient to replace the missing founder evidence.

I would pass on the current evidence. The reason is specific: Simulithic has more proof that its founders can build a testing product than that they can overcome the obstacles to making customers depend on it. One verifiable episode could change my decision: a customer confirms that the founders repaired a release-blocking coverage failure within seven days, after which that customer paid and used Simulithic on every release for four weeks. That would connect resistance, learning, and demand in the same observable sequence.
