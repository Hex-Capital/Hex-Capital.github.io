# Dydema -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Dydema’s founders have turned domain knowledge into something usable. Weaver’s EEG/fMRI research and Oosthuizen’s machine-learning work now have a concrete expression: a live classification API and demonstrations of brain-controlled games and scrolling. Their collaboration spans a college neurotechnology experiment and engineering work at Syntra. That earns more attention than neuroscience credentials alone. It establishes that they can build in this domain; it does not yet establish that what they build compounds.

The decisive question is what Dydema owns after another team reproduces Wizard. The current service fits licensed pretrained models and classical methods to labeled EEG trials. Dydema reports collecting recordings, but the dossier establishes neither exclusive reuse rights nor a measured advantage from that corpus. Hardware elsewhere in the stack does not give this company a hardware moat. EMOTIV already combines devices, APIs, and brain-data infrastructure. Against that competitor, convenient classification needs an accumulating asset behind it.

The strongest bull case is that these founders have found the correct bottleneck: insufficient recordings. Their commissioned-data offering could finance the acquisition of an asset that eventually makes every developer’s classifier better. That would fit my preference for infrastructure bottlenecks and systems whose participants create value for one another. Today, however, those are two unproven transitions: recordings becoming defensible property, and one customer’s activity improving another customer’s results. Wizard’s reported 52% zero-shot left-versus-right accuracy across nine datasets, against 50% chance, shows why generalization matters. Withholding that task is sensible technical restraint. It supplies no evidence that the bottleneck has been solved.

Dydema has made monetization concrete: $1 to create a model, $1 per month to host it, and $0.003 per prediction window. Developers can understand what they would buy. Completed purchases and contribution economics remain unknown. The market argument is less concrete. A $2.77 billion BCI estimate includes medical applications that Wizard expressly excludes; it cannot establish accessible demand for this offering. Consumer demonstrations with a theoretical ceiling around 1.3 bits per second leave substantial work between an intriguing interface and a useful replacement for existing controls. The API provides a plausible developer entry point, but the dossier does not identify a supported expansion into adjacent paying demand.

I would pass at 41/100. I can accept missing revenue when a network is already forming, or missing distribution when an exceptional prototype and demonstrated capital efficiency justify the exception. Dydema’s shipped product deserves credit, but neither override is established here. There is no documented integrity violation, artificial financing deadline, or founder instability driving this decision; the concern is the economics of a reproducible service without a demonstrated compounding asset. My single reopening condition is an independently reproduced benchmark showing that additional consented recordings, under Dydema’s exclusive reuse rights, improve held-out-user classification beyond the same pretrained models without those recordings. That would turn the data thesis into evidence.
