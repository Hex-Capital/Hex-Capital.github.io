# Obia -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: a small logistics operator describes what must happen inside a building, and software finds somewhere it can legally and economically happen. That would give an operator considerable freedom. Obia’s dossier establishes a narrower business: AI assembles property research, while brokers conduct tours and negotiate. I can imagine the transitions—reliable permitted-use answers, then feedback from actual approvals, then searches organized around operations rather than listings—but those are my hypotheses. Obia has not yet supplied evidence of that chain. I give the future dimension its missing-evidence default.

Why does finding a warehouse require becoming an amateur zoning researcher? Obia identifies something concrete: operational requirements are scattered across brochures, municipal codes, maps, and broker conversations. Reconciling them could return hours and decision-making power to tenants. But zoning also encodes real constraints; making it searchable does not remove an artificial restriction. And “free to tenants” means landlords pay the brokerage fee, whose rate is undisclosed. I see a useful reduction in information friction, with no demonstrated transfer of the underlying brokerage economics. That earns some credit for agency, without treating every inconvenient process as an extractive gatekeeper.

The strongest argument against passing is Obia’s October 23, 2026 account of work for a logistics operator: zoning screening, combining sites, and comparing projected throughput per dollar of rent. Throughput per dollar is a much more revealing question than square footage. It suggests the team is learning the customer’s actual business, and it fits my unstated preference for unglamorous workflow repair. A four-person brokerage could earn commissions while learning which properties accommodate which operations. But this engagement remains company-reported, the customer is unnamed, and no repeated customer action or completed transaction is disclosed. CoreNet sponsorship puts the team near prospective customers; it does not establish that customers have changed their behavior.

That possible learning loop is also where the investment case currently breaks. Obia reports collecting search requirements, but the dossier establishes neither exclusive data nor control over a scarce technical dependency. My pattern of backing concealed infrastructure would apply if brokerage work produced a difficult-to-recreate operating asset; its possible emergence earns no credit today. Crexi already combines AI research with proprietary transaction data, and Tandem already operates an AI office brokerage. Obia needs to show what compounds through its own work. Its reported logistics analysis is a reason to investigate that question, rather than an answer to it.

Samuel Cho’s Amazon, Lux Labs, and BIASLab background and Jason Henkel’s conversational-AI work provide relevant competence. They do not establish a dated, pre-company obsession with property operations or first-believer recruiting proof, so the founder dimension stays at its default. More consequentially, the website identifies Obia Inc., while the terms identify Henkel as a sole proprietor doing business as Obia; ultimate leadership is also unverified. That is an accountability question, not evidence of a leadership shell game. The dossier establishes neither equal-control deadlock nor severe privacy abuse. I also checked the exceptions that could justify investing before revenue: Obia has not demonstrated the combination of unusual founder depth and technical proof, extraordinary user behavior, or a distinctive technical artifact that would support an override. I would pass with my own money. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an independently confirmed logistics customer returning for a second site because Obia’s accumulated zoning knowledge uncovered a usable property conventional brokers had missed.
