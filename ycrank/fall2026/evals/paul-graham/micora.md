# Micora -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The question I want to ask Micora’s founders is what went wrong at their first installation. What failed, who fixed it, and how long did that take? Akemoto has shipped heat pumps, and Choo has deployed solar and batteries at Tesla. Those are relevant experiences. But the dossier contains no specific obstacle followed by a founder-built workaround. I cannot turn their employment histories into evidence of resourcefulness. That leaves my most important criterion at its missing-evidence default.

Micora has something more substantial than a diagram: it reports deployments on Pacific islands and at manufacturing sites. That deserves provisional technical credit. But the interesting claim is **100 kWp installed autonomously per day**, while the product documentation describes forklift placement ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/micora), [Micora](https://www.micora.com/)). These could describe different steps in the same process. The missing fact is how much work remains between unloading the equipment and supplying electricity. A machine that unfolds panels quickly may leave the expensive part of installation untouched.

The strongest argument for investing is that these founders have an unusually useful combination of experience for discovering that expensive part. Robotics, manufacturing, heat pumps, and solar deployment all involve making physical things work outside a laboratory. Micora also offers purchase and 12- or 24-month leases that include engineering, delivery, assembly, and commissioning ([Order](https://www.micora.com/order)). That puts the company close to the operational work where an overlooked opportunity could live. But the dossier does not establish that a founder encountered this particular problem firsthand and built Micora to solve it. Relevant experience supports the possibility; it does not complete the origin story.

Who wants the crude version badly enough to buy another one? Remote diesel users are a plausible narrow well. The reported installations suggest the team has reached some of them. Yet there is no identified customer, repeat order, payment, or operating history that establishes pull. BoxPower already sells prefabricated microgrids, so Micora must discover which installation burden makes a buyer choose its system ([BoxPower](https://boxpower.io/technology)). Final pricing follows site review. Until a deployment shows the actual labor, delivery time, and customer economics, the configurator establishes an offer rather than a demonstrated purchasing advantage.

Micora fits my pattern of technically experienced teams entering difficult industrial territory. What is missing is the measured proof that makes such a bet compelling. The October 21, 2026 LAKI collaboration concerns planned robotic deployment; it does not validate current installation performance ([Videoland](https://news.videoland.com.tw/article/02faa288-c248-44af-b1ea-7ddbadbccb6f.html)). Nor does collaboration alone establish dependence on a partner. Similarly, four vacancies do not prove premature delegation, and shared schooling does not establish either a durable or fragile founding relationship. There is no documented evidence-driven route change, so that criterion stays at default. Micora’s reported deployments are the best argument for an exception, but they lack the inspectable performance or unmistakable customer pull needed to supply one.

I would pass on the evidence available. The founders may be good enough to build this, but I cannot yet see how they behave when building it becomes difficult. **My flip condition is one customer-confirmed field case documenting a named installation blocker, the founders’ shipped fix and elapsed time, and the resulting measured deployment performance.** That would test the people and the central product claim in the same encounter, with distinct evidence for each.
