# Silica -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Silica’s possible secret is that affordable measurement of torque at the joint output will make robot learning materially better than estimating forces from motor current. That proposition is testable: compare learning performance on the same task with and without Silica’s sensing. But it is my reconstruction. The dossier identifies neither a prevailing belief that named peers reject nor an experiment that establishes the opposing truth. HEBI and Sensodrive already sell torque-sensing products. The investment question begins where “robots need better actuators” stops.

What could Silica own? Robot developers collecting physical-interaction data are a plausible initial customer group. Yet the Q Series has no documented exclusive technology, measured performance advantage, or manufacturing advantage within that group. Its specifications remain design targets. Existing competitors establish that sensing is available; they do not establish that Silica cannot improve it. I therefore leave monopoly potential unproved rather than declare the product a commodity. The distinction matters: an unanswered question deserves diligence, but it cannot supply the reason to invest.

Enrico Milletti’s materials and manufacturing work at Apple, together with his experience as Artefact’s founding engineer, makes the strongest opposing case. An actuator’s advantage could reside in how it is built and calibrated, rather than in the novelty of measuring torque. This fits my preference for financing neglected physical constraints underneath expanding software capabilities. Silica could supply something robot developers repeatedly need. But those credentials establish relevant capability; they do not establish full-time commitment, retained ownership, or control of essential engineering. A solo founder has no co-founder relationship to repair. I would neither penalize that structure nor invent evidence of economic alignment.

The commercial sequence is still prospective. As of October 27, 2026, prototypes are scheduled for December and production for January 2027; opening preorders does not establish that anyone has ordered. Hardware unit sales suggest one coherent revenue engine, but absent prices, gross margins, and customer purchasing volumes, the $18.62 billion category estimate cannot support power-law arithmetic. Founder contact, call booking, and custom inquiries identify an intelligible route to engineering buyers, earning modest distribution credit. They provide no evidence yet that qualification costs and sales cycles fit the eventual contract value.

Silica also lacks the evidence that would justify an exception to these unresolved gates. There is no independently demonstrated technical breakthrough, persistent customer behavior, or qualified production output. The disclosed backers do not establish the proprietary operator network I sometimes favor. Conversely, the dossier documents no part-time founder, investor-dominated capitalization, revised foundational claim, or regulatory obstruction. The compressed manufacturing schedule raises an execution question; it does not prove the elaborate coordination failure that would warrant an automatic rejection.

I pass on the current evidence. The strongest bull case is that Milletti can turn manufacturing knowledge into an actuator whose economics make direct force measurement practical for a much larger population of robots. That would change the business substantially. My verifiable flip condition is a named robot developer placing a paid production order after a buyer-run comparison demonstrates materially better learning performance per dollar than available torque-sensing alternatives. That event would move Silica into serious investigation. Today, the dossier establishes a relevant engineering problem and a credible builder, while leaving the secret and the means of capturing its value unresolved.
