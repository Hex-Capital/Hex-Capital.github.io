# Epsilab -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine agents handing work to one another, recovering from interruptions, and finishing assignments that take days. Where do they practice making consequential mistakes safely? Epsilab’s proposed answer—executable workplaces with states, rewards, and verifiers—belongs in that world. But the six-year picture is mine. The dossier supplies neither the intervening steps nor a demonstrated artifact showing that future arriving early. I want to distinguish an attractive destination from evidence that this team has found the road.

The founders give me more to hold. Evan Hu shipped talk2arxiv, which has 531 GitHub stars; Hu and Fengjiao Peng previously built Shortbread, where Peng reports $500K revenue and 2 million episodes sold. Those are acts of building and selling, rather than credentials alone. Their existing working relationship also matters. But the dossier does not establish a pre-company obsession with reinforcement-learning environments, or show how experience selling episodes became an independently discovered insight about agent training. I credit demonstrated initiative and commercial experience here; Shortbread’s customers are not evidence that anyone uses Epsilab.

The strongest argument for writing a check is underneath the product description. Versioned environments, trajectory collection, and failure replay could become the workshop that many agent developers need. That fits my unstated pattern of backing concealed infrastructure beneath a visible wave. Epsilab could also learn something valuable from the messy workflows its founders previously operated, although the dossier does not establish that connection. The difficulty is ownership: no exclusive workflow rights, validated verifier advantage, or accumulated failure corpus is documented. HUD already offers an environment marketplace, and Prime Intellect combines environments with training and compute. Epsilab needs a reason those platforms would need its particular workshop.

Why does the customer have to build these environments internally today? Who controls the underlying workflows, and what expense disappears when Epsilab takes over? The dossier leaves those economics open: licensing and commissioned development are inferred business models, with no public pricing or quantified burden. There is also no documented completed user action through Epsilab’s product. YC backing and 46 LinkedIn followers cannot answer that question. I am comfortable funding before revenue, but my known pre-revenue exception rests on a functioning prototype and concrete counterparties; neither is established here. The extraordinary-user-behavior exception is likewise unavailable.

I pass at this evidence level. This is a team with credible building history pursuing a potentially important enabling layer, but I cannot yet identify the specific future asset my money would help it own. I see no documented governance deception, severe privacy violation, or dependence on one closed model supplier; those red flags remain unestablished. Workflow-data consent and custody still need an answer before the infrastructure thesis becomes investable. My one verifiable flip condition is a named lab’s repeat-use pilot showing that Epsilab’s rights-cleared environment produces independently measured gains on held-out, long-horizon tasks. That would turn the workshop from a plausible description into something another builder actually needs.
