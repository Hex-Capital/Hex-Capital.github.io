# 0.security -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

By 2032, I can imagine software shipping with a standing adversary attached: every change gets attacked, investigated and repaired before a human security team wakes up. 0.security’s scheduled testing, persistent repository context and remediation pull requests point in that direction. But getting there requires reliable exploit verification, safe remediation and permission to operate continuously inside sensitive systems. The dossier establishes components of that journey; it does not establish that those transitions are happening. That future is my hypothesis, and I cannot give the company credit for my daydream.

Doruk gives me something firmer to hold. Cyber-defense work at Migros, leadership of cyber/autonomous systems for a rover team and a credited JSONata vulnerability report show contact with difficult technical reality. These are better signals than simply announcing an autonomous security startup. Fabian’s institutional-finance background offers a plausible commercial complement. Still, the reported exits have undisclosed terms, and the dossier does not date a sequence of independent security experiments preceding the company. I see relevant builders, with incomplete evidence of the long obsession that would make me their first believer.

Why does a developer have to wait for a periodic penetration test? Which part of that wait buys scarce judgment, and which part is scheduling machinery? Those questions could reveal a lovely business. The $30 monthly entry point and absence of per-seat fees could put security work into more developers’ hands. But 0.security has not yet shown which institutional toll it removes, what that toll costs or whether customers gain meaningful control over remediation. Accessible pricing is an opening, not proof that a broken system has changed.

The strongest argument against passing is that the team has already put something into people’s hands. As of October 27, the harness had 526 stars and 58 forks; a named Spotify engineer described scanning almost 30,000 files in under a minute. Company-reported work with EY and defense organizations could conceal substantial early trust. My pre-revenue exception matters here: a working prototype and exceptional founder evidence can justify a check before sales arrive. But the testimonial establishes a completed scan, not verified fixes or repeated reliance. Forks represent more effort than applause, yet their use is unknown. I would not mistake the software’s claimed reach across three billion devices for customer adoption.

0.security fits my pattern of finding opportunity in unglamorous workflow repair. It has not yet demonstrated the other pattern I prize: controlling the concealed infrastructure beneath a larger wave. A public harness can help distribution, but exclusive technical advantage does not follow from publishing it, and accumulated customer context remains an unverified advantage. Aikido already combines overlapping security workflows. On the red flags, sensitive source-code access deserves attention, while offered own-cloud deployment, air-gapped models, PII filtering and audit logs are concrete architectural responses. The dossier establishes neither a severe privacy violation nor dependence on one closed model supplier. Founder control and ultimate accountability remain unanswered; that is a question, not evidence of a shell game.

I pass with my own money today. The decisive gap is between demonstrated security craft and evidence that this becomes a necessary, continuously trusted layer. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independently referenceable enterprise deployment showing 90 consecutive days of recurring autonomous investigations and customer-accepted, verified remediation pull requests. That would turn “the autonomous cybersecurity team” into observable behavior inside an institution.
