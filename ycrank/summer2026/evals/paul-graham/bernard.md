# Bernard -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what broke. Bernard has built a real system, but the dossier never names an obstacle the founders encountered, what they personally did next, or how quickly the situation changed. Dan Katzman’s operations background and David Goodfellow’s automation experience are relevant credentials, not evidence of resourcefulness. With nine people already on the team, I especially want to know whether the founders still install the product, diagnose failed deployments, and sell reluctant servicers themselves. The evidence does not answer that.

The strongest signal is that a narrow group appears to use the crude product now. Bernard is not offering “AI for home services” in the abstract. It handles appliance-repair intake and pre-diagnosis, is live with large authorized servicers, and reportedly processes thousands of calls daily. Mr. Appliance of West Loop is one named user. These are meaningful pre-seed signals because appliance servicers have an immediate job: identify the machine, symptom, and likely parts before wasting a technician visit. But the claims lack cohort retention, expansion, or revenue figures, so I cannot tell whether customers are pulling Bernard back or merely running pilots.

There is also a functioning technical artifact. The team has deployed calling, scheduling, diagnosis, parts preparation, an Android application with more than 100 downloads, and integrations with Compass, Encompass, Marcone, Early Repair, and Samsung GSPN. That is substantially better than a diagram. Yet the central technical claim remains unmeasured. Bernard publishes no diagnostic-accuracy rate, first-visit completion improvement, dataset size, or reduction in wasted dispatches. A probabilistic recommendation that technicians must verify may still be valuable, but “thousands of calls” measures throughput, not correctness.

The schlep is real. Recording consent, TCPA compliance, parts catalogs, manufacturer systems, and servicer-specific knowledge are exactly the sort of unpleasant details that keep a vertical product from being a weekend project. But the dossier does not show that either founder personally collided with appliance repair before starting Bernard. Katzman worked in benefits operations and Goodfellow in fraud, geo-compliance, and automation. They may be well equipped to execute, but that is different from discovering the problem through their own use. Nor is there evidence that customer observations caused them to discard one route and ship another.

The bull case stings. Large servicers processing thousands of daily calls could produce a proprietary repair-outcome corpus unusually quickly. If those deployments are paid and recurring, Bernard may already have more pull than its 100 Android downloads or 139 LinkedIn followers suggest. This resembles the concrete pre-round proof spike I often find persuasive. But the spike is incomplete: no revenue, retention, accuracy, or outcome improvement is disclosed. Meanwhile ServiceTitan already generated $961 million in FY2026 revenue and can bundle voice intake, dispatch, and existing job data. Bernard’s accumulated-data rights are not yet a moat without evidence that the data measurably improves repairs.

I would not write the check today. The operational gate fails because there is no documented founder response to resistance, and the customer evidence is not yet unmistakable enough to override it. My flip condition is one documented deployment where a named integration or workflow failure blocked rollout, the founders personally shipped a workaround within two weeks, and the customer subsequently renewed or expanded its paid usage. That single sequence would show both the running back and the narrow well. Until then Bernard is a promising vertical product whose users are more legible than its founders’ motion.
