# Nodus Compute -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a small AI team buying a completed computation without caring which machine ran it. Getting there requires portable workloads, dependable recovery, and enough interchangeable supply that moving actually saves money. Nodus Compute is working on pieces of that transition. But that future is my extrapolation: the dossier does not yet show a distinctive behavior emerging that makes Nodus essential to it. Cheaper GPU access is a useful starting point. What does that cheapness let someone do who otherwise could not?

The prepaid GPU commitment is where my curiosity catches. Why should a startup pay for an idle machine while another startup needs one? The commitment holder bears the utilization risk; the provider receives the contracted payment. Nodus proposes to recover some of that stranded value and give buyers more freedom over placement. That is a recognizable system problem. Yet the dossier does not establish how much committed capacity is transferable, what permission providers require, or what sellers actually recover. Published supply prices of $2.60 per H100 SXM hour and $0.29 per RTX 4090 hour exclude Nodus fees, so they cannot establish the customer's final savings or the company's economics.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that the founders have built beyond a routing diagram. Container execution, saved-file restoration, checkpoint integrity checks, and generation fencing address the annoying details that make cheap capacity expensive when jobs fail. Founder-reported pilots with ten startups and savings above 30% suggest someone may already care enough to entrust workloads to the product. I give that evidence credit, with the limitation that the October 2026 claim arrives through a mirrored search snippet without payment status, repeat usage, or a measurement method. Restoring files also leaves customer code responsible for reloading checkpoints; ephemeral workloads are not resumed. I want to know whether customers keep using Nodus after their first interruption.

Kotra and Varshney have relevant exposure, including infrastructure and AI internships and Varshney's Google cost-optimization work. They met at Scale AI and kept discussing GPU-market problems afterward. That supports technical familiarity and a relationship formed before fundraising. It supplies less evidence of independently pursued obsession: the dossier gives me no dated sequence of earlier compute experiments or first believers recruited around them. My exception for a pre-revenue technical company remains available, but it needs unusually strong founder history and prototype validation. These pilots make that case worth investigating; they do not yet settle it.

Nodus fits my pattern of looking beneath the visible AI product for useful infrastructure. The unresolved issue is control. Its recovery software is a real implementation asset, but the dossier demonstrates neither privileged supply nor a workload-learning advantage competitors cannot obtain. Modal already pools clouds, and SF Compute already addresses capacity resale. There is no evidence here of dependence on one closed model supplier, a severe privacy violation, or a founder-control conflict; missing certifications and two co-founder titles do not establish those red flags. I pass with my own money today because the documented advantage remains execution quality in an overlapping market. One result would change my mind: an independently reproducible, customer-confirmed 90-day cohort showing repeat production use and more than 30% lower cost per successfully completed workload after Nodus fees, including interruptions. That would turn the central promise into behavior I could underwrite.
