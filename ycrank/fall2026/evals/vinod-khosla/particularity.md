# Particularity -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Particularity’s consequential possibility is continuous consumer protection: investigate commercial websites before enough people are harmed to generate complaints. Checkout prices and cancellation flows change faster than manual investigations can follow. Automating that work could multiply scarce enforcement expertise. But the dossier gives no baseline for investigator hours, consumer losses, or coverage gained. I can describe a valuable outcome; I cannot yet underwrite an orders-of-magnitude consequence. That distinction matters when I am buying an option with my own money.

The product attacks a real chain of work: navigate, test, preserve evidence, map findings to statutory elements. The weakest link determines the value. Timestamping and hashing can establish that a record remained unchanged; they cannot establish that the interaction proves a violation. Particularity publishes no benchmark for either finding violations or producing usable evidence. Existing agency capabilities matter here: UNCTAD documents the EU eLab’s mystery-shopping and recording tools. The decisive comparison is the cost of a legally useful investigation against that existing workflow. Adjacent compliance vendors’ revenue does not establish agency demand for Particularity.

The strongest case against passing is the founders’ unusually relevant combination. Kumaravel brings Berkeley EECS and consumer-protection experience from the Senate Banking Committee; Bannon brings AWS security and compliance infrastructure experience. They could understand both what an investigator needs to prove and how to preserve the underlying interaction. This fits my unstated preference for converting scarce expert work into a repeatable platform. A regulator-directed product could eventually accumulate adjudicated examples that improve subsequent investigations. But that learning loop is a possibility, not a demonstrated asset. Relevant experience also does not establish fast belief revision or recruiting against a named weakness; I retain the founder criterion’s missing-evidence default.

Particularity’s largest kill risk is whether autonomous investigations produce evidence an agency can act on economically. Its October 15, 2026 launch and requests for introductions to state attorneys general show market entry, not resolution of that uncertainty. The dossier supplies no experiment, acceptance threshold, test budget, or response to failure. Pricing and operating costs are also undisclosed, leaving the economic criterion at its default. None of this makes a two-person pre-seed team unusually deficient. It means my money has no demonstrated risk-removal mechanism to purchase yet. My tolerance for regulatory timelines does not resolve evidence suitability, and I see no basis to accuse this team of premature scaling or an undifferentiated model wrapper.

I pass at 25/100. I checked the exceptions: the dossier establishes neither a quantified giant value pool nor extraordinary founder commitment or technical progress sufficient to override the missing proof. It also establishes no harmful-category or founder-conduct red flag. This is an unproven fit, not an active contradiction. **My flip condition is a completed agency pilot, independently reviewed by its enforcement lawyers, demonstrating at least 10x lower fully loaded cost per accepted investigative package than the agency’s existing process, with comparable detection coverage and evidentiary quality.** That single result would connect the proposed automation to useful enforcement capacity and economic leverage.
