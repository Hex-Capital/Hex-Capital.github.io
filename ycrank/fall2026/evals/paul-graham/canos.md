# Canos -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Canos has built something, but I cannot yet see what the founders did when something broke. That distinction matters. Ming-shan Tsai and Bin Li previously worked together on veterinary AI pathology after meeting in Oxford in 2024. That is useful shared experience. But the dossier contains no specific obstacle, founder-built workaround, and elapsed time. I would leave resourcefulness at its missing-evidence default. Their degrees cannot answer the question that determines whether I write a check.

The strongest evidence is the technical work. Canos reports 341 dogs and 896 plasma sequencing runs across four studies, with 90.6% sensitivity at 95.1% specificity in a 303-dog diagnostic analysis. These are inspectable claims about an implemented model, and Origin is accepting blood draws and processing samples ([Canos Science](https://www.canos.ai/science), [Origin](https://www.canos.ai/origin)). But nested cross-validation on research datasets does not establish that the marketed assay works prospectively on samples arriving from ordinary veterinary practices. I give meaningful, discounted credit for the research and functioning sample workflow. I do not give credit for clinical performance they have yet to demonstrate.

The plausible small beginning is dogs already undergoing cancer treatment. Their owners and veterinarians have a recurring question: is the disease returning? That could produce repeated testing and a useful longitudinal dataset. Yet OncoTrack’s accepted trial participants receive testing free, while Origin’s $699 price establishes an offer rather than a sale. There are no disclosed completed-test counts, returning users, or payments ([OncoTrack](https://www.canos.ai/relapse-monitoring), [Canos](https://www.canos.ai/)). Demand therefore stays at its default. Nor does offering screening, baselining, and monitoring establish that the founders changed direction after learning something; adaptability stays at its default too.

The founders’ previous veterinary diagnostics work gives Canos a credible connection to the problem. Collecting blood through mobile vets, group draws, and nationwide veterinary kits also exposes the operational work a useful product must absorb. That earns modest credit for an informed, difficult wedge, though the dossier does not establish the specific encounter that originated Canos. The strongest case against passing is that this team can combine laboratory work, computational pathology, and veterinary distribution while competitors master only parts of the system. But the prior product’s reported reach into 10,000 clinics belongs to ZYTCA. It proves neither Canos distribution nor Canos demand. And CanCan already offers sequencing-based canine blood testing, so using cfDNA cannot carry the differentiation argument by itself.

Canos partially matches my unstated pattern of technical teams producing numerical proof before asking investors to believe a large story. The missing distinction is independently validated proof. I see no documented partner dependence, premature delegation, or obstinacy; missing evidence does not establish those red flags. I also checked the exceptions: there is no unmistakable user pull, independently validated biological result, or working prototype coupled with exceptional corrective action strong enough to replace the missing founder evidence. I would pass today. One verifiable flip condition would be a dated OncoTrack case in which a specific collection or assay failure prompted a founder-built fix within weeks, followed by the same veterinary practice paying for repeat testing. That would connect resistance, action, and need—the connection this dossier leaves open.
