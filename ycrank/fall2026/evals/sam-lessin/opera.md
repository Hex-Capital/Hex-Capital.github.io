# OpEra -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

OpEra has built an optimization implementation; it has not established what my money buys. Proposed post-money, competing term sheets, and why this check is scarce are all undisclosed. So is the experiment the round finances. I will not infer an auction from YC membership, but I also cannot underwrite a cheap, non-consensus entry from silence. Those two missing pieces matter more to my decision than the adjacent market’s $11.03 billion estimate.

The actual asset is more interesting than the “AI decision engine” description. Rossignol and Chalita worked together on retailer routing at MIT, and Chalita’s public implementation models carrier tariffs and combines heuristic assignment with mathematical optimization. Their backgrounds include McKinsey ML engineering and industrial work at BMW and Sanofi. That is credible evidence of people connecting optimization research with operating problems, beyond two impressive résumés. It earns the strongest credit here. The remaining distinction is consequential: working code using operational data establishes technical capability; it does not establish a production system that planners trust when conditions change.

The strongest argument against passing is that OpEra could own the difficult translation between a business’s actual constraints and an executable plan. Its reported results—38% lower delivery costs, 10% more on-time deliveries, a 10.4% higher sugar-sales premium, and 54% fewer unfulfilled marketplace orders—suggest potentially meaningful customer value. This fits my preference for software reorganizing neglected physical operations. But the retailer result is a historical backtest against nearest-store, single-order dispatch. It does not establish savings against an already optimized incumbent, and none of these reported outcomes establishes a purchase, renewal, or recurring planner usage. Customer benefit and customer conversion are separate underwriting facts.

Who owns the resulting workflow? OpEra proposes connecting WMS, ERP, and Excel data, but customer-specific models become a proprietary dark pool only if the company earns durable access and customers keep using them. Neither exclusive data rights nor retained usage is documented. Meanwhile, OR-Tools supplies available solver components, and FICO acquired Nextmv in May 2026. The defensible work would be capturing operating constraints reliably and maintaining that connection economically. A natural-language interface alone offers little protection. Constraint-translation errors are an unresolved operating risk, although the dossier does not establish that OpEra assumes those errors will disappear.

Delete the next financing and the economics remain unknown: no disclosed pricing, revenue, gross margin, burn, or implementation cost. Bespoke deployment could support a useful, profitable business; it could also consume every dollar of contract value in integration and maintenance. Neither possibility earns affirmative credit. Likewise, a six-month experiment proving repeatable deployment would be a sensible use of proceeds, but it is my proposed experiment, not a capital plan the founders have supplied. The documented exceptions in my framework require conversion, operating economics, acquisition interest, or an explicit alternative financing structure. OpEra’s technical results do not yet supply that override. Its CEO and CTO roles also provide no basis for alleging an equal-founder decision committee.

I pass on the present evidence. The founders’ relevant implementation work makes this worth revisiting, but it cannot carry the price, capital-use, distribution, and cash questions simultaneously. One verifiable event would flip me toward underwriting a check: a named customer renewing a paid production deployment with positive contribution after implementation and ongoing support. That would establish that the operational model causes a purchase and can support itself. I would then have something concrete to price.
