# Dipole Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The fact I need first is missing: what broke, what did the founders do, and what changed because of it? Deepankur Thureja and Gabriele Pasquale have spent roughly a decade in photonics, with ETH and EPFL doctorates, Harvard postdoctoral work, a Nature paper, patents, and scientific prizes. Those facts establish intelligence and technical depth. They do not establish resourcefulness. The dossier names no Dipole obstacle followed by a founder-built workaround, elapsed time, or observable result. I cannot substitute résumés for the running-back test.

The proposed machine is genuinely difficult. Dipole wants large-port optical switching with sub-microsecond reconfiguration and a controller synchronized to GPU workloads. But both the port scale and reconfiguration time are targets. There is no published prototype throughput, insertion loss, reliability result, workload benchmark, or production deployment. Thureja’s ETH-assigned patents concern adjacent photonic research, not a measured Dipole switch. This matters more because Google already built optical circuit switching internally, while nEye, Lumentum, and NVIDIA are supplying overlapping architectures. In this market, an elegant diagram is not yet a technical proof.

Nor is there evidence that a narrow group of users wants the crude version now. A launch-roundup mention and three engineering openings show company formation, not pull. Dipole is soliciting hyperscaler and neocloud design partnerships, but no named design partner, paid pilot, LOI, repeat test, or revenue is documented. The forecast that the optical-circuit-switch market will grow from $560 million in 2026 to $2.52 billion in 2032 does not answer the simpler question: who is waiting for this particular switch? Depending on a large partner to supply both validation and a route into production is a red flag until the founders show that they can create motion themselves.

There is some evidence for the origin of the capability, though not the need. Both founders have lived inside photonics, semiconductor devices, nanofabrication, and quantum materials. They also chose a field full of foundry qualification, yield, integration, and reliability work—the sort of schlep that can hide a valuable company. But the dossier does not show that either founder encountered the data-center networking problem firsthand, and it explicitly lacks prior networking operations or sales experience. There is also no rejected hypothesis or evidence-driven product change, so flexibility remains untested rather than disproven.

The strongest bull case is that two unusually capable experimental physicists have entered a market just as optical networking is moving into AI-cluster procurement. Competitors raising between $81 million and $850 million, plus Lumentum’s hyperscaler wins, show that customers spend real money here. A technical breakthrough could therefore matter enormously. This is also the kind of hard-science team for which a known exception might apply—but such exceptions require the numerical proof to exist. Dipole has not yet supplied it. The team also misses the concrete pre-round proof spike that appears repeatedly in my strongest recent bets.

I am passing now. Dipole fails the operating gate because the dossier proves neither founder action under resistance nor a working artifact or urgent-user pull. This is an evidence judgment, not a claim that the founders cannot build it. One verifiable condition would flip me: a named neocloud running a paid 64-port Dipole deployment for eight consecutive weeks, with independently measured sub-microsecond reconfiguration and higher GPU utilization than its electrical-network baseline.
