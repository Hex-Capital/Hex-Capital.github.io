# Stoa -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Stoa’s public RFQ marketplace turns quote PDFs into structured orders for H100s, B200s, and GB200 NVLs, while Berat Celik and Eren Berke Saglam have no public repositories. That pairing defines Stoa: a schlep-rich market wrapped around a hacker-poor public record. Stoa has found genuinely unpleasant work—KYB, export rules, settlement, inspection, and unreliable counterparties—but has not yet shown the artifact or stunt that proves the founders are animals rather than well-credentialed market designers.

Coinbase (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness because Brian Armstrong ran toward both the software and the compliance grind. Stoa shares the important mechanism: fragmented private quotes, regulated participants, physical delivery, and assets whose condition matters. But Stoa stops precisely where the schlep becomes sharp. Stoa does not inspect hardware, take title, guarantee participants, or assume counterparty disputes. The exchange is not the deepest schlep; surviving the first shipment of defective GPUs is. Stoa therefore sits below Coinbase even though this is its strongest dimension.

Loopt (2005) is the low rung on hacker genealogy: no cited pre-company technical artifact drove the check, and Sam Altman’s animal force had to override weak product timing. Stoa has three impressive technical credentials—Cornell Tech, UIUC computer science, and Dartmouth computer science—but credentials are not hacker tracks. Berat Celik and Eren Berke Saglam have no public repositories, while Kaan Yigit’s GitHub has no disclosed contributions or stars. For pre-startup hacker output, Stoa presents no evidence—applying the absent-evidence default. Stoa also lacks Loopt’s known-exception signal: the dossier supplies no force-of-personality episode capable of overriding that absence.

Stoa’s working RFQ flow earns real credit on resourcefulness because Stoa shipped before accumulating press or disclosed funding. Over the next 18 months, however, resourcefulness will compound only if the founders personally manufacture liquidity: calling dealers, reconciling serial numbers, rescuing failed shipments, and closing the first trades by hand. The dossier reports 146 LinkedIn followers but no completed transaction, participant count, or unscalable revenue stunt. Stoa has built the machine; the animal test is whether the founders drag the first hundred trades through it themselves.

Kaan Yigit’s prior work as a quantitative developer on Uniper’s commodities desk is adjacent to Stoa’s market structure, but Stoa provides no evidence that Yigit personally struggled to source physical GPUs or built the product for his own workflow. The organic-idea score would flip upward if Stoa showed that Yigit had previously cleared these trades through spreadsheets and group chats, or that one founder was already a repeat buyer. Without that, Stoa looks discovered through analogy—GPUs should trade like commodities—rather than through a Dropbox-like forgotten-USB moment. That is a meaningful distinction.

Stripe (2010) had two brothers who had already built Auctomatic together; the relationship itself was part of the evidence. Stoa has the preferred number—three founders—and useful coverage across software, commodities, and securities registrations. But Stoa discloses no prior working relationship among Celik, Yigit, and Saglam. A trio assembled around an attractive market can fragment when the first year becomes manual brokerage rather than software. Stoa gets credit for configuration, not yet for cohesion.

Compute Exchange’s July 2026 physical-GPU marketplace launch makes my 36-month forecast concrete: by July 2029, Stoa will have stopped presenting itself primarily as an open exchange and will operate as a managed GPU RFQ brokerage plus pricing-data API, producing roughly $1–3 million in annualized revenue. Stoa’s strongest feature—the ugly transaction work—pulls it toward services, while Stoa’s weakest feature—the absence of demonstrated hacker output—makes a self-serve liquidity breakthrough less likely. The data product may become useful, but only after brokerage creates the data.

Berat Celik’s fintech experience, Yigit’s commodities background, Saglam’s Series 7 and 63 registrations, and Stoa’s 0% fee for eligible data partners form the strongest opposing case: Stoa could use subsidized order flow to become the Bloomberg terminal for AI hardware. Stoa also fits my unstated preference for regulatory-grey-zone marketplaces, and Airbnb (2009) warns against rejecting an initially awkward market merely because public traction is absent. But Airbnb had cereal boxes; Stoa has 146 followers and no disclosed trade. The bull case wins—and my verdict flips to Invest—if Stoa verifies ten repeat institutional counterparties and at least $10 million of completed hardware volume within twelve months, with the founders personally resolving delivery or inspection failures.
