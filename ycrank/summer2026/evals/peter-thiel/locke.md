# Locke -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Parth Badhwar’s White House, Deloitte policy, campaign, and public-affairs software experience makes **Locke** a regulated-sector insider with no demonstrated monopoly mechanism. That is **Locke’s** signature: unusually credible access to the government-affairs world paired with unusually weak evidence that access compounds into pricing power. A government-adjacent business is not automatically Palantir. Without a secret about why Quorum, FiscalNote, or traditional lobbyists structurally cannot follow, Locke risks becoming a technology-enabled consultancy—valuable perhaps, but not power-law shaped.

**Palantir** is the high rung because government relationships, integration depth, and institutional trust became barriers that ordinary software vendors could not buy quickly. **Locke** shares the regulated terrain: lobbying campaigns, compliance briefs, policymaker communication, public-contract proposals, and Lobbying Disclosure Act obligations all reward specialized knowledge. The analogy breaks where it matters. Locke’s disclosed customers are merely “first few partners,” apparently early-stage companies rather than government agencies, and Locke has disclosed neither contracts nor a proprietary regulatory dataset. Regulation currently governs Locke’s conduct; it does not yet protect Locke from competition.

The generic “AI for X” pass pattern fails because model access diffuses within months while customers retain interchangeable vendors. That failure mechanism appears directly in **Locke**: Quorum already serves more than 2,000 organizations, FiscalNote has $84.1 million in ARR, Plural starts at $59 per month, and Polintel and Sporos also advertise agentic analysis. **Locke** adds human lobbyists and contracting execution, but that is a delivery bundle rather than a structural moat. Established lobbying firms can license models; software incumbents can add services. Competition is for losers, and Locke has described a crowded contest more clearly than an escape from it.

Quorum’s software-only posture gives **Locke** a possible contrarian secret: government affairs may not be a software category at all, but an integrated execution business in which monitoring, stakeholder mapping, lobbying, and procurement must share one operating system. Over 18 months, **Locke** could compound engagement records into proprietary knowledge about which policymakers, messages, and proposal structures produce outcomes. Yet no such dataset is documented, and Locke has not named the institutional reason Quorum or FiscalNote cannot build the same loop. The secret is plausible but still inferred; secrets that founders do not articulate usually do not exist.

Locke’s two active openings—with technical compensation up to $200,000 and 3.00% equity—signal intent to build beyond a two-person service firm, but **Locke** discloses no two-to-five-year technical milestones or architectural commitments. The definite-plan score would flip if Locke committed to a specific progression: dominate one narrow regulatory vertical, encode its stakeholder graph, automate measurable campaign outcomes, and expand only after achieving category control. Today the consultation CTA, growing pipeline, and simultaneous hiring for policy and technical staff describe motion rather than destination. Indefinite optimism often looks busiest just before it discovers that activity is not strategy.

Parth Badhwar is closer to the domain-fit logic behind **Anduril** than to a generic Summer 2026 founder: **Locke** is built by someone who worked inside the White House, Deloitte’s policy team, political campaigns, and Clipbook’s go-to-market operation. The parallel ends at operator scale. Luckey had shipped Oculus to a billion-dollar exit; Badhwar has no disclosed prior exit, scaled product, technical achievement, or co-founder history. Locke therefore earns real founder-market-fit credit, but not the lineage override that rescued Asana or Quora when their monopoly cases were incomplete.

FiscalNote’s $84.1 million ARR suggests that demand is real, so my 36-month forecast is that **Locke** reaches roughly $3–8 million in annualized revenue as a technology-enabled lobbying and government-contracting firm, with revenue remaining materially tied to policy headcount rather than proprietary software. **Locke** may be acquired by Quorum as a services capability or remain independent, but it will not establish monopoly pricing power within that period. The mechanism is straightforward: Badhwar’s relationships win retainers, while the absence of exclusive data or a named incumbent constraint prevents non-linear expansion.

Locke’s strongest bull case is a Palantir-style relationship graph hiding inside a service business: **Locke’s** first few partners seed proprietary records connecting legislation, stakeholders, outreach, proposals, and outcomes, while Badhwar’s White House and Deloitte background accelerates trust. That case stings because regulated workflows often look like consulting before accumulated integration becomes infrastructure. The dossier still gives the bear case priority: no named customers, no revenue, no documented dataset, and at least five AI-enabled competitors already attacking the workflow. I would flip to Invest if Locke verifiably reached $3 million ARR at greater than 70% gross margin while raising prices without customer churn—evidence that Locke’s knowledge compounds faster than its headcount.
