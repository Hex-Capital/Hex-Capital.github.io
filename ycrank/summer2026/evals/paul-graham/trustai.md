# TrustAI -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Hannah Chung and Medha Venkatapathy have built together since meeting at MIT three years ago, but TrustAI contains more evidence of a strong pair than of relentlessly resourceful founders. That is TrustAI’s signature: unusually good founder configuration attached to an unusually thin animal trail. The 51-test product is real work. Yet there is no named customer, revenue stunt, rejection overcome, or unscalable operation showing what Chung and Venkatapathy do when the obvious route stops working. I would keep reading, but I would not yet write the check.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for founder configuration because the Collisons had years of shared history and had already built Auctomatic together. TrustAI has the important first half of that pattern: Chung and Venkatapathy met three years ago, have reportedly built together since freshman year, and cover commercial and technical work. TrustAI diverges where Stripe became unmistakable. “Building together” is vague, while Auctomatic was a shipped company with a $5 million outcome. The pair looks structurally right, but the shared-history claim has not yet been tested by an equally concrete artifact.

**Kiko** (2005) is the low-rung warning on resourcefulness because selling a failed startup on eBay could look like hustle while actually marking retreat from the original problem. TrustAI’s pivot from browser automation to agent governance is not itself bad; animals often change direction. But TrustAI cannot borrow the prior product’s claimed 47-fold speed result, and the dossier names no paid assessment, manual customer installation, or improbable sale after the pivot. The Airbnb exception does not rescue TrustAI: cereal boxes were evidence of determination, not merely a new description of the product.

TrustAI’s 51 preregistered tests, analyst-reviewed reports, ERP permission mapping, and integrations with SAP, Oracle, NetSuite, Jira, and ServiceNow put TrustAI in a genuine schlep zone. Over 18 months, that work could compound because every ugly authorization path and failed control can improve the next assessment. The opposite outcome is equally mechanical: analyst review becomes consulting labor, framework mappings become checklists competitors can copy, and SAP or Oracle absorbs the authorization layer. TrustAI benefits only if the schlep creates reusable knowledge faster than it creates professional-services headcount.

Hannah Chung worked at Virtu Financial, the World Bank, and IFC, while Medha Venkatapathy researched LLM post-training, but none of those facts says either founder personally needed TrustAI. TrustAI’s organic-idea score would flip upward if Chung could name an agent she deployed, the sensitive system it reached, and the control failure that forced her to build the first assessment. Without that story, founder-market fit remains inferred from adjacent credentials. That is better than two MBAs discovering compliance through a market report, but it is not Drew Houston repeatedly losing his USB stick.

**Dropbox** (2007) had a demo that revealed more hacker genealogy than a résumé could: Drew Houston made the artifact, put it before hackers, and let the response expose technical taste. TrustAI has a functioning catalog and sample report, while Venkatapathy’s LLM research and U.S. Physics Team qualification are serious intellectual signals. But TrustAI’s public hacker trail is faint: Chung has no public repositories, and Venkatapathy’s named seismic-isolation repository has zero stars. The unstated pattern therefore cuts against my lean. Technical credentials are not the same as hackers leaving tracks.

Noma Security’s $132 million war chest makes a broad agent-governance platform a difficult place for TrustAI to remain undifferentiated. By July 2029, I predict TrustAI will have narrowed into ERP-specific assessment and compliance services for SAP, Oracle, and ServiceNow deployments, reaching $1 million to $3 million in annual recurring revenue without becoming the default runtime-control layer. TrustAI’s strong pair should survive the pivot, while the missing animal evidence and analyst-heavy workflow make a Stripe-like breakout less likely than a useful, services-assisted security business.

The EU AI Act’s August 2, 2026 duties create the strongest bull case: TrustAI could become the Coinbase-like compliance layer precisely because Noma, WitnessAI, and Zenity emphasize runtime enforcement while TrustAI produces analyst-reviewed evidence. That case stings because unpleasant regulatory work often looks small before it becomes infrastructure. But TrustAI’s sample report says reconstructed results transfer to production only as hypotheses, leaves domain-specific controls unassessed, and discloses no named customer or revenue; inconsistent Conduct AI contract references add avoidable sloppiness in a product selling trust. I would reverse my verdict if TrustAI documented three named enterprises renewing production-agent assessments worth at least $1 million in combined annual recurring revenue.
