# Salem Robotics Inc -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when something broke. Caleb Horan and Janak Panthi have worked on radiation-survey robotics at Los Alamos and UT Austin, but the dossier does not name a facility objection, failed deployment, or integration obstacle followed by a founder-built workaround. That matters more to me than their credentials. Salem Robotics may have resourceful founders, but there is not yet evidence of resourcefulness under commercial resistance.

There is evidence that they are fierce nerds. Panthi’s 2025 closed-chain manipulation planner was tested across 10 tasks on Spot and UR5 and reportedly planned four times faster than the comparison method. It became a public ROS implementation rather than remaining a diagram or proposed milestone ([Janak Panthi/GitHub, Aug 2026](https://github.com/UTNuclearRoboticsPublic/closed-chain-affordance)). This is Salem’s strongest fact: the team built and measured a difficult technical artifact before obtaining customer permission. The BSD license weakens exclusivity, not the proof that they can build.

The idea also arose in the right way. These founders encountered radiation surveys through laboratory and Los Alamos work, and they are attacking the ugly parts: doors, valves, instrument handling, facility mapping, safety verification, and compliance records. That is genuine schlep blindness. But the schlep is also the danger. A planner that succeeds in 10 research tasks is not yet a system a nuclear operator trusts to run prescribed routes and generate regulatory records. Salem claims theoretical safety guarantees without naming an operator or regulator that has accepted them.

The strongest case for investing now is the known-exception pattern represented by Bindwell: unusually young or commercially unproven founders can be rescued by direct problem exposure and numerical technical proof. Salem shares those mechanisms. The material difference is that its measured result has not yet crossed into a customer’s acceptance test, while its core research is reusable by competitors. Energy Robotics, ANYbotics, and Gecko Robotics have raised between $17.9 million and $347 million, so four-times-faster planning matters only if it compresses deployment time or enables a workflow those companies cannot perform.

Who wants the crude product now? The answer is still blurry. Salem reports contract-scoping conversations in nuclear and oil and gas, but no named facility, completed validation, payment, repeat mission, or operator demanding an unplanned workflow ([Launch YC, Aug 2026](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/SLg-salem-robotics-autonomous-robots-for-hazardous-spaces)). The $25,000 accelerator prize, 28 Launch YC votes, and 124 LinkedIn followers are investor legibility, not user pull. The nuclear inspection market may grow from $525.84 million in 2025 to $1.01 billion by 2032, and the broader inspection-robot market is much larger, but market arithmetic cannot substitute for one technician choosing the robot twice.

My 36-month expectation is that Salem remains below $1 million ARR with several validation projects but no repeatable nuclear deployment model. The bear case against that forecast is real: two founders with shared execution history, direct nuclear experience, a working cross-platform artifact, and active scoping conversations could turn one successful facility validation into a valuable installation and compliance substrate. I am not ready to assume that conversion. The verifiable flip condition is a named nuclear operator paying for a validation and then renewing or expanding it after the founders ship a site-specific workaround within four weeks. That single event would show both user pull and the running-back behavior currently missing. Until then, I would not write the check.
