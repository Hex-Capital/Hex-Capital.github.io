# Grip -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Jonas Gruetter’s 12-ton excavator system achieved 70% boulder-removal success, while Grip has disclosed zero customer deployments or revenue. That pairing is Grip’s signature: a technically credible founder-shipping bet with almost no evidence that the market is buying. Grip may be able to build the machine; the unresolved question is whether wet-waste sorting is “just working” commercially or remains a science project searching for a budget.

**Perplexity** is the high rung on founder iteration: Aravind Srinivas repeatedly returned within a week with the discussed product built. Grip has a relevant precursor—Gruetter shipped reinforcement-learning control into field-tested heavy machinery—but Grip has not shown the equivalent customer-feedback loop, deployed prototype, or improving throughput numbers. The shared mechanism is rapid learning from real-world failures; the divergence is that Grip’s successful and failed grasps are still a claimed future dataset rather than evidence of iteration between facility conversations.

**Lyft** represents the low-rung revenue failure mechanism: even substantial usage can be a false signal when the resulting market position lacks durable economics. Grip sits between that pattern and Anduril’s already-compounding contract base, but much closer to the low rung: Grip reports no revenue, paid pilots, signed LOIs, deployments, uptime, or throughput. At pre-seed I do not require scale, but Grip needs one legible instance of buying behavior before I treat operator interest as anything beyond founder-led discovery.

The EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation entered into force in 2025, but Grip’s connection to it is inferred and the cited rules emphasize revised compostability standards rather than requiring facilities to install robotic removal systems. Grip’s catalyst score would flip upward if a named regulation imposed measurable contamination limits, enforcement dates, and capital spending on composting or biogas operators. Without those transmission mechanisms, Grip is skating toward a policy theme rather than a clearly reopened procurement market.

Recycleye has raised $24.6 million, EverestLabs has disclosed $16.1 million, and ZenRobotics has operated since 2007, so Grip does not yet occupy a Stripe-like control point. Over 18 months, Grip’s grasp dataset compounds only if installations generate transferable learning across facilities; otherwise each wet feedstock becomes a new integration project and Grip fights incumbent suppliers site by site. Grip could become an index on automated waste processing, but the current wedge is a narrow machine category inside a waste-sorting-equipment market growing only 7% annually.

**Color Genomics** worked because the difficult underlying capability—sequencing—was already commodity infrastructure, leaving workflow and distribution as the single miracle. Grip does not yet have that architecture: the 2026 GRAB benchmark says cross-category food-waste grasping requires multimodal grippers, while Grip has disclosed neither contamination tolerance nor uptime. Grip therefore needs wet-waste perception and manipulation to work, custom hardware to survive corrosive facilities, and deployment economics to beat established sorting systems; those are multiple partially independent miracles, not merely GTM around a validated capability.

Grip’s absence of deployments combined with Gruetter’s demonstrated robotics shipping leads to a specific 36-month forecast: Grip will pivot from wet organic waste into a more controlled adjacent market such as food-processing or industrial-material handling and will remain below $1 million ARR. Grip’s technical iteration should uncover a tractable manipulation task, but slow facility procurement and feedstock-specific integration will prevent the original wet-waste product from producing a compounding revenue curve.

The 2026 GRAB benchmark also supplies the strongest bull case: wet waste is genuinely unsolved, Gruetter has unusually relevant field-robotics experience, and the EU regulation may create urgency before incumbents adapt their dry-stream systems. Grip could become a Color Genomics-style workflow layer on top of increasingly standard arms, sensors, and models, making custom handling rather than new science the real product. The case still loses today because Grip offers no throughput, uptime, paid deployment, or customer evidence establishing that simplification. Three paid facilities producing at least $500,000 ARR, greater than 90% uptime, and transferable performance gains across distinct feedstocks would flip my verdict.
