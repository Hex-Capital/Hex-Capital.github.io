# Liquid Energy -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Liquid Energy’s decisive question is what its founders have learned by operating compute on intermittent power. Ravikumar has built software at Jelly.fm and Lotus; Khosa signed three brokerages at AJMER. Those are actions, but neither establishes experience delivering solar-powered inference. Sekhon’s listed roles do not establish it either. The dossier gives me no operating artifact that connects this team’s knowledge to NanoDC’s hardest problem: delivering useful compute reliably when generation varies. Founder-specific knowledge remains unproven, rather than disproven.

The strongest case for investing is that Liquid Energy attacks a physical constraint that better software cannot remove. JLL reports grid-connection waits averaging over four years in primary markets; the company advertises first-token delivery within eight weeks. If that difference survives actual deployment, customers could buy years of earlier access to compute. Carrying generation with the module also distinguishes NanoDC from Rune’s use of existing solar facilities. This fits my recent preference for infrastructure bottlenecks. The market opening is credible; the broader modular-data-center estimate of $29.04 billion in 2024 establishes substantial surrounding expenditure, but does not establish how much Liquid Energy can serve or which adjacent buyers its configuration unlocks.

Hardware survives someone copying the code. Whether its economic advantage survives someone assembling competing hardware is the harder test. Liquid Energy describes proprietary modules and cooling, but the dossier identifies no measured cooling advantage, manufacturing yield, exclusive rights, or operating dataset competitors cannot reproduce. Storage sizing and measured uptime are also undisclosed. The company’s terms say specifications remain in development. That is no proof of misconduct, but it prevents me from treating the advertised deployment schedule as demonstrated performance. I cannot capitalize a future manufacturing learning curve before there is evidence that it exists.

Liquid Energy also lacks an observed mechanism through which one customer improves the product or distribution for another. A fleet might eventually generate useful operating knowledge; that is presently a hypothesis. Its direct capacity-inquiry channel establishes a sales route, while prices, completed contracts, revenue, and contribution economics remain unknown. YC participation, Velocity membership since 2024, and 247 LinkedIn followers do not answer those questions. These gaps receive the stated uncertainty defaults. They are not evidence that customers rejected the product or that the economics fail.

My exceptions for prelaunch investments matter here: a demonstrated prototype or unusually revealing founder execution can justify investing before revenue or a user loop. The supplied record establishes neither at that strength. Liquid Energy matches the physical-infrastructure pattern, but not the permissionless contribution or customer-created value patterns that can make a small company compound. There is no documented integrity violation or artificial financing deadline. The older storage-and-waste-heat description also does not establish repeated abandonment; changing direction can reflect learning. My pass rests on unestablished engineering advantage and exact-domain execution, not an invented founder red flag.

I would pass with my own money. The strongest objection is that reliable, rapidly deployable power-plus-compute could become valuable before conventional traction becomes visible. That possibility deserves attention, but the dossier cannot distinguish a proprietary system from an integration project. One verifiable condition would reopen the decision: an independently audited, paid NanoDC deployment demonstrating contracted inference availability and positive contribution margin over 90 consecutive days, with all power and storage costs included. That would connect the claimed hardware to customer value.
