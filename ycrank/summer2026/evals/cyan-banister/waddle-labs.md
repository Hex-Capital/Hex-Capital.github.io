# Waddle Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Waddle Labs ran roughly 1,000 randomized LEGO pick-and-place repetitions overnight, making Waddle Labs a physically legible robotics company whose weakest point is not the artifact but the humans’ unresolved authority over it. I can watch the robot fail, revise, and try again; I cannot tell whether Yiding Song or Hanming Ye makes the final call when the founders disagree. That high-touch, low-tiebreaker pairing is the analytical signature: real “Tiz,” potentially unusual “Biz,” and an unanswered question about who owns the “Rizz.”

Niantic turned observable product behavior into conviction because I had lived inside Ingress before the round found me. Waddle Labs shares that load-bearing mechanism: its agent watches camera feeds, writes control code, tests the result, and produced a policy in about 20 minutes rather than merely describing one in a deck. Waddle Labs diverges from the Niantic rung because the preview API requires customers to supply robots; a normal human cannot experience it within 48 hours, and the 1,000 repetitions are internal rather than independent usage. I can touch the robot, but I cannot yet touch customer love.

HQ Trivia showed what happens when a tactile product temporarily conceals ambiguous founder authority: explosive usage buys time, not a tiebreaker. Waddle Labs names Song and Ye only as “Founder,” says they met at MIT’s Research Science Institute and became Harvard roommates, and supplies no named CEO, operating authority, vesting arrangement, or conflict protocol. Waddle Labs therefore carries the exact failure mechanism I learned not to romanticize. Roommate chemistry is lovely until a model dependency, visa constraint, or product pivot forces one person to choose.

Yiding Song researched in-context program induction while Hanming Ye developed parameter-free adaptation for robot diffusion policies, putting Waddle Labs closer to the Anduril pattern than to credential cosplay on founder-problem fit. Song and Ye did not discover robotics through a TAM slide; their separate technical work converged naturally on Waddle Labs’s control architecture. The gap is biographical depth: Palmer Luckey had already shipped Oculus from years of garage obsession, while Waddle Labs presents six months of robot traces and academic research, not a prior physical product taken to scale. The founders have touched the problem, but I do not yet know whether the problem has marked them.

Physical Intelligence has raised $1.07 billion and Skild AI about $1.7 billion, so Waddle Labs is entering a consensus-hot category through a less consensus-shaped door. Waddle Labs would move into my highest “magically weird” tier if sophisticated robotics investors rejected reusable code-generating control on the specific belief that foundation models must own the entire stack—and external deployments then proved them wrong. No such passes or pricing evidence appears. The shared skill library is clever, but clever inside a fashionable market is not automatically Crusoe-weird.

Waddle Labs compounds its frontier score only if every connected robot contributes reusable skills, intervention traces, and recovery behavior over the next 18 months. Waddle Labs already claims six months of data, yet customers provide the hardware and published systems such as CaP-X and ASPIRE expose overlapping mechanisms. This is a software company standing with both feet in the physical world: more Flock Safety than generic AI wrapper if deployments accumulate, more upstream-model reseller if policy performance continues rising mainly with larger reasoning budgets.

Waddle Labs will, by July 2029, have narrowed from a general robot-control API to one industrial workflow—most plausibly machine tending or pick-and-place—and reached $1 million to $3 million ARR from fewer than 20 deployments. Waddle Labs has the tactile iteration loop to discover a valuable wedge, but ambiguous leadership and dependence on externally supplied robots make broad horizontal control unlikely. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing: the preview API is probably not the business; the overnight failure-and-revision loop is.

Hanming Ye’s NeurIPS robotics work, Song’s program-induction research, and 1,000 autonomous repetitions make the strongest bear case sting: Waddle Labs may be two brilliant researchers commercializing a published technique that Physical Intelligence, Skild AI, robot manufacturers, or frontier-model vendors can absorb before a moat forms. Waddle Labs also has two founders with no CEO while both remain subject to international-student course-load and work restrictions. The internal robot evidence still wins narrowly because working physical loops expose proprietary failure data that papers do not—but I would flip to Pass if Waddle Labs has neither named a controlling CEO nor established three repeat external deployments by January 2027.
