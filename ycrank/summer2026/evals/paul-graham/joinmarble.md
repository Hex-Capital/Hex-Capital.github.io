# Marble -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when Marble first failed inside a restaurant. Six named operators are live, but the dossier never identifies a bad count, rejected integration, reluctant manager, or broken ordering workflow and then shows what Khanna and Chaliha shipped in response. That leaves the most important founder test unanswered. Their ten-year friendship and relevant résumés are favorable, but credentials are not evidence of resourcefulness under resistance.

The product itself is considerably stronger evidence. Marble has a functioning mobile system using computer vision and voice input across A&W Restaurants, The Kati Roll Company, Sophie’s Cuban, Kokomo NYC, HolyShred, and Dead Letter No. 9. Its claimed 99% counting accuracy and 97% forecasting accuracy are measured technical assertions, and one customer reports 40% less food waste, eliminated stockouts, and more than 30 manager hours saved each week. But all three figures are company-reported, without sample sizes, error distributions, or independent verification. This is a real artifact before permission, not yet a conclusive technical proof.

The narrow well is multi-unit restaurant operators still running inventory through spreadsheets, clipboards, and manual counts. These users can tolerate a crude interface if it stops stockouts and saves a manager most of a workweek. Named deployments and quantified outcomes are meaningful pre-seed pull. What is missing is evidence that locations repeatedly use Marble, pay for it, expand it, or refer peers without a $500-or-Oura-Ring incentive. A testimonial proves value at one moment; retention proves that the product has become part of the restaurant.

Khanna’s family restaurant experience is the strongest part of the case. He managed multi-unit restaurants before covering the sector at Goldman Sachs, while Chaliha built AI/ML systems for electronic trading at Bloomberg. They encountered both sides of the problem: ugly physical operations and difficult software. Counting heterogeneous inventory, integrating POS and vendors, controlling orders, reconciling invoices, and producing schedules form a genuine schlep-heavy wedge. The same burden is also the danger. Restaurant365 already serves more than 50,000 locations with over 100 POS connections, and Nory has raised $62.6 million around substantially overlapping automation. Marble must turn its location-specific corrections into an advantage faster than incumbents can copy its multimodal capture.

The bear case stings because Marble resembles a favorable unstated pattern: technical founders have produced numerical proof and deployed it before a round. Yet the proof is not independently measured, and the claimed learning substrate has no disclosed dataset size, retention, or cross-customer benefit. The ordinary exception rule cannot rescue the missing founder evidence: that would require unmistakable recurring pull or independently validated proof, neither of which is in the dossier. Nor is there evidence of an evidence-driven product-route change; the Truffle-to-Marble rebrand is a name change, not a running-back move.

I would not write the check yet. This is not because the market is crowded; good founders can run through crowded markets. It is because I cannot yet see how these founders behave when the restaurant fights back. There is no disclosed dependence on a major partner, premature sales hiring, or ceremonial launch, so the standard red flags are mostly absent. The verifiable flip condition is simple: show one named deployment where an integration or counting failure threatened adoption, the founders personally shipped a workaround within weeks, and the customer subsequently expanded paid recurring use to additional locations. That would satisfy the founder gate and convert today’s promising product evidence into an investable proof.
