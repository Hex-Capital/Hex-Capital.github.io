# Callbook AI -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. The interesting future is not simply that machines place collection calls; it is that credit obligations become continuously renegotiable, with software choosing the right channel, timing, and resolution for each borrower before delinquency hardens. Callbook AI has not mapped that future or the transitions required to reach it. The dossier describes today’s collections category with AI substituted for call-center labor. Its scale proves the present product exists, but it does not yet reveal which new financial behavior becomes possible six years from now.

The founders are the strongest part of this company. Diego Avellaneda brings more than six years of embedded-ML work, two peer-reviewed publications, and a production voicemail detector. Daniel Martinez previously helped build Treble.ai to more than $8 million in ARR. They also worked together on Hadron Lab more than nine years before Callbook. That is not a fashionable category assembled around a fresh model release; it is technical tuition, a durable relationship, and evidence that the team understands enterprise distribution. The CEO and CTO roles are legible, although the ownership split and contractual tiebreaker remain undisclosed.

The working artifact is real. Callbook reports negotiating with more than 100,000 people weekly, managing two million borrowers across 23 million interactions, and recovering over $11 million for customers including Banco W, Sistecrédito, and Aseguradora Solidaria. Borrowers are making payment commitments through the system, which is costly behavior rather than waitlist theater. But every scale figure is company-reported, the measurement periods are missing, and the current site may blend activity from the earlier general-purpose calling product. Even the claimed 100% head-to-head pilot win rate is difficult to interpret without cohort definitions. That caps how much conviction I can extract from otherwise exceptional pre-seed usage.

The broken-system economics are only half exposed. Manual teams leave accounts unworked, and Callbook’s voicemail detector reportedly cut voicemail-processing expenditure by 60%. That identifies waste. It does not identify a protected rent or show that the product transfers meaningful control to borrowers; creditors still define the alternatives, while automation makes their collection machinery cheaper and more persistent. Regulation F’s seven-call presumption and electronic opt-out requirements are therefore central product constraints. At this volume, consent, data custody, and autonomous negotiation errors are gates, not compliance chores, and no independent audit or error rate is public.

The tiny voicemail model is the mundane artifact I keep returning to: it was trained on regional recordings, achieved more than 96% held-out accuracy, and removed an expensive step in a live workflow. The bull case is that Callbook compounds those narrow artifacts into the decisioning and communications substrate for Latin American consumer credit. The bear case stings more than ordinary voice-AI commoditization: Salient, InDebted, and Skit.ai already offer overlapping negotiation and compliance systems, while Callbook has disclosed neither exclusive conversation-data rights nor a learning curve competitors cannot reproduce. Twenty-three million interactions are not automatically a moat.

I expect Callbook AI to reach roughly $8 million to $15 million in ARR by August 2029 as a strong regional collections operator, but I do not yet see evidence that it becomes a defining enabling layer. This is the kind of exception I sometimes make—deep technical history plus a functioning product can outrun an immature future narrative—but I am not writing the check from my own money today. One condition flips me: an independent six-month audit showing that a named creditor’s Callbook cohort produces at least 20% higher net recovery than its incumbent process after complaints, compliance costs, and erroneous commitments are included.
