# Kebra -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Luca Hadife and Mohamed Hosny make Kebra a strong hacker pair working on a problem neither founder is known to have suffered personally. That is Kebra’s signature: good co-founder configuration, weak organic origin. Hadife built multimodal AI systems at Microsoft; Hosny taught Computer Architecture and Signal Processing at Berkeley and worked on data-center switches at Arista. They can build Kebra. The unanswered question is why they had to.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for co-founder configuration: two brothers who had already hacked commerce together before building the developer tool they wanted. Kebra has the right shape—two technical Berkeley graduates with complementary audio, ML, and systems experience—and four overlapping university years are better than founders paired by an accelerator. Kebra diverges where it matters: the dossier names no project Hadife and Hosny built together, no long working relationship, and no evidence that they have already learned how the other behaves when something breaks at 3 a.m.

**Loopt** (2005) is the low rung’s warning: force and technical promise can temporarily override a product whose urgency has not caught up with its builders, but that is a Known Exception, not a pattern to imitate. Kebra’s dossier says no founder worked in field service and provides no evidence that either founder personally reconstructed warranty claims, chased technicians for serial numbers, or used Kebra as a customer. Kebra is not the polished two-MBA AI wrapper from my pass example—the founders are real engineers—but the organic-idea failure mechanism is the same. They appear to have found an industry problem rather than a problem that found them.

Kebra’s 1:24:06 field recording and automated SMS request for a nameplate photograph show more artifact than slideware. Over 18 months, animal resourcefulness would compound if Hadife and Hosny personally install Kebra in unpleasant shops, clean malformed ServiceTitan records, and submit warranty claims by hand until the software learns the workflow. Kebra currently has no reported customer, revenue, signed LOI, or unscalable acquisition stunt. A working demonstration proves the founders can program; it does not yet prove they will behave like animals when technicians ignore the SMS and warranty portals reject the output.

California Penal Code §632, ServiceTitan interfaces, QuickBooks records, supplier systems, warranty portals, Gmail, and Messages make Kebra unusually schlep-heavy for an AI application. That is attractive. Kebra could move up a full tier if the consent flow, technician follow-up, and portal submissions are already operating across several paying contractors, because each ugly exception would become accumulated knowledge rather than another API call. If Kebra merely transcribes recordings and hands structured text to systems controlled by ServiceTitan, the schlep belongs to the customer while Kebra remains a replaceable feature.

**Dropbox** (2007) turned hacker genealogy into evidence with Drew Houston’s functioning demo on Hacker News. Kebra has credible technical ancestry—Microsoft multimodal AI, Berkeley EECS teaching, signal processing, computer architecture, and Arista switching—but Kebra has no public repositories, competitive-programming record, hacker-community trail, or named pre-startup artifact. The credentials raise the probability that the product works. They do not carry the conviction of seeing an odd, useful thing the founders made before anyone was watching.

ServiceTitan’s $961.0 million FY2026 revenue makes my 36-month prediction straightforward: by July 2029, Kebra will have narrowed from a general “AI-native” field-service back office to warranty-claim and equipment-documentation automation, while remaining below $3 million in annual recurring revenue. Kebra’s capable pair should find a technically defensible subproblem, but the weak organic origin means Kebra will probably discover the real pain through selling rather than starting with it. ServiceTitan’s overlapping AI products will force that narrowing.

Rilla’s estimated $70 million in annual recurring revenue is the strongest case against that forecast: recording field conversations can become a large business, and Kebra attacks a nastier workflow than coaching by turning speech into billing, parts, and warranty actions. Kebra could be a small **Coinbase** (2012) pattern—the consent rules and integration grind everyone mistakes for execution risk become the asset. But Coinbase had Armstrong’s rejected application, hand-built wallet, and visible appetite for compliance; Kebra has no equivalent animal track and no reported usage. I would flip to Invest if Kebra showed 20 paying field-service businesses using it on most weekly jobs, with completed warranty claims or invoices measurably increasing after deployment.
