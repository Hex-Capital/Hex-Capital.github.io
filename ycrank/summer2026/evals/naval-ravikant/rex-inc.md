# Rex -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Rex begins with specific knowledge earned through action. Merlin Kafka spent four years as Sequence’s CPO; Lewis Blackwood was a founding Sequence engineer after engineering at GoCardless. Together they worked across billions in receivables, more than 50 ERP integrations, and hundreds of finance teams before founding Rex in January 2026 ([Rex](https://www.rex.inc/company)). This is not résumé proximity. They built and sold into the exact system they are now replacing.

The product has also crossed from agent theater into financial work. Synthesia deployed Rex in under three weeks, recorded 22,000 autonomous actions in four months, and used it to retrieve more than $40 million in purchase orders across 30–40 procurement portals. Rex now manages more than $500 million in receivables, while follow-up cycles reportedly fell from weeks to hours ([Rex customer story](https://www.rex.inc/customers/synthesia); [Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/rex-inc)). A two-person company executing that volume has found leverage.

The question is what remains after HighRadius or Kolleno copies the visible product. Editable procedures, exception histories, approval decisions, and auditable action records can become proprietary operating context. The 22,000 actions prove that this context is being created, but only one implementation is described publicly. HighRadius has marketed autonomous finance since 2019, Kolleno already offers overlapping agents, and Rex depends on third-party AI and external portals. Code is copyable; customer-specific judgment may not be. Today Rex has evidence of the raw material for a moat, not evidence that the moat compounds.

The initial wedge has room to maneuver. Collections leads naturally into disputes, deductions, cash application, invoice submission, and customer correspondence because the same integrations and procedural engine govern each workflow. The independently estimated AR-automation market was $4.8 billion in 2025, growing at 13.2%, and the broader order-to-cash transaction layer is larger than the first collections use case ([Grand View Research](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/accounts-receivable-automation-market-report)). Outcome- and SLA-based subscriptions create direct value capture, but no contract value, realized revenue, gross margin, or renewal has been disclosed. Managed receivables are throughput, not revenue.

The sharpest weakness is that customers do not yet create value or distribution for one another. Every enterprise appears to require its own integrations, procedures, security review, and sales motion. That misses my strongest unstated pattern: a permissionless contribution loop in which each participant improves the system for the next. None of my known exceptions resolves this—the company already has deployment proof, so prototype ambiguity is not the issue. The bear case is that Rex becomes an excellent, labor-intensive enterprise automation vendor whose learning remains trapped inside each tenant. I find no disclosed integrity, artificial-deadline, founder-instability, or adverse-dealing red flag, but incomplete SOC 2 Type II certification raises friction precisely where financial autonomy requires trust.

I would invest a bounded pre-seed check. The founders and deployment evidence are unusually strong; the durable substrate is merely plausible. My 36-month base case is $10–25 million in ARR from enterprise order-to-cash automation, with growth constrained more by deployments and trust than model capability. One verifiable condition flips me to a pass on follow-on financing: across three named customers, Rex fails to show declining human approvals per 1,000 actions after six months without an increase in financial-action errors. If the system does not learn, it is software-enabled services; if it does, institutional context becomes leverage.
