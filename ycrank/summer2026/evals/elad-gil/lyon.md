# Lyon -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: recent transaction-model advances let financial institutions replace months of task-specific feature engineering, and Lyon becomes large if one private representation can support enough production decisions per customer to overwhelm the cost of training and deploying inside each VPC. The controlling variable is therefore successful adapters per institution—not transactions processed or benchmark accuracy. Lyon has one strong observation: a model trained on 28 billion transactions produced 4× the precision of existing premium-card-conversion rules, followed by a credit deployment at the same fintech.

That expansion makes the evidence unusually informative for pre-seed. The customer serves tens of millions of active users, so this was not a toy dataset, and moving from conversion into credit suggests the representation may travel across tasks. An additional insurer supplies a weak repeatability signal. But every result remains company-reported, both customers are unnamed, and there is no revenue, retention, or production credit-performance disclosure. This is meaningful product pull, not yet product-market fit.

The technical shift is real but the economic step change is still inferred. Research published in 2025 supports reusable models for tabular and transaction data, while Lyon’s customer-specific training, embeddings, and LoRA adapters offer a plausible way to amortize feature engineering across credit, fraud, collections, churn, and cross-sell. No dossier evidence quantifies deployment time, model-training cost, customer savings, or gross margin. Training from scratch inside every VPC may replace repeated modeling work—or merely relocate it into an expensive vendor engagement.

That is the strongest bear case. Customers own the data and trained intelligence, so Lyon has no demonstrated pooled-data flywheel. H2O.ai, Pecan AI, Feedzai, and Taktile already occupy adjacent parts of the stack, with hundreds of millions of dollars in aggregate funding. As the market crystallizes, improved open research could let internal bank teams or existing decision platforms reproduce the modeling layer. If each installation requires bespoke schemas, security work, and retraining, the business inherits services economics while competitors capture the durable decision workflow.

Lyon partly answers this through architecture rather than rhetoric. It operates within customer permissions, retains reusable adapter and deployment tooling, has SOC 2 Type 1 and 35 monitored controls, and is already attempting a second task in one account. Stronger underlying models should improve its system rather than directly erase it. Still, Lyon owns the representation layer, not yet the decisions, actions, feedback loops, or operational system around them. The verifiable flip condition is three financial institutions each running at least three paid production adapters, with deployments completed in under eight weeks. That would demonstrate both workflow expansion and repeatable economics.

Gabriel Noya’s Stanford EE/CS work, NVIDIA experience, and edge-AI optimization win support technical execution, but the dossier documents neither financial-services operating depth, second-time-founder learning, nor costly personal commitment; this dimension stays at its missing-evidence default. I would nevertheless make a small pre-seed investment because the 28-billion-transaction deployment and cross-task expansion carry more weight than founder polish. My 36-month base case is $3–7 million ARR from 6–10 financial institutions, driven primarily by additional adapters sold into existing customers; failure to achieve multi-task expansion would turn this into a pass quickly.
