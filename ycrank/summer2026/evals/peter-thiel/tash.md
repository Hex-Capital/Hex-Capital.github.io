# tash -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

What important truth does tash know? The strongest version is that collectors want diversified exposure to trading cards without performing the work of sourcing, authentication, custody, and resale themselves. But the dossier never shows that peers reject this proposition, nor an experiment that could falsify it. The current product is only a waitlist, and its advertised CL50 history uses interpolated intermediate points while the final Card Ladder series remains unfinished. That is not yet a secret proved against reality; it is a financial wrapper supported by an unresolved performance claim.

The founders are the strongest part of tash. Three met in high school, all four attended Berkeley, and they became roommates rather than assembling for YC. They have collectively traded more than $1 million of cards and collectibles, while the prior exchange recorded over $22,000 of volume across 710 trades and 250 listed cards. This establishes durable alignment, founder-market contact, and some ability to ship together. It does not establish that every founder is full-time, that core engineering is internal, or that they remain economically exposed through meaningful ownership. The foundation appears promising, but the dossier leaves important parts of the foundation unverified.

The business is weaker precisely where the team is stronger. Rally, Collectable, Dibbs, and Alt have already financed variations of fractional ownership, valuation, custody, and trading. A multi-card vehicle differs from a single-card security, but product configuration is not a monopoly mechanism. There is no proprietary technology, exclusive inventory, dominant collector community, unique data set, or demonstrated liquidity loop. The proposed future moat—transaction data, sourcing relationships, performance history, and liquidity—exists only after tash wins the market it is supposed to defend.

The bull case is that the prior exchange activity and the founders’ personal trading history reveal suppressed demand, while Rialto Markets and North Capital supply a regulated route that competitors struggled to construct. Regulatory complexity can occasionally become a barrier rather than merely a burden. But the exception requires persistent customer behavior strong enough to outweigh the legal risk. Here, the index has not launched, SEC qualification remains pending, the acquisition channel is a YC launch and waitlist, and comparable platform Collectable halted secondary trading in 2024. Unlike Polymarket, where users continued transacting despite legal hostility, tash has no current-product transaction behavior to justify overriding the regulatory and liquidity gate.

Nor is the power-law engine visible. The underlying sports-card market was $13.51 billion in 2025, but tash discloses neither fees nor a regulated-index SAM, gross margins, expected assets under management, or the economics of acquiring and storing inventory. Issuance, sourcing, custody, qualification, broker-dealer execution, and secondary liquidity must all work before the managed portfolio becomes compelling. Meanwhile, the stale terms and privacy policy still describe the previous exchange product. This looks less like definite execution toward one dominant engine than a transition between two models.

I would pass. The likely 36-month outcome is a pivot back toward a conventional card marketplace, vault, or portfolio-tracking product after regulated index liquidity proves too thin. One verifiable fact would change the judgment: a qualified offering that exceeds $1 million of investor purchases and then sustains at least $250,000 of monthly secondary volume for six consecutive months without subsidized trading. That would convert the index thesis from financial packaging into evidence of a bounded market with a liquidity mechanism.
