# Baud -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The attractive fact is not a $256.8 billion accelerator forecast. It is that one frontier training recipe already consumes 6,144 H100s, 4.3 MW, and an estimated $44 million in rentals. If Baud can eliminate multiplication from both forward and backward passes without sacrificing model quality, it attacks a real resource bottleneck rather than manufacturing demand. But the dossier never quantifies how much of that power, hardware, or cost disappears. The consequence could be enormous; today it remains an unpriced option.

The technical idea is genuinely non-consensus. Baud changes the arithmetic representation itself, then builds the ASIC, compiler, and distributed-training system around that change. This is more interesting than another accelerator rearranging conventional model math. Yet 1,000-plus tokens per second for a small model on one FPGA is not a 5–10x benchmark. There is no named H100, Groq, Cerebras, Etched, or d-Matrix workload tested under equivalent model quality, latency, batch size, power, and cost. “Bit-exact in most cases” also hides the migration risk inside the phrase “most cases.” Technical novelty earns attention; an unaudited discontinuity does not earn belief.

The team is unusually well composed for this risk. Sarang Zambare has deployed ML across more than 100,000 Peloton devices and worked at Caper before its acquisition; Eric Taylor has four tape-outs and experience at NVIDIA, NXP, Arteris, and Enfabrica. Software representation and silicon execution have named owners. That is risk-tuned recruiting, not three generic AI résumés. What is absent is evidence that the founders revised a consequential belief after a failed experiment, or that they can recruit the compiler, systems, and model-research talent needed to compete with rivals funded at the $450 million-to-multibillion-dollar level.

Baud is spending against the correct uncertainty, but the experiment is underspecified. The FPGA cluster, GlobalFoundries 12nm validation, design-partner program, and planned year-end 2026 tape-out all move toward physical proof. They do not state the decisive threshold: model-quality loss, training convergence, tokens per joule, total cost per trained parameter, yield, or the result that would stop the program. A tape-out is an activity. The useful milestone is a falsifiable comparison showing that multiplication-free training survives silicon and wins economically.

The strongest bull case is that the FPGA result understates a representation-level advantage that compounds when implemented in silicon, and that design partners are already helping Baud find the workloads where it wins. A three-person team with this complementarity could discover the right route faster than heavily capitalized competitors optimizing conventional arithmetic. The bear case still controls my conclusion: Etched reports working silicon and more than $1 billion in contracts, Groq serves millions of developers, and Baud has disclosed neither revenue nor partner count while requiring model migration. This can become important technology without becoming important economics.

I would not pass because pre-seed proof is incomplete; that would confuse uncertainty with failure. I would also not write the check yet because the largest claims—training superiority and cost collapse—remain unbenchmarked. My flip condition is verifiable: first silicon must demonstrate at least a 5x improvement in tokens per joule or total training cost against a named current accelerator on the same model and quality target, with a disclosed path to price parity after fabrication and cluster utilization. Without that, Baud is likely to become a specialized small-model inference product rather than a replacement for frontier training infrastructure.
