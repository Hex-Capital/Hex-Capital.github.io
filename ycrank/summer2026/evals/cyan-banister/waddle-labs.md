# Waddle Labs -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. A factory technician describes a task, an agent watches the cell, writes the control program, tests it, learns from failure, and leaves behind a reusable skill for the next robot. Waddle Labs has a credible leak from that future: six months of real-hardware operation and roughly 1,000 overnight LEGO pick-and-place repetitions. But the dossier does not map the intervening transitions—safe deployment, cross-robot transfer, standardized evaluation, and economically reliable operation. Better foundation models are an input, not a bridge.

The working artifact is the strongest product evidence. It decomposes goals, writes editable code, invokes VLA models, observes execution, and replans. Running a robot unattended a thousand times is more meaningful than a waitlist because physical failures consume time and equipment. Still, this is costly founder behavior, not costly customer behavior. The preview API has no disclosed external deployments, repeat users, paid pilots, or named LOIs, so I cannot tell whether anyone outside Waddle has rearranged a workflow around it.

Yiding Song and Hanming Ye have real technical continuity: program induction, multimodal models, diffusion policies, and six months operating robots together. Their relationship predates the company through MIT’s Research Science Institute, and the Summer 2025 teleoperation product shows they were building before the current agent-control framing. That is meaningful Tizz. The same history creates a diligence problem, however: the product changed, Oscar Melendez disappeared from the listed founding team, and the dossier provides no pivot or governance timeline. Two current founders plus an unexplained former co-founder makes accountable control a question, not a footnote.

The sharp bull case is that these are unusually young technical founders accumulating exactly the asset that could matter: execution traces, intervention data, and a shared skill library beneath the crowded robot-foundation-model race. Pre-revenue did not prevent CosmicBrain AI from qualifying when its prototype was paired with signed industrial proofs of concept and a founder’s lifelong robotics record. Waddle has the prototype mechanism, but not those two corroborating facts. Its evidence remains internally generated, while public code-as-policy systems such as CaP-X and ASPIRE reduce the architectural novelty.

I also cannot yet find the broken rule. Robotics programming is brittle and recollecting data is expensive, but Waddle does not quantify that rent, identify who captures it, or show whose occupational control returns when the API works. Meanwhile, policy quality and task cost rise with third-party model capability and reasoning budget. That is an enabling layer built partly on someone else’s controlled layer, with neither safety interlocks nor a demonstrated proprietary constraint. The accumulated traces could reverse this dependency; today they have not.

My 36-month base case is that Waddle pivots toward robot data, evaluation, or teleoperation infrastructure before reaching $1 million ARR from its general agent-control API. I am not writing a check today. One verifiable event flips me: a named industrial customer repeatedly deploying Waddle across at least two robot types, with documented failure recovery and paid expansion. Before investing, I would also require the founder departure, equity control, execution safeguards, and ultimate decision-maker to be made explicit.
