# Nex -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders have earned the right to attack this problem. Najmuzzaman Mohammad shipped a ChatGPT plugin used by 48,000 people and worked in HubSpot product; Francisco Dias was HubSpot CRM’s fourth engineer and later built AI infrastructure there. Their discontinued CRM reportedly produced revenue and enterprise proofs of concept. Credentials describe proximity. Shipping the CRM, WUPHF, and the plugin demonstrates specific knowledge acquired through action.

The problem is durability. Nex converts natural language into code-backed workflows across more than 1,000 integrations, but Clay, Gumloop, and Relevance AI already combine models, organizational data, integrations, and workflow runtimes. If a competitor copies the conversational interface and workflow generation, SOC 2 Type 1, RBAC, and integration count remain useful enterprise infrastructure—not a compounding advantage. The customer-specific context graph could become scarce data, but the dossier provides no retention, workflow-volume, or decision-reuse evidence showing that it already does.

The market is large enough to support a company, but not yet legible as an outlier. AI sales-assistant software is projected to grow from $3.01 billion in 2025 to $18.18 billion by 2033. Nex charges $100 per seat per month and offers custom Team pricing, so direct monetization is designed into the product. But a price is not price discovery: there is no disclosed payer count, current revenue, renewal behavior, or unit economics. Clay’s $100 million ARR proves demand while also proving that Nex is entering an occupied market rather than creating a new one.

The permissionless signal is WUPHF: 1,200 GitHub stars, 94 forks, and a Show HN launch with 260 points and 115 comments. This fits the pattern of turning individual operational knowledge into scalable output. It does not yet create a network. A fork does not necessarily improve the product for another customer, and every new deployment may still require equivalent onboarding, integrations, and support. The earlier agency offer of 70% of revenue could distribute Nex, but there is no evidence of partner-created liquidity or reusable customer contributions.

The strongest bull case is that exceptional product builders can outrun an initially weak network loop. Nex has already moved through an AI-native CRM, context infrastructure, and WUPHF to find a sharper GTM wedge; perhaps this is disciplined updating rather than drift. I see no evidence of compromised integrity, artificial financing pressure, or founders seeking reassurance. But the sequence of three positions in roughly six months is the closest red flag: it has not yet shown that learning compounds faster than positioning changes. The known-exception analogy is Notion—strong builders and a real prototype preceded observable distribution—but Nex differs because funded competitors can reproduce its central interaction model immediately.

I would not write the check yet. This is a capable team with unusually relevant knowledge building a useful product in a real market, but the highest-quality evidence belongs to the founders while the weakest belongs to leverage created by users. Talent can build software; it cannot declare a moat. My verifiable flip condition is 50 paying teams with greater than 120% net revenue retention after twelve months, accompanied by evidence that accumulated context graphs or reusable workflows reduce deployment effort as usage grows. That would show Nex becoming a compounding system rather than a well-executed automation vendor.
