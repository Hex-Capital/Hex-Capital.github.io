# Autoresearch, Inc. -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Autoresearch, Inc. could make scarce research expertise reproducible: supply a measurable problem, let machines search, buy a better answer. That could change the cost of developing AI. But 4,188 experiments are an input, not a consequence. The dossier does not establish how much expert labor disappears, which spending pool changes, or how much cheaper a successful result becomes. I can lose one check on an improbable breakthrough. I still need evidence of what that breakthrough buys.

The technical work deserves attention. Autoresearch published training code and reports median validation bits-per-byte of 0.9016 across 25 interleaved confirmation runs. That is substantially more informative than a demonstration selected from thousands of attempts. It provides a method for checking a result. It does not establish a 5–10x discontinuity against Karpathy’s research loop, Predibase, or a competent internal ML team. The company reports using 50 B200 GPUs and stock coding agents over two days; the decisive quantity is improvement per dollar against a credible alternative, not experiments completed.

The founders are the strongest affirmative signal. Karan Brar’s ML infrastructure experience at DynamoAI, Thomas Boser’s production ML background at Reducto and Discord, and Jad Ghalayini’s Cambridge work in compilers and formal verification supply complementary skills for building reliable experimentation infrastructure. This is relevant technical depth. The dossier supplies no observed belief revision or difficult recruiting win, so I cannot credit those behaviors. Likewise, the repeated benchmark testing earns some credit for removing measurement uncertainty, but it leaves the larger kill risk unresolved: whether optimization against a customer’s evaluation survives contact with unseen production workloads. No costed, threshold-based test of that risk is disclosed.

The strongest argument against passing is that Autoresearch could turn a scarce scientific workflow into a repeatable platform. Persistent memory, experiment lineage, and statistical verification could let each campaign improve the machinery behind the next. My willingness to back execution-heavy infrastructure also argues against demanding a new model architecture as the only acceptable innovation. Yet customer ownership of research artifacts and enterprise data restrictions constrain the proposed cross-customer learning loop; its compounding benefit remains an inference. Stock coding agents do not prove this is a wrapper, but orchestration breadth alone does not resolve that red flag either. The exception case would require demonstrated technical acceleration or a sufficiently established consequence to justify overriding the missing economics. Neither is established here.

Paid signup plans and enterprise MSAs describe how Autoresearch, Inc. intends to charge. They establish neither revenue nor a route to lower total research cost. There is no disclosed campaign price, compute cost, expert-supervision requirement, or success rate. I assign the absent-evidence default rather than assume bad economics. Recursive’s disclosed financing above $650 million and Sakana’s approximately $400 million across announced rounds demonstrate funded competition; they do not validate customer demand for this service. Autoresearch needs an advantage that survives customers building their own loops.

I pass at **33/100**. My one verifiable flip condition is a customer-auditable study across three independent paid deployments showing at least a **5x reduction in fully loaded cost to reach a prespecified, production-validated performance target**, against each customer’s existing workflow, including failed runs, compute, and human supervision. That would connect the team’s technical competence to economic consequence. Today, the dossier establishes an experimentation capability whose investment case remains unproven.
