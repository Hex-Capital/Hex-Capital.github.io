# Financial Datasets -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years from now, financial agents will continuously read disclosures, reconcile restatements, compare operating metrics, and explain their work without a person rebuilding the data pipeline for every question. Getting there requires standardized tool invocation, machine-readable financial history, and enough verification that institutions trust an agent’s answer. Financial Datasets has a credible leak from that future: the founder reports one million daily API requests and thousands of agents, while Julius and OpenBB have already integrated the product. The dossier does not fully map the remaining institutional and technical transitions, but this is more than consensus “agents are coming” language.

The magically weird artifact here is 17,000 issuer-specific XBRL tags. Public filings look standardized until somebody tries to make a machine reason across them; then every issuer’s taxonomy, restatement, ticker change, and acquisition becomes operating scar tissue. Singh spent two years mapping that mess, and Financial Datasets now audits 20,000 points across 1,000 companies and 75 sectors. That is a useful enabling layer beneath the visible financial-agent wave. It is not yet a fortress: the company owns neither the underlying filings nor exclusive distribution, and its price data comes from a licensed provider.

The broken rule is only partially exposed. SEC disclosure infrastructure was built for filing compliance and human interpretation, leaving developers to pay an integration tax through scraping, normalization, or incumbent data contracts. Financial Datasets returns some agency to builders through a self-service API and MCP server, but the dossier never quantifies that tax, identifies who captures the rent, or shows that customers obtain meaningfully more control rather than another interchangeable feed. This is an irritating system; I am not yet convinced it is an artificial gate.

Virat Singh looks like a first believer rather than an accelerator tourist. Investment banking, engineering work at Airbnb, Acorns, Faire, GoPro, and Sony, the 2024 financial-dataset library, two years of manual taxonomy work, and Dexter’s 26,600 GitHub stars form a coherent pre-company trail. The missing piece is recruiting proof: this remains a one-person company operating a maintenance-intensive data product. Solo leadership at least makes accountability unambiguous—there is no governance shell game—but I need evidence that his reach can recruit the team required to maintain institutional-grade coverage.

The strongest bear case stings. One million daily requests may represent enthusiastic free-agent traffic rather than durable spending; the dossier supplies prices but no revenue, paid-customer count, or retained cohort. MCP standardization improves distribution while also lowering switching costs. Massive, Intrinio, Alpha Vantage, Finnhub, public SEC feeds, and internal ingestion systems overlap with the product, while Financial Datasets has no exclusive rights and depends on another supplier for prices. If the issuer mappings are merely expensive maintenance rather than compounding proprietary infrastructure, this becomes a popular developer utility trapped between open data and larger vendors.

I would still invest a measured personal check. The concealed infrastructure beneath financial agents fits better than another thin agent interface, and the founder has produced both a labor-intensive asset and observable distribution before assembling a conventional team. My 36-month prediction is $4 million to $9 million in ARR, concentrated in filing-derived fundamentals and operating KPIs rather than commodity price feeds. One condition flips me to a pass: a disclosed cohort of paying accounts retaining less than 70% of its API volume after 90 days would show that the impressive request count is experimentation, not embedded behavior.
