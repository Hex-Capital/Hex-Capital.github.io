# Asim -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032: a robot receives an unfamiliar instruction in a room full of people, and something independent of its task policy decides whether that instruction is safe to execute. I want that layer to exist. Asim’s progression from physical and simulated testing toward a runtime watchdog gives me a plausible route there. But two transitions remain unproven: discovering hazards must produce transferable safety knowledge, and that knowledge must support interventions fast enough to leave the robot useful. The company’s illustrative demonstrations are an early artifact of this direction, not evidence that those transitions already work.

The strongest evidence sits with the people building it. Tilley brings human–robot interaction research; Lafargue brings machine-learning research and Sony experience; Neau brings computer vision and social robotics. Lecourtois’s June 2026 first-prize robot-safety watchdog at the YC Paris–Unaite hackathon is particularly useful: someone built something before there was a company to promote. Meeting future co-founders through that work is a small first-believer signal, too. This is more than four impressive biographies assembled around a fashionable noun. Still, the record establishes technical commitment more clearly than a long-running safety obsession, customer understanding, or command of the business.

The tempting counterargument to my hesitation is that safety infrastructure must precede widespread deployment. Waiting for substantial revenue would miss precisely the enabling layer I like to fund. Asim also fits my pattern of looking underneath the visible robotics wave: private, withheld scenarios offer a concrete assessment-integrity mechanism. But withholding a test does not by itself make the test indispensable. Robocurve already offers an MIT-licensed evaluation harness and announced a $10 million seed round in September 2026. Asim needs something customers cannot obtain simply by running another benchmark. A growing body of transferable failure knowledge could become that asset; the dossier does not establish that corpus today.

Why does a person have to stand beside the robot? Because an emergency stop cannot interpret every dangerous instruction in advance. Removing that supervision could return useful time and expand where machines can work. Yet this dossier identifies a genuine engineering constraint more clearly than an artificial gatekeeper or extractive rent. Safety obligations are not themselves the broken rule. Nor do A3 membership, NVIDIA Inception membership, or YC financing show that an operator has trusted Asim with a consequential task. There is no documented repeat customer behavior here, so that dimension receives my missing-evidence default. The website’s certification disclaimer defines a boundary; it does not demonstrate technical failure.

I would keep talking, but I would not write my personal check today. My willingness to back pre-revenue robotics applies here; it does not supply missing evidence of industrial commitment. There is also no documented severe privacy problem, dependence on one closed model supplier, or governance violation that independently kills the bet. Leadership accountability and custody of deployment data remain unanswered questions. My single flip condition is a paid runtime pilot renewed after 90 days by an independent robotics customer, with customer-verified logs showing prevented hazards and continued useful operation. That would turn Asim’s strongest promise—an independent safety layer—into behavior someone has paid to preserve.
