# OpenHack -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

OpenHack could make continuous security expertise available wherever software is written. That consequence interests me. But the dossier establishes a $12.6 billion application-security market, not how much expert labor OpenHack can eliminate or how much additional software it can secure. A spending pool is the beginning of the calculation. I still need the multiplier. I would pass with my own money on the evidence presented.

The strongest technical fact is the reported discovery and reproduction of Papermark authentication bypass CVE-2026-36755. An exploitable vulnerability is considerably more informative than an agent demonstration. Building an application model and testing findings could attack the real bottleneck: deciding which alerts deserve an engineer’s attention. But one disclosed result does not establish coverage, reliability, or superiority. Semgrep already offers AI triage; Terra offers continuous agentic pentesting with human oversight. OpenHack supplies no comparative measurement showing that its verification removes five or ten times more work. The working artifact earns technical credit; the proposed advantage remains unmeasured.

The strongest objection to passing is Ananay Arora himself. Apple engineering, ASU security research, and bug bounties from Apple, Meta, Perplexity, YC and DHS give him relevant experience discovering failures in real systems. His scanner has attracted 430 stars and 65 forks, and the website displays six customer or team logos. My actual investing patterns include small technical communities becoming platforms and scarce expert workflows becoming repeatable production systems. OpenHack has the beginnings of both. Yet stars do not establish repeated operational dependence, and logos do not establish payment. Nor does the founder record document belief revision or complementary recruiting. A solo founder is normal at this stage; the specific learning and recruiting evidence required by that dimension is simply absent.

My exceptions for an extraordinary instigator addressing an enormous value pool, or a breakthrough with overwhelming societal consequence, deserve consideration here. Arora’s domain record makes that argument credible enough to investigate. It does not yet establish either override. Equally, I would not label OpenHack a generic wrapper merely because it uses existing models: the exploit reproduction is substantive work. The relevant red flag is the possibility that differentiation ultimately rests on assembling a workflow competitors can reproduce. The MIT-licensed scanner makes a proprietary learning advantage especially important, and none is demonstrated. Customer-specific context could become an asset; today that is an inference.

The next dollar should test whether verified findings actually replace expert work across unfamiliar applications. Browser and sandbox verification remain beta, with Docker required for sandbox execution. The dossier gives no founder-defined kill-risk experiment, budget, threshold, or stop decision, so milestone discipline receives its absent-evidence default. Economics receives the same treatment: a free entry plan and negotiated AI credits reveal neither inference cost nor human intervention per useful finding. **My flip condition is one independently audited, matched-workload study across three customer applications showing at least a 10x reduction in fully loaded cost per validated vulnerability versus the existing scanner-plus-human workflow, without lower vulnerability recall.** That result would connect capability, consequence, and economics. Until then, I see a credible security builder whose central investment mechanism remains unproven.
