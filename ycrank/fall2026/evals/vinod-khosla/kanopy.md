# Kanopy -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Kanopy could make power-line inspection cheap enough to run far more frequently. That matters: ordinary cameras replacing specialized surveys could change how utilities find hazards before they become outages or fires. But the $31.95 billion vegetation-management market includes cutting trees, not just finding them. The dossier supplies neither the inspection spending baseline nor a quantified reduction in failures or resource use. I can see a consequential problem. I cannot yet calculate a consequence large enough to justify buying a low-probability option with my own money.

The strongest evidence is that something has been built and used. Kanopy reports 3,093 mapped miles, including more than 300 miles across four states in two weeks. Its camera-only reconstruction approach offers a concrete alternative to specialized hardware, while AiDASH and Overstory emphasize satellite monitoring. But different inputs do not establish superior economics or measurement. The advertised 2–4-inch accuracy lacks independently retrieved validation, error distributions, and a comparable incumbent benchmark. Mapping mileage demonstrates operation; it does not establish that dangerous clearances are reliably detected. That distinction determines whether this becomes infrastructure software or another inspection interface.

The bull case deserves attention. Six utilities and contractors signed LOIs or MSAs covering 270,000 miles. Those agreements are meaningful evidence of access to buyers with an existing problem, even though they establish neither purchased volume nor revenue. Yann’s utility advisory experience and Nicolas’s computer-vision and robotics work supply complementary capabilities around that problem. Kanopy fits my less-public pattern of backing technical workflows that can become repeatable production systems and work with installed infrastructure. Calling this a generic AI wrapper would outrun the evidence. Equally, assuming a proprietary learning advantage from repeated surveys would outrun it.

Clearance reliability is the first risk I would spend money to remove. Release notes documenting pole-association and wire-fitting corrections identify concrete engineering work; they do not disclose a decisive validation threshold or the dollars required to reach it. The dossier also provides no observed material belief revision or difficult recruit hired to own a specific weakness. I give the founders credit for relevant experience without converting credentials into evidence of those behaviors. Their small team and undisclosed revenue are ordinary pre-seed facts, not objections.

The economic question survives even if reconstruction works. Customer-owned cameras remove one equipment requirement, but capture labor, repeat visits, compute, quality assurance, and human review could still dominate cost per usable mile. Kanopy discloses no bottom-up comparison. Meanwhile, Schneider’s proposed AiDASH acquisition creates a plausible distribution threat through existing grid-software relationships. Competing against that distribution before proving accuracy and delivery cost would trigger my concern about scaling ahead of risk removal; the dossier does not establish that the team is doing so.

I pass today. My exceptions permit incomplete milestones or unconventional economics when an exceptional instigator and an enormous, explicit consequence make the option compelling. Relevant founder backgrounds and broad market scope do not yet establish that override here. My verifiable flip condition is a utility-run comparative deployment demonstrating at least 10x lower fully loaded cost per inspected mile, with hazardous-clearance misses no worse than the incumbent method across representative operating conditions. That would turn the camera-only proposition into evidence of a cost curve worth underwriting.
