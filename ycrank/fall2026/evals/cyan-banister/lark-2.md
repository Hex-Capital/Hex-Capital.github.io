# Lark -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a distributor’s best buyer taking a vacation without leaving the purchasing department helpless. The judgment accumulated in that person’s head travels with the business; people handle exceptions while software handles replenishment. Lark is working on a necessary piece: combining operating rules with inventory data and approved ERP actions. But that future is my hypothesis. The dossier establishes purchasing automation, without showing how individual customer implementations become a reusable system for carrying judgment between people and machines. That is the distance I need to understand before writing a check.

Why does a buyer have to reconcile email, spreadsheets, deliveries, and sales history before deciding what to order? Because the information and the rules live in different places. Lark attacks that specific institutional mess and could return time and discretion to purchasing staff. There is no identified licensing restriction or quantified incumbent rent here; the burden is coordination labor. Subscription terms establish how Lark intends to charge, but public pricing does not show how much of the recovered value stays with the customer.

Interline Hardware is the strongest evidence. Its testimonial describes approximately 60 hours saved monthly across purchasing workflows covering more than 60,000 inventory items, with first use less than a month after kickoff. Someone let this small company into a consequential operating process. That requires organizational effort and trust, and it matters more to me than follower counts. The separate unnamed customer’s reported 50 hours saved weekly deserves less weight; I would neither combine those figures nor treat them as proof of revenue. The named deployment nevertheless makes Lark considerably more interesting than a narrated agent demo.

The founders provide some reason to believe they can turn that opening into a business. Jason’s industrial supply-chain agent work at Traba is relevant operating context, and his reported 400K ARR in eight weeks belongs to Datost, not Lark. Michael’s reported volunteer organization of more than 10,000 people suggests an ability to recruit participation. Those are useful commercial and organizational signals. They do not yet establish a dated, sustained obsession with distributor purchasing. Jason is identified as CEO; being brothers supplies no evidence of equal control or a governance problem.

The strongest argument against my hesitation is that unglamorous workflow repair can quietly become indispensable infrastructure. Customer-specific ERP mappings might be the beginning of that asset. But configuration becomes infrastructure only when something valuable survives beyond the next bespoke implementation. Lark’s general setup estimate of one to two months exposes the work involved, while Didero already executes overlapping procurement workflows. Multiple advertised agents do not establish control of a scarce dependency. Approval limits, logs, and pause controls are encouraging safeguards; the dossier does not establish a severe privacy violation or dependence on one closed model supplier, so I would not invent either red flag.

I am neutral on investing my own money today. Lark fits my recurring interest in mundane operating wounds, and its working customer deployment deserves attention. A pre-revenue exception could absolutely apply, but the combination of a demonstrated future pathway and longstanding founder obsession that would justify it is incomplete here. My flip condition is a second unrelated distributor deploying the same purchasing-rule engine without bespoke engineering and independently documenting sustained use for three months. That would test whether Lark is accumulating a transferable capability beneath the workflow, rather than rebuilding the workflow customer by customer.
