# Maingen -- Paul Graham Evaluation

Eight SolarBench tasks built from hundreds of hours with solar operators make Maingen a schlep-heavy company whose weakest signal is organic founder pain. Maingen has already turned telemetry, manuals, inventory, emails, and irreversible expenditures into 880 graded simulated weeks. But Phillip Yan encountered energy software at Axpo rather than living as a solar operator, while David Yang approached the problem from Scale AI and model evaluation. Maingen looks like two good hackers who found an ugly problem, not two users who could no longer tolerate their own problem.

**Stripe** (2010) is the high rung for schlep blindness: the Collisons converted the miserable details of payments into the product itself. Maingen shares that mechanism because industrial agents cannot be trained without translating alarms, contracts, work orders, and expert judgment into executable environments. Maingen remains below Stripe because SolarBench is still an eight-task simulation; no proprietary data agreement, paid laboratory contract, or real operational deployment is public. The schlep is genuine, but Maingen has demonstrated the map rather than ownership of the territory.

**Kiko** (2005) exposed the low-rung failure mechanism for organic ideas: clever hackers can build a plausible product without being attached enough to the underlying problem, and an acquihire-shaped exit then reveals the difference. Maingen’s shift from an “AI OS for solar O&M” to datasets for frontier laboratories is a smaller version of that warning. Yan’s Axpo work and Yang’s Scale AI experience give Maingen unusually relevant context, but the dossier never says either founder personally needed SolarBench. The Loopt exception does not rescue Maingen here: I overlooked Loopt’s weak timing because Sam Altman’s force of personality was itself overwhelming evidence, and Maingen has not yet produced an equivalent animal signal.

SolarBench’s 880 graded weeks and a launch post with 68,000 views show that Maingen shipped before pitching, which is the beginning of resourcefulness. Over the next 18 months, this dimension compounds only if Maingen uses the benchmark to get inside laboratories and operators, manually constructs requested environments, and turns every awkward contract into reusable machinery. Hundreds of interview hours are good tracks, but they are not cereal boxes: Maingen has no reported revenue, named customer, or stunt showing that Yan and Yang will route around a locked door rather than publish another benchmark.

David Yang’s ICLR 2025 workshop paper and prior Scale AI work put Maingen above technical-by-credential-only founders, while Phillip Yan’s energy-trading tools and Pax-Automata repository add a second builder. The unstated hacker pattern is present, though faintly: Maingen’s public repositories have roughly ten stars rather than the pre-company hacker trail of Aaron Swartz or Patrick Collison. The score would flip into the highest band if Maingen had released a technically difficult environment framework that outsiders adopted, or if SolarBench became a benchmark frontier laboratories publicly optimized against.

**Reddit** (2005) is the useful single-anchor parallel for founder configuration: two technically capable founders with a relationship predating the startup are much safer than strangers assembled around an idea. Yan and Yang competed on opposing middle-school Science Bowl teams, and Maingen now combines Yan’s energy work with Yang’s evaluation experience. That history is not proof that Maingen’s founders have built together for years, but it avoids the red flag of cofounders paired weeks before applying. Two founders with complementary coverage is the right shape.

$4–8 million in annual revenue within 36 months is my forecast for Maingen, with most of it coming from custom energy and industrial evaluation environments sold to two or three frontier laboratories rather than from a self-service platform. Maingen’s high-schlep, low-organic-pain signature points toward a valuable expert business before it points toward a defining software company: operator research and bespoke grading rubrics win contracts, while the absence of a founder’s own recurring need makes product standardization harder. Maingen can still become the industrial evaluation layer, but the first form will look more like painstaking data work than magic software.

Scale AI’s $870 million of 2024 revenue makes the strongest opposing case sting: Scale AI, Turing, or Invisible Technologies could hire industrial specialists, bundle comparable evaluations, and reduce Maingen’s eight-task benchmark to a recruiting sample. Maingen’s positioning change compounds that red flag, and no current proprietary-data barrier prevents bundling. The evidence keeping me on the invest side is that Maingen already did the work incumbents are structurally tempted to dismiss as too narrow: hundreds of operator hours encoded into priced actions and all-or-nothing rubrics by a credible two-hacker pair. A year from now, one named frontier laboratory must be paying Maingen for a repeat environment or proprietary dataset; without that verifiable condition, I would flip from Invest to Pass.
