# TrueMile -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Harpreet has worked inside the problem. Managing his family’s fleet from age 19 and reporting more than $7M in historical trucking revenue is more relevant to TrueMile than his software employers or degree. Those are founder-reported operating claims, however, with limited corroboration in the dossier. They earn credit for specific knowledge, within that evidentiary limit. The $7M belongs to the family operation; it establishes no current TrueMile revenue.

The decisive question is what survives when another carrier copies the software. TrueMile connects sourcing, planning, dispatch, and communications to its own fleet. That creates an opportunity to learn from consequences. It does not yet establish an asset competitors cannot reproduce. Ordinary trucks are purchasable hardware. Execution data becomes valuable when it produces a persistent advantage, and neither the dataset nor measured optimization gains are disclosed. aifleet already operates the same broad model. Owning the feedback loop matters only if the feedback changes the economics.

The strongest case for investing is that trucking knowledge may let Harpreet identify constraints outsiders miss, then use coordinated agents to capture those savings directly through transportation charges. Recurring Texas and Sunbelt lanes provide a concrete starting buyer. The cited $464.60B U.S. full-truckload market establishes considerable spending beyond that starting geography, although it does not establish a validated expansion path. TrueMile could become a much better carrier before outsiders understand its software. That possibility deserves attention. Today, freight charges remain an inferred business model, with no disclosed rates, completed transactions, or contribution margins. Industry operating costs of $2.336 per mile make small optimization gains potentially consequential—and easy to consume through operating expenses.

TrueMile partially fits my pattern of bringing fragmented offline activity into a coordinated system. The missing mechanism is participant-created value: there is no evidence that another shipper improves service or acquisition for existing shippers. Soliciting two dedicated-lane partners is an acquisition effort, not an observed network. My exceptions for products without a network depend on affirmative prototype or capital-efficiency evidence; exceptions for unclear monetization depend on behavior already compounding. Neither override is established here. The conflicting carrier and software-platform descriptions warrant clarification, but they do not establish dishonesty or any other founder-integrity veto.

I pass at this evidence level. TrueMile has a credible source of operating insight, but the dossier does not show how that insight compounds faster than fleet costs and competitors. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: a six-month lane-level operating record showing that adding compatible shipper demand increases contribution profit per truck for existing lanes, after all fleet costs and human dispatch labor. That would demonstrate the economic mechanism the current story asks me to assume.
