# Olam Labs -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The missing fact is what happened when something broke. Olam Labs has plainly shipped a substantial system, but the dossier never names a blocker, the founders’ workaround, or the time it took to change the outcome. I cannot infer resourcefulness merely from Om Buddhdev’s staff-engineering work at AI Dungeon or Shreshth Sharma’s reinforcement-learning experience. This is missing evidence, not adverse evidence, but it leaves the most important founder test unanswered.

The artifact itself is unusually strong. The team built deterministic multiplayer environments with identical human and model action spaces, processed nearly one billion tokens per day, and produced 86,729 graded table-talk turns by July 2026 ([methodology](https://olamlabs.ai/research/social-arena); [evaluations](https://olamlabs.ai/evaluations)). More than 1,000 daily matches show that this is functioning infrastructure rather than a diagram. The deduction is that the grading remains partly LLM-generated and the claimed connection between social games and production-agent behavior has not been demonstrated. They have measured the machine they built; they have not yet proved it measures what customers care about.

There is real pull, though it is less precise than the aggregate numbers suggest. Thousands of early-access users and 1,000-plus daily matches are considerably better than a waitlist. But Olam has not disclosed retention, named a recurring research team, or shown that a frontier lab paid for and acted on an evaluation. Public players may want a social-strategy game while model labs want evidence about deployment failures. Those are different wells. The current usage proves that people return to the environment in aggregate, not yet that the intended buyer cannot work without the result.

The founders have plausible organic proximity to the problem. Buddhdev worked on agents, infrastructure, and multiplayer products at AI Dungeon; Sharma worked in data engineering and reinforcement learning. Their instrumented corpus, privacy requirements, and enormous compute load also expose genuine schleps. But the dossier labels founder-market fit as inferred and never documents the moment either founder encountered an evaluation failure personally. Nor does it show a rejected hypothesis followed by a materially changed product route. The long cofounder history is reassuring, but it is not evidence of how they respond to contrary product data.

The strongest bull case is that the working product and unmistakable activity override the missing founder anecdote. Olam has already assembled a proprietary substrate while better-funded evaluation companies remain concentrated on pairwise voting, application traces, or simulated workflows. That matches the pattern of technical founders building a programmable primitive before its category is settled. The bear case stings more: Patronus AI can move into the same environments, the public product consumes nearly one billion tokens daily for free, and every disclosed enterprise revenue stream is inferred. If game performance fails to predict production behavior, the corpus is expensive entertainment rather than an evaluation moat.

I expect Olam Labs to move within 36 months from public game benchmarks toward private post-training simulations for model labs and reach roughly $1 million to $3 million in ARR. I am not writing the check yet because the operational gate remains unproven. One condition would flip me: a named model lab pays for a private evaluation, changes a training or deployment decision because of it, and renews. That single event would establish transfer validity, urgent buyer pull, and workable economics strongly enough to justify overriding the missing resourcefulness record.
