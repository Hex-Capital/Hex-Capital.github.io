# Stoa -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

AI chips are a $123 billion market heading toward $286 billion by 2030, but Stoa does not yet show that it changes this cost pool by orders of magnitude. Better price discovery could reduce spreads, idle inventory, and financing uncertainty. Those are useful consequences, not yet a 100x societal consequence. The dossier never quantifies today’s transaction friction, utilization loss, or capital trapped by opaque GPU pricing.

The strongest fact is $300 million in RFQs during the first month. It proves that three founders can aggregate institutional demand quickly. It does not prove liquidity. No executed volume, fee revenue, repeat settlement rate, or functioning H100 index is disclosed. A marketplace full of requests but few completed trades is a bulletin board, and Stoa’s largest kill risk is precisely the conversion from notional interest into inspected, financed, delivered, and paid transactions.

There is no technical discontinuity yet. A 48-hour blind-RFQ process may beat forwarded inventory lists, but the dossier supplies no incumbent cycle time, spread reduction, settlement improvement, or auditable 5–10x benchmark. Compute Exchange already spans physical GPUs, reserved capacity, and forward contracts; Procurri adds inspection, logistics, and asset disposition. More importantly, Stoa disclaims inspection, title, authenticity, condition, delivery, and payment. Contractual ownership of bid data is not a moat if the highest-quality transactions leave the platform through disintermediation.

The founders are the reason not to dismiss this as another thin marketplace. Berat Celik is a repeat founder who left an xAI opportunity; Eren Berke Saglam traded derivatives at Citi; Kaan Yigit built pricing and trading systems at Uniper. The combination of software, commodity infrastructure, and financial-market experience is unusually relevant, and a decade-long relationship lowers team risk. What is missing is evidence of belief revision or recruiting against the operational risks they do not own—hardware inspection, export controls, logistics, and counterparty credit.

The known exception is **Block**: a small technical team used an initially modest transaction wedge to enter an enormous value pool and evolve into broader financial infrastructure. Stoa could follow the same mechanism through GPU price indices, collateral valuation, clearing, or lending. The material difference is that Block’s wedge completed payments; Stoa has disclosed $300 million of requests without disclosing that counterparties actually settle. My 36-month base case is therefore a pivot toward GPU pricing data and asset-backed financing, reaching roughly $1–4 million ARR rather than becoming the dominant physical exchange.

I pass today. This is capable execution in a large market, but the current product is an intermediary workflow rather than a Black-Swan technical mechanism. The one verifiable flip condition is six consecutive months showing at least $25 million of monthly settled volume, repeat counterparties, and materially lower all-in spreads or settlement time than Compute Exchange and brokers. That would demonstrate that transaction data is becoming infrastructure rather than merely being collected.
