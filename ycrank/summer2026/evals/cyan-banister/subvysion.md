# SubVysion -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

An autonomous rover mapping buried utilities is SubVysion’s analytical signature: unusually tactile frontier hardware paired with founders who appear adjacent to the problem rather than *of* it. SubVysion can drive onto a construction site, collect GPR data, and put underground infrastructure into a worker’s phone; Maxwell Stone’s Roboflow and railyard-mapping experience proves technical proximity, but neither Stone nor Wenson Tang has documented years excavating, locating utilities, or carrying strike liability. I can touch the machine. I cannot yet touch the biography that made the machine inevitable.

**Flock Safety** is the high rung because the physical artifact could be seen operating in the wild, and that observation revealed both product truth and distribution. SubVysion shares that load-bearing mechanism: SubVysion reports live deployments on U.S. construction sites, and a contractor became both the first customer and an investor after founder-led cold calling. The divergence is verification. Flock’s cameras produced an observable outcome; SubVysion claims “100% visibility” without publishing miss rates, validation standards, or performance across soil, depth, and material conditions. The rover is real enough to earn a near-top physical-world score, but the underground truth remains frustratingly invisible.

**DeepMind** exposed the low-rung founder-origin pattern: extraordinary technical people without the autobiographical problem contact I normally require, rescued by rare polymath compounding. SubVysion sits above that rung because Stone built on-device inference used in more than 10,000 pharmacies and worked on railyard mapping, while Tang built a $500 LiDAR search-and-rescue drone and worked around emergency-dispatch software. SubVysion still lacks the Anduril or Crusoe origin: no founder is documented standing beside a severed gas line, running a utility-locating crew, or growing up inside construction. Technical adjacency is a rebuttal, not a hard no, but it is not lived ownership.

**Exodigo’s $200 million-plus financing** means SubVysion is entering a validated race, not discovering an aesthetically abandoned desert. SubVysion’s magically weird element is the integrated behavior—a small autonomous rover collecting multi-sensor GPR, reconstructing a centimeter-level map, and letting a contractor look underground through AR—rather than the category itself. Over 18 months, every verified scan could compound into a sensor-signature dataset and make SubVysion smarter; every unverified scan instead compounds liability while Exodigo’s 86.1 million mapped square feet compounds faster. “Magically weird” survives here, but “round likely underpriced” is weakened by a market already crowded with financed believers.

**Maxwell Stone is explicitly SubVysion’s CEO**, while Wenson Tang is COO, so SubVysion clears the first half of my governance test. The dossier provides no evidence of majority operating authority, founder equity allocation, vesting, or an exit-conflict protocol after Stone and Tang joined forces in January 2026. The counterfactual is clean: a written operating agreement giving Stone final authority on financing, product, and sale decisions would move SubVysion into my upper governance tier; a 50/50 structure would push SubVysion toward the HQ Trivia failure pattern. Titles are useful. Tiebreakers are what matter when the rover misses a pipe.

**SpaceX** is the single-anchor parallel because hardware difficulty can become a moat precisely when conventional investors recoil from capital intensity. SubVysion carries a smaller version of that pattern: autonomous field equipment, GPR interpretation, survey positioning, 3D reconstruction, construction workflows, and liability must all work outside the laboratory. SubVysion is less frontier-science than SpaceX and faces established alternatives from Exodigo, Groundhawk, and vGIS, but underground mapping is ugly, operationally demanding, and safety-adjacent—the kind of unsexy physical problem I like. I am never playing the game I seem to be playing: SubVysion is not really selling AR; SubVysion is selling permission to put a bucket in the ground.

**Contracts on U.S. job sites** lead me to predict that by July 2029 SubVysion will reach $2 million-$5 million ARR as a tech-enabled utility-locating service, with autonomous rovers assisting human operators rather than replacing them. SubVysion’s tactile product can win project revenue quickly, while SubVysion’s incomplete validation record and thin lived construction background will force field labor, quality-control review, and insurance into the delivery model. The artifact gets SubVysion through the gate; operating reality turns the software-shaped dream into a services-heavy business.

**SubVysion’s first customer becoming an investor** is the strongest bull fact, but the bear mechanism stings harder: founders from Penn building in a newly financed robotics category can look like frontier outsiders while actually arriving after Exodigo mapped 86.1 million square feet and raised more than $200 million. SubVysion also combines my red flags—no documented tiebreaker, no lived excavation history, and a “100% visibility” claim resting on a detection problem that published research still calls technically difficult. Live contracts and the customer-investor conversion keep SubVysion above that bear case, because someone with a real job site saw enough to risk both operational trust and money. I would write a small check, but I flip to Pass if SubVysion cannot demonstrate zero missed utilities across 100 independently verified excavations.
