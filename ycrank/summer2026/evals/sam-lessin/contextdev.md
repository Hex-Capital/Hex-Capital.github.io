# Context.dev -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: no post-money valuation, no competing-term-sheet count, and no evidence this is genuinely the first scarce check. Context.dev may be a good product, but I cannot tell whether I am buying a non-consensus belief or paying a YC auction price for the now-obvious thesis that agents need fresh web data. The absence of terms earns the financing gate’s default, not the benefit of the doubt.

The operating evidence is much better than the deal evidence. Context.dev reports 400-plus customers—including Mintlify, Rho, Passionfroot, and Klarna—alongside 5,000-plus developers and 11,018 weekly npm downloads. Dench attributes more than $3 million of pipeline to monitoring built with the product. That is not merely an audience admiring a developer tool; an API embedded in customer workflows is producing commercially relevant action. The strongest version of the case is that request history, failure feedback, and approximately 70% cached-brand coverage become a proprietary operating dataset that improves as usage compounds.

The problem is that this dark pool is not yet demonstrated. Firecrawl, Tavily, and Exa already sell overlapping retrieval or extraction infrastructure and have raised $16.2 million, $25 million, and $352 million respectively. Context.dev’s broader surface—brand data, screenshots, transactions, style guides, and crawling—makes replication more tedious, not structurally impossible. Source sites still control blocking, cached brand records may remain unchanged for three months, and failed requests are simply not billed. If reliability is governed by third parties while competitors can purchase the same proxies and models, 5,000 developers may create load without creating durable advantage.

Yahia Bakour is the part I underwrite most confidently. He has led checkout engineering at Amazon, managed AI automation at Sunrun, and sold Essense.io and Stock Alarm. Those are shipped receipts across production software, AI systems, and company-building—not résumé inference. A single accountable founder also avoids the concealed-decision problem common to equal-founder committees, although the discrepancy between YC’s team size of one and the launch claim of four people should be reconciled.

The bull case stings: a repeat founder has already assembled SOC 2 certification, five SDKs, 400-plus customers, and real customer outcomes before the round, while much larger competitors validate demand. At pre-seed, that can be enough to override missing historical margins. But it does not override both missing gates here. There is no named use of proceeds, dated experiment, or pass/fail metric; “serve more agents” would finance an ongoing infrastructure race, not reveal one discrete truth. Pricing tiers from $25 to $499 per month show how Context.dev charges, but without paid-customer mix, gross margin, burn, or proxy and rendering costs, they do not show that the business survives without another round.

My verifiable flip condition is straightforward: I invest if the company offers a non-auctioned sub-$10 million post-money round and commits the check to proving, within twelve months, that at least 100 paying customers retain above 90% while serving requests at positive gross margin. That would convert impressive usage into a finite venture experiment and show that the workflow compounds economically rather than feeding a capital factory. Until then, this is a credible company attached to an un-underwritable deal.
