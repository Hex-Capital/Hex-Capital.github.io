# Erinys -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence could be large: legal services consume $396.8 billion annually in the United States, and Erinys wants to replace the firm’s operating structure rather than make one lawyer marginally faster ([Grand View Research](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/us-legal-services-market-report), 2025). But a large cost pool is not a quantified consequence. The dossier supplies no baseline for plaintiff-firm overhead, matter cost, lawyer throughput, consumer access, or settlement time. I cannot tell whether success makes legal expertise 2x cheaper or merely bundles software, staffing, financing, and administration more conveniently.

The claimed discontinuity is organizational, not yet technical. Erinys contrasts its turnkey firms with Eve and Supio, but Eve already serves more than 1,000 firms and powers 200,000 cases ([Eve](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/eve-surpasses-1-000-plaintiff-firm-customers-powers-over-200-000-cases-302735833.html), Apr 2026). There is no measured advantage in case selection, resolution speed, staffing leverage, recovery rate, or cost per matter. An internal workflow-learning loop could become valuable, but without an operated firm, case volume, or benchmark it is a hypothesis attached to a service stack—not a 5–10x technical result.

The largest kill risk is neither model quality nor lawyer recruitment. It is whether Erinys can participate economically in firms without violating nonlawyer ownership and fee-sharing rules. Arizona provides an Alternative Business Structure route, while most states retain Rule 5.4 restrictions ([Arizona Courts](https://www.azcourts.gov/cld/Alternative-Business-Structure); [ABA Rule 5.4](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_5_4_professional_independence_of_a_lawyer/)). Yet the dossier names no jurisdictional experiment, license application, compliant contract structure, cost, deadline, or stop condition. Supplying working capital before proving the economic architecture risks spending dollars around the existential uncertainty rather than against it.

The founders have technical energy but not yet the necessary gene pool. Zyuzin and Guo built a working OSINT investigation prototype querying 22 government databases in April 2026, then the team shifted to a broader plaintiff-firm network by August. That action shows tactical flexibility. It does not show why the belief changed, and three University of Maryland founders with no documented bar admission, legal-practice experience, regulatory recruit, or plaintiff-firm operator leave the central risks unowned. Cybersecurity and finance backgrounds are useful ingredients; they are not substitutes for recruiting the people who understand case economics and professional-responsibility boundaries.

The strongest bull case is the known exception represented by Block: a small team can begin with an undramatic tool and grow into infrastructure around an enormous existing value pool. Erinys shares the platform-shaped ambition and willingness to change its route. The material difference is evidence. Erinys has no disclosed member firm, revenue, repeat usage, regulatory permission, or matter-level learning loop. The dossier’s only traction is YC backing, a referral solicitation, and a prior prototype for a different product. I expect that by August 2029 the company is more likely to have pivoted into plaintiff-side intake and investigation software with less than $3 million ARR than to operate a national network of affiliated firms.

I would not buy the option today. The downside is one check, but uncapped upside requires a mechanism, not just a $396.8 billion denominator. One verifiable result would flip me: three regulator-approved Erinys-operated firms collectively closing 100 matters at no more than 20% of the fully loaded cost per resolved matter of matched conventional plaintiff firms. That would simultaneously establish legal operability, a proprietary production loop, and Chindia-price economics. Until then, this is an ambitious systems-integration thesis exposed to better-capitalized software substitutes.
