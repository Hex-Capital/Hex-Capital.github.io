# OS3 -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Move the clock to 2032. Hotels, warehouses, and factories use mobile manipulators for the physical work that falls between fixed automation and expensive human attention. OS3 offers a plausible route there: internet and first-person video bootstrap control, early robots collect task and failure data, better models reshape the hardware, and the cycle repeats. The reported hospitality deployments are a small leak from that future. But they are still a blurry leak—no venue, unit count, paid status, task-success rate, intervention rate, or uptime establishes that the future has actually arrived.

The strongest part of OS3 is below the fashionable humanoid story. The team designs the mobile two-arm body, trains its own control models, and proposes a hardware-data-model loop spanning hotels, warehouses, and production lines. That could become a real enabling layer rather than another interface sitting on somebody else’s model. Yet control is still asserted more than demonstrated. Pudu already markets the two-arm D7, Apptronik has raised nearly $1 billion, and OS3 has not disclosed a dataset, performance advantage, or scarce component that competitors cannot reproduce.

Why are hotels short-staffed? The dossier tells me 65% report shortages, but not which rule or economic rent makes those jobs remain unfilled after higher pay, benefits, flexible hours, job fairs, and advertising. The robot substitutes for missing labor; it does not clearly return occupational control or income to an individual. That distinction matters. A staffing statistic describes demand, but it does not reveal the hidden system OS3 uniquely understands.

The founders are credible but not yet inevitable. Rishabh Chanana brings a robotics-and-NLP graduate specialization, ML production experience, research, and a patent; Chris Hailey brings large-scale Coinbase engineering and a prior project reportedly reaching $10K MRR. They also built together for six hours after meeting outside a 2025 MCP hackathon. That demonstrates velocity and complementary technical-commercial ability. It does not yet show years of pre-company robotic obsession, multiple independently shipped machines, or operating scars inside hospitality. The unexplained Observee-to-OS3 history makes me want a clean account of the pivot and who ultimately controls decisions, although the dossier does not establish a governance violation.

The bull case resembles the pre-revenue exception I made for **CosmicBrain AI**: physical-AI infrastructure, a functioning machine, and commercial proofs can outweigh missing revenue. The material difference is verification. CosmicBrain had signed industrial proofs of concept and a founder’s long record of building robots; OS3 gives me unnamed “deployments” and specifications without observed task performance. A robot being present in a hotel is not the costly user behavior I need. A hotel reorganizing shifts around it, paying for it, and renewing after repeated failures would be.

My 36-month expectation is that OS3’s primary market moves from hospitality to the more structured warehouse or production-line environments already named on its deployment page, with ARR still below $3 million by August 2029. Hotels contain an unruly mixture of people, liquids, kitchens, elevators, and safety exposure; the absence of intervention and uptime data makes that complexity the decisive risk. The in-house loop could eventually digest it, but well-capitalized competitors will be collecting their own data at the same time.

I am not writing the check yet. This is technically ambitious, directionally aligned, and more substantial than a consensus AI wrapper, but the present artifact has not produced a legible behavioral or economic truth. My one verifiable flip condition is a named hotel publishing a paid deployment of at least 90 days with task-success, human-intervention, uptime, and renewal data. That would turn the deployment claim into the mundane artifact I can underwrite.
