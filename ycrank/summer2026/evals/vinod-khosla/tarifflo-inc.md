# Tarifflo Inc. -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Tarifflo attacks a real tax on commerce, but not yet a consequential rewriting of commerce. The dossier identifies a $23.78 billion global customs-brokerage market, consultants charging $250–500 per hour, and filing errors affecting one in eight entries. Yet the proposed consequence is recovering overpaid duties and reducing compliance labor—not making a resource, expert service, or infrastructure layer 100x more available. The $1.3 million recovered for Orly is excellent customer value. It does not establish societal-scale option value.

The product has more substance than a generic AI wrapper. An ABI filer code, direct CBP connection, licensed-broker review, 1 million products audited, and 10 million linked shipment records represent difficult regulatory plumbing. Bryce Judy also has unusually direct founder-market fit: KPMG customs work, a CBP broker license, and published HTS-classification research. But Tarifflo provides no measured accuracy, cost, or throughput benchmark against Zonos, KlearNow, iCustoms, or a conventional broker. Regulatory implementation is useful work; it is not yet a 5–10x technical discontinuity.

The bull case is that this is an execution-heavy infrastructure company hiding inside a contingency-recovery service. Public companies were reportedly signing onto the earlier classification product by February 2025, and normal audits could generate proprietary classification and correction outcomes. Free filing could become the distribution wedge while recovery finances adoption. That matches my unstated willingness to back operational businesses when installed infrastructure and a large value pool permit expansion. The Block exception is relevant because a modest initial tool evolved into broader financial infrastructure; the material difference is that Tarifflo has not demonstrated either a similarly expansive adjacent platform or a learning loop competitors cannot reproduce.

The risk-removal plan is the weakest part of the case. The dossier names classification liability, ABI private-beta dependency, policy maintenance, and delayed contingency payments, but it gives no ranked kill risk, decisive experiment, pass/fail threshold, cost, or stop response. One successful recovery does not answer whether automated findings remain accurate across changing Chapter 99, PGA, Section 122, and Section 232 rules. Capital spent scaling audits before measuring false positives and broker-review burden would fund activity rather than remove existential risk.

Customer economics are attractive but company economics remain undisclosed. Charging importers nothing upfront and collecting only after refunds easily beats a consultant invoice at the point of purchase. It may also create long cash-conversion cycles under 300-day correction windows, with unknown review costs and recovery rates. This can become a good compliance business without becoming an asymmetric venture outcome. My 36-month base case is a specialized duty-recovery company at roughly $5–15 million in annualized revenue, not an autonomous global customs platform.

I pass today. One verifiable result would flip the decision: an independently auditable, named-customer cohort showing Tarifflo processes customs compliance at least 10x cheaper than broker-led or competing software workflows, with equal or lower error rates and improving performance from proprietary outcome data. That would convert regulatory plumbing into a technical learning system and make the broader platform thesis investable.
