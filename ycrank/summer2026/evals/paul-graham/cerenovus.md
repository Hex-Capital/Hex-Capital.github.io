# Cerenovus -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The most important missing fact is not revenue. It is what broke. Cerenovus moved from July’s $40-per-seat Compendium product for startups to an enterprise diagnostic system by August 2026, but the dossier does not identify the customer objection, technical failure, or distribution obstacle that caused the change. Nor does it show what the founders built in response and how quickly that altered the result. Harvard, Math 55, quantum research, and portfolio-management experience are evidence of ability. They are not evidence of resourcefulness under resistance.

The strongest fact is that this is more than a slide deck. The product reportedly takes read-only access to multiple systems, resolves entities, reconstructs processes, runs 26 investigative sweeps, preserves permissions, and links findings to original evidence. Pilot customers are described as live. That is a substantial technical artifact for a pre-seed company. But nearly every claim is self-reported: there is no accuracy benchmark, deployment duration, named customer result, or measured threshold. I believe they have shipped something difficult. I do not yet know whether it works unusually well.

The user evidence is weaker. “CEOs, CFOs, COOs, owners, investors, and advisers” is a list of buyers, not a narrow well. The live pilots are unnamed, and the dossier gives no repeated investigation, payment, retention, referral, or operational outcome. Compendium’s 122 Product Hunt points and 130 followers belong to a different product; its pricing cannot establish demand for the current one. Four hundred LinkedIn followers establish visibility, not pull. Who needs the crude version now, and what do they do with it twice?

There is a plausible running-back interpretation of the product change. The founders began with their own frustration organizing information, shipped Compendium, and then rapidly moved toward reconstructing business operations. But the stable goal and revised route are clearer than the evidence connecting them. Without the falsifying observation, this could be fast learning or merely movement toward a larger-sounding customer. The original need was authentic enough to earn some credit, while the enterprise wedge introduces real schlep—permissions, inconsistent records, security review, entity resolution, and human verification—but these students have not shown that they personally encountered those enterprise problems before choosing the market.

The bull case stings. Three young technical founders have already shipped two related forms of knowledge software, obtained live pilots, and built the beginnings of a customer-specific data substrate. If reviewed exceptions and decision outcomes accumulate, Cerenovus could become more useful with every investigation. The incumbents’ scale might even help define the wedge: Glean searches, Hebbia analyzes documents, while Cerenovus tries to tell an executive where an operating process is leaking. A working product can override limited early customer history, but only when the proof or user behavior is unmistakable. Here neither is yet strong enough to replace the missing resourcefulness evidence.

My 36-month prediction is that Cerenovus will narrow into private-equity diligence or portfolio-company operations, remain below $2 million ARR, and abandon the current horizontal “knowledge company” positioning. Broad overlap with Glean, Coworker.ai, Hebbia, and Sana is survivable if one user niche pulls hard; it is dangerous when the niche is still described as nearly every senior business buyer. I would pass today. The verifiable flip condition is two named pilots converting to repeated paid investigations after the founders document a specific access, accuracy, or workflow blocker they solved within weeks.
