# Hickory & Company -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Hickory & Company has a founder I can underwrite more readily than its financing. Proposed post-money: undisclosed. Competing term sheets: undisclosed. Whether my money would be the first genuinely scarce check: unknown. The reported September 2026 financing of $500,000 establishes funding, not an attractive entry price. YC, Album, and Salt appearing as backers does not establish an auction either. I will not invent one. But nothing here establishes that I am buying a belief other investors have cheaply discarded.

The strongest evidence is that this team understands both sides of a difficult institutional boundary. Joshua Howland previously built Seis, reporting more than 500,000 accounts and $10 million in revenue 16 months after launch; those are prior-company operating receipts, not Hickory traction. Scott Grow brings more than 20 years of compliance experience, a CRCM credential, and an existing working relationship with Howland at Seis. Kevin Anderson adds banking-systems engineering experience. That combination fits my recurring interest in people who can translate between mistrustful systems: sponsor banks retain accountability while fintech partners control daily operations. Seis subsequently closed, so its scale proves experience, not a profitable outcome or exit.

The product could make that translation economically useful. Source-linked research, institution-specific policies, acceptance criteria, and employee approval put Hickory inside the work that establishes whether a bank can trust its partners. The claimed live-bank integration is an encouraging technical foothold. It does not establish a purchase, repeat usage, or control over proprietary access. Customer policies might become a durable asset, but configuring them does not automatically give Hickory ownership of that asset. Humanfold’s prospect lists, outreach, and 22 launch videos show sales activity; they do not show that owned trust caused a customer to buy.

The strongest objection to passing is that this team may already possess the difficult part: enough banking credibility to get software into a live institution. A three-person company needs very few substantial bank contracts to become meaningful. Human approval also makes the product more credible than a promise that autonomous agents will stop making mistakes. Nevertheless, Parcha already offers configurable workflows and audit logs, while Bretton AI works across financial-crime systems. Hickory’s expert implementation could win business, but the dossier does not yet establish why that expertise compounds into proprietary distribution rather than recurring implementation labor. The AI red flag is therefore unresolved differentiation and economics, not demonstrated recklessness about accuracy.

What exact truth does the next check reveal? Hickory’s dossier supplies no proceeds-linked experiment, deadline, or pass/fail metric, so I use the prescribed default rather than manufacture a venture moment from its product roadmap. Likewise, absent pricing, margin, burn, and runway data leave the path to financing independence unestablished. Human review is sensible product design, but its cost belongs in the economics. My known exceptions require documented conversion and retention, unusually strong external demand, or a financing structure that removes repeated fundraising. Prior-company scale and an unnamed integration do not supply those overrides. These are missing underwriting facts, not evidence that a pre-seed business is failing.

I pass on Hickory & Company today. The team’s operating history earns substantial credit; it cannot also stand in for attractive terms, customer conversion, and a capital plan. One verifiable milestone would reopen my underwriting: a named sponsor bank renewing a paid deployment at positive contribution margin after implementation and required human review are fully counted. That would demonstrate that the translation layer produces an economic result someone repeatedly purchases. I would then have a functioning base business to price.
