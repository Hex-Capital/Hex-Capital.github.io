# hardware intelligence -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders possess specific knowledge earned through action. Rishov Sarkar built AMD AI Engine simulators, worked on LLVM compilation flows at Siemens EDA, and maintains the 77-star LightningSim simulator. Athreya Anand led agentic-workflow work at Google and encountered chip design through AWS Inferentia and Tesla. Wave is therefore not an AI tourism project. The team has worked on both sides of the interface: the hardware toolchain and the agents acting upon it.

The product also attacks real friction. Wave reads multi-gigabyte VCD and FST dumps, traces signals, proposes fixes, reruns the simulator, and produces an auditable waveform diff. Running on-prem with the customer’s model key respects the confidentiality of chip designs. This is a credible tool built by credible builders. But a useful tool is not automatically a durable company.

The decisive question is what remains when a competitor copies the workflow. Today the answer is unclear. Synopsys Verdi already provides AI failure analysis, natural-language commands, signal tracing, waveform comparison, and the entrenched FSDB ecosystem. ChipAgents analyzes designs, testbenches, logs, and waveforms across more than 120 semiconductor companies, while Wave still needs customer-specific FSDB integration. On-prem deployment protects the customer, but it also prevents customer designs from automatically creating a proprietary shared dataset. The software improves through company labor, not through permissionless contributions from users.

The strongest bull case is that founder knowledge becomes the moat over time. Personal pilot onboarding could reveal undocumented verification workflows, integrations could accumulate into switching costs, and the terminal could become the control plane for a larger portion of chip development. The EDA market is substantial—SEMI reported $5.47 billion of electronic-system-design revenue in Q4 2025—and incumbents often leave room for a faster product with better taste. This is the known-exception case: unusually capable founders can outrun an initially illegible wedge.

The evidence does not yet earn that exception. Hardware intelligence has announced pilot onboarding, but no named pilot, repeat usage, price, revenue, proprietary model, or exclusive workflow asset. Its $20 billion market claim describes the parent category without showing how waveform debugging expands causally into adjacent spend. Founder-led demonstrations are validation of execution; they are not validation of distribution or value capture. There is no documented integrity, morale, or financing red flag, but there is also no unstated-pattern override: this is software serving hardware engineers, not hardware, an open network, or a system where each participant improves the product for the next.

My base case is that within 36 months Siemens acquires the team and Wave before hardware intelligence reaches $3 million ARR: high-value domain talent, but a point product whose advantage is absorbed by an incumbent distribution and file-format ecosystem. I would pass today. One condition flips the decision: evidence that at least three semiconductor teams are paying for Wave and that each deployment produces an exclusive reusable asset—such as proprietary verification knowledge or integrations—that measurably improves subsequent deployments.
