# Sintropix -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Moving a customer’s monthly close from day 20 to day 1 gets my attention. If Sintropix can make reliable accounting expertise radically cheaper across borders, the consequence could extend beyond faster financial reports. But closing 20 times earlier does not establish 20 times less work or cost. The dossier supplies a $675 million LATAM accounting-software market estimate, not a quantified accounting labor pool that Sintropix can eliminate or make universally affordable. I see a useful result. The orders-of-magnitude consequence remains unestablished.

The strongest evidence is that these founders have already changed what they build. Alfonso and Borja moved from logistics in December 2025 to manual bookkeeping in April 2026 and a proprietary ERP by September. Alfonso contacted more than 100 accountants; ten clients now span four countries, according to the September 19 reporting. That is meaningful contact with reality. Alfonso’s Toku product experience and Borja’s computing and AI background give them complementary tools. The sequence supports tactical flexibility, although the dossier does not identify which customer evidence changed their beliefs or show difficult recruiting against a specific weakness.

There is a serious bull case here: doing the bookkeeping exposes exceptions that a software vendor watching demonstrations will miss. Sintropix could turn that operating knowledge into repeatable automation, with its claimed 100-plus integrations supplying the workflow access. Its reported onboarding reduction from 30 days to 24 hours strengthens that possibility. My portfolio’s unstated pattern includes execution-heavy financial businesses, so lack of scientific novelty alone cannot settle this decision. The relevant known exception is **Block**: a small financial workflow wedge can expand into infrastructure. The material difference is that Sintropix has not yet established the larger value pool or expansion mechanism that would justify overriding weak technical evidence.

Today, the product evidence stops short of that override. Campfire already advertises matching, coding, and intercompany reconciliation; Rillet offers an AI ledger with approvals and audit trails. Sintropix’s customer outcomes deserve limited technical credit, but neither establishes a measured advantage against those alternatives. Account mappings and reconciliation histories might eventually improve automation; the dossier labels their defensibility as inferred. There is no demonstrated proprietary learning loop. That leaves exposure to my AI replication red flag, without enough evidence to conclude the founders are merely wrapping a standard model.

The first experiment should attack accounting reliability and the human work hidden behind it. Automated journals can propagate errors into financial statements, yet the dossier contains no accuracy benchmark, exception-handling labor measurement, or budgeted pass/fail test. That earns the missing-evidence default for risk sequencing, not a penalty for being pre-seed. Likewise, services starting at 20 UF monthly establish a selling price, not delivery economics. Without accountant hours, review costs, and migration effort per entity, I cannot tell whether software is collapsing the cost curve or people are absorbing the complexity.

I pass at 32/100. These founders show more adaptability than the product currently shows discontinuity. Ten clients make the service-led bull case credible; they do not yet establish scalable autonomous accounting. My flip condition is an independently audited deployment across three countries showing at least a 10x reduction in total accounting delivery cost—including migration, review, and exception handling—against customers’ prior workflows, with equivalent or better accuracy. That result would turn Sintropix’s central claim into evidence worth underwriting.
