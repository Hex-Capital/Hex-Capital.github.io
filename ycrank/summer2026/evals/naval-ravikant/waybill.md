# Waybill -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The founders possess knowledge acquired by doing. Rishi Laddha procured aerospace hardware and managed supply chain, cross-border operations, and government relations at LAT Aerospace. Tushar Goyal built WareIQ’s inventory and demand-forecasting system for more than 400 Indian brands. This is complementary, problem-specific experience—not credentials searching for a market. It is Waybill’s strongest evidence.

The product also attacks genuine coordination waste. A hardware team can submit a BOM and delegate quoting, payment, freight, customs, delivery, inventory, and invoice reconciliation to one principal. Requests spanning an obsolete RF component, 20 tons of aerospace-grade titanium, and four Cessna aircraft suggest unusual breadth after only two weeks live. The bull case is that the founders are turning fragmented Asian suppliers and brokers into a price-discoverable network, then using transaction history to improve every subsequent purchase.

But a service that coordinates a network is not yet a network effect. Waybill reports suppliers across India, China, and Southeast Asia and retains order, price, and lead-time history, but it provides no transaction volume, exclusive supply, repeat usage, or measured sourcing advantage. If a competitor copies the software tomorrow, the current defense is execution across customs, authenticity verification, and logistics. That is difficult work, but difficult work without compounding leverage becomes an agency with software.

The crowded market makes this distinction decisive. Cofactr, Forge, Jiga, and Luminovo already automate overlapping procurement workflows. Waybill’s broader operating responsibility may be a better wedge, and the $10.1 billion procurement-software market leaves room to maneuver, but the dossier does not yet prove that sourcing aircraft and titanium is a repeatable adjacent market rather than impressive inbound curiosity. Requests are evidence of demand; completed transactions are evidence of a business.

There is also no demonstrated user-to-user loop or direct value capture. No published price, take rate, revenue, completed transaction, or gross margin identifies who pays Waybill and how much value it retains. The known exception for an unusually strange, capital-efficient prototype does not rescue this: managed procurement is legible, competitors are funded, and no obsessive usage has emerged. I see no integrity, artificial-deadline, or founder-behavior red flag. The risk is structural rather than personal.

My 36-month base case is a useful managed-procurement company reaching $2 million to $6 million in annualized revenue, but remaining labor- and working-capital-intensive rather than becoming a compounding network. That outcome can still be valuable; it is not yet a nonlinear angel bet. I would change my decision if at least 20 paying hardware teams completed repeat purchases and their accumulated transaction data produced a measured landed-cost or lead-time advantage unavailable to a new entrant.
