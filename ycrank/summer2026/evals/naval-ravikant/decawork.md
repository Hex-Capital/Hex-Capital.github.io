# Decawork -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

$29 billion of identity security expanding to $56 billion gives Decawork pivot-room, while zero evidence of user-added network value leaves Decawork as a domain-credible enterprise control plane without compounding distribution. The highest dimension is market optionality: identity, agent security, IT automation, credential infrastructure, and run observability are adjacent surfaces. The lowest is network architecture: every Decawork customer can buy value independently, and the Nth enterprise does not inherently improve the first. Large market, linear business. Code leverage inside the product; labor leverage around the product.

**Uber** is the high rung for market pivot-room: black cars expanded into consumer rides, delivery, and logistics without abandoning the underlying coordination layer. Decawork shares the useful abstraction—a control plane mediating many actors across many systems—and Decawork already moved from autonomous IT administration to agent identity, permissions, monitoring, and retirement. The divergence is distribution. Uber’s marketplace made each new driver and rider improve liquidity; Decawork’s $29 billion identity-security market offers many directions, but Okta, Astrix, Oasis Security, and Token Security already occupy them. Pivot-room is real. Uncontested pivot-room is not.

**Perplexity** is the low network rung: a single-sided product where one user does not directly increase another user’s utility. Perplexity overcame that weakness through permissionless product distribution and Aravind Srinivas’s rare retrieval knowledge. Decawork has the same missing network mechanic without the documented override. Decawork’s integrations and accumulated policy templates may create switching costs, but private customer data is not a network; it is tenancy. No marketplace, community, shared corpus, or developer ecosystem is disclosed. The upside can compound through software margins, but not through users adding value for users.

Founder-led 30-minute demonstrations make Decawork’s leverage architecture legible over the next 18 months: every integration increases product utility, while every enterprise sale, security review, and gateway deployment increases human coordination. Decawork keeps credentials outside agents and routes tool calls through a gateway—good code leverage in execution—but the buyer remains an IT or security committee, and the inferred motion is negotiated direct sales. No public repository, self-serve product, organic developer adoption, or media-native channel appears. “Product and Media are New Leverage” applies cleanly: Decawork has leveraged production but permissioned distribution.

Sarthak Aggarwal built enterprise AI at NVIDIA and Ema, while Aman Raj built Barclays compliance tooling used by 240-plus teams; that places Decawork above the generic ex-FAANG-opportunity founder. Decawork’s prior autonomous-IT product also ran across multiple enterprises, evidence that the founders have touched the systems they now want to govern. The counterfactual that flips specific knowledge into the top tier is concrete: attributable security infrastructure shipped publicly, or named enterprises repeatedly using the current agent-control product under production load. Until then, Decawork shows trained domain experience and execution—not yet the non-replicable obsession visible in a founder who has spent a decade building the same artifact.

**Stack Overflow** is the single-anchor parallel for the 10-year-partner test: Atwood and Spolsky had public work, practiced the user’s craft, and shipped before the financing story. Decawork’s founders also shipped—Aman Raj’s fintech served 52,000 families and processed $1.5 million—but Decawork provides no public co-founder history, attributable GitHub work, or evidence about how prior ventures ended. Intelligence and energy read above average from NVIDIA, Ema, Barclays, and the production pivot. Integrity cannot be inferred from institutional logos. Play long-term games with long-term people, but first establish that the people have played together.

Okta’s 8,200-integration catalog makes my 36-month forecast that Decawork will pivot toward an embedded agent-governance layer, reach $1 million–$4 million ARR, and either sell to Okta for under $100 million or remain a subscale enterprise-security vendor. Decawork can win design partners because 88% of surveyed organizations report agent-security incidents, but Decawork must reproduce integrations, trust, certifications, and procurement access that incumbents already possess. A broad market keeps Decawork alive; a non-networked, direct-sales architecture caps the independent outcome.

The 88% incident rate is also the strongest bull case: Decawork may be early to a mandatory control layer, Sarthak Aggarwal and Aman Raj have relevant production experience, and the prior product already operated across multiple enterprises. **Anchorage Digital** proves that permission can become a moat when regulation and trust are the product; Decawork could similarly turn gateway concentration, audit logs, and scoped credentials into the trusted choke point for every enterprise agent. The case still loses today because Decawork has no disclosed current-product adoption, certification, moat, or public technical artifact, while Okta and three venture-backed competitors already provide overlapping controls. I would flip to Invest if Decawork verifies at least five named enterprises running the current gateway in production with repeat usage and demonstrates an integration or policy ecosystem that grows without proportional sales labor.
