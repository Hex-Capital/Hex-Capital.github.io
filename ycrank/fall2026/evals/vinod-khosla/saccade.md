# Saccade -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

A 15-minute test that makes Alzheimer’s pathology assessment broadly accessible could change who gets evaluated and when. Saccade has not yet established that consequence. Its claim of detection up to 15 years before symptoms does not quantify additional patients helped, diagnostic spending displaced, or improved outcomes. The $10.2 billion diagnostics market is a spending pool, not evidence of a resource multiplier. Earlier information becomes valuable when it changes a decision; the dossier notes that asymptomatic detection alone does not establish eligibility for Kisunla.

The technical work gives me a reason to investigate. UCSB documents custom cameras, illumination, chassis, and thermal management around a VR eye-tracking attachment. This is tangible engineering. But measuring eye movements reliably and detecting Alzheimer’s pathology reliably are separate achievements. Saccade’s homepage explicitly discloses unvalidated diagnostic and individual-risk performance. ViewMind already uses eye movements during cognitive tasks, while the blood test cleared by the FDA in May 2025 creates another clinical comparator. Neither the 15-minute duration nor the patent-pending label establishes superior accuracy, earlier detection, or lower cost.

The strongest bull case is that an inexpensive measurement system could generate a repeatable clinical dataset linking oculomotor behavior to confirmed pathology. That fits my preference for scientific workflows that become learning platforms. Bhardwaj’s medical-device and soft-robotics background supports the ability to build the instrument, and reported deployment in San Francisco clinics offers a possible route to collecting evidence. But the dossier does not document pathology-linked data, repeat assessment volumes, or complementary clinical recruits. The exceptions I make for an extraordinary instigator attacking an enormous consequence remain possible here; the supplied evidence does not establish that override. Founder learning and recruiting therefore receive the missing-evidence default, without a penalty for having only two people.

My next dollar would address one question: does the signal survive a blinded test against confirmed pathology? The dossier supplies no ranked experimental plan, budget, numerical threshold, or stop condition, so risk-reduction discipline also receives its default. Clinic deployment could accelerate that experiment. It could also commercialize uncertainty before resolving it. The launch’s diagnostic claims and the homepage’s research disclosures make that distinction consequential, although they do not establish deliberate misconduct. I would not fund broader distribution on the evidence presented.

Economics remain equally open. Fifteen minutes is an assessment duration, not a delivered cost: hardware utilization, staff time, unusable recordings, and confirmatory testing all matter. No pricing or operating model establishes parity with existing pathways, so economics receives its default rather than a subsidy-dependence penalty. **I pass with my own money today.** One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independent, preregistered study in Saccade’s intended asymptomatic population demonstrating at least 90% sensitivity and 95% specificity against confirmed amyloid pathology. Those are my proposed diligence thresholds, not company results. They would turn an engineered instrument into evidence of a diagnostic mechanism worth underwriting.
