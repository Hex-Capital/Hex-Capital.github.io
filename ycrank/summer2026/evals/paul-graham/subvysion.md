# SubVysion -- Paul Graham Evaluation

SubVysion is a schlep-rich company without a clearly organic origin: Maxwell Stone and Wenson Tang are already operating on construction sites across the United States, but neither is described as having personally suffered through underground-utility locating. SubVysion’s highest signal is willingness to combine cold calls, autonomous hardware, GPR interpretation, surveying, and field liability. Its lowest is the missing Dropbox-style story explaining why these founders had to build SubVysion for themselves. This is an animal pursuing someone else’s pain.

**Coinbase** (2012) is the high rung for schlep blindness because compliance was not incidental to the product; enduring it became the advantage. SubVysion has the same basic mechanism in physical form. SubVysion must drag a rover through job sites, reconcile multi-sensor GPR with surface positioning, produce centimeter-accurate maps, and persuade contractors to trust the result near infrastructure whose strikes cause an estimated $30 billion in annual damage. The divergence is important: Coinbase embraced a legible regulatory grind, while SubVysion claims “100% visibility” without publishing validation standards, insurance arrangements, or responsibility for missed utilities. SubVysion is running toward the schlep, but has not yet proved it can survive the liability hidden inside it.

The polished AI-wrapper from my pass example fails because its founders discovered a market before discovering a problem they themselves needed solved. That failure mechanism partly appears in SubVysion. Stone built on-device inference deployed in more than 10,000 pharmacies and worked on railyard mapping; Tang built a $500 LiDAR search-and-rescue drone. Those experiences make SubVysion technically adjacent to their lives, but adjacency is not organic necessity. Nothing says Stone once hit an unmarked gas line or Tang spent mornings deciphering utility paint. SubVysion could overturn this weakness if its first workflow came directly from a failure Stone encountered in railyard mapping, but the dossier does not establish that.

Wenson Tang cold-called contractors, converted one recipient into SubVysion’s first customer and investor, and helped put SubVysion onto live job sites before YC. That sequence compounds. Each site forces the founders to improve detection, positioning, contractor onboarding, and the awkward human process around uncertain readings; each successful deployment makes the next cold call less hypothetical. This is closer to Airbnb’s cereal-box lesson than to ordinary founder-led sales, though no disclosed contract value or unscalable stunt justifies the very top rung. Animals leave tracks. SubVysion has left some.

Maxwell Stone contributed to Roboflow’s roughly 2,400-star inference repository and shipped on-device systems used in more than 10,000 pharmacies, while Tang built a working LiDAR drone for $500. SubVysion therefore has hacker evidence rather than CS-degree evidence. The score would fall a tier if Stone’s role at Roboflow were merely employment on mature infrastructure and the rover were assembled mainly from vendors’ components. It would rise to the top if SubVysion published its own reconstruction system, field benchmark, or raw-sensor breakthrough. The present artifact is sufficiently non-trivial that I assume the hackers are real.

**Stripe** (2010) had two brothers whose long working history removed much of the cofounder risk before the company began. SubVysion also has the correct number—Stone and Tang cover machine learning, mapping, software, and field operations—but the founders reportedly joined forces only in January 2026. Attending Penn together does not prove they have endured bad news together. SubVysion gets credit for a complementary pair, not for the Collisons’ tested configuration.

Exodigo’s $200 million in funding makes the 36-month prediction unusually concrete: SubVysion will become a tech-enabled subsurface-survey service rather than a rover software platform and reach $3 million to $6 million in annualized revenue from paid project work. SubVysion’s schlep appetite can win regional contractor relationships even without a unique technical layer, while its weak organic origin makes it more likely to follow customers into services than insist on an elegant product abstraction. The cold-call-to-customer-to-investor sequence is how that business begins.

Exodigo’s 100-plus customers and 86.1 million square feet mapped are the strongest case that SubVysion gets crushed before reaching that range. SubVysion also carries a genuine red flag: “100% visibility” is a dangerous sentence when soil, depth, material, positioning, and insurance remain unresolved. The exception case is Airbnb—the idea-level objection can be overridden by founders who keep finding a way—and SubVysion’s first customer investing after a cold call is real evidence of that override. I would still invest, because SubVysion has shipped into the unpleasant part instead of presenting slides about it. I would reverse the verdict if SubVysion cannot show repeat paid deployments from at least three independent contractors with measured strike-detection accuracy within twelve months.
