# Waybill -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

The first facts I need are absent: proposed post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and whether this is genuinely the first scarce check. That earns the financing default, not a YC discount. The capital plan is equally undefined. Waybill says what the product does—source parts, compare quotes, route payment, clear customs, deliver inventory—but not what one finite experiment the round will fund or what dated result would dramatically change the company’s value. A founding-engineer opening is a use of cash, not a venture moment.

The strongest evidence is the founder-system fit. Rishi Laddha has operated aerospace procurement, supply chain, and government relations at LAT Aerospace; Tushar Goyal built inventory and demand-forecasting infrastructure used across more than 400 Indian D2C brands. Together they have shipped on both sides of the bridge: physical cross-border procurement and the software layer that records inventory decisions. This is not generic AI tourism. Waybill is also taking responsibility for the ugly work—supplier settlement, freight, customs, authenticity, last-mile delivery—which can create a valuable translation layer precisely because cleaner software competitors prefer not to own it.

But operational breadth is both the opportunity and the trap. A request for 20 tons of aerospace-grade titanium or four Cessna aircraft after two weeks is remarkable evidence that buyers will hand Waybill difficult problems; it is not evidence that a purchase closed, repeated, or produced positive contribution margin. The supplier network across India, China, and Southeast Asia and the retained order, price, and lead-time history could become a proprietary data pool. Today, no disclosed transaction scale, retention, sourcing advantage, or measured reliability proves that the data already changes purchasing behavior. Cofactr, Forge, Jiga, and Luminovo also mean the public procurement-software thesis is hardly undiscovered.

The bull case stings: these founders may be assembling a difficult physical operating system whose defensibility emerges from execution receipts rather than code. Broker verification, customs classification, fallback suppliers, and landed-cost history can compound into access that a workflow-only competitor cannot cheaply reproduce. Those unusual launch requests may be the first sign of exactly that pull. Still, the dossier does not show the conversion evidence that would justify overriding the missing price and capital-plan gates. Gartner’s forecast of agentic supply-chain software reaching $53 billion in 2030 is market fashion, not underwriting; the next model release does not solve counterfeit parts, working capital, or customs liability.

Delete the next financing and the model becomes unknowable. Waybill publishes no fee schedule, transaction margin, gross margin, burn, runway, or evidence that customers—not Waybill—finance the procurement cycle. A bundled payment covering parts, freight, duties, and delivery can support an excellent cash-generating business, or it can conceal working-capital exposure and negative contribution economics. Until one controllable cash metric appears, I am being asked to finance operational complexity on the assumption that data accumulation eventually pays for it.

I pass today. My flip condition is one documented cohort of at least 10 hardware teams completing repeat orders at positive contribution margin under a round priced below a $10 million post-money valuation, with the capital explicitly funding that dated repeat-purchase test. That single result would show scarce pricing, a real venture moment, workflow-caused purchases, and a path that does not require a Series A. Without it, Waybill is a well-matched team operating an interesting service, but not yet a check I can underwrite with my own money.
