# Orvixo -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Ethan Phillips and Kolya Schwinge make Orvixo an unsexy industrial-software swing whose highest placement is difficult physical-world operations and whose lowest is CEO authority: two Cambridge physicists are modeling factories, but neither is identified as the tiebreaker. Orvixo is pointed at machines, labour, tooling, and material constraints—the ugly operational substrate I like—yet Orvixo has two co-founders, no named CEO, and no disclosed governance protocol. Biz and Tiz are plausible. Rizz and command remain invisible.

**Crusoe** is the high rung because oilfield infrastructure made difficult software inseparable from difficult physical operations. Orvixo shares the load-bearing mechanism: every deployment must encode real machines, people, materials, capacity, and operating rules, so Orvixo cannot survive as a clever demo floating above the factory. The divergence is decisive, though. Crusoe owned capital-intensive equipment and lived on well pads; Orvixo has disclosed no hardware, regulatory moat, patent, proprietary dataset, or production deployment. Orvixo is visiting the frontier until a factory lets it run the schedule.

**GameCrush** is the low-rung warning: ambiguous authority let investors and internal conflict strip away the product’s DNA. Orvixo names Ethan Phillips and Kolya Schwinge only as co-founders, with no single CEO, equity structure, or tiebreaker protocol. That is not a cosmetic omission for Orvixo; factory integrations create constant arguments about product standardization versus customer-specific work. The **HQ Trivia** exception does not rescue Orvixo because HQ had tactile, explosive user pull, while Orvixo has no public customer, pilot, revenue, or repeat usage.

Ethan Phillips brings quantum-computing research, semiconductor analysis, and Attio growth experience; Kolya Schwinge brings applied ML and a senior engineering role at IFS. Over 18 months, Orvixo’s founder-origin mechanism either compounds through hundreds of planner conversations or fails inside integration purgatory: factory knowledge is tacit, political, and acquired beside the machines. Orvixo has technical adjacency, but no prior factory-planning employment or autobiographical moment of contact with the problem was found. My unstated sourcing pattern fails here—the founders appear to have approached manufacturing through capability, not because a broken production line once kept them awake.

Oden Technologies has raised $44.68 million, Maneva $38.4 million, and Sight Machine $86.97 million, which makes Orvixo’s category funded rather than magically weird. Orvixo may still be underpriced because no evidence says the Summer 2026 round is being chased, but “AI for factories” is no longer an aesthetic rejection in search of a believer. The counterfactual is sharp: Orvixo would move into my invest range if credible manufacturers or industrial investors rejected it specifically because factory-language model configuration looked too bespoke, followed by one planner proving those skeptics wrong. Without that rebuttal-versus-hard-no story, Orvixo receives the non-consensus default rather than a Crusoe premium.

**Flock Safety** is the single-anchor parallel for physical verifiability: a camera on a street produced an artifact I could see operating. Orvixo can eventually create an equally observable consequence—a schedule changes, a machine runs, a rush order ships—but Orvixo’s current artifact is described only through website claims about connecting ERP, MRP, and shop-floor data. A normal person cannot use Orvixo within 48 hours, and no factory has publicly shown Orvixo moving production. The physical world is present as the customer environment, not yet as product proof.

The $1.744 billion APS market growing at 20.4% supports a specific forecast: by July 2029, Orvixo will have retreated from autonomously “running factories” into an IFS-adjacent scheduling copilot or integration product and will remain below $1 million ARR. Orvixo’s industrial difficulty will create real consulting demand, but Orvixo’s absent operational proof, incumbent overlap, live-data burden, and unresolved founder authority will make full scheduling ownership too dangerous for early customers. The factory will permit advice before it permits command.

**DeepMind** is the bull case that stings: Cambridge physicists can be polymaths, and Kolya Schwinge’s IFS experience may be the hidden lived contact that public research failed to capture. Orvixo could combine symbolic optimisation with language interfaces precisely when manufacturers are finally ready to replace brittle APS systems; the DeepMind known exception says cross-domain technical compounding can override my outsider-founder bias. Orvixo still lacks DeepMind’s extraordinary prior artifacts, and no named factory has validated reliability, latency, or operational outcomes. I pass today, but one verifiable condition flips me: a named manufacturer publicly reports that Orvixo scheduled live production for six consecutive months with a measured improvement in on-time delivery or machine utilization.
