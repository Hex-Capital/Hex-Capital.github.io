# Control Seat -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The proposed secret is that a two-person company can unify industrial control, historical data, and AI investigation without forcing plants to replace their installed SCADA systems. What would prove it wrong? If each deployment remains a bespoke integration project, then Control Seat has discovered a consulting business, not a proprietary technology company. The dossier reports no reduction in deployment time, no technical result incumbent systems cannot reproduce, and no disputed belief rejected by named peers. “AI can now write complex software faster” is a fashionable timing claim, not yet a falsifiable secret.

The founders are the strongest fact. Warren Shepard and Jack Grodnick met in their first year at Dartmouth and later encountered industrial controls at SpaceX, Tesla, and related operating environments. This is not a convenience pairing assembled for YC. Their histories explain why they noticed the problem and why core engineering can remain in-house. But the dossier does not establish founder ownership, compensation, or explicitly confirm that both are full-time and economically exposed, so founding alignment is promising rather than complete.

Battex Chile provides real product evidence: its battery was remotely monitored and controlled through Control Seat’s MQTT infrastructure. The brownfield architecture is also sensible. Supporting OPC UA, MQTT, databases, and existing SCADA systems avoids demanding an immediate replacement of years of plant configuration. The bull case is that integration work becomes a proprietary map of industrial operations, with each deployment making subsequent installations faster and creating switching costs. This fits the unstated preference for operators turning neglected physical infrastructure into software leverage.

But resemblance to a moat is not a moat. No deployment-scale, retention, exclusive-data, dominant-share, or tenfold performance evidence is public. VTScada and Ignition are supported while FactoryTalk and Siemens WinCC remain “coming soon,” and at least $567.6 million has already financed Seeq, Litmus, and Augury across overlapping analytics, edge-data, and predictive-maintenance surfaces. Control Seat has not defined a bounded customer segment it can dominate; “industrial projects” is a route into a large competitive category, not a small monopoly market.

Distribution and economics expose the same ambiguity. Directly selling software plus PLC, SCADA, networking, deployment, and support can win early customers because one accountable vendor is valuable. It can also bind revenue to scarce engineering labor. Custom pricing, one deployment of undisclosed commercial value, and no reported sales cycle, implementation cost, gross margin, or repeatable buyer leave no arithmetic for a fund-returning engine. In 36 months, I expect Control Seat to remain below $5 million ARR and derive a material share of revenue from bespoke integration unless the product standardizes around one industrial vertical.

The strongest objection is that pre-seed industrial companies should be judged by proof of operation, not polished SaaS metrics: two long-aligned domain operators have already controlled a real battery remotely, and the compatibility layer could turn installed-system inertia from a barrier into distribution. That argument keeps me from a strong pass. It does not justify investing my own money today, because no known exception removes the failed monopoly gate: there is neither an independently verified zero-to-one technical event nor persistent customer behavior demonstrating that bespoke work compounds into software. My verifiable flip condition is three paid deployments in the same narrow vertical, each launched in under two weeks with at least $100,000 of recurring annual software revenue and declining integration hours. Until then: capable founders, real machinery, but commodity risk rather than a last-mover position.
