# Locke -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

Locke has already converted specific knowledge into sales. Parth Badhwar worked inside the White House, Deloitte’s policy team, a national campaign, and government-focused startup GTM before selling an integrated government-affairs service to defense, healthcare, and GovTech clients. More than $500K in annual revenue run-rate after six weeks is unusually dense evidence at pre-seed: the founder understands enough of the buyer, language, and transaction to make someone pay.

The problem is not demand. The problem is leverage. Locke’s agents analyze legislation, monitor signals, and draft proposals, but Quorum already offers policy data, AI intelligence, stakeholder management, and professional services to more than 2,000 organizations. If a competitor copies Locke’s software, what remains today is a lobbying firm with relationships and labor. The dossier identifies no proprietary dataset, exclusive access, model advantage, or workflow lock-in. Hiring policy staff alongside technical staff reinforces the risk that revenue scales with headcount.

The strongest bull case is that the service is merely the data-acquisition wedge. Every policymaker interaction, proposal, compliance brief, and contract outcome could train a proprietary system that learns which actions produce funded contracts. The initial revenue would then finance an asset unavailable to software-only competitors. But “could become data” is not yet a data moat. Locke has published no retention, outcome, data-scale, or cross-client learning evidence. A future compounding asset cannot defend the present valuation.

The market offers room beyond bill tracking: regulatory strategy, procurement intelligence, proposal execution, and government contracting share information and relationships. Locke is already operating across three regulated sectors. Still, the only quantified adjacent market is the $1.10B global advocacy-software category, and the dossier does not establish how the same product expands into a much larger transaction layer. Breadth of services is not automatically breadth of market.

Direct value capture is the second real strength. A run-rate above $500K implies paying clients rather than free usage, pipeline, or letters of intent. Yet pricing, contract duration, gross margin, concentration, and realized collections remain undisclosed. ARR language can make project revenue look recurring, and a two-person firm hiring expensive policy talent may be buying revenue with founder time and future payroll.

The unstated-pattern test is weak. Locke does not yet form a permissionless network, turn fragmented providers into searchable supply, or let clients create value for one another. No observed referral, contribution, or matching loop exists; each new account appears to require additional company labor. I see no disclosed integrity problem, manufactured financing deadline, or other founder red flag. I also see no known-exception mechanism strong enough to override the missing leverage: neither a forming network nor a uniquely durable prototype is visible.

I would not write the angel check yet. This can become a profitable AI-enabled government-affairs firm without becoming a nonlinear technology company. My forecast is that within 36 months Locke reaches roughly $3M–$6M ARR but remains a headcount-scaled services business unless it productizes contract-outcome intelligence. The flip condition is verifiable: show that retained clients generate proprietary outcome data and that revenue per policy employee rises for two consecutive quarters while software gross margin exceeds 70%.
