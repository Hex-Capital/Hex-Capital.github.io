# Collar -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

In 2032, I can imagine a CFO asking why an investor changed position and getting an answer assembled from six years of conversations, guidance, and holdings. Collar’s source-linked earnings assistance points toward that world. Getting there requires reliable institutional memory, then permission to use that memory during consequential conversations. The dossier establishes targeting, CRM, and live talking points; it does not establish those transitions or an artifact that reveals a different future behavior. I can supply the daydream. I cannot give Collar credit for supplying it.

Kazel Koh and Jamie Yau give me more to work with. Private-credit experience and an asset manager’s chief-of-staff role put them near the information problem, and both founded businesses before Collar. They met in 2022 and began collaborating in 2025; this is more substantial than two impressive résumés meeting at an accelerator. But the documented move from private-fund diligence in September 2026 to public-company IR in October leaves the particular obsession unproven. I would not call that opportunism. I also cannot reconstruct years of independent experiments, shipped artifacts, or first believers from the evidence provided.

Why does an IR team lose time assembling information it already possesses? Collar identifies scattered spreadsheets, documents, and relationship records. That is a credible operating irritation, and repairing mundane workflows fits my investing pattern. The dossier does not identify a gatekeeper collecting a specific rent or a rule withholding economic control from an individual. The buyer gets preparation time back; the institutional arrangement largely survives. Q4’s April 2026 AI CRM launch and Irwin’s FactSet integration also show that established vendors understand this irritation. Collar needs a reason customers will entrust it with the relationship memory those vendors already want to hold.

The strongest argument for writing a check is that earnings preparation could create that trust quickly. Collar reports roughly 40 hours saved monthly and 50% less Q&A preparation time, with IR partners representing $300 billion in combined market capitalization. A small team solving a recurring, consequential problem can become indispensable before anyone notices. Those figures deserve attention, but market capitalization measures the partners’ scale, and the savings claims lack cohorts or measurement methods. The prior diligence product’s advertised $1,000 monthly price does not establish current IR revenue. I see an offered product and reported utility, with limited evidence of repeated costly user commitment. My exception for pre-revenue companies requires affirmative prototype and founder evidence strong enough to carry the bet; missing revenue alone is not the issue here.

Collar’s privacy promise also matters: declining to train shared models on customer content respects the custody of sensitive conversations. I would not demand that the team weaken that promise to manufacture a data moat. Customer-specific histories may support retention, but 12,000 mapped portfolio managers do not establish exclusive control of an enabling asset. Nor does the dossier establish severe privacy abuse, deceptive leadership, or dependence on one closed model supplier; those red flags remain unsubstantiated. I pass because the positive evidence supports useful application software more strongly than the future-facing bet I want to own. My one verifiable flip condition is a referenceable issuer cohort using Collar through two consecutive earnings cycles, with records showing that its accumulated conversation history materially improves subsequent answers and drives renewal. That would turn the institutional-memory story into observed behavior.
