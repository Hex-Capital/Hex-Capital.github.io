# Petrarch -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

I start with three blanks: post-money valuation, competing-term-sheet count, and use of proceeds. YC participation does not prove an auction, but it does not prove scarce capital either. With no round terms, Petrarch gets the financing-silence default. More importantly, the dossier names no finite experiment the check will fund—only an operating model spanning sourcing, legal clearance, redaction, restructuring, and resale. Capital that makes five activities happen faster is not yet a venture moment.

The strongest evidence is that the dark pool may already exist. Petrarch has completed transactions involving construction plans, autonomous-driving recordings, and codebases; in one case it sourced, redacted, and transferred several thousand construction plans within a week. That is materially better than 1,091 LinkedIn followers or a sourcing fellowship because assets actually crossed the bridge. The restructuring network and in-house PII workflow connect two mistrustful systems: legacy companies with legally messy dormant data and AI buyers that need usable, cleared inputs.

The bull case stings. Samuel Hahn sold PII-redaction and compliance products, Ian Lee built training-data infrastructure and advised Scale AI on quality filtering, and Sudhish Swain shipped computer vision into hospitals. Company-reported buyers range from first-time founders to Fortune 500 enterprises. If those transactions were paid, repeatable, and exclusive, Petrarch could accumulate proprietary supply, rights history, and pricing data while competitors merely list datasets. That would be a real translation-layer business, not another generic AI application.

But the exception evidence is not yet hard enough to override the missing deal and capital plan. No revenue, repeat purchase, exclusivity, buyer-model improvement, commission rate, or gross margin is disclosed. Defined.ai, Troveo, Human Native/Cloudflare, and Datarade already cover adjacent sourcing and licensing functions, while Petrarch can be disintermediated after introducing buyer and seller. My 36-month base case is therefore a services-heavy data broker producing $1 million–$3 million in annualized revenue, not a compounding marketplace: the workflow creates transactions, but the dossier does not show that Petrarch owns either side after the first one.

Deleting the next round exposes the problem. There is no burn, runway, take rate, contribution margin, or controllable cash target from which to underwrite a profitable path. The three founders have unusually relevant data experience, but none has documented operating receipts inside restructuring or legacy industrial asset sales, and the dossier does not identify who has final authority among three Harvard classmates. Those are distinct risks: missing supplier-side depth limits the bridge, while unnamed decision ownership can turn a complex legal marketplace into a committee.

I pass at the disclosed evidence level. The one verifiable flip condition is twelve months of transaction records showing at least three repeat buyers, positive contribution margin after redaction and legal-review costs, and contractual exclusivity over the supplied datasets. That would prove the apparent dark pool survives the first transaction and could finance Petrarch without another capital-factory round.
