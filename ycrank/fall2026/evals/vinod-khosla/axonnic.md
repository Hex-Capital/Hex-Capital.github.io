# Axonnic -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Human dexterity could be a consequential bottleneck in automating physical work. Axonnic’s wager is that muscle signals expose something cameras miss: the effort and preparation behind a successful grasp. If that information makes delicate manipulation learnable, the consequence extends beyond better motion capture. But the dossier does not quantify labor displaced, production capacity unlocked, or a plausible order-of-magnitude resource improvement. I can see the mechanism for a large outcome. I cannot yet calculate its consequence.

The technical work deserves attention. Axonnic reports 4.58° average joint-angle error at 50 predictions per second after 78 minutes of training data, with held-out Apple Vision Pro recordings used for validation. That is concrete prototype evidence. It does not establish the economically important claim: that recording muscle activity produces substantially better autonomous robots. Predicting human joint angles and teaching a robot to sew reliably are different accomplishments. MANUS supplies glove-based capture; Hemlock already pursues EMG data. Leaving fingers uncovered may improve collection, but Axonnic has not demonstrated a 5–10x advantage against either alternative.

The strongest argument against passing is that Axonnic may be collecting precisely the missing supervision for physical intelligence. Its policies subsequently operate using robot sensors, so customers need not instrument every deployed robot with human muscle sensing. The Proception hand integration, Italian manufacturing LOI, and recordings of electricians, plumbers, and demolition crews give this possibility substance. This matches my preference for turning scarce expert workflows into repeatable learning platforms. Yet the platform depends on autonomous transfer and reusable data rights; neither is established. The LOI and reported Perloro Packing pilot may describe related demand evidence, so I would not count them as two independent validations.

Williams brings EMG research and practical hardware experience; Patankar brings medical context and robotics software work. Those are relevant ingredients for this particular problem. They do not document rapid belief revision or the recruitment of complementary people to eliminate a named weakness, so that dimension stays at its missing-evidence default. Likewise, the integration proves progress without establishing a deliberate sequence of dollars spent against the largest kill risk. There is no disclosed experiment with a threshold, budget, and stop condition. No revenue is normal at this stage; the missing economic evidence is the cost of producing a useful, transferable robot policy, including calibration, retargeting, and support.

I pass at 32. Axonnic has genuine sensing work, so the generic AI-wrapper red flag does not fit. But my exceptions for an extraordinary instigator attacking a quantified giant value pool, or demonstrated exponential progress with overwhelming consequence, are not established here. Nor does the dossier show a path to unsubsidized cost advantage; that remains unknown rather than adverse. My one verifiable flip condition is a paid, controlled sewing trial showing at least 5x more defect-free autonomous output per total dollar than the customer’s best vision- or glove-trained baseline, sustained across held-out demonstrators and recording days. Include collection, recalibration, hardware, and support costs. That result would connect Axonnic’s sensing achievement to a production discontinuity worth buying.
