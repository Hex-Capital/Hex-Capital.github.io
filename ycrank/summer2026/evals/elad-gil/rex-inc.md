# Rex -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: tool-using agents now let enterprise finance teams automate judgment-heavy order-to-cash work across fragmented systems, and the controlling variable is whether Rex can absorb growing transaction volume without proportional finance headcount or costly human supervision. Synthesia provides unusually useful pre-seed evidence. Rex completed 22,000 autonomous actions in four months, retrieved more than $40 million in purchase orders across 30–40 portals, and handled a doubling of inbound AR email without added headcount. Those results connect autonomy to customer economics, although the dossier does not yet specify error rates, intervention rates, or a repeatable threshold across deployments.

This is more than an output wrapper. Rex converts customer practices into editable procedures, operates across ERP, CRM, billing, inbox, and procurement systems, takes actions under customer guardrails, and retains approvals and audit history. Better models should expand the exceptions it can resolve while the company continues to own permissions, workflow state, and responsibility for execution. The under-three-week Synthesia deployment is important because enterprise integrations often destroy the economics of this category. It is still one detailed implementation, however, not proof that onboarding remains fast across heterogeneous ERP environments.

Product-market pull is the strongest part of the dossier. More than $500 million in receivables under management and weeks-to-hours reductions in follow-up cycles are operational evidence, not registrations or an undated waitlist. Outcome- and SLA-based pricing also aligns Rex with completed work rather than AI-seat consumption. Revenue and retention remain undisclosed, so receivables volume cannot be treated as ARR, but a two-person company already performing consequential work inside a named customer has crossed a meaningful pre-seed threshold.

The bear case is market structure. HighRadius serves more than 600 clients and has raised $475 million; Kolleno already markets agents across collections, reconciliation, disputes, and risk; Tabs approaches the same budget from contract-to-cash. Rex has not shown a structural barrier, and its claimed institutional context may be reproducible by a well-funded competitor that implements the same customer procedures. If deployments require substantial hidden services or approval labor, the product retains BPO economics while presenting an AI interface. Incomplete SOC 2 Type II certification further constrains the enterprise channel precisely when incumbents can sell security and integration coverage.

The founders make that competitive risk more tolerable. Merlin Kafka spent four years as Sequence’s CPO, while Lewis Blackwood was a founding Sequence engineer, an early GoCardless engineer, and previously founded Personably. They worked together for four years around finance infrastructure before starting Rex. This is documented second-time learning and direct workflow exposure, although the dossier does not establish personal desperation or costly commitment beyond leaving established roles. Their advantage must appear in deployment speed and exception handling, not biography.

I would invest, with the view that Rex can reach $15–30 million ARR within 36 months by becoming the execution layer for enterprise receivables rather than another O2C dashboard. The single flip condition is clear: I would pass if the next five enterprise deployments cannot each reach at least 80% autonomous completion of their designated workflows within 90 days without incremental customer AR headcount. That test simultaneously resolves implementation economics, model durability, and whether the Synthesia result generalizes.
