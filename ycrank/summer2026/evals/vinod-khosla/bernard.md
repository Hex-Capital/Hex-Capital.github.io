# Bernard -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Bernard addresses a real operational mess, but the consequence-of-success case is too small as documented. The dossier quantifies a $1.90 billion appliance-repair-software market and a $6.1 billion field-service-management market; it does not quantify repair spending, wasted truck rolls, technician scarcity, or the number of households whose economics change. Better preparation before dispatch may improve first-visit completion. That is useful. Nothing here yet shows a 10x cost collapse or a societal bottleneck being removed.

The strongest evidence is deployment, not technical discontinuity. Bernard claims thousands of calls handled daily, usage by large authorized servicers, and a named deployment with Mr. Appliance of West Loop. Appliance-specific repair histories, parts data, manufacturer guidance, and completed-job feedback could create a better learning loop than generic dispatch software. But there is no diagnostic-accuracy rate, first-visit-completion comparison, avoided-truck-roll figure, or benchmark against ServiceTitan, Probook, or human dispatchers. An operating product is not automatically a Black Swan.

The bull case stings because my broader pattern does admit execution-heavy software wedges. Bernard could use appliance pre-diagnosis to become the system that controls intake, parts, scheduling, technician knowledge, and eventually the entire repair transaction. The initial hardware wedge at Block similarly looked incremental before expanding into financial infrastructure. The material difference is that Bernard has not demonstrated an equivalent value pool or expansion mechanism: its documented SAM grows only 5.62% annually, incumbents already bundle adjacent workflows, and ServiceTitan generated $961 million in FY2026 while adding its own AI voice and dispatch capabilities.

Capital should now attack diagnostic reliability, the largest named kill risk. The dossier acknowledges probabilistic recommendations and defers final judgment to qualified technicians, but supplies no cheapest decisive experiment, threshold, cost, or stop condition. Nine people, multiple integrations, and enterprise deployments may prove implementation capacity; they do not prove that spending is removing the core risk faster than incumbents can copy the workflow. Compass, Encompass, Marcone, Early Repair, and Samsung GSPN dependencies also make integration breadth a source of fragility rather than technical defensibility by itself.

The founders are credible operators: Katzman brings strategy, operations, and partnerships experience; Goodfellow brings enterprise automation and operational-product engineering. That combination fits the deployment problem. Yet the evidence shows neither a material belief revised after customer data nor difficult recruits mapped to diagnostic, appliance, or model-risk ownership. Likewise, paid subscriptions establish willingness to charge, not Chindia-price economics: Bernard publishes no pricing, labor savings, customer payback, retention, or cost per resolved repair.

I pass. My verifiable flip condition is a third-party-auditable cohort across at least ten servicers showing a 5x reduction in pre-dispatch diagnostic labor, at least a 2x increase in first-visit completion, and customer payback within twelve months. That would convert Bernard from a vertical workflow integrator vulnerable to bundled incumbents into a measurable cost-collapse engine with a proprietary learning loop.
