# GrayPass -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

GrayPass’s possible secret is that authorization should expire between login and a sensitive action, and behavioral evidence can renew it without interrupting the legitimate user. The test is whether this prevents unauthorized payments, permission changes, or exports at an acceptable false-rejection rate and latency. But who disputes the proposition? BioCatch, TypingDNA, and Twosense already recognize that authentication cannot end at login. The dossier establishes an important problem; it does not establish an important truth these competitors reject. I give the secret the absent-evidence default rather than mistake an unproved disagreement for a refuted technology.

The decisive question is whether application-enforced, action-bound authorization creates a company that can own something. GrayPass returns short-lived signed proof, but a signature authenticates the decision’s provenance; it does not establish the accuracy of the behavioral judgment underneath it. The company describes behavioral continuity as experimental and unapproved for live enforcement. Meanwhile, TypingDNA supplies authentication APIs, and Twosense connects behavioral mismatches to policy remediation. There is a product distinction here, but no demonstrated monopoly mechanism within a bounded customer segment. Ownership of an SDK and models establishes ownership of code. It does not establish that customers cannot obtain the same result elsewhere.

The founding structure is more encouraging than the competitive structure. Mathur and Ranjan met on their first day of high school in 2021 and subsequently built projects together. This avoids the specific danger of strangers assembling a company to enter an accelerator. Their shared history earns affirmative credit; their software and computational neuroscience backgrounds do not independently prove commitment or control. Full-time status, founder ownership, and responsibility for essential engineering remain undisclosed. I would investigate those facts without assuming either economic alignment or a founding defect.

The strongest objection to passing is that GrayPass might capture the enforcement point while established vendors remain suppliers of signals. An application must actually stop a protected action; another fraud dashboard cannot substitute for that control. This also fits my interest in adversarial information systems: determining whether the expected person remains in control is economically consequential. European pilots, a reported San Francisco customer win, and the CEO’s claim of “five figures in revenue” suggest someone will engage with the product. Those are meaningful pre-seed signals. Yet the customer claims come through founder search snippets, and the dossier does not show customers using behavioral continuity to enforce live decisions. The bull case requires evidence that this control point becomes difficult to replace.

Founder outbound, demonstrations, and workflow-specific evaluations constitute an actual acquisition route, so distribution deserves more than its default. Its economics remain unsettled. At the $500 monthly starting price, $100 million in ARR would require roughly 16,667 customers before higher usage charges or larger contracts. That is illustrative arithmetic, not a forecast: negotiated action metering could produce much larger accounts. But application-specific enforcement hooks create implementation work, and neither contract expansion nor delivery margins establish that this sales motion can support that scale. The disclosed revenue’s unspecified period also prevents treating it as recurring revenue.

I pass on GrayPass today because the competitive gate fails, not because a two-person company lacks mature metrics. My exceptions for independently demonstrated technical breakthroughs or persistent transaction demand do not remove this risk: pilots and early receipts establish interest without establishing an irreplaceable position. The founders’ history makes the team worth following, but cannot turn overlapping authentication capabilities into a monopoly. One verifiable result would change my pass to investigate: an independent, paid production comparison in a named customer segment showing tenfold fewer successful unauthorized actions than the best available alternative at comparable false-rejection rates and latency. That would supply evidence of a technical advantage where today there is principally an architectural claim.
