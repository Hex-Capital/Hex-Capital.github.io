# Robocurve -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Inspect Robots v1 and one physical-robot pilot make **Robocurve** a technically coherent workflow layer with no visible buying curve. That is **Robocurve’s** signature: highest on single-miracle architecture, lowest on revenue velocity. Connecting 40-plus VLAs and more than 260 language models suggests the underlying model capability already exists; the unresolved question is whether independent physical evaluation is a product customers repeatedly purchase or merely useful research infrastructure.

**Color Genomics** is the high rung on single-miracle architecture because Color built workflow infrastructure atop commodity sequencing rather than betting on new chemistry. **Robocurve** shares that mechanism: Inspect Robots orchestrates existing models, ROS, Isaac Lab, and physical hardware instead of requiring a robotics breakthrough. **Robocurve** diverges because reproducible tasks across robot form factors, evaluator credibility, and a paid engagement model remain unresolved simultaneously. Those are business and operating dependencies rather than new science, but they keep Robocurve below the Color rung.

**Mistral** is the low revenue rung because pre-revenue, capital-intensive AI normally triggers my red flag; Mistral cleared it only as a Known Exception after ChatGPT reopened the category and founders had shipped Llama at Meta. **Robocurve** has one pilot, 96 GitHub stars, 13 forks, and no disclosed revenue or repeat usage. That is not “just working” yet. Robocurve lacks Mistral’s overriding catalyst and prior at-scale shipping evidence, so silence on a purchasing curve remains the dossier’s hardest negative signal.

Forty-plus VLAs and a stated industry target of general-purpose robotics by 2028 provide **Robocurve** with a plausible market tailwind, but not the specific recent catalyst that reopened defense for Anduril or foundation models for Mistral. For **Robocurve** to flip from a middling catalyst score into the top tier, a concrete event must force labs or customers to buy independent evaluation—an insurer underwriting autonomous robots, a regulator requiring third-party testing, or a major deployment failure exposing vendor-run benchmarks. Without that event, “more models need evaluation” is category growth, not market re-opening.

**Perplexity** is the founder-shipping anchor: Aravind Srinivas repeatedly built discussed features between meetings. Jay Chooi contributed to Inspect Evals and worked at the UK AI Security Institute; Aris Zhu built navigation and fleet-management systems at Amazon Robotics and Yondu Robotics; **Robocurve** then shipped Inspect Robots v1 and completed a physical pilot. Those are real artifacts, not credential theater. **Robocurve** still lacks evidence of Perplexity’s tight build-feedback-build cadence, but Chooi and Zhu have independently shipped on both sides of the software-robotics interface.

Ninety-six GitHub stars and 13 forks can compound **Robocurve’s** reach over 18 months by making Inspect Robots the common interface through which labs publish comparable results. The index outcome is attractive: every additional VLA, robot platform, and benchmark could increase the value of **Robocurve** without Robocurve predicting which vendor wins. The opposite mechanism is equally live—the MIT license, vendor-run substitution, and absence of switching costs could make Inspect Robots a useful standard owned economically by nobody. Stripe captured payments flowing through its APIs; Robocurve has not shown that physical evaluations must flow through Robocurve.

One completed pilot and no published pricing point toward a specific 36-month outcome: by July 2029, **Robocurve** will have pivoted from an independent public evaluator into vendor-facing robot QA and deployment observability, with less than $2 million ARR and independent comparative ratings no longer the primary product. **Robocurve’s** software is technically de-risked enough to survive, but buyer conflicts and the economics of continuously operating physical environments will pull Robocurve toward private testing contracts rather than a neutral benchmark institution.

Aris Zhu’s Amazon Robotics experience, Jay Chooi’s evaluation background, integration with 40-plus VLAs, and the absence of a direct physical-evaluation competitor form the strongest bull case: **Robocurve** could be Color Genomics for robotics, a workflow layer atop the hard thing that becomes an index on embodied AI. That Known Exception genuinely stings. The dossier still gives **Robocurve** only one pilot, unpublished comparative results, no conflict-management policy, and no evidence that labs will outsource evaluation instead of using their own hardware and telemetry. I would flip to Invest if Robocurve reaches at least $500,000 ARR from five independent robot developers, with each purchasing a repeat evaluation within twelve months; that would convert the current false signal into a buying curve.
