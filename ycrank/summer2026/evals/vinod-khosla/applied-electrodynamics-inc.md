# Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Bilgehan Avser’s electromagnetics PhD, 35+ patents, and Apple antenna work make **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** a technical-risk-heavy, founder-defiance-light bet: extraordinary career hardware experts attempting a difficult instrument, with no documented evidence that they reject the assumptions their industries taught them. **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** may solve three real miracles—radio penetration across variable materials, 1-mm reconstruction, and sub-minute room-scale scanning—but the world-state still looks like construction with less rework, not construction fundamentally abolished. That is an impressive product. It is not yet a consequential company.

**Commonwealth Fusion** is the high rung for technical risk because plasma confinement, magnet performance, and commercial fusion economics repelled investors before the $115M Series A; failure was embedded in the physics. **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** shares the load-bearing mechanism: its 1-mm claim must survive material-dependent attenuation, multipath interference, and reconstruction ambiguity, and FCC classification could constrain the usable radio architecture. But **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** diverges where it matters—the dossier describes completed radio hardware and imaging algorithms, not a capital-hostile scientific program requiring more than $50M before a commercial milestone. The physics is nontrivial. The financing risk is not yet Commonwealth Fusion risk.

**Glean’s** weakness on founder defiance was domain-expert continuity: an ex-Google search founder extending expertise rather than contradicting it, rescued by the consequence of reorganizing Fortune 500 information. The same red-flag pattern is stronger at **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.**: Avser, Brian Huppi, Paul Leutheuser, and George McLean spent years building antennas, sensing hardware, product enclosures, and imagers at Apple, Nest, and Humane, then continued directly into another sensing product. **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** has found no documented McKinsey-was-wrong moment—no public rejection of RF-imaging orthodoxy, no thesis reversal, and no controversial technical position beyond self-published performance claims. Credentials predict competence. They do not predict an instigator.

The company-cited **$20B+ of annual avoidable construction rework** gives **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** a mechanism that can compound over 18 months: verified scans could generate material-specific training data, improve reconstruction, and make every deployed device more accurate. But **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** currently spreads that mechanism across construction, industrial inspection, security, cargo, and defense—buyers with different workflows and regulatory constraints—while Walabot already sells a $199.95 RF scanner. Unless one beachhead turns WaveSight from a better inspection tool into the default source of as-built truth, success captures part of a $20B cost pool rather than breaking an economic regime. “Make a great company and the money takes care of itself” does not mean every expensive inefficiency becomes a great company.

The founders’ **8+ years of shared work at Humane** could conceal substantial learning, but **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** provides no documented pivot, public thesis update, or “we were wrong” moment. The learning-rate score flips upward only if Applied Electrodynamics, Inc. can show that a failed assumption at Humane or during WaveSight development caused a specific change in frequency architecture, target market, or product form—and that the revised system performed better. Humane’s HP acquisition is not itself evidence of learning. **Slash** earned the exception through a visible pivot; biography alone does not earn it.

**QuantumScape** required deep capital because battery chemistry, manufacturing yield, and automotive-scale production had to be financed long before commercial proof. **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** has hardware, imaging algorithms, preorder registration, and five people, but no published price, factory plan, deployment budget, or milestone requiring more than $50M. **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** therefore does not yet match my concentrated-check advantage; a generalist hardware investor can finance product validation. Required capital may increase if the real business becomes defense imaging or industrial nondestructive testing, but I do not award capital intensity before the roadmap exists.

WaveSight’s undisclosed preorder count and FCC coordination burden lead to a falsifiable forecast: within 36 months, **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** will shift its primary go-to-market from general construction toward industrial nondestructive testing or U.S. defense, reaching $2M-$6M ARR without becoming a broad construction standard. **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** has the technical depth to build a valuable instrument, while fragmented procurement, Walabot’s low-cost substitute, and material-dependent performance make horizontal construction adoption unlikely. The highest dimension produces a working product; the lowest dimension produces a narrower ambition than the technology initially implies.

The strongest bull case is the **Glean exception**: elite domain experts can win without defying their field when proprietary execution turns an obvious problem into a category. **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** brings 250+ reported patents across its members, Apple-grade hardware experience, hundreds of Build Expo conversations, and a claimed leap from Walabot’s four-inch depth to across-room 1-mm mapping. That case stings because the founders may have already built the miracle. It does not yet win: **Applied Electrodynamics, Inc.** has no independent performance validation, no disclosed preorder count, uncertain ownership of those patents, and only a $457.32M GPR proxy for market scale. I would flip to Invest if an independent general contractor or industrial operator verifies 1-mm-scale performance across representative materials and signs at least $10M of purchase commitments.
