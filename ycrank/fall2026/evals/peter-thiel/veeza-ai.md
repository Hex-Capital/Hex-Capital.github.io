# Veeza AI -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

Veeza AI knows whose problem it is solving: Egyptian travelers who face scarce visa appointments and repeated paperwork. What does it know that its competitors believe is false? The dossier establishes the inconvenience and a functioning service, but no disputed proposition or experiment demonstrating a secret. Computer-use agents completing government forms could create considerable value. Their availability to every visa platform leaves the ownership of that value unresolved.

The initial market is concrete: Egyptian applicants, with Arabic and English assistance. But geography is a starting point, not a monopoly mechanism. Atlys already offers AI assistance; iVisa/HelloGov combines automation with specialists. Veeza’s document storage establishes neither exclusive information nor meaningful switching costs. More fundamentally, the company automates access to appointment inventory it does not control. Its terms acknowledge that appointment scarcity and portal changes can interrupt service. Better execution could win customers while leaving the underlying business exposed to competition.

The strongest objection to passing is that customers are already arriving. Veeza reports growth from 1,000 to more than 7,000 users since joining YC, approximately 400 WhatsApp community members, and paying customers. Founder content, WhatsApp conversations, and advertising constitute an actual acquisition route; proposed travel-agency introductions remain prospective. This is stronger evidence than a fashionable launch announcement. It supports demand and gives the team a practical channel for learning. Without channel conversion, acquisition cost, or repeat-purchase evidence, however, it does not yet establish distribution that compounds.

Consumer travel is compatible with my revealed investment behavior; physical technology is not a prerequisite. Nor would I dismiss Aly Moursy’s personal experience traveling on an Egyptian passport or his product work at Efreshli. But those credentials do not establish founding alignment. Reporting describes a solo founder subsequently adding a team member, without documenting that person’s role, shared working history, ownership, or either person’s full-time status. I assign the missing-information default rather than invent a founding red flag. There is also no documented repeat-founder reconstruction of a previously operated visa business that would justify an unusually strong prior.

The revenue engine is intelligible: charge EGP 1,500 for several Schengen destinations and EGP 4,000 for UK or US applications. That earns more credit than an unspecified monetization plan. Yet human review, retries, and support must fit inside those fees, and neither contribution margins nor purchase frequency is disclosed. The reported doubling of revenue has no absolute baseline or measurement period. A useful regional service can emerge from this model; the dossier does not establish the economics of a defining company.

I pass with my own money. The exception for demonstrated customer demand deserves consideration, but these users establish willingness to seek assistance, not a mechanism preventing competitors from capturing the same demand. There is no independent technical breakthrough or realized exceptional economics that removes that concern. One verifiable result would reopen the decision: an independent operating audit showing Veeza completes comparable Egyptian Schengen applications at one-tenth the fully loaded cost of competing services, with equivalent accuracy. That would turn an automation claim into evidence of proprietary advantage.
