# OpenVector -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Andrey Gizdov’s CVPR 2025 vision research makes OpenVector a tactile physical-world bet with a governance-shaped hole in the middle. OpenVector can watch a car wash, recognize an event, and operate DRB SiteWatch; I can see the machine doing the work. Yet OpenVector names Gizdov and Vishal Urlam only as co-founders, with no CEO or tiebreaker. The technical “Tiz” is visible. The Biz and Rizz still belong to two people standing behind one steering wheel.

Flock Safety is the high rung: ordinary cameras became observable infrastructure in the physical world. OpenVector shares that load-bearing mechanism because OpenVector connects live streams to calls, messages, webhooks, POS actions, and a $449-per-month Station. The divergence matters: Flock had cameras appearing in neighborhoods, while OpenVector currently offers simulations, refundable $100 reservations, and fall-2026 California delivery. I can touch the promise, but not yet photograph a fleet of deployed artifacts.

HQ Trivia is the low-rung warning: a magnetic product temporarily concealed unresolved founder authority until the partnership fracture became part of the failure. OpenVector’s Gizdov and Urlam met at MIT and won the Google Prize together at Grand Hack 2025, but OpenVector provides no named CEO, operating hierarchy, or conflict protocol. Friendship and prize-winning collaboration are not tiebreaker authority. “Who decides?” is not paperwork trivia; it becomes existential the first time a 92%-confidence action incorrectly changes a customer’s POS record.

Gizdov’s Harvard, MIT, and Weizmann work and Urlam’s Tata Power, IoT, and physical-computing experience give OpenVector authentic contact with the technology. OpenVector’s weaker founder-origin signal is contact with the customer’s pain: neither founder is shown running a restaurant, warehouse, gym, or car wash. Over 18 months, technical experience compounds only if deployments create corrections, video-event data, and operational trust; otherwise OpenVector remains brilliant vision research visiting somebody else’s workplace. The founders are of the machinery, but the dossier does not yet prove they are of the problem.

Spot AI’s $93 million, Coram AI’s $66 million, Ambient.ai’s $72 million-plus, and Verkada’s $700 million-plus make OpenVector less non-consensus than the phrase “active operator” suggests. OpenVector would flip into truly magically weird territory if named design partners were paying for autonomous actions that the funded security incumbents refused to attempt, or if sophisticated investors passed because operating a POS from a camera seemed too dangerous. Today OpenVector discloses only unnamed design partners and a YC check. This may be underpriced physical AI, but the dossier does not yet show the beautiful rejection that tells me everyone else has misunderstood it.

SpaceX is the clean parallel for capital intensity: a difficult artifact becomes defensible because making it work in reality is harder than describing it. OpenVector’s optional offline Station, continuous-time dynamics, predictive coding, and attention-driven spatial sampling give OpenVector more technical bone than an AI wrapper. A $6,499 appliance operating existing cameras is still far from rocket-scale frontier hardware, however, and Jetson Thor lowers the same edge-compute barrier for competitors. The swing is unsexy industrial infrastructure, but the moat must come from installations and proprietary event-response data—not the box.

OpenVector will pivot by July 2029 from a general-purpose “operator for any camera” into car-wash automation, reaching $3 million to $7 million in ARR through camera-to-DRB and payment workflows. OpenVector’s physical verifiability will create real pull, while its weakest dimension—unclear strategic authority—will make the horizontal vision difficult to hold; one vertical with visible errors, savings, and integrations will force the decision. The professional-daydreamer version is horizontal intelligence. The business version is one ugly workflow repeated across hundreds of sites.

Ambient.ai and Coram AI supply the strongest bear case: OpenVector could be an undifferentiated camera-agent feature trapped between well-funded incumbents, foundation-model commoditization, and two co-founders without a declared CEO. OpenVector’s separate Foveal Vision positioning strengthens that objection because it suggests the founders have not decided whether they sell operational automation or bio-inspired compute efficiency. I still write a small personal check because Gizdov’s published vision work, Urlam’s physical-computing background, and the Station’s camera-to-POS action form a tangible technical artifact—not a four-logo AI-agent deck. I flip to Pass if OpenVector has neither a shipped Station nor a named multi-site paying deployment by March 2027.
