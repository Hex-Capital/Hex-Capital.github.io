# Derya -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: agentic software now lets hardware companies outsource fragmented freight operations, but Derya becomes large only if each monthly plan produces durable software-like gross profit after the human work required to source, clear, track, and rescue shipments. That contribution margin per account—not the $13 trillion logistics headline—is the controlling variable. The dossier identifies the mechanism but supplies neither plan pricing nor operating hours per shipment, leaving the primary gate only partially proven.

Demand is the strongest evidence. Derya reports $80,000 of contracted revenue and $3.2 million of sourced freight opportunities in its first month, while TVO committed 20 containers. Contracted revenue is weaker than collected revenue or renewal, but this is unusually informative pre-seed pull: a customer trusted an unfinished two-person company with physical goods, customs exposure, and delivery responsibility. The open question is whether this represents recurring product-market fit or founders temporarily performing an excellent logistics service.

The technology case is less developed. Gartner projects agentic supply-chain software growing from under $2 billion in 2025 to $53 billion in 2030, but category growth does not establish Derya’s economic step change. Specialists still execute services, the company assumes exception-heavy operational responsibility, and its flat fee cannot absorb unlimited workload. Without gross-margin or labor-per-shipment evidence, AI may improve coordination while leaving the underlying service economics intact.

Derya nevertheless owns more workflow than a thin AI interface: it sources, books, clears, tracks, stores, and distributes freight, with customer approvals and operating guardrails. Better models should improve parts of that loop rather than erase the entire product. But there is no documented integration architecture, proprietary exception data, automated-action rate, or technical barrier. Flexport, Forto, Nuvocargo, and 5U AI already cover adjacent combinations of forwarding and automation, so breadth alone is not defensibility. The unstated-pattern case for funding a new layer during a discontinuity is real; the countervailing red flag is a service-heavy product claiming software leverage before demonstrating software economics.

The founders materially strengthen the case. Karaca worked in maritime law and at CEVA/CMA CGM, previously built Ivy Consultants to six-figure ARR, and Turna built ship-operations software used on more than 30 vessels representing $200 million in fleet value. Their eight-year working relationship and prior company-building experience indicate useful second-time learning, although the dossier does not document the costly commitment or desperation required for full credit.

My 36-month base case is that Derya reaches roughly $3 million to $8 million of ARR but remains an operations-intensive managed freight business rather than a category-defining software platform. The bull case stings: $80,000 contracted in month one may be the first evidence that customers prefer an unbundled, no-markup logistics office, and the initial workflow could generate reusable distribution into procurement and inventory. The relevant known exception—physical operations earning product-style margins—does not yet apply because no margin reconstruction is documented. I would monitor rather than invest today. The flip condition is verifiable: demonstrate at least $1 million of annualized recurring revenue at 70% or better gross margin without increasing operations headcount for two consecutive quarters.
