# Altair Health -- Sam Lessin Evaluation

Altair Health has moved beyond a billing demo: a founder reports that it handles an actual substance-use-treatment practice’s billing and collects several hundred thousand dollars monthly from insurers. That earns attention. It does not establish what my money buys. Proposed post-money, competing term sheets, and the experiment this round finances are all undisclosed. I cannot call this an auction, but I cannot underwrite a cheap first check either. Those are unresolved investment gates, even for a functioning product.

The strongest evidence is the path from clinic visits to a design partner to responsibility for collections. A practice handing over a consequential workflow is a much stronger signal than Altair’s 121 LinkedIn followers or its EHR waitlist. This fits my preference for businesses that connect systems whose participants cannot easily coordinate: here, independent practices and insurers. The potential dark pool is practice-specific billing knowledge and payer-response history. But customer access is demonstrated; proprietary ownership and repeatable acquisition are not. One deployed relationship does not yet establish that the next practice becomes cheaper to win or serve.

The strongest objection to passing is that Altair may already have found the right way to make AI economically useful. Agents propose actions, a theorem prover checks formalized insurer policies, and human billers handle exceptions. That architecture acknowledges errors rather than assuming they disappear. A narrow billing service could accumulate operating knowledge while collecting fees for work customers already purchase. The problem is that a proof establishes compliance with encoded rules; it cannot establish that the insurer’s requirements were encoded correctly or completely. Without verification coverage, exception rates, or labor per claim, I cannot distinguish scalable automation from a competent billing operation with an elaborate software layer. Adonis and Anomaly already address overlapping workflows, so the architecture needs operating receipts to become an advantage.

Aouss’s customer wins at Grafana Labs and AppDynamics—including Coca-Cola, HSBC, and Barclays—are concrete commercial execution evidence. Jad brings healthcare-ML research and founding engineering experience at Helical AI. That combination supports selling and building across enterprise software and healthcare. It provides less evidence of prior operation inside insurance billing itself. The reported year between initial conversation and full clinic deployment also matters: Altair has crossed a difficult implementation boundary, but that deployment gives me no basis yet to assume fast replication.

Delete the next financing from the model. What remains? Client collections, an undisclosed managed-billing fee, unknown human-service costs, and no disclosed burn or break-even plan. The several hundred thousand dollars belongs to the practice; it is neither Altair revenue nor demonstrated incremental collections caused by its software. The separate $395/year prior-authorization listing cannot fill that gap. I would manage this business against monthly cash contribution per practice after all billing and exception labor. A free EHR could improve distribution, but until that connection is observed, it is another product to support.

I pass at **39/100**. Altair’s embedded workflow makes this more interesting than a generic AI application, and missing financing information is not evidence of bad financing. But the exceptions I make to price and capital-plan gates require unusually hard conversion, retention, cash economics, or proprietary-access evidence. This dossier establishes meaningful customer responsibility without establishing those economics. One offer would change my decision: a documented sub-$10-million post-money round funding a six-month, auditable test of whether managed billing can reach monthly cash break-even, with human exception labor fully included. That would give scarce capital a specific truth to reveal.
