# Bear -- Elad Gil Evaluation

Bear’s opening is that improving agents let SMBs buy completed accounting through one provider that owns both the software and the service. The controlling variable is human work per correctly closed customer account: does that fall enough to turn fixed-price accounting into a business with substantially better margins? That is my underwriting hypothesis. The dossier establishes the service model but supplies neither a measured threshold nor results against it. The $740.1B accounting-services estimate does not resolve how much value Bear can capture.

The strongest evidence is architectural. Bear describes a proprietary ledger, document reconciliation, invoices, payroll, and accountant-validated filings. Its disclosed customer workspaces, permissions, audit trails, and evaluations against reviewed outputs give it actual workflow state. Better models could complete more of that workflow while Bear retains the customer relationship and responsibility for delivery. This fits my preference for integrated institutional work. The remaining distinction is between having an evaluation process and demonstrating that it reliably reduces exceptions.

There is also meaningful demand for a pre-seed company. Sami reports more than 10 paying customers; the July 2026 ledger post reports over $80M processed for dozens of Finnish companies. I give the paying relationships credit, discounted for the undated founder claim. Transaction volume demonstrates accounting activity, but it is neither revenue nor evidence of attractive account economics. This is enough to take the product seriously without inferring retention, expansion, or a repeatable acquisition channel.

The strongest argument against passing is that ownership of the ledger could let Bear redesign accounting around agents more completely than competitors working inside existing systems. A modest initial customer base could then become distribution for additional financial services. That possibility matters. But Balance already combines agents with accountant signoff, and Integral and Skalar demonstrate that capital is entering this category. Owning the ledger also asks customers to migrate. Bear needs an economic advantage large enough to justify that friction. The July research blog attributes the opportunity to better agent performance during 2024–2026, but provides no quantified reduction in delivery cost or improvement in margins. The risk is an accounting practice whose software improves while its labor requirements remain substantial.

The founders offer relevant financial-data engineering experience: Sami and Riki worked at Token Terminal, and Riki built petabyte-scale infrastructure. For the narrower founder criterion, Sami’s reported acquisition of Angomon supplies some evidence of prior entrepreneurial experience, although the buyer, outcome, and lessons are unspecified. I would not infer desperation from a dropout biography. My research-stage exception requires documented frontier research and a credible capital path; that evidence is absent here. The exception for changing service or physical-business economics requires demonstrated unit-economic change. Bear has not established it. Nor does the dossier establish a scarce-talent acquisition case that would substitute for an independent business.

I would pass on a personal check today. Bear’s workflow ownership is substantially stronger than its demonstrated market economics, and I do not want to mistake the former for proof of the latter. My single flip condition is a six-month paid customer cohort showing at least 60% gross margin after all delivery costs—including accountant review, corrections, and support—with no material accounting or filing errors. That would directly test whether Bear’s technology changes the service’s economics.
