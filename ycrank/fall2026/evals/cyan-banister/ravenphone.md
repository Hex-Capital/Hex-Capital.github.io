# Raven -- Cyan Banister Evaluation

Six years forward, I can imagine asking my phone to prepare a meeting and finding the relevant email, revised document, and message already handled. Raven supplies a small, concrete leak from that world: an organizer-confirmed prototype that searches flights, generates interfaces, and changes calendar events. But the distance matters. Reliable execution must come first, permission to act across workplace systems next, and habitual delegation after that. The dossier demonstrates pieces of execution; it does not yet establish the transitions that make this an employee’s everyday phone.

Why does that future require another device? Raven’s illustrated Gmail-to-Drive-to-Slack workflow attacks an understandable irritation: the employee becomes the integration between applications. Returning that person’s attention is attractive. But there is no identified fee, access restriction, or institutional rule that Raven removes, and no measured coordination cost. Rabbit already executes across existing devices, while Brain has SoftBank distribution. Raven needs a reason the phone itself unlocks something those alternatives cannot deliver. Otherwise, the useful workflow can survive while the hardware proposition disappears.

Isa Usmanov is the strongest reason to keep looking. His YC biography reports $3.4M-plus GMV and 32,000-plus users at droov, $1.1M-plus ARR at an earlier ed-tech business, and a Reinio sale to a laundromat chain. Those are prior-company claims, not Raven traction, but they indicate experience turning software into something customers transact through. The Android project adds relevant building experience. What remains unestablished is a sustained obsession with this particular problem and evidence that he can recruit the complementary team needed to operate a phone business. I see a commercially experienced builder; I cannot yet trace the long-running conviction behind this device.

The strongest argument against passing is that Raven has already escaped the slide deck. The hackathon win corroborates a working artifact, and founder-reported initial shipments could put it into real hands. An employee who voluntarily changes phones to delegate work would be making precisely the inconvenient commitment I want to notice. But the reported 160,000 demo views and hundreds of signups do not establish that behavior, and shipment quantities, payment status, and repeat usage are undisclosed. Raven does not need revenue to qualify for my pre-revenue exception; it needs the combination of exceptional founder obsession, working technology, and concrete outside commitment. Here, the artifact is clearer than the other two.

Raven also touches two patterns I like: mundane workflow repair and a possible control layer beneath future applications. The first is visible; the second remains a possibility. The dossier identifies no controlled execution asset, proprietary context base, or scarce distribution channel. Meanwhile, email, documents, and calendar-changing authority make consent and recovery part of the product’s usefulness. Missing safeguard documentation does not establish a severe privacy violation, but it prevents me from treating broad access as an advantage. The branding ambiguity likewise warrants clarification, not an accusation about leadership.

I pass with my own money today. My verifiable flip condition is an independently documented workplace pilot showing that employees repeatedly choose Raven over their existing phones for completed cross-application work, with measured time returned and inspectable permission and recovery controls. That single observation would connect the appealing future to an actual change in someone’s life. Right now, Raven has demonstrated that a phone can act. I still need evidence that people want to entrust this phone with the work.
