# Daqstra -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Daqstra has identified an expensive annoyance, not yet a consequential discontinuity. The dossier names a $48.65 billion test-and-measurement market and an energy pilot targeting more than $100,000 in annual customer savings, but neither figure establishes the resource consequence if this works. Reconnecting instruments, scripts, and files wastes engineering time. Eliminating that waste could support a useful company. The evidence does not show a 10x increase in physical-R&D throughput, a radically lower cost per validated design, or a newly accessible class of experiments.

The product is more substantive than an AI wrapper. Daqstra has operated live LOX/IPA static-fire campaigns, deployed across multiple rocket-engine stands, and built bidirectional integrations with NI, Siemens, Modbus, OPC-UA, and custom-TCP hardware. Sending commands, redlines, and abort sequences distinguishes it from telemetry-only software. But an integration list is not a technical benchmark. There is no measured result against LabVIEW, vendor PLC software, Nominal, or Sift showing 5x faster configuration, 10x more tests per engineer, or materially better safety and reliability.

The founders are the strongest part of the option. Darell Chua and Anchit Kumar built and tested liquid rocket engines together, co-authored an AIAA paper on distributed data acquisition, and accumulated systems, autonomy, and more than ten Mojave campaign experiences before starting the company. Active aerospace programs reportedly use the product day to day. This is genuine workflow knowledge, not three generic AI résumés searching for an industrial use case. What remains unproven is the criterion I care about: no documented belief revision, complementary magnet recruit, or example of assigning a new owner to a risk the founders discovered they could not solve themselves.

The bull case is that a scarce scientific workflow becomes a repeatable production platform. Persisted configurations, calibration lineage, test histories, and executable procedures could compound into proprietary operational knowledge while every deployment makes the control layer harder to remove. The Block exception is superficially relevant: a small team can begin with modest technical novelty and expand across a giant existing value pool. The material difference is that Daqstra has not bounded its software value pool or demonstrated a platform expansion beyond bespoke multi-vendor integration. Nominal has raised $182.5 million and Sift $67 million for overlapping territory, while instrumentation vendors can move upward from installed hardware.

Capital should now attack the safety and repeatability kill risk, not finance more connectors. A decisive milestone would compare Daqstra with the incumbent stack across several independent customer sites, measuring configuration time, completed tests per engineer, abort reliability, and deployment labor. Today the signed pilot supplies a savings target but no price, gross margin, onboarding cost, or evidence that the same implementation can be reproduced without founder-heavy services. My 36-month base case is an integration-intensive business at $1 million to $3 million ARR, useful to a narrow set of aerospace and energy teams but not yet a category-defining R&D operating system.

I pass today. The maximum loss is manageable, but the upside has not been shown to be 100x rather than a better control-and-data layer in a crowded market. The single flip condition is verifiable: three paying industrial customers independently documenting at least a 5x increase in completed physical tests per engineer, with no degradation in abort reliability and without bespoke deployment work dominating the contract. That result would convert integration competence into a technical discontinuity and make the platform case investable.
