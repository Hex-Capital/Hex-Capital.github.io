# Allia Health -- Elad Gil Evaluation

The market map is straightforward: behavioral-health practices now need interoperable operations and outcome-aligned payer relationships, independent clinicians are the buyer and supply base, and the controlling variable is whether Allia can convert free-EHR adoption into profitable reimbursed care through its clinical group. The relevant ratio is not TAM or registered clinicians. It is contracted clinicians who submit paid claims, remain active, and generate contribution margin. Today that funnel is 8,000 daily clinician users to 600 activated clinicians, with $50M of annualized care capacity under signed national payer contracts. That is enough to make the belief falsifiable, though not yet validated because care begins in September 2026.

Product-market pull is unusually strong for pre-seed. One thousand practices use the system daily across nearly one million monthly visits and 300,000 patients. This is operating behavior, not a waitlist. The reported six clinician-hours saved weekly supplies a plausible adoption mechanism: replacing intake, notes, scheduling, prescribing, billing, referrals, and outcome measurement with one system. The weak point is economic evidence. Allia has demonstrated workflow adoption and payer contracting, but no paid claims, realized revenue, retention cohort, or medical-loss and contribution-margin data.

The product is more durable than a generic AI feature because it owns permissions, clinical documentation, prescribing, billing, referrals, claims, and longitudinal outcomes. Better models should improve task completion inside that system rather than eliminate the system of record. The free EHR also creates reusable distribution into the clinical group. But Headway has already reproduced much of the software wedge, offers its own free insurance-native EHR, and has $327.5M of financing. Therefore Allia’s moat cannot be “AI plus clinician data.” A well-funded competitor could reproduce that layer. The defensible asset must become the operating and contracting performance of the integrated group.

The strongest bear case is that Allia has disguised a regulated, service-heavy provider organization as software. The North American mental-health EHR market is only $0.77B, national operations introduce multi-state compliance and payer complexity, and SOC 2 and HITRUST certification were still in progress while the platform handled protected health information. If the group retains ordinary healthcare margins, the free software merely subsidizes clinician acquisition. The February 2026 Part 2 changes and CMS Integrated Behavioral Health Model improve the institutional environment, but the dossier does not quantify faster reimbursement, better margins, or lower delivery costs resulting from them.

I still lean invest because Allia has already assembled both sides of the transition: daily workflow distribution and signed payer capacity. The founders also combine healthcare research, mental-health experience, technical work, and clinical co-founders, although the dossier documents neither second-time startup learning nor the costly commitment that would justify a high founder-motivation score. No known exception is needed—the company clears the market gate on current evidence—but the red flag around premature scaling remains real. The single flip condition is the first contracted cohort: if by March 2027 Allia cannot document paid claims from at least 300 activated clinicians with positive contribution margin and at least 70% six-month clinician retention, I would pass.
