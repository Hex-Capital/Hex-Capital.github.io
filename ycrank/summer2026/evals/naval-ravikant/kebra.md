# Kebra -- Naval Ravikant Evaluation

The strongest evidence is what the founders have done, not where they worked. A two-person team has put a multimodal product into field-service companies across Florida, Pennsylvania, and California, with Quality Air named as an active user. Hadife’s Microsoft AI work and Hosny’s systems background explain how they could build it; technicians and back-office staff actually using Kebra show that they have begun learning the exact problem through action. What is missing is evidence of outcomes—jobs processed, hours saved, claims completed—or prior field-service knowledge that predates the product.

Kebra reconstructs fragmented jobs, requests missing evidence, and writes claims, parts orders, notes, and invoices into existing systems. That is useful leverage, but useful software is not necessarily durable software. The Company Brain contains customer-specific audio, images, transcripts, communications, and operational records, yet there are no retention, dataset-scale, or accuracy-improvement measurements. The privacy policy says customer content remains tenant-specific and is not used to train generalized models. If a competitor copies the code, the dossier does not establish a proprietary model or cross-customer data advantage that survives.

The bear case is unusually concrete because ServiceTitan, Jobber, and Housecall Pro already control the systems of record and distribute overlapping AI features. Kebra’s overlay avoids replacement friction, but the same position makes it dependent on integrations while incumbents can bundle individual workflows. Each new contractor appears to require another sale, implementation, and isolated Company Brain. There is no permissionless contribution loop, price-discoverable network, or evidence that one customer improves the product for another. The product works while the team works; it has not yet shown that value compounds while they sleep.

The bull case stings. A live product across three states is meaningful at pre-seed, the $5.10 billion FSM market is growing toward $9.17 billion by 2030, and the same capture-and-completion capability can move from HVAC into plumbing, electrical, claims, parts, and broader field operations. A capital-efficient working prototype can justify an exception to missing network and monetization evidence, as Notion once did. The material difference is that Kebra enters a legible category against heavily financed incumbents, while the dossier shows neither a strange new product language nor a durable asset those incumbents cannot reproduce.

No integrity, artificial-deadline, founder-morale, or advisory-obligation red flag appears in the evidence. The problem is structural rather than personal. There is also no disclosed price, paid customer, recurring revenue, take rate, or unit economics. Live usage earns product credit, but it cannot be silently converted into evidence of direct value capture.

My base case is that Kebra can reach $2–5 million ARR within 36 months through founder-led sales, but remains an integration-heavy workflow vendor rather than a compounding network. That may become a good business; it is not yet the asymmetric option I would buy with my own money. I pass and reopen if at least 20 paying field-service companies remain after 12 months and demonstrate that accumulated Company Brain context measurably improves automation accuracy or expansion revenue.
