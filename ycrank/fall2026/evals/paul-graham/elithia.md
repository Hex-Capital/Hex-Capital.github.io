# Elithia -- Paul Graham Evaluation

The useful fact about Elithia is that physician expert witnesses and medical-legal firms reportedly buy it. These are people with a specific, unpleasant job: reconstructing a patient's history from thousands of pages. That is a promising place to start. But I want to know what happened when Sarthak Jain tried to make the first customer successful. Which records broke the product? What did he change? How quickly? The dossier gives me sales and a feature list, but no episode connecting resistance to a founder-built workaround. That leaves my most important founder test unanswered.

Jain's Oxford computer science background and unstructured-data engineering at Winton make his ability to build plausible. Elithia describes a concrete workflow: remove duplicate and blank pages, organize records, produce a chronology, link findings to source pages, and export to Word or PDF. That earns partial technical credit. It is more substantial than an ambition to automate claims. But the evidence remains a company-described product, without an inspected output or measured accuracy result. Source links make mistakes easier to investigate; they do not establish how often important facts are omitted. Technical credentials cannot supply that missing measurement.

The strongest case for investing is that Elithia has chosen the person who must actually understand the medical evidence. A physician reviewing another case could become a repeat customer without requiring an entire insurer to adopt a new system. Reported sales deserve meaningful credit even without a revenue figure. The first 1,000 pages free also give these users a way to test their own records. What remains unknown is whether they return and pay for the next case. The conference appearance under Minerva Labs suggests access to the niche, but it does not answer that question. Nor does a family medical background establish that Jain personally encountered this problem before deciding to build.

Competition would not make me pass by itself. DigitalOwl's overlapping chronologies and evidence-linked analysis make the decisive question more specific: what does an expert witness repeatedly need that the existing products handle badly? Elithia could discover that through close service and build around it. That is the bull case that troubles my pass: a technically capable founder may already be learning something valuable from paying specialists that public research cannot reveal. But the dossier contains neither a documented customer-driven revision nor the operational work that would demonstrate this advantage. The Minerva Labs name change supplies no evidence of a changed product route.

Elithia only partly matches my recurring preference for concrete proof before a round: it reports sales, but supplies neither a numerical technical result nor demonstrated repeated use. Its product is also an application rather than the programmable substrate that appears in several of my investments; that limits the relevance of that pattern without disqualifying the company. I see no documented dependence on a major partner, premature delegation, or refusal to accept contrary evidence. Leaving an MBA establishes none of those red flags. Still, my exceptions require unusually convincing working-product evidence, unmistakable user pull, or independently validated proof. The reported sales are encouraging but too thin to justify an override. I would pass on this evidence. One verifiable flip condition would be a documented customer episode in which Jain fixes a specific review failure within weeks and the customer subsequently pays to process additional cases. That would connect the founder, the artifact, and the need.
