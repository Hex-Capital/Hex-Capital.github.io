# Stratum Industries -- Peter Thiel Evaluation

The proposed secret is that government adjudication can be decomposed into auditable model steps while human officers retain only the final decision. What would prove it wrong? If one agency cannot deploy Stratum on a bounded application class with materially faster processing and no deterioration in accuracy, appeals, or disparate outcomes. The dossier offers no such experiment. Instead, federal policy already favors AI adoption, and three funded vendors sell adjacent automation. This is presently a consensus slogan, not a falsified consensus.

The sharper problem is the absence of a small market. Stratum proposes permits, patents, drug approvals, grants, and benefits across federal, state, and local government. These are not one workflow; they have different rubrics, evidence standards, procurement authorities, and consequences of error. GovWell already serves more than 150 agencies and 5,000 processes, while Polimorphic and Clariti have each raised $28 million. Calling Stratum “cross-domain” enlarges the competitive field without identifying a segment it can dominate. No proprietary dataset, exclusive integration, measured technical advantage, or switching cost exists today.

The founders are the strongest evidence. Raj and Vihaan Jagiasi are brothers rather than convenience-matched co-founders. Vihaan was founding engineer number three at document-oriented AI company Cadastral before its June 2026 acquisition; Raj reports averaging 220% of quota at Deel and ranking among more than 1,000 representatives. This combines in-house agent engineering with unusually credible founder-led selling. Yet neither founder has documented government-procurement or adjudication experience, and the dossier provides no cap-table or prior joint-shipping evidence. Long alignment is plausible; complete economic and operating alignment remains unproved.

The bull case stings because the pieces fit unusually well. April and May 2025 federal directives created a procurement opening; the product retains human authority, cites its work, and targets an enormous backlog; Raj may sell what Vihaan can build. A successful deployment could accumulate agency-specific rubrics and reviewed cases, turning today’s commodity models into tomorrow’s proprietary workflow data. This could resemble the exception made for a crowded AI market after a genuine zero-to-one technical event. But Stratum has disclosed no equivalent event—no customer, pilot, accuracy result, processing-time reduction, or repeated use.

Nor is the economic machine visible. Direct agency sales are inferred from a demo form, but the buyer, contract price, sales cycle, implementation burden, and gross margins are absent. The $34.4 billion global smart-government TAM cannot establish that one adjudication product supports a power-law outcome. Broad government ambition plus heterogeneous compliance creates a coordination stack: procurement, integrations, testing, impact assessments, appeals, and program-specific accuracy must all work before scale. Definite optimism requires a definite plan.

I would pass today. The likely path is a retreat from “the entire U.S. government backlog” into one narrower permitting or licensing workflow, where Stratum may build a respectable business but will meet incumbents on their established ground. One condition flips the decision: a named agency deploys Stratum on a bounded adjudication class and independently verifies at least a 10× reduction in processing time without worse error or appeal rates, while granting access to reviewed case data competitors cannot obtain. That would convert slogan into secret and product into a possible monopoly.
