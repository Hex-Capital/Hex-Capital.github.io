# Simantic -- Elad Gil Evaluation

AI-generated firmware increases the amount of code embedded teams can produce; those teams now need validation that runs at software speed. Simantic’s controlling variable is the share of hardware-dependent testing it can reliably move ahead of physical prototypes. If that share becomes material, simulation becomes infrastructure for every coding agent touching firmware. The founders’ reported waits of weeks or months establish why this matters. The dossier does not establish how much of that delay Simantic actually removes, or the silicon agreement required before customers trust its results.

The product has meaningful substance. Executing compiled firmware, inspecting registers and peripherals, and building and testing pull requests reaches into the engineering workflow. Better coding models should increase demand for that loop. But faster code generation does not automatically produce cheaper validation: someone must build and maintain accurate peripheral models. Simantic documents incomplete and unmodeled targets, while its silicon-validation standard describes a plan rather than demonstrated coverage. There is a credible economic discontinuity here, but no measured reduction in engineering hours, prototype iterations, or validation cost.

The strongest case for investing now is that this becomes the independent testing layer across embedded coding agents. Embedder and BoardRepo could provide distribution at the moment firmware is generated or boards are designed. That fits my preference for infrastructure spanning a technology transition. The counterweight is substantial: Renode already supports peripheral modeling and CI, and Simantic’s own Python SDK defaults to it. Owning the PR execution loop earns credit; owning a difficult-to-reproduce simulation advantage remains unproven. I would want the time and money required to reproduce its validated coverage, rather than treating privately developed engines as sufficient evidence of a moat.

Demand is the weakest part of the current case. The announced partnerships identify plausible channels, but BoardRepo describes planned testing, and there is no documented repeat production usage. The $100 monthly Developer plan establishes an offer, not willingness to pay. Approximately $600,000 raised and 938 LinkedIn followers do not resolve that distinction. More concretely, the SDK asks users to defer production pipelines. That limits how much confidence I can place in the existing integrations becoming an indispensable release gate.

Hong’s firmware and robotics experience and Shahriar’s Neuralink, Tesla, and semiconductor work make the problem selection credible. Their biographies do not establish prior startup learning or costly personal commitment, so I would leave that dimension at its default. Nor does this dossier justify a research-stage exception to demonstrated demand: relevant engineering experience is different from documented frontier research with a credible path to financing the remaining technical work. Simantic also avoids the direct manufacturing-margin problem, although maintaining customer-specific models could still create service-heavy economics.

I would monitor Simantic and hold my personal check. The opportunity is attractive, but the evidence currently supports a useful developer tool more strongly than a large infrastructure company. One verifiable result would change my decision: a 90-day cohort of three unrelated paying engineering teams making firmware merges conditional on Simantic’s checks, with subsequent silicon testing confirming those checks meet each team’s documented acceptance threshold. That would connect workflow ownership to trusted deployment and customer pull—the missing link in this market map.
