# Caveman -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

Cheap intelligence could remove an important bottleneck to deploying agents. Caveman has not yet quantified that consequence. The dossier’s $863.7 million optimization-market estimate measures a vendor category, not the spending displaced or expertise made accessible. I want the arithmetic connecting cheaper inference to radically more useful work. That arithmetic is absent. This leaves the consequence case unproven; it does not establish that the opportunity is small.

The technical claim deserves attention. Caveman reports 89% token removal with 94% evidence retention on long documents—roughly ninefold compression. But retained evidence is not completed work. The omitted 6% could contain the fact that determines whether an agent succeeds. JetBrains’ test of the earlier skill found 8.5% fewer output tokens but 11.6% higher aggregate billed cost, driven by an outlier. That result does not invalidate the October 13 Cloud launch. It establishes why I would underwrite cost per correctly completed task, including retries and optimization overhead, rather than tokens removed. There is a potentially meaningful mechanism here, but no demonstrated 5–10x advantage against Edgee or LiteLLM.

The strongest argument against passing is Julius Brussee’s distribution. One founder has produced a repository with 115.5K stars and 6.7K forks; the earlier extension reached 20,000 users. His agent-runtime and retrieval experience also matches the problem. My preference for tiny teams with an engaged technical community registers here. So does my willingness to back execution-heavy businesses when the value pool is enormous. Caveman could use that reach to become the trusted place where developers test optimization decisions. But stars spanning several product generations do not establish committed adoption of the new infrastructure. Nor does the product transition alone demonstrate belief revision after disconfirming evidence. The exception for an adaptable team attacking a giant existing value pool remains plausible, rather than established.

Recording workloads, replaying proposals, and using shadow tests and canaries is sensible engineering. Those controls also address the central product danger: saving money while quietly degrading task performance. They do not establish a company-level milestone with a decisive threshold, budget, deadline, and stop condition. I therefore give no additional risk-retirement credit for the existence of deployment gates. Similarly, the published $0.00035/event, $0.012/minute, and $0.0001/routing-decision rates tell me what Caveman charges, not whether customers retain substantial savings after paying it. Missing revenue is normal at this stage. Missing evidence of net economic advantage leaves the investment mechanism unresolved.

I pass with my own money. The relevant red flag is competition through reproducible gateway functionality; the dossier has not established that Caveman is merely a wrapper, but Apache-2.0 code and overlapping competitors make proprietary learning especially important. **One result would change my decision:** an independently reproducible, 30-day production study across three unrelated customers showing at least 5x lower all-in cost per successfully completed task against their existing configurations, with predefined quality thresholds and no material regression. That would connect the compression mechanism to economic discontinuity. Developer attention gives Caveman an unusually good opportunity to run that experiment.
