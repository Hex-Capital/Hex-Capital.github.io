# Perceptron ML -- Vinod Khosla Evaluation

The consequence case is presently too small and too vague. Perceptron ML could reduce expensive attorney hours and prevent fabricated citations, but the dossier quantifies neither the hours displaced nor the cost reduction. A $1.445 billion legal-AI market growing to $3.918 billion by 2030 describes a software category, not a 100x change in access to legal expertise. I see a useful business if it works. I do not yet see an outcome whose consequence compensates for a 90% probability of failure.

The grounding engine is the only potentially non-incremental mechanism. Claims allegedly cannot surface unless traced to checked primary sources, and Michael Marcotte’s NVIDIA work on production agents and formal verification is relevant technical preparation. Three deployed systems—timekeeping, monitoring, and research—suggest the team can make software work inside real firms. But there is no accuracy benchmark, no measured reduction in attorney time, and no disclosed comparison with Harvey, which already supports more than 25,000 customer-created agents. Private deployment and firm-specific training are implementation choices until Perceptron demonstrates a 5–10x capability or cost advantage.

The strongest bull case is organizational rather than scientific. Personal generative-AI use among legal professionals rose from 31% to 69% while firm-level legal-AI adoption remained only 34%. That gap creates demand for governed deployment, and the anonymized case studies suggest Perceptron may already be crossing it. Execution-heavy workflow companies can become platforms, and a two-person team able to ship bespoke systems deserves more credit than another undelivered AI pitch. The problem is that the same evidence supports the bear case: “built to order” may mean implementation labor, while Harvey, Legora, and Eve already possess capital, integrations, customers, and overlapping customization capabilities.

The largest kill risk is whether source-gated generation is materially more reliable than ordinary retrieval systems without becoming too slow or expensive. The dossier supplies no experiment, error threshold, cost, owner, or stop condition for that risk. Three deployments are encouraging product activity, but they do not establish that capital is being spent against the decisive uncertainty. The July-to-August repositioning from a cross-industry event engine to law firms could reflect fast belief revision; without the evidence that prompted it, it could equally be category shopping. Neither founder has documented legal-practice experience, and no risk-specific legal or commercial recruit is identified.

The economics are worse than merely undisclosed. Pricing, recurring revenue, deployment labor, inference cost, and support burden are all absent, while the product is explicitly customized around each firm’s matters and environment. That is a path to a good consultancy before it is a path to software economics. The known-exception case for an initially incremental product would require either an enormous existing value pool with room for platform expansion or an unusually demonstrated instigator. A projected $3.918 billion category, two technically capable founders, and three anonymous deployments do not yet clear that override.

By August 2029, I expect Perceptron ML to remain below $5 million ARR as a legal-AI implementation shop or to have pivoted into formal-verification tooling for regulated AI, rather than becoming an independent legal platform. I pass today. One result would flip me: a blinded third-party benchmark across at least five firms showing a 5x reduction in attorney hours with fewer than one material citation error per 10,000 generated claims at repeatable software economics.
